Emulsified Asphalt

Emulsified asphalt is a water‑based asphalt material composed of asphalt cement, water and an emulsifying agent that is supplied in PennDOT material classes such as TACK, NTT/CNTT, UTFCEM, CQS‑1hPM, etc., with each shipment accompanied by certification of temperature limits, handling and storage requirements, and where required a compatibility or stone‑coating test[1]. It functions as a binder that coats aggregate particles to create a uniform, tacky film which quickly cures to seal cracks, fill voids and ruts, provide a watertight joint, and furnish early load‑carrying capacity—especially when polymer‑modified—to enable surface treatments such as micro‑surfacing, seal‑coat, fog‑seal, prime‑coat or cold‑recycled base stabilization[2]. The material must be placed under specified temperature conditions (typically air, surface and aggregate above 60 °F for seal coats or at least 50 °F for polymer‑modified micro‑surfacing) so that the emulsion breaks and sets within one hour, after which traffic can be reopened[2].

Material purpose and applicability

Emulsified Asphalt is a water‑based asphalt material consisting of asphalt cement, water and an emulsifying agent, supplied in PennDOT material classes such as TACK, NTT/CNTT, UTFCEM, CQS‑1hPM, etc., and must be shipped with certification that specifies minimum and maximum mix or application temperature limits, handling and storage requirements, and where required a compatibility or stone‑coating test. In the polymer‑modified micro‑surfacing system described in the Pavement Policy Manual (section 5.4), polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt is applied to structurally sound pavements that do not require major base repair; it cures quickly, performs well under traffic when proper controls are maintained, and can be used to fill ruts, re‑profile a travel lane or restore skid resistance without a full structural overlay. Within the same micro‑surfacing system (section 5.4), the polymer‑modified emulsion is introduced after the first quarter of mixing so that it coats aggregate together with cement, additive and water, leaving a uniform skid‑resistant surface that seals cracks, fills voids and ruts, must set within one hour before compaction and traffic, and may serve as a scratch course, seal coat, leveling course or surface treatment. For tack‑coat, seal‑coat and fog‑seal operations (Sections 460, 470, 472), the emulsion is spread over existing pavement surfaces, curbs, gutters, vertical structures or joints to leave a uniform residual film that completely fills surface voids, provides a watertight joint and creates an adhesive layer into which subsequent coarse aggregate or hot‑mix overlay can embed; application is permitted only when air, surface and aggregate temperatures are above 60 °F (or when the air temperature is 40 °F and rising for tack coat) and rain is not imminent and freezing temperatures are not expected within 24 hours for seal coat or within 8 hours for fog‑seal, with a typical residual rate between 0.30 and 0.70 gallon per square yard. In cold‑recycled asphalt base courses (Sections 341, 342), emulsified asphalt is added to reclaimed material according to the approved mix design; it acts as a stabilizing agent that coats reclaimed aggregates, provides binding, enables the mixture to reach the specified moisture content and compaction, and must cure for at least two weeks before a wearing or binder course is placed; metering is performed with a positive‑displacement pump capable of flow rates down to 4 gallon per minute. The Pavement Policy Manual (section 5.13) likewise states that in cold‑recycled base‑course applications emulsified asphalt serves as the asphalt binder mixed with milled existing pavement or reclaimed aggregate to bind the material together, giving the mix early load‑carrying capacity, and that polymer‑modified emulsions improve the initial strength of the recycled mixture. For full‑depth reclamation projects (section 5.15), emulsified asphalt is used as an asphalt‑based additive applied after pulverization to bind and strengthen the reclaimed material, thereby providing structural stability to the rebuilt base layer, improving resistance to water‑related damage and enhancing fatigue resistance under traffic loading; it may be combined with granular materials, cement or lime for further performance improvement. In ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses (Section 489), polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt identified as UTFCEM is placed as the first thin membrane and immediately overlaid with hot‑mix asphalt concrete, serving as the foundational friction layer for the wearing course. For slurry seals (Section 482), emulsified asphalt of class SS‑1h or CSS‑1h is mixed with fine aggregate, water and optional filler to form a seal that binds the aggregate, seals cracks, fills voids, corrects surface erosion and provides a new wearing surface. In asphalt surface treatment (Section 480), emulsified asphalt is applied as the first layer at a rate of 0.25 to 0.50 gallon per square yard, followed by No. 67 coarse aggregate placed at 25 to 45 pounds per square yard and rolled; after curing the first application, a second application may be placed. For prime‑coat applications (Section 461), emulsified asphalt conditions and treats an absorbent non‑asphalt base course or existing surface, providing a binder film that prepares the substrate for subsequent layers. In concrete curing compounds (Section 711), emulsified asphalt classified as RS‑1 or CRS‑1 is used as the binder component of a black liquid membrane‑forming curing compound that meets ASTM C309 Type 4 performance requirements and serves to treat and cure concrete pavement surfaces. [1][2]

Emulsified asphalt is specified for use as a stabilizing agent in cold‑recycled asphalt base courses that are placed as compacted layers between 2 inches and 5 inches thick. The same material may also be incorporated as a metered additive in the Central Plant Mix Cold Recycled Asphalt Base Course, where it serves the identical stabilizing function. For these cold‑recycled applications the placement of emulsified‑asphalt‑stabilized material is prohibited from September 1 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0, 2‑0, 3‑0, 4‑0, 9‑0, and 10‑0, and from October 1 to April 30 in all other districts; placement may be permitted within those windows only when no freezing temperatures have occurred for 24 hours and the ambient air temperature is at least 45°F and rising. When the seasonal or temperature restrictions cannot be satisfied, another stabilizing material shall be selected. Polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt is considered appropriate for cold‑recycled base courses when the project experiences traffic volumes over 3,000 two‑lane AADT, a high percentage of truck traffic, or early/late season recycling where initial curing may be impaired, and when rapid strength development is desired. It should not be used on projects that carry significantly heavy truck traffic (200 or more daily ESALs), have poor drainage, contain underlying aggregates larger than 2 inches or excessive soils, or where traffic cannot be controlled or detoured during curing; in such cases a conventional hot‑mix base is preferred. Additionally, the cured base must remain below a 2 % moisture content before overlay, and a fog seal using an approved emulsified‑asphalt product may be applied to control dust or raveling. [1][2]

Composition and characteristics

In micro‑surfacing applications defined by Section 483, the emulsified asphalt is a Class CQS‑1hPM polymer‑modified emulsion that contains a latex‑based polymer modifier, water that must be free of harmful soluble salts, coarse and fine aggregates that meet the quality requirements of Bulletin 14 (with fine aggregate Type B or better and an appropriate SRL), and, when required, a filler such as non‑air‑entrained Type 1 Portland cement or hydrated lime to keep the percent by weight passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve within specification; additional manufacturer‑supplied additives are included to control set time and the aggregate‑filler‑emulsion proportions must follow the weight percentages given in Table A of that specification. During surface treatment as described in Section 480, the emulsified asphalt develops a tacky surface when applied at a rate of 0.25 gallon per square yard to 0.50 gallon per square yard, which permits the aggregate to adhere when backrolled. For slurry seals covered by Section 482, the emulsified asphalt is an emulsion of Class SS‑1h or CSS‑1h that is combined with fine aggregate, optional filler, and water that must be free of harmful soluble salts and have a hardness not exceeding 150 ppm (9 grains) unless softened; the mixture is designed and tested according to ASTM D3910 and must conform to the weight‑percentage limits set out in Table A, with dry aggregate rates ranging from 6 to 10 lb per square yard for Type I, 10 to 15 lb per square yard for Type II, or 15 lb per square yard or more for Type III applications. In the black liquid‑membrane forming curing compound specified in Section 711, emulsified asphalt is identified as either Class RS‑1 or CRS‑1 and must satisfy the performance requirements of ASTM C309 for Type 4; this classification defines its role within the curing compound rather than detailing its internal makeup. In cold‑recycled asphalt base courses, altering the emulsion composition by adding polymers provides improved initial strength, and the design guidelines specifically recommend polymer‑modified emulsions for situations that demand quicker stability or face curing challenges such as traffic volumes over 3,000 two‑lane AADT, a high percentage of truck traffic, early or late season recycling where initial curing may be impaired, and any areas where quick stabilities are desired. The mix‑design procedure also limits the types of emulsified asphalts that may be used on cold‑recycled base courses, so selecting a different source or proportioning of the emulsion directly influences whether these performance benefits can be realized. For full‑depth reclamation mixes, the mix‑design requires choosing both the type and the quantity of emulsified asphalt, and any change in composition or proportioning directly alters the stabilization benefits, modifying how well the treated layer resists water‑related damage, its fatigue resistance under traffic loads, and its compatibility with other additives such as granular material, cement, or lime. In seal‑coat applications, the performance of emulsified asphalt depends on its exact makeup; a change in the source or gradation of aggregate or a change in the type of asphalt obligates submission of a new seal‑coat design because such compositional variations can affect penetration, adhesion and durability. For cold‑in‑place recycled asphalt base courses, variations in the gradation or composition of reclaimed asphalt pavement may require on‑site adjustments to moisture and emulsion content to obtain satisfactory coating and the specified compaction, and a change in the source or performance grade of the asphalt binder mandates a new mix design because the original assumptions no longer apply. The proportioning of liquid components must be tightly controlled; equipment capable of automatically metering liquids with a variation of not more than ± 2.0 % by weight of liquids is required, adequate total liquids must be maintained to ensure thorough mixing, and water may be added at the mill head using a calibrated meter. In full‑depth reclamation, the residual asphalt rate is limited to between 0.02 and 0.10 gallons per square yard and the reclaimed layer must be kept at optimum moisture content without exceeding more than 3 %; if field conditions differ from the mix design, adjustments to water, additive or asphalt quantities are permitted to obtain a satisfactory material. For tack‑coat applications, the composition of emulsified asphalt is documented by reporting its asphalt residue content on the required Certificate of Compliance and Bill of Lading, and if the material remains stored for 30 days or longer after certification it must be resampled and retested at no additional cost to confirm compliance with Bulletin 25 requirements. In polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing systems, any change in the latex‑based polymer modifier or in the proportion of polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt directly influences the cure rate needed to open the surface to traffic within one hour, and aggregate source and gradation must meet the tables in Section 703; a change in sources of materials requires submission of a revised job‑mix formula before using any new material. Other additives supplied by the manufacturer are used to control mix set time when ambient conditions vary, and during construction polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt must be introduced only after the first quarter point of the mixer, with each material‑control device calibrated before production of each mix type. Placement requires a minimum atmospheric temperature of 50 °F to achieve a uniform consistency without skips, lumps or tears, and the mixture must set within one hour; if it takes longer a warning is issued, and if it exceeds one hour and twenty minutes placement must be stopped. If aggregate quality testing shows a percent‑within‑limits below 90 %, the lot is rejected, which can alter the final surface texture and increase the risk of excess flushing or uneven seams, and application rates outside acceptable ranges are considered defective work that may require an additional minimum of 15 pounds per square yard to satisfy performance criteria. For crushed‑aggregate base courses type DG, the water proportion used with emulsified asphalt may not vary by more than two percentage points from the optimum moisture; otherwise the compacted mixture may fall outside the required gradation limits, unsatisfactory results or a change in the supply source may require a change in the job‑mix formula, and control accuracy must be maintained so that at least 90 % of sampled mixtures remain within the tolerance limits defined in Table A. Key material properties

The mechanical strength of emulsified‑asphalt cold recycled mixtures is low when first placed and only increases as the mixture cures for at least one week under favorable weather characterized by low humidity and high temperature [1]. During that curing period the structural strength builds, allowing the mix to achieve the stability required before a wearing surface is applied because the mixes are less resistant to abrasion than hot‑asphalt pavements [1]. Use of polymer‑modified emulsions improves the initial strength and helps attain quicker stability, which is especially beneficial when early or late season recycling may impair curing [1]. For seal‑coat applications the air, surface and aggregate temperatures must be above 60 °F before placement, and rolling pressure must be maintained between 40 psi and 50 psi while the roller speed does not exceed 10 mph to ensure that at least half of the coarse aggregate becomes embedded [2]. The applied thickness for seal coat work must remain within ±10 % of the design rate, and placement should be avoided when rain is imminent or freezing temperatures are expected within 24 hours after application [2]. Fog‑seal operations require the same temperature condition (>60 °F) and must not be placed if freezing is anticipated within eight hours; additionally the underlying seal coat must have cured for at least two days before fog sealing, and a residual asphalt rate between 0.02 and 0.05 gal/yd² is targeted to obtain adequate film thickness [2]. In cold‑recycled base‑course work the mixture moisture content must be less than 2 % prior to overlay, the compacted layer must achieve at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density, and the emulsified‑asphalt metering system may vary no more than ±2.0 % by weight; a continuous temperature display on the hot‑asphalt tank is required for monitoring thermal conditions during placement [2]. Tack‑coat applications require an air temperature of 40 °F and rising, a dry surface to permit emulsion breakage, and the asphalt residue content shown on the bill of lading together with a residual rate tolerance of 0.02 gal/yd² is used to control film thickness [2]. Ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses specify that the Class UTFCEM emulsified asphalt must meet the prescribed asphalt content by mass and aggregate gradations expressed as percent passing the 75 µm, 2.36 mm and 6.3 mm sieves [2]. Full‑depth reclamation stabilized with emulsified asphalt calls for a moisture condition of either 50 % of the design optimum moisture content or 3 % total moisture, whichever occurs first, compaction to at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum density at optimum moisture, an indirect tensile strength not less than 50 psi and a seven‑day compressive strength not less than 280 psi [2]. Certification and handling requirements define minimum and maximum mix or application temperature limits, storage conditions, and temperature‑dependent viscosity measured at 275 °F and 329 °F (expressed in Pa·s or centipoises), as well as specific gravity at 60 °F, percent asphalt residue by weight, and a compatibility/stone‑coating test to verify coating ability and water resistance; the asphalt residue content must be documented on the Certificate of Compliance, and if the material is stored for 30 days or longer after certification a new sample must be taken and retested [2]. Within polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing systems the latex‑based polymer modifier must enable the mix to cure fast enough so that traffic can be placed on the pavement within one hour, the mixture must set within one hour and not exceed one hour twenty minutes, a laboratory test strip is required to show a set time no greater than ten minutes, and mechanical strength is achieved by compacting the micro‑surfacing with a pneumatic‑tire roller at pressures of 40–60 psi before opening to traffic [2]. Physical quality limits for the micro‑surfacing include excess flushing not greater than five percent of the surface, clumps no larger than 1½ in., and longitudinal ridges not exceeding one‑quarter inch, all of which contribute to a uniform finished texture [2]. For slurry seal mixes the water incorporated in the emulsion must be free of harmful soluble salts and its hardness may not exceed 150 ppm (9 grains); any excess must be reduced with a softener, and the emulsified asphalt itself must conform to class SS‑1h or CSS‑1h [2]. Chemical stability is further indicated by the fact that water may be sprayed into the spreader box without harming the mix, and placement of the micro‑surfacing must occur when the atmospheric temperature is at least 50 °F throughout the entire placement, with application prohibited if rain is imminent or freezing temperatures are expected within 24 hours after application [2]. Seal‑coat applications using RAP require that the existing roadway surface exposed to direct sunlight be below 120 °F, and placement must be stopped if the material begins to stick to delivery‑truck tires until the temperature falls below that limit [2]. The effectiveness of a second‑application emulsified asphalt treatment relies on its ability to remain tacky so that coarse aggregate adheres during backrolling, and the backrolling pressure must be between 40 psi and 50 psi to obtain proper bonding [2].

The Pavement Policy Manual classifies polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt used in micro‑surfacing by material type, designating mixes as Type A or Type B which are presented as alternatives to 4.75 mm Superpave scratch courses and are selected according to the guidance in Table 5.2. [1] The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications further classify emulsified asphalt for seal‑coat, fog‑seal, ultra‑thin bonded wearing course, and full‑depth reclamation applications by product‑type codes such as MS‑2, CMS‑2, SS‑1, CSS‑1 and related variants, requiring conformity with Bulletin 25; analogous class designations are used for ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses (UTFCEM) and full‑depth reclamation where allowable classes include CMS‑2, SS‑1h, CSS‑1h, SS‑1hP, HFMS‑2, etc., as well as Class AEP, E‑1 Prime, or EDP. Behavior and mechanisms

In cold‑recycled mixtures that employ emulsified asphalt, the structural strength is lower at construction and increases as the mixture cures over several weeks; a minimum of one week of curing under favorable weather conditions (low humidityhigh temperature) is required before a wearing course can be placed. Because these mixes are less resistant to abrasion by traffic than hot‑mix asphalt, they are not suitable for high‑ADT roads until sufficient strength has developed or detours are provided. Incorporating polymer‑modified emulsions raises the initial strength and is recommended when traffic volumes exceed 3,000 two‑lane AADT, truck percentages are high, or early/late season recycling may impair curing, thereby providing quicker stability. When emulsified asphalt is used for full‑depth reclamation, it acts as a stabilizer that improves fatigue resistance to repeated loading and also protects the reclaimed material from water‑related damage, increasing moisture durability. In Section 344—FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION, the layer must cure for seven days to develop at least 50 psi indirect tensile strength, and heavy traffic is prohibited until that strength is attained; limited local light vehicular traffic may be allowed once a stable condition is observed and no damage appears at speeds less than 10 mph. In Section 483—POLYMER‑MODIFIED EMULSIFIED ASPHALT PAVING SYSTEM (MICRO SURFACING), after setting the surface is compacted with a pneumatic‑tire roller before traffic to prevent pick‑up under loading. The stabilized layer must be kept damp after placement either by daily water application or by applying an emulsified‑asphalt primer followed by a fog seal at a residual rate between 0.02 and 0.10 gal/yd²; before overlay the moisture content may not exceed 50 % of the design optimum moisture content or 3 % total moisture content, whichever is reached first. The material will only penetrate and bond effectively when the air, surface, and aggregate temperatures are above 60 °F; if rain is imminent or freezing temperatures are expected within 24 hours after placement, application must be avoided. In Section 483—POLYMER‑MODIFIED EMULSIFIED ASPHALT PAVING SYSTEM (MICRO SURFACING), the mixture is highly sensitive to moisture, so application must be avoided if rain is imminent or freezing conditions are expected within 24 hours after placement, and any mixture that becomes wet before it sets must be removed and replaced. Water may be sprayed into the spreader box during spreading without harming the mix. Placement is permitted only when the entire surface can achieve satisfactory penetration and adhesion and the ambient temperature does not fall below 50 °F for the whole placement period; temperatures lower than this are prohibited even for night work. Seal coat may be placed only when the sun‑heated surface temperature is below 120 °F, and if the material becomes tacky enough to adhere to delivery‑truck tires placement must be halted until the roadway cools back below that limit. The mixture must properly set within one hour of placement and may not exceed a set time of one hour and twenty minutes; if this limit is exceeded placement operations must be stopped. Service criteria deem excess emulsified asphalt flushing greater than 5 % of the finished surface area, bleeding at joints, or washboard/ripple patterns exceeding 100 linear feet unacceptable. Shaded areas may require longer set times because cooling slows curing. [1][2]

In cold‑recycled base courses the behavior of emulsified asphalt is controlled primarily by a physical curing process that raises structural strength over several weeks, and a minimum of one week of curing under low humidity and high temperature is required before a wearing course can be placed; polymer‑modified emulsions provide improved initial strength of the recycled mix, especially when traffic volumes exceed 3,000 two‑lane AADT or when early/late season conditions could impair cure, while the mixes are less resistant to abrasion than hot mixes and only achieve adequate load‑bearing capacity after curing, making them unsuitable for high ADT roads without detours until sufficient strength is obtained[1]. Full‑depth reclamation relies on the coating action of the emulsion to create a barrier that limits water ingress and an elastic film that improves fatigue resistance under repeated traffic loads; the selection of type and amount of emulsified asphalt, together with other stabilizers such as granular material, cement or lime, further influences these physical and mechanical effects[1]. For seal‑coat applications (Section 470) the material may only be placed when the entire surface is in condition to allow satisfactory penetration and adhesion and when air, surface and aggregate temperatures are above 60 °F; rain must not be imminent and freezing temperatures must not be expected within 24 hours after placement, and mechanical handling controls performance because the seal coat must be placed at the design rate within ±10 % of that rate, coarse aggregate is spread immediately, rolling begins within five minutes using pneumatic‑tire rollers that travel at a speed of less than or equal to 10 mph, apply a contact pressure from 40 psi to 50 psi, and make at least two passes before traffic is allowed[2]. Fog‑seal applications (Section 472) require the same temperature requirement of above 60 °F, rain must not be imminent and freezing temperatures must not be expected within 8 hours after application, the underlying seal coat must have cured a minimum of 2 days before fog sealing, and a calibrated distributor targets a residual asphalt rate between 0.02 to 0.05 gal/yd² on clean, dry pavement[2]. Tack‑coat performance (Section 460) is governed primarily by the physical condition of the substrate and ambient temperature; it may only be placed when the air temperature is 40 °F and rising and the existing surface is dry, allowing the emulsion to break and set without disturbance, and a uniform distributing pressure with an application rate tolerance of 0.02 gal/yd² is required to achieve a consistent residual film[2]. Prime‑coat behavior (Section 461) is controlled by the ability of the emulsion to physically penetrate and be absorbed by the treated surface followed by chemical curing that locks the binder in place; application rates between 0.30 gal/yd² and 0.70 gal/yd² determine how much material is available for this penetration‑absorption process, and mechanical handling such as avoiding disturbance after placement ensures the cured film remains intact[2]. In cold‑recycled base courses (Sections 341, 342) the performance of an emulsified‑asphalt stabilized mix is governed by precise liquid dosing with equipment that meters liquids with a variation of not more than ±2.0 % by weight, thorough mixing, moisture control, and curing time; the layer must be cured for at least 2 weeks or until its average moisture content drops below 2 %, after which it is compacted to a minimum of 96 % of the PTM 402 target density[2]. Central‑plant mixing (Section 342) relies on a positive displacement pump that can meter additive flow down to 4 gallons per minute and equipment that automatically meters liquids with a variation of not more than +2.0 % by weight, providing precise dosage of emulsified asphalt and water; adequate total liquid is then added with calibrated meters to achieve thorough mixing and aid compaction while avoiding stripping of the asphalt material, and uniform injection of water into hot binder together with continuous mixing controls the physical homogeneity that dictates workability and final strength[2]. Full‑depth reclamation (Section 344) is dominated by moisture control; the reclaimed mix must be kept at optimum moisture content and for emulsified‑asphalt stabilized layers the moisture should be at 50 % of the design optimum or 3 % total moisture content, whichever occurs first, before overlay, and the mixture must be compacted to a minimum density of at least 95 % of the laboratory‑compacted maximum density; chemically, the emulsion is applied as a prime material followed by an asphalt fog seal at a residual asphalt rate between 0.02 and 0.10 gal/yd², providing the binding action that stabilizes the aggregate matrix, while mechanically the mixture must be compacted to achieve a compressive strength of at least 280 psi[2]. In polymer‑modified micro‑surfacing (Section 483) the chemical mechanism is provided by a latex polymer that forms a rapid‑setting film, water and field‑set‑time additives dictate how quickly the emulsion breaks and cures, physically a uniformly graded aggregate and filler skeleton created to the specified weight percentages supports traffic within one hour after mixing, the atmospheric temperature must be at least 50 °F for the entire operation and the mix may not be applied if rain is imminent or freezing temperatures are expected within 24 hours after application, and mechanically the squeegee box must generate sufficient turbulence to keep the mix from setting in the box or forming lumps, flexible front and rear seals prevent loss of material during spreading, pneumatic‑tire rollers operating at tire pressures of 40 psi to 60 psi are required before traffic opening to develop the intended surface strength and texture; the curing window must be achieved within one hour and may not exceed one hour twenty minutes[2]. For pervious asphalt pavement systems (Section 420) performance is governed by temperature, compaction and formulation controls: placement must occur when the air or surface temperature is at least 50 °F, the coating is applied as a thin, uniform film using specified AASHTO emulsions in two or more coats to achieve adhesion, and subsequent compaction of the pavement must be limited to no more than four passes with smooth steel‑wheeled static rollers so that surface porosity is not reduced[2]. During a second application of emulsified asphalt (Section 473) performance is controlled by how much emulsion is laid down (0.25 gal/yd² to 0.50 gal/yd²) and the quantity of No. 8 coarse aggregate spread (15 lb/yd² to 30 lb/yd²); when the emulsion develops a tacky surface, its chemical stickiness enables the aggregate to bond, but this adhesion is only achieved after mechanical action: pneumatic‑tire rollers are used at a contact pressure of 40 psi to 50 psi and, if needed, backrolling continues until the aggregate adheres[2]. In seal‑coat work with aggregate from RAP (Section 473) placement is governed primarily by the temperature of the existing pavement; the material must be applied only when the sun‑heated surface is below 120 °F, and if the emulsion begins to adhere to the tires of delivery trucks the operation must be stopped until the surface cools again[2].

Durability and deterioration

Cold‑recycled base courses that rely on emulsified asphalt develop a lower structural strength at construction and therefore require a minimum of one week of curing under favorable weather—low humidity and high temperature—before a wearing course can be placed [1]. Because these mixtures are less resistant to abrasion by traffic, surface damage may occur if traffic is allowed before adequate strength develops, especially on roads with high average daily traffic or heavy truck traffic [1]. The higher void content of such mixes creates durability concerns, making the placement of an impermanent wearing surface essential [1]. Polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended when traffic volumes exceed 3,000 two‑lane AADT, a high percentage of trucks is present, or early/late season recycling may impair initial curing, since they provide improved initial strength [1].

For emulsified‑asphalt seal coats the material must be placed only when air, surface and aggregate temperatures are above 60 °F and rain is not imminent; application shall be avoided if freezing temperatures are expected within 24 hours after placement because insufficient curing before traffic can cause distortion, flushing of the emulsion to the surface and loss of coarse aggregate, especially in shaded areas that may require longer set times [2]. If less than 50 percent of the seal‑coat aggregate becomes embedded, a fog seal may be required to restore performance, and fog seals themselves must be placed only when air, surface and aggregate temperatures exceed 60 °F, rain is not imminent and freezing temperatures are not expected within eight hours after placement; the underlying seal coat must cure at least two days before the fog seal is applied and the fog seal must fully cure—particularly in shaded areas—before traffic is allowed, otherwise premature loading can damage the emulsion [2].

Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses stabilized with emulsified asphalt require a minimum cure period of two weeks or an average moisture content less than 2 percent before a wearing or binder course is placed; failure to meet this condition can lead to raveling or loss of stability [2]. Damage from construction equipment such as marring, distortion or edge scuffing must be corrected immediately, pilot‑car speed control is required when raveling is likely, and compaction shall reach a minimum density of 96 percent of the target density to avoid further deterioration [2]. Placement of emulsified‑asphalt cold‑recycled base courses is prohibited during the colder months specified for each district; it may proceed only when freezing temperatures have not occurred for at least 24 hours and the ambient air temperature is 45 °F or higher, because freeze‑induced damage is a primary concern [2].

Emulsified‑asphalt tack coats shall be placed only when the air temperature is 40 °F and rising and the existing surface is dry; otherwise the emulsion may not break and set properly, and after placement the treated surface shall be protected from disturbance or physical damage until curing is sufficient to minimize tracking, with any uncoated, lightly coated or excess‑material areas corrected before the next pavement course is laid, because failure can lead to loss of bond strength and premature deterioration [2]. The emulsified‑asphalt prime coat must be left undisturbed after placement so that it can fully penetrate and cure; premature disturbance can compromise its integrity, and once cured the treated surface must be maintained and protected against damage, with any damaged areas repaired to the Representative’s satisfaction before subsequent construction layers are placed [2].

For full‑depth reclamation stabilized with emulsified asphalt the layer shall be kept damp and its moisture content should be at 50 percent of the design optimum moisture content or 3 percent total moisture content, whichever is reached first, before overlay; excessive working of the chemically stabilized material may detrimentally affect the ultimate strength of the stabilized layer, and compressive strengths greater than 900 pounds per square inch may result in shrinkage cracking requiring rework [2]. Heavy traffic must not be allowed on the reclaimed material during the seven‑day cure period because it can cause damage [2].

Polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing may be placed only when the ambient temperature is at least 50 °F, rain is not imminent and the mixture may not be exposed to freezing temperatures within 24 hours after placement; seasonal restrictions prohibit application from September 16 to April 30 in certain districts and from October 1 to April 30 in others, because failure to meet these limits can cause the emulsion to set prematurely or lose adhesion, leading to loss of uniformity, side‑build up, lumps or inadequate skid resistance [2]. The specification requires proper setting within one hour and mandates stopping placement when set time exceeds one hour and twenty minutes; defects that indicate deterioration include excess flushing greater than 5 % of the finished surface area, bleeding at joints, tear or drag marks beyond the size limits, clumps larger than 1½ in, washboard or ripple patterns exceeding 100 linear feet, longitudinal ridges or depressions over a ¼‑inch measured with a straightedge, seams with bumps greater than a ¼‑inch, edge placement variance beyond ±3 in per 100 ft, and rut depressions detectable with a 12‑foot straightedge [2]. The principal durability issue for emulsified‑asphalt seal coats is premature traffic loading; the seal coat must be given sufficient time to completely cure before the road is opened to all traffic because if this curing period is not observed the still‑wet coating can be damaged or stripped, compromising its protective function, and when required the surface should be swept with a power broom to remove loose chips before and after opening the road to traffic [2].

Polymer‑modified emulsions provide improved initial strength and thereby reduce deterioration in cold‑recycled mixes, especially when traffic volumes exceed 3,000 two‑lane AADT, the percentage of truck traffic is high, recycling occurs early or late in the season where curing may be impaired, or rapid stability is required [1]. Conversely, cold‑recycled mixtures that have higher void content and lower abrasion resistance are more prone to damage and therefore require a wearing surface; such mixes must receive at least one week of curing under favorable weather (low humidity and high temperature) or their strength development – and thus their resistance to deterioration – may be compromised [1]. Emulsified asphalt performs best when the air, surface, and aggregate temperatures are above 60 °F; lower temperatures may impede penetration and adhesion and increase the risk of deterioration [2]. Application shall not be made if, in the Representative’s opinion, rain is imminent or freezing temperatures are expected within 24 hours after placement because moisture and freeze‑thaw cycles promote loss of bond [2]. Use of dry or only slightly damp aggregates that do not contain excessive free moisture reduces the chance of flushing or stripping [2]. The aggregate should be rolled into the emulsion within five minutes at a speed no greater than 10 mph with contact pressure between 40 and 50 psi to ensure rapid embedment and stability; in shaded areas longer set times may be required to avoid premature traffic loading that could cause surface distortion [2]. For fog‑seal applications the emulsion shall be placed only after the air, surface, and aggregate temperatures have risen above 60 °F, when rain is not imminent and freezing temperatures are not expected within eight hours after application; the underlying seal coat must be cured for a minimum of two days before fog sealing and the emulsified asphalt should be allowed to cure fully before traffic [2]. Maintaining the residual asphalt rate between 0.02 and 0.05 gal per square yard and applying fine aggregate within five minutes of emulsion placement also supports durability [2]. In cold‑recycled asphalt base courses susceptibility to deterioration is reduced when the mix retains adequate total liquids for thorough coating, when it is cured for at least two weeks before placing the wearing or binder course or until the average moisture content is less than 2 %, and when placement occurs only after no freezing temperatures have occurred for 24 hours and the ambient air temperature is 45 °F and rising [2]. Placement must not occur from September 1 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0, 2‑0, 3‑0, 4‑0, 9‑0, and 10‑0 and from October 1 to April 30 in all other districts; otherwise deterioration risk increases [2]. For chemically stabilized full‑depth reclamation the susceptibility rises if the cured compressive strength exceeds 900 lb per square inch because shrinkage cracking may then occur [2]. Keeping the reclaimed layer damp by daily water or an emulsified‑asphalt prime followed by a fog seal, and maintaining moisture at 50 % of the design optimum or 3 % total whichever is reached first (no more than 3 % above optimum) before overlay helps limit cracking [2]. The tack coat’s susceptibility to deterioration is reduced when it is placed only under conditions that meet the specified temperature and moisture limits, namely an air temperature of 40 °F and rising on a dry existing surface, and when the coating is applied at a uniform rate that achieves the required residual asphalt content; using a calibrated distributor that can control application pressure and rate helps maintain this uniform residual rate while protecting the treated surface from damage and allowing the coat to break and set without disturbance further limits degradation [2]. In joint sealing operations susceptibility rises when the joint surface contains loose or harmful material, when the applied coat does not fully fill voids, or when traffic is allowed before the seal has cooled to a stable temperature; conversely, cleaning the area, controlling the application rate so that residual asphalt completely fills surface voids and provides a watertight joint, using two or more applications if necessary, removing excess asphalt material and immediately covering the sealed area with a light application of dry sand, and ensuring newly compacted courses are not loaded until they have uniformly cooled to 140 °F or less all serve to lower deterioration risk [2]. Applying a thin, uniform coating of the specified emulsified‑asphalt types (AASHTO SS‑1, CSS‑1, SS‑1h, CSS‑1h, or TACK) in two or more applications on vertical surfaces helps protect those interfaces and reduces deterioration; a tack coat shall not be used between lifts of any pervious asphalt [2]. Placement should be avoided when the air or surface temperature is lower than 50 °F because colder conditions increase the risk of premature failure; compaction must be limited to not more than four passes to preclude a reduction in surface porosity, and the material must not be over‑compacted to the point that it is not free draining or the aggregate is crushed [2]. In slurry‑seal applications the durability of the emulsion is reduced when the mixing water contains harmful soluble salts or has a hardness greater than 150 ppm (9 grains); keeping the water free from such salts and below that hardness reduces the likelihood of emulsion breakdown and subsequent deterioration [2]. For polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing susceptibility to premature deterioration rises when the mixture is placed at atmospheric temperatures below the required minimum, when rain is imminent, or when freezing temperatures are expected within 24 hours after placement; the specification requires the atmospheric temperature to be at least 50°F for the entire placement, forbids application if rain is imminent or if freezing temperatures are expected within 24 hours after application, and mandates removal and replacement of any rained‑on mixture before it sets [2]. No application of the polymer‑modified system is permitted from September 16 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0, 2‑0, 3‑0, 4‑0, 10‑0 and 5‑0, and from October 1 to April 30 in Districts 6‑0, 8‑0, 9‑0, 11‑0, 12‑0 and 5‑0; excess emulsified asphalt (flushing) greater than 5 % of the finished surface area is not allowed, and lumps, balls, unmixed aggregate or foreign objects larger than 1½ inches are also prohibited [2]. Maintaining the mixture within the prescribed set‑time—properly setting within one hour of placement and not exceeding one hour and twenty minutes—and allowing water to be sprayed into the spreader box without harming the mix help maintain performance [2]. Proper compaction with a pneumatic‑tire roller at 40 psi to 60 psi and achieving uniform application rates also reduce the likelihood of deterioration [2]. For seal‑coat applications allowing sufficient time for the emulsified asphalt seal coat to completely cure before opening the road to all traffic reduces susceptibility to damage, while sweeping the surface with a power broom to remove loose chips before and after traffic exposure helps protect the coating [2].

Material interactions and compatibility

Emulsified asphalt used for full‑depth reclamation provides a stabilizing binder that improves resistance to water‑related damage, which helps mitigate stripping between binder and aggregate, and it works well with other additives such as granular materials, cement, and lime, indicating good compatibility when these components are incorporated into the reclaimed mix. [1] In cold‑recycled asphalt base courses the emulsified asphalt is added to the reclaimed material in accordance with the approved mix design, and the reclaimed material, aggregates and emulsion are combined at the mix‑design‑specified optimum moisture and emulsion content so that the binder can adequately coat the aggregate particles; when RAP gradations or composition differ markedly the quality‑control plan permits field adjustments to obtain satisfactory coating and the specified compaction, and adequate total liquids must be maintained in the mixture, with additional water added at the mill head using a calibrated meter if necessary. [2] A pugmill mixer equipped with a volumetric liquid‑additive system and spray‑bar controls the additive based on milling depth, width and speed, ensuring consistent distribution of the emulsified binder throughout the reclaimed aggregate. [2] After placement the emulsified‑asphalt‑stabilized base must cure for at least 2 weeks or until the average moisture content is less than 2 percent before any wearing or binder course is installed, allowing the binder to develop adequate adhesion with the aggregates and preventing stripping. [2] In central‑plant mix operations a positive‑displacement pump capable of metering the additive down to 4 gallons per minute is used, and all liquid‑metering equipment must stay within + 2.0 % by weight of liquids; the plant’s mixing train—including RAP feed hopper, pugmill and spray bars—must produce a homogeneous blend of reclaimed aggregate, asphalt material and water without stripping the asphalt material, thereby preserving adhesion between binder and aggregate. [2] For asphalt surface‑treatment construction the emulsified asphalt must be placed only when the existing pavement surface, surrounding air and aggregate are above 60 °F so that satisfactory material penetration and adhesion can occur; within five minutes after application a single layer of coarse aggregate is spread and rolled at a speed not exceeding 10 mph with a contact pressure of 40 psi to 50 psi, and if less than 50 percent of the aggregate becomes embedded the Representative may require a fog‑seal overlay. Influence of production and placement

Plant mixing, production and placement temperatures for emulsified asphalt must be kept within the minimum and maximum mix or application temperature requirements documented for each shipment because those limits govern the material’s viscosity and coating ability (Section 702) [2]. The temperature‑viscosity chart supplied with each lot provides the required laboratory mixing and compaction temperature ranges based on rotational viscosities measured at 275 °F and 329 °F, and polymer‑modified grades follow supplier‑recommended mixing and compaction temperature ranges (Section 702) [2]. Handling and storage practices must keep the shipment container free of contamination before loading (Section 702) [2]. For seal‑coat applications the air, surface and aggregate temperatures must be above 60 °F; rain must not be imminent and freezing temperatures must not be expected within 24 hours after application (Section 470) [2]. Fog‑seal placement also requires that the air, surface and aggregate temperatures be above 60 °F, but placement is prohibited if rain is imminent or freezing temperatures are expected within eight hours, and the underlying seal coat must have cured a minimum of two days before fog sealing (Section 472) [2]. For pervious asphalt mixtures that incorporate emulsified asphalt placement is prohibited when the air or surface temperature is lower than 50 °F (Section 420) [2]. Polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing must be placed only when the atmospheric temperature is at least 50 °F for the entire operation, and any imminent rain or a forecast of freezing within 24 hours after application will prevent satisfactory penetration and adhesion (Section 483) [2]. Cold‑recycled asphalt base course placed in‑place may be mixed and placed only when ambient air is at least 45 °F and rising with no freezing temperatures during the preceding 24 hours; otherwise placement is prohibited within the seasonal windows (Section 341) [2]. Tack‑coat applications may be applied only when the air temperature is 40 °F and rising and the existing surface is dry (Section 460) [2]. During mixing adequate liquid content must be maintained to ensure thorough coating of reclaimed material, and water may be added as needed to achieve this condition (Sections 341, 344) [2]. After each lift of a pervious mixture the mix should be allowed to cool to about 100 °F before the next layer is laid, which promotes adequate setting without premature hardening; lifts are restricted to a maximum four‑inch compacted thickness (Section 420) [2]. Immediately after placement of a seal coat a single layer of coarse aggregate must be spread and rolled within five minutes; rollers shall travel at a speed less than or equal to 10 mph and apply a contact pressure from 40 psi to 50 psi to obtain proper embedment, and the surface must be protected from traffic until the material obtains adequate stability, adhesion and sufficient curing (Section 470) [2]. For the second application of an emulsified‑asphalt surface treatment compaction must be performed with pneumatic‑tire rollers delivering a contact pressure of 40 psi to 50 psi, and when the emulsion surface is tacky backrolling with a pneumatic‑tire or steel‑wheel tandem roller until the aggregate adheres promotes proper bonding; vibratory rolling is prohibited (Section 480) [2]. Compaction of cold‑recycled base course must reach at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density, and if this level cannot be obtained additional scarifying, moisture adjustment or re‑compaction is required (Section 341) [2]. Full‑depth reclamation compaction must achieve a minimum of 95 % of the laboratory maximum density within four hours of the water/additive mixing operation (Section 344) [2]. Compaction of pervious mixtures must be performed with smooth steel‑wheeled static rollers and limited to no more than four passes, preserving the designed porosity and preventing aggregate crushing that would degrade drainage performance (Section 420) [2]. Finishing should shape the surface without excessive working because over‑working the chemically stabilized mix may detrimentally affect its ultimate strength (Section 344) [2]. Proper spreading and finishing must create a uniform surface without lumps or excess material; water may be sprayed into the spreader box to aid spreading without harming the mix (Section 483) [2]. Continuous aggregate delivery, as required by an approved Aggregate Delivery Plan that specifies enough trucks to keep the micro‑surfacing machine moving without start‑stop cycles, helps maintain a uniform mix (Section 483) [2]. Newly compacted courses must not be subjected to traffic or loads for 24 hours or until the course uniformly cools to a temperature of 140 °F or less; the same restriction applies when both lanes that form a longitudinal joint are placed on the same day (Section 413) [2]. After placement tack coat must be allowed to break and set without disturbance before paving resumes, and fog‑seal layers must be allowed to cure fully with permanent pavement markings deferred until three days after the last fog‑seal day (Sections 460, 472) [2]. Cold‑recycled base course must cure for at least one week under favorable weather conditions (low humidity–high temperature) before placing the wearing course, and strength continues to increase over several weeks, improving resistance to traffic abrasion; early‑stage loads are unsuitable for high ADT roads unless detours are provided until adequate strength is obtained (Section 5.13) [1]. Cold‑recycled base course must also cure for at least two weeks or until the average moisture content falls below 2 % before any wearing or binder course is installed (Section 341) [2]. Full‑depth reclamation requires keeping the layer damp—by daily water or an emulsified‑asphalt primer followed by a fog seal—and curing must continue until the seven‑day strength requirement is met, with the rate influenced by weather (Section 344) [2]. Polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt for micro‑surfacing is formulated to cure fast enough for traffic to be placed within one hour without damaging the surface; each mix design includes a defined design cure time and manufacturers may supply additives that control set time in the field to accommodate varying ambient environmental conditions, thereby influencing final performance (Section 483) [2]. The mixture is required to set within one hour of placement, and if it exceeds one hour and twenty minutes to properly set, placement must be stopped (Section 483) [2]. For emulsified‑asphalt seal coats sufficient time must be allowed for the seal coat to completely cure before opening the road to all traffic (Section 471) [2].

Construction‑related variations that most strongly influence the long‑term behavior of emulsified asphalt are described in a number of specifications. In the Pavement Policy Manual, Section 5.13 COLD RECYCLED ASPHALT BASE COURSES > C, the mixture is highly sensitive to insufficient curing, inadequate compaction thickness, improper reclaimed‑asphalt‑pavement (RAP) composition, exposure to traffic before the mix has gained strength, and failure to use polymer‑modified emulsion when required; the mixture must cure at least one week under low humidity and high temperature before a wearing course is placed, compacted depth must stay between three and five inches, and if the RAP lacks coarse aggregate the mix must be supplemented with virgin coarse material (No. 8, 57, or 67) to meet design specifications; placing the mix on roads with heavy truck traffic or high AADT before cure, or neglecting polymer‑modified emulsions where traffic exceeds 3,000 two‑lane AADT, can lead to premature abrasion and loss of strength [1]. Selection, specification, and improvement

The choice of emulsified asphalt is first limited to the material classes enumerated in Section 702; every shipment must be accompanied by a Certificate of Compliance and a Bill of Lading, and any batch that remains stored for thirty days or more must be resampled and retested in accordance with Bulletin 25 [2]. For tack‑coat applications the material shall be one of the Section 702 classes, applied with equipment calibrated to control the residual rate within a tolerance of 0.02 gallon per square yard; placement may occur only when the air temperature is at least forty degrees Fahrenheit and rising and the existing surface is dry [2]. Prime‑coat applications require an application rate between 0.30 and 0.70 gallon per square yard after the specific rate has been approved, and the treated surface must remain undisturbed until the inspector declares it fully cured [2]. When emulsified asphalt is placed on roller‑compacted concrete bases the same tack‑coat requirements of Section 460.3(b) apply, and the equipment used must be approved to avoid damaging the RCC surface [2]. Ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses require Class UTFCEM as defined in Bulletin 25, sourced from an approved producer listed in Bulletin 15, and must be accompanied by quality‑control testing and certification per Sections 106.03(b) and 702.1(b)1 [2]. In full‑depth reclamation projects the stabilizing additive is selected after comparing the existing layer composition with Publication 242; acceptable classes include CMS‑2, SS‑1h, CSS‑1h, SS‑1hP, HFMS‑2 or CSS‑1hP, and the residual asphalt rate for the prime material and fog seal must fall between 0.02 and 0.10 gallon per square yard; application must occur while the mix is at optimum moisture content but not exceeding three percent, and before overlay the base moisture shall be either fifty percent of design optimum or three percent total, whichever occurs first, with the cured base required to retain a minimum indirect tensile strength of fifty pounds per square inch [2]. For sealing notched wedge joints the contractor may specify emulsified asphalt only if it is PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT; the sealant shall be applied evenly at least six inches on each side of the joint and at a rate that completely fills surface voids to create a watertight joint [2]. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses must use an emulsified asphalt selected from the grades MS‑2, CMS‑2, SS‑1, CSS‑1, SS‑1h, CSS‑1h, HFMS‑2h, HFMS‑2, HFMS‑2s, HFMS‑2P, SS‑1hP or CSS‑1hP, each conforming to Bulletin 25 and the approved mix design; mixing and application equipment must meter the liquid within plus or minus two percent by weight, placement is limited to periods when no freezing has occurred for at least twenty‑four hours and ambient temperature is forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or higher and rising, and the stabilized base must cure for at least two weeks or until average moisture content falls below two percent before any wearing or binder course is placed [2]. Manual asphalt patching requires the emulsified asphalt used to seal patch edges to match either Class TACK or NTT, the same PG‑binder employed in the existing pavement, or the specific grade PG 64S‑22 to ensure compatibility with the existing binder [2]. When a central plant mixes a cold‑recycled asphalt base the plant must provide a positive‑displacement pump capable of delivering as low as four gallons per minute and a liquid‑metering system that keeps weight variation within plus two percent; the plant shall supply a metered emulsified‑asphalt delivery line together with cement and water additive systems so that reclaimed material can be mixed without stripping the binder, maintaining sufficient total liquids for thorough blending [2]. For polymer‑modified micro‑surfacing the emulsion shall be Class CQS‑1hPM from Bulletin 25, sourced from an approved producer with certification per Sections 106.03(b) and 702.1(b)1; a latex‑based polymer modifier is required to achieve a set time of no more than one hour (maximum one hour twenty minutes), and a test strip must demonstrate that traffic can be opened within one hour while the maximum set time does not exceed ten minutes per ISSA TB‑102; application may proceed only when the surface can achieve satisfactory penetration and adhesion, ambient temperature is at least fifty degrees Fahrenheit throughout placement, rain is not imminent and freezing temperatures are not expected within twenty‑four hours, and seasonal restrictions prohibit placement from September 16 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0, 2‑0, 3‑0, 4‑0, 10‑0, 5‑0 and from October 1 to April 30 in Districts 6‑0, 8‑0, 9‑0, 11‑0, 12‑0, 5‑0 [2]. In pervious asphalt pavement systems emulsified asphalt is used to coat vertical surfaces that will contact the mix; a thin, uniform film shall be applied in two or more passes using one of the AASHTO grades SS‑1, CSS‑1, SS‑1h, CSS‑1h, or TACK, and it shall not be used as a tack coat between lifts of pervious asphalt [2]. Second‑application surface treatments require an emulsified asphalt application rate of 0.25 to 0.50 gallon per square yard followed by spreading No. 8 coarse aggregate at fifteen to thirty pounds per square yard with a mechanical spreader; rolling shall be performed with pneumatic‑tire rollers providing contact pressure from forty to fifty pounds per square inch, and backrolling continues until the aggregate adheres while excess flushing must not exceed five percent of the finished area [2]. Slurry seals employ an emulsion selected from Class SS‑1h or CSS‑1h as referenced in Section 702; the mix design is tested to ASTM D3910 and the dry‑aggregate rate depends on seal type—six to ten pounds per square yard for Type I, ten to fifteen for Type II, fifteen or more for Type III [2]. Crushed‑aggregate base courses incorporate emulsified asphalt of Class RS‑1 or CRS‑1 meeting Section 702; the mixture follows the aggregate gradation in Table A, with optimum moisture content maintained within plus or minus two percentage points of the target and at least ninety percent of daily test samples remaining within tolerance [2]. General asphalt seal coats must use an emulsified asphalt that is one of the options enumerated in Section 702; no additional selection criteria are provided beyond that listing [2]. The polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt paving system used for micro‑surfacing is selected primarily on the basis of existing pavement condition; it should be applied only when the pavement is structurally sound and does not require extensive base repair, and any rutting must not be attributable to base failure. The treatment is favored for four‑lane roadways where only the travel lane is rutted or in special situations such as over trolley tracks or granite blocks because it can re‑profile the surface without a structural overlay and restore skid resistance; on jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) and jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) micro‑surfacing may be used directly but a Superpave scratch or leveling course must not be placed beforehand [1]. For cold‑recycled base courses the type of emulsified asphalt is chosen according to traffic, curing conditions, and required early strength; polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended when traffic volumes exceed three thousand two‑lane AADT, when a high percentage of truck traffic is present, when recycling is performed in the early or late season so that initial cure may be impaired, or whenever rapid early stability is desired, and the selection must follow the test procedures and allowable emulsion types listed in Bulletin 27 for cold‑recycled projects [1]. In full‑depth reclamation the selection of emulsified asphalt is driven by the mix‑design process, which determines both the quantity and the specific type of stabilizer to be used; the design method prescribed in Publication 27 identifies the test procedures that must be applied and enumerates the emulsified‑asphalt grades approved for PennDOT FDR projects, and the associated quality‑control requirements of Bulletin 27 must be followed [1].

[2] For prime‑coat applications, the contractor must select an application rate within the range of 0.30 gallon per square yard to 0.70 gallon per square yard, obtain approval before placement, and allow the emulsified asphalt to cure undisturbed until the Inspector‑in‑Charge confirms it is completely cured; excess coating may be blotted up or removed after twenty‑four hours with sufficient dry fine aggregate. Performance evaluation and implications

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), evaluation of emulsified‑asphalt stabilized full‑depth reclamation begins with laboratory testing to determine the appropriate type and dosage of the emulsion because the type and content of stabilizer used should be determined by laboratory testing [1]. The same manual requires in‑situ stiffness verification using Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing on the existing pavement before rehabilitation and again after construction to assess the stiffness of the newly stabilized layer [1]. Additional subgrade assessments may be performed with a dynamic cone penetrometer or a light weight deflectometer as supplemental tests of the subgrade [1]. The manual does not prescribe any specific long‑term monitoring procedure for emulsified asphalt [1]. According to the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 Specifications, each batch of emulsified asphalt must have a verification sample obtained and tested in accordance with Bulletin 25; one‑third of the sample is tested initially and forwarded with results to the Long‑Term Surveillance (LTS) program while the remaining third is retained for possible further review [2]. For grades that require it, a compatibility or stone‑coating test shall be performed in accordance with AASHTO T 59 Section 17 and a statement of aggregate compatibility must be included in the certification package [2]. Temperature‑viscosity data and specific gravity at 60 °F may be recorded as part of the certification package [2]. For polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt used in micro‑surfacing (Section 483), the tests identified in Table B shall be performed for each mix design and the results provided to document the mix characteristics; the mix design is submitted on a certified form together with a quality‑control plan required by Section 106 at project start and at least annually thereafter [2]. Daily mixing‑equipment meter readings are recorded on a run sheet, and the mixture composition and application rate shall be certified within one working day after testing [2]. Aggregate acceptance sampling requires a minimum sample size of three pounds following PTM 607; the sampled material is tested according to PTM 616 and PTM 100, a percent‑within‑limits (PWL) is determined, and any stockpile with PWL less than 90 shall be rejected [2]. For Type DG crushed‑aggregate base courses (Section 312), the joint mix formula must be established within the limits of Table A and confirmed by testing according to AASHTO T 27; at least twice daily samples of the completed mixture are analyzed either at the plant or after spreading, and control accuracy shall be maintained so that at least 90 % of test samples fall within the tolerance limits of Table A [2]. For black liquid membrane‑forming curing compounds (Section 711), compliance with ASTM C309 for Type 4 is required; the material must remain sprayable at temperatures above forty degrees Fahrenheit, control sagging, pigment settling, leveling and de‑emulsification, and retain those properties for at least one year [2]. The Representative shall evaluate the completed seal‑coat surface one day after sealing and after final sweeping; if aggregate embedment is less than 50 percent, a fog seal may be required in accordance with Section 472 [2]. A 100‑foot test strip shall be constructed and the residual asphalt rate targeted between 0.02 to 0.05 gallons per square yard, with the distributor’s application rate determined according to PTM 747 [2]. For tack coats, a minimum 100‑linear‑foot test section shall be prepared for each paving course; the distributor must maintain uniform pressure and control the application rate within a tolerance of 0.02 gallon per square yard, and tack coat may be applied only when air temperature is 40 °F and rising and the existing surface is dry [2]. Cold‑recycled base courses stabilized with emulsified asphalt shall be evaluated by determining the in‑place density of each 3,000‑square‑yard area according to AASHTO T 310 and compacted to a minimum density of 96 % of the PTM 402 target density; the material shall be cured for at least two weeks before placing the wearing or binder course, after which three random cores of at least 1,200 g each shall be dry‑cut or extracted in accordance with PTM 749 [2]. For centrally mixed cold‑recycled base, one test location per 3,000 sq yd is designated; if depth is deficient by ½ inch or more from the indicated depth the pavement is considered defective [2]. Equipment used to meter liquids shall have a variation not greater than +2.0 % by weight [2]. Full‑depth reclamation requires compaction to at least 95 % of the laboratory‑compacted maximum density at optimum moisture content per PTM 106; a 300‑foot control strip is constructed with nuclear density readings after each pass until no appreciable increase occurs, and an average in‑place density from ten random tests must satisfy the specified minimum [2]. A seven‑day cure period shall precede opening to traffic, and proof rolling with the heaviest expected vehicle shall demonstrate load sustainment [2]. Strength testing for emulsion‑based mixes or mixes stabilized with foamed asphalt shall use specimens compacted in a Superpave Gyratory Compactor to a thickness between 2 and 3 inches using 50 gyrations; indirect tensile strength must reach at least 50 psi according to Bulletin 27, and compressive strength must meet a minimum of 280 psi [2]. Long‑term performance evidence is provided by the Long‑Term Surveillance (LTS) program, which evaluates retained verification samples whenever a failure is reported on the forwarded portion [2]. The Department’s QA teams shall periodically review the QC plan, inspect production and testing facilities, and take QA samples at the plant or at the point of incorporation, accompanied by a Certificate of Compliance and Bill of Lading [2]. If emulsified asphalt is stored for 30 days or more after certification it shall be resampled and retested at no cost to confirm continued compliance with Bulletin 25 [2]. For micro‑surfacing, the test strip must be opened to traffic within one hour after placement, achieve a maximum set time of ten minutes as measured by ISSA Test Method TB 102, exhibit an on‑site mixture set time not exceeding one hour twenty minutes, be compacted with a pneumatic‑tire roller at 40–60 psi, and display excess flushing less than five percent of the finished surface together with acceptable limits on tear/drag marks [2].

Polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt used in a micro‑surfacing system cures quickly and, when proper controls are maintained, performs well under traffic; it is very cost‑effective on four‑lane roadways where only the travel lane is rutted, may be used for rut filling to re‑profile the asphalt pavement without any additional resurfacing, and can be effectively used to restore skid resistance. The material can be formulated as Type A to seal cracks, fill voids and shallow ruts less than half an inch deep, as Type B to address moderate ruts from one‑half to one‑and‑one‑quarter inches deep, or as a Rut Fill mix to fill deep two‑inch ruts in a single pass, and when design specifications call for greater total pounds per square yard on surface courses a double application may be required. Placement is permitted only when the entire surface is capable of satisfactory penetration and adhesion and the atmospheric temperature is at least fifty degrees Fahrenheit for the whole placement; the mixture must not be applied if rain is imminent or if freezing temperatures are expected within twenty‑four hours after application. Continuous mixing introduces the polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt beyond the first quarter point of the mixer to ensure thorough coating of aggregate before it reaches the spreader, preventing side build‑up, lumps, or tears that would reduce skid resistance and durability; the mixture is then spread uniformly by means of a mechanical‑type squeegee box attached to the mixer, and the spreading equipment must be operated to achieve a uniform consistency without skips, lumps, or tears in the finished surface. The polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt must achieve a proper set within one hour; if it takes longer than one hour and twenty minutes to properly set, placement must be stopped, and a test strip must demonstrate a set time of ten minutes maximum before traffic may be opened within one hour, confirming that the material’s behavior meets performance criteria. Before opening to traffic, compaction is performed with a pneumatic‑tire roller at tire pressures of forty to sixty psi, no areas of excess emulsified asphalt (flushing) greater than five percent of the finished surface area are permitted, and sweeping the surface with a power broom to remove loose chips both before and after traffic helps protect the cured emulsion and maintain performance. Within cold‑recycled base courses, polymer‑modified emulsions provide improved initial strength of the recycled mix, which is especially valuable when traffic volumes exceed three thousand two‑lane AADT, a high percentage of truck traffic is present, or recycling occurs in early or late season where initial curing may be impaired; because the recycled mix still starts with lower structural strength than hot mixes, a minimum of one week of curing under favorable weather (low humidity high temperature) is required before the wearing course can be placed, influencing construction timing and long‑term durability. In cold‑recycled base courses the emulsified asphalt must be selected from Bulletin 25‑approved types and added to the reclaimed material exactly as called for in the approved mix design; equipment capable of automatically metering liquids with a variation of not more than plus‑or‑minus two point zero percent by weight ensures adequate total liquid content, enabling thorough mixing of reclaimed material and aggregate and allowing the base course to be compacted to at least ninety‑six percent of the PTM 402 target density. The stabilized mix must be cured for at least two weeks or until its average moisture content drops below two percent before any wearing or binder course is placed, preventing premature raveling or loss of strength; a fog seal with approved emulsified‑asphalt material may then be applied to control dust and surface deterioration during service, the residual asphalt rate being targeted between zero point zero two and zero point zero five gallons per square yard. Acceptance of the reclaimed base requires a minimum compressive strength of two hundred eighty pounds per square inch after seven days, which governs the timing for opening to traffic and links the emulsion’s moisture retention and residual rate directly to pavement performance, durability, construction sequencing, and maintenance. For full‑depth reclamation, emulsified asphalt added for stabilization contributes water resistance and fatigue strength to the reclaimed layer, thereby enhancing overall pavement durability; it can be combined with granular material, cement or lime, allowing designers to tailor mix proportions during the FDR mix‑design process, and selecting an appropriate amount and type of stabilizing material is a key step in the mix design and construction specifications for PennDOT projects. Emulsified asphalt’s ability to flow into surface voids and form a continuous film enables it to be used for sealing the edges of wearing courses adjacent to curbs, where application at least twelve inches wide and at a controlled rate that completely fills the voids creates a watertight joint that helps protect the pavement from water intrusion and thus improves durability. For tack‑coat applications the coarse aggregate must be spread at a design rate of fifteen to twenty‑five pounds per square yard and rolled within five minutes; each rolling pass shall travel at a speed less than or equal to ten miles per hour and apply a contact pressure of forty to fifty psi, actions that embed the aggregate before the emulsion sets and are essential for surface stability and durability. Application rates must stay within plus‑or‑minus ten percent of the design rate and traffic may not be allowed until the coating has cured sufficiently, especially in shaded areas where set times are longer, to prevent distortion, flushing of the emulsion, or loss of aggregate. For prime coats such as AE‑P and E‑1, emulsified asphalt must be applied at rates ranging from zero point three to zero point seven gallons per square yard so that it can penetrate and be absorbed by the treated surface until an inspector confirms complete cure; twenty‑four hours after application any excess prime coat material must be blotted with sufficient dry fine aggregate and the surface must remain protected before subsequent construction. Fog‑seal emulsified asphalt is applied only when air, surface and aggregate temperatures are above sixty degrees Fahrenheit and rain or freezing temperatures are not expected within eight hours; the underlying seal coat must cure a minimum of two days before fog sealing, and the residual asphalt rate shall be targeted between zero point zero two to zero point zero five gallons per square yard. In pervious‑asphalt pavement systems emulsified asphalt is prescribed as a thin, uniform coating on vertical surfaces that will be in contact with asphalt mixtures, using classes such as AASHTO SS‑1, CSS‑1, SS‑1h, CSS‑1h or TACK applied in two or more applications; the specification also prohibits the use of a tack coat between lifts of any pervious asphalt, which avoids compromising the designed porosity and compaction limits. During profile‑milling repairs loose material must first be cleared from the spot, an emulsified‑asphalt tack coat is then applied, and the area is backfilled with an asphalt mixture of the same type; this bonding layer promotes adhesion between new and existing pavement, helping the patched area meet smoothness tolerances and maintain durability as part of construction and maintenance.
