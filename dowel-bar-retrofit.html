Dowel Bar Retrofit

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Dowel Bar Retrofit is a topic under Pavement Preservation.

Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Dowel Bar Retrofit is a pavement rehabilitation technique employed when concrete slabs have lost effective load‑transfer capacity across transverse joints or cracks, manifesting as faulting, working transverse cracks, or poor ride quality on heavily channeled roadways[1]. The treatment restores that capability by cutting full‑depth slots in the wheel paths, inserting epoxy‑coated smooth steel dowel bars seated on non‑metallic chairs with expansion caps, and backfilling the voids with rapid‑setting grout or concrete patching to reestablish proper stress distribution between adjacent slab sections[1][2]. It is applied only where the existing slab retains adequate compressive strength, aggregate interlock remains (hairline cracking), and the subgrade and drainage are stable, thereby improving ride quality, extending service life, and postponing more extensive rehabilitation[1].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Dowel Bar Retrofit is employed when concrete pavement slabs have lost effective load‑transfer capability, a condition that manifests as working transverse cracks, mid‑panel cracks, deteriorated or failed transverse joints, faulting, poor load transfer across joints, edge spalling, or corner breaks. These load‑related distresses diminish the ability of adjacent slab sections to share traffic loads, leading to reduced structural performance and ride quality, particularly on heavily channeled roadways. By installing epoxy‑coated smooth dowel bars in the wheel paths across the affected cracks or joints, the treatment restores the lost load transfer, reestablishes slab functionality, improves ride quality, and extends the pavement’s service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213)

The objective of a dowel‑bar retrofit is to reestablish the pavement’s load‑transfer capacity across active transverse joints or cracks, thereby restoring the slab’s intended structural and functional performance. By installing epoxy‑coated dowels in the wheel paths where transfer has been lost—often on heavily loaded roadways—the treatment directly addresses faulting and poor load transfer rather than merely slowing deterioration. When distress is primarily load‑related, the retrofit provides the necessary structural improvement, and it can be combined with slab jacking to recover settled sections. Restoring this capability preserves the pavement system, retards further degradation, and consequently extends its service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213)

Distress characterization and causes

The distress that calls for a dowel‑bar retrofit occurs in the wheel paths of heavily loaded concrete roadways where transverse joints or cracks develop, often manifesting as early‑stage hairline cracking and the onset of faulting or poor load transfer across the joint. Because the aggregate interlock is still present, the problem is limited in extent but can quickly degrade ride quality and pavement performance if left unattended; the source does not quantify a progression rate. Restoring load transfer with dowel bars prevents further deterioration and maintains safe, operational service of the pavement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90)

The distress that a dowel‑bar retrofit is intended to correct stems from two interrelated mechanisms. Repeated heavy traffic loads generate high wheel pressures on transverse joints and cracks, leading to load‑related cracking and faulting when the pavement’s ability to transfer load is compromised. At the same time, climate‑ and material‑related factors degrade the concrete over its service life: water infiltrates through joint gaps or cracks, especially where drainage is inadequate, carrying chlorides and other incompressible materials into the structure; subgrade weakness or instability reduces the support beneath the slab; aggregate deterioration and frost damage further erode durability. When these mechanisms act together, any improvement in load transfer will be short‑lived unless water ingress, drainage failure, and subgrade condition are addressed, which is why dowel‑bar retrofits are applied only where existing drainage functions properly and the subgrade is stable, thereby restoring effective load transfer across the joint or crack. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90)

Applicability and treatment selection

Dowel‑bar retrofit is suitable for concrete pavements whose distress is chiefly load‑related—such as joint faulting, slab movement, or transverse joints and hairline cracks where aggregate interlock remains—and where the existing slabs still possess adequate compressive strength and proper air content. The treatment is most appropriate when traffic consists of heavy, channeled loads that generate significant shear across the joints, and when the required patching is modest—generally less than about ten percent of the pavement area, excluding sound existing patches—so that a cost‑effectiveness analysis is not triggered. Site conditions must include a stable subgrade and a functional drainage system so that water infiltration does not undermine the repair, and any underlying climate or material durability issues should have been addressed before installation. Under these combined pavement, traffic, environmental, and site criteria, inserting dowel bars across deficient joints or cracks restores load transfer without necessitating an overlay. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90)

When the pavement requires extensive patching—more than about ten percent of the surface—or when the dominant distress stems from climate‑ or material‑related durability problems such as inadequate drainage, frost damage, or aggregate polishing, dowel‑bar retrofit is not the preferred treatment. In those situations designers should consider alternatives that address the underlying cause rather than merely restoring load transfer, including full‑depth patching, concrete or asphalt overlays, slab stabilization, joint rehabilitation, jacking, or other rehabilitation methods. Likewise, if the pavement’s drainage is compromised, the subgrade is unstable, or the cracks are larger than hairline so that aggregate interlock has been lost, dowel‑bar retrofit should be avoided and more appropriate treatments such as slab stabilization, full‑depth joint patching, or other joint repair techniques should be selected. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90)

Condition assessment and timing

To decide whether a dowel‑bar retrofit is warranted, the evaluator begins with a systematic visual survey of all transverse joints and cracks, recording the type, extent, and frequency of distress; particular attention is given to hairline cracking where aggregate interlock remains intact and any indication of joint faulting that suggests loss of load transfer. The inspection also confirms that drainage is adequate and that the underlying subgrade appears stable, because retrofitting addresses only transfer deficiencies. Quantitative counts of each distress type are tabulated. Where distress is noted, cores are extracted both at the deteriorated joints and at randomly selected locations to assess subbase or subgrade condition and to detect hidden deterioration beneath the surface. Non‑destructive testing follows: deflection tests and fault‑level measurements are performed to locate joints that have lost load‑transfer capacity; ground‑penetrating radar, MIT‑Scan 2 (for dowel‑bar alignment), and MIT‑Scan T2 (for slab thickness) may be employed to verify the presence or condition of existing dowels. Additional nondestructive tools such as friction testing, roughness surveys, MIRA assessments, and tensile bond testing can further substantiate the need for retrofit. When these inspections, measurements, and tests collectively indicate compromised load transfer on heavily loaded sections, a dowel‑bar retrofit is indicated. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90)

Dowel‑bar retrofit should be applied when a Portland‑cement concrete pavement begins to lose its ability to transfer load across transverse joints or cracks while the slab, subgrade and drainage remain sound. It is indicated for working transverse cracks, mid‑panel cracks, or deteriorated transverse joints that have progressed beyond hairline cracking yet still retain aggregate interlock, particularly on heavily loaded roadways where faulting or poor load transfer has been observed. The method may also be used as a preventive‑maintenance treatment on pavements in overall good condition that exhibit only minor joint or crack distress and possess significant remaining service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213)

Materials and repair details

Dowel‑bar retrofit calls for smooth dowel bars that are either epoxy‑coated or, as described in one guide, designated as Type A coating which requires no lubricant, whereas Type B coated bars must receive a bond‑breaker lubricant applied at least one hour before concrete placement [1][2]. The technique is applied in wheel paths across transverse joints or cracks that lack dowels, and six dowel bars are typically installed for each joint or crack [1][2]. A power‑driven self‑propelled saw is used to cut parallel slots, providing two parallel cuts per slot and three slots across each wheel path with the cuts aligned parallel to the roadway centreline [1][2]. After sawing, the core of each slot is removed with a jackhammer whose weight does not exceed thirty pounds, reduced to fifteen pounds if damage to surrounding concrete is observed [2]. The slots are then cleaned before dowel placement [1]. Each slot is lined with a quarter‑inch‑thick preformed cellular polystyrene insert that matches the cross‑sectional dimensions of the slot; when the joint width exceeds a quarter inch, an appropriate expansion‑joint material replaces the polystyrene [2]. Dowel bars are seated on plastic or non‑metallic chairs that provide at least a half‑inch clearance to all slot surfaces, and a 1½‑inch non‑metallic expansion cap is placed over each bar end leaving a quarter‑inch gap between the dowel tip and the bottom of the cap [2]. Alignment tolerances limit vertical tilt or horizontal skew of either end of the dowel to a maximum of 0.25 in, while longitudinal translation is limited to one inch and horizontal translation also to one inch [2]. Finally, the voids around the dowels are backfilled with concrete patching material, which is placed, vibrated, and cured in accordance with standard practice [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 432–433)

Performance of a dowel‑bar retrofit is governed primarily by the condition and properties of the existing concrete, the compatibility of the dowel material with that concrete, and the state of the supporting layers [1][2]. The host slab must possess at least 4,925 psi compressive strength and 650 psi flexural strength before any retrofitting work proceeds [2]. In addition, the concrete should have an appropriate air content to develop a reliable bond with epoxy‑coated steel dowels, and excessive patching—particularly when a large percentage of the joint area is patched—reduces the ability of the retrofit to transfer load [1]. Visual inspection must reveal no severe durability problems such as weak or frost‑damaged aggregate, polishing‑prone aggregates, or other signs that would compromise interlock; only hairline cracking should be present so that aggregate interlock remains effective [1]. The dowels are smooth steel bars coated with epoxy or a bond‑breaker coating, and the coating must match the concrete environment to avoid corrosion and maintain slip resistance [1][2]. For Type B dowels a lubricating bond‑breaker must be applied unless already treated at the factory, while Type A bars may remain uncoated [2]. Non‑metallic expansion caps of 1½ inches are installed on each dowel with a quarter‑inch clearance between cap and dowel tip, and plastic or non‑metallic chairs provide at least a half‑inch gap from all slot surfaces to keep the dowel centered [2]. Geometric tolerances are critical: transverse joint openings must be kept between one‑quarter and one‑half inch along their full length, vertical differentials may not exceed one‑quarter inch, longitudinal joints should remain under three‑quarters inch, dowel tilt or skew is limited to a quarter‑inch at each end relative to the midpoint, and horizontal translation is limited to one inch [2]. Slot depth is set at the mid‑depth of the slab (or at the thinner slab when thickness varies), and grout placed in the slots must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is reopened [2]. The supporting subgrade and subbase must be stable, well‑drained, free of unsuitable material, and compacted within tolerance; unstable support or water infiltration can undermine load transfer [1][2]. Surface friction and ride quality should be acceptable before installation, especially on heavily loaded wheel paths where the retrofit is intended to function [1]. Proper cleaning of the joint, timely removal of debris, and use of appropriate jack‑hammer weight are required to achieve sound bonding of epoxy and concrete patching [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 432–447)

Treatment procedures

The retrofit process begins with a field survey and marking of joint locations, after which the slots are cut across the joint or crack using a power‑driven, self‑propelled saw (or diamond saw) that makes two parallel cuts per slot, three slots being placed in each wheel path and oriented parallel to the centreline [1][2]. The cuts extend full depth so that the intended dowel bar will be centered at approximately mid‑depth of the slab, following the original joint geometry [2]. The concrete between the parallel cuts is removed either with a lightweight jackhammer not exceeding 30 lb or by the lift‑out method employing lifting devices that raise the slab without disturbing the subbase, and any unsuitable material exposed in the subgrade is discarded and replaced with compacted coarse aggregate to meet tolerances [2]. After removal, the slot faces are cleaned; an air‑blast of at least 100 psi dry air removes residual dust, and a final cleaning is performed within 30 minutes before placing the fill material [1][2]. The dowel bars, which are epoxy‑coated smooth steel, are prepared according to type: Type B bars receive a coat of bond‑breaker lubricant (or the same coating applied in the shop) while Type A bars may be installed without it [1][2]. A ¼‑inch‑thick polystyrene filler is cut to fit the slot, non‑metallic expansion caps are positioned on the bar ends, and the dowel is seated on a plastic chair that provides at least ½ inch clearance and maintains allowable tilt, skew and translation [2]. An epoxy bonding compound is applied to the bar, after which the slot is filled with rapid‑setting grout or concrete patching material; the fill is vibrated to fully consolidate around the dowel without displacing it [1][2]. The surface of the grout or patch is finished to match the surrounding pavement profile, the joint is sawed and sealed, and final checks verify that joint widths and vertical differentials comply with specified limits before traffic is reopened [1][2]. Curing follows the requirements of Section 516.3(j); the grout must achieve a compressive strength of at least 2,500 psi before traffic may be allowed, and traffic reopening follows Section 516.3(q) [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 432–447)

The retrofit begins with a pre‑placement meeting that establishes the work plan and confirms that all lifting equipment—cranes, excavators and any special rigging—has been documented for size, weight, load distribution and capacity in accordance with Section 1105 [2]. A power‑driven, self‑propelled saw is then used to make two parallel full‑depth cuts per dowel slot across the wheel path, a requirement noted by both guides [1][2]. After the cuts are made, the concrete between them is removed with a jackhammer that must not exceed thirty pounds in weight, reduced to fifteen pounds when the existing slab is suspected of damage, and the material extracted from the slots is cleared; the slots are cleaned no later than twenty‑four hours after cutting, with a final cleaning performed within thirty minutes before the patching concrete is placed [1][2]. The dowel bars, which are epoxy‑coated, receive lubricant at least one hour prior to placement and are positioned in the freshly cut slots using chairs that hold them at the proper elevation; the subbase beneath each slot is prepared and the slab surface is leveled to meet the required tolerance before the dowels are set [1][2]. Once the bars are in place, a grout or concrete patching material is poured around them and must be allowed to cure until it attains a minimum compressive strength of 2 500 psi, at which point traffic may be restored [1][2]. The guides further restrict temporary openings to traffic to no more than three days, require vertical joint differentials not to exceed one‑quarter inch (or half an inch in limited cases), and prescribe the use of shims for any slabs that remain ungrouted until the grout reaches strength; final release of traffic is governed by Section 516.3(q) [2]. Neither source specifies particular ambient temperature or humidity limits, but the curing requirement implicitly demands conditions suitable for achieving the specified compressive strength within the prescribed time frame [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 432–447)

Quality and workmanship

The dowel‑bar retrofit inspection must verify that the entire system conforms to the specified material and performance criteria. The dowels and tie bars shall be supplied by a manufacturer whose pre‑cast product specifications are listed in Bulletin 15, and the concrete mix used for the repair must achieve at least 4,925 psi compressive strength and 650 psi flexural strength. All dowel surfaces are required to receive the prescribed bond‑breaker coating, typically a Type B lubricant, before placement, and the joint filler must be the correctly sized polystyrene material. Expansion caps of non‑metallic 1½‑inch diameter must be installed with a uniform ¼‑inch clearance from the dowel tip, while chairs must provide a minimum clearance of ½ inch.

Preparation work begins with cutting the existing slab using a power‑driven, self‑propelled saw capable of producing two parallel cuts per slot; any jackhammer employed for concrete removal may not exceed 30 lb to avoid damage. The slots are cut so that the dowel will sit at mid‑depth and must be located within ±1/8 inch of the positions shown on the shop drawings. After cutting, all loose material and slurry must be removed – a thorough cleaning is required no later than 24 hours before concrete placement, with final cleaning completed within 30 minutes of placement. Joint gaps transverse to traffic are limited to a maximum of ½ inch (with a minimum of ¼ inch), and vertical differentials across any joint may not exceed ¼ inch.

During installation the dowel assembly is examined for correct application of the bond‑breaker, proper insertion of the polystyrene filler, and accurate placement of the expansion caps. Placement tolerances are strictly controlled: vertical tilt or horizontal skew must stay within a 0.25‑inch displacement, and longitudinal as well as transverse translation may not exceed one inch. The dowel must remain stationary throughout concrete placement, vibration, and finishing so that the repaired section matches the existing pavement profile. Grout placed in the dowel slots and beneath the slab is tested until it reaches at least 2,500 psi compressive strength before any traffic is permitted.

The completed work is inspected for proper surface finish, intact sealant reservoirs, and the absence of defects that would require replacement. Any spalls or cracks identified during inspection must be repaired in accordance with the specified procedures prior to final acceptance. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 432–447)

A satisfactory dowel‑bar retrofit is confirmed when the dowels and their slots are positioned in accordance with the approved shop drawings to within plus or minus one‑eighth inch, and each end of a dowel does not deviate more than a quarter‑inch from the midpoint in vertical tilt or horizontal skew. The longitudinal and transverse offsets of the dowel assembly relative to the intended joint location must be limited to one inch each. The slot must be cleaned thoroughly with the final cleaning completed no later than thirty minutes before concrete placement, and the dowel must rest on non‑metallic chairs that provide at least a half‑inch clearance to all slot surfaces; the dowels are lubricated at least an hour prior to concrete placement. Transverse joint gaps after installation must be kept between one‑quarter and one‑half inch along their entire length, while longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters inch. Vertical differentials across any joint are limited to a quarter inch, and any differential exceeding half an inch requires slab removal. The bedding grout surrounding the dowel must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is permitted, and all exposed dowels are protected with bond‑breaker material until the grout cures. Finally, the finished pavement surface must match the existing profile; any deviation from these criteria renders the work defective and requires replacement at no cost. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 432–447)

Performance and service life

The dowel‑bar retrofit restores lost load‑transfer capacity at concrete joints or cracks, re‑establishing proper stress distribution across slabs and thereby improving the pavement’s overall condition. By installing smooth epoxy‑coated bars in the wheel paths, the treatment enhances functional performance under heavy traffic loads and contributes to greater durability of the slab system. When applied to hairline cracks where aggregate interlock remains, it preserves structural integrity without substantially increasing the slab’s inherent strength. Classified as a preventive‑maintenance intervention, the technique is used while the pavement is still in good condition and has significant remaining service life; the restored load transfer retards further deterioration, extends useful life, and postpones more costly rehabilitation or reconstruction. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213)

Monitoring and follow-up

After completing a dowel‑bar retrofit, the work must be inspected to verify that all dowel and bedding grouts have achieved the required compressive strength before traffic is permitted, that grout levels in the ports remain flush with the slab surface, and that joint gaps and vertical differentials stay within the specified limits; any settlement or edge differential exceeding a quarter‑inch triggers removal, re‑grading and resetting of the slab prior to final grouting. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Additional work is required when observable defects exceed the acceptance limits defined for dowel‑bar retrofit areas: spalls or cracks that are wider than 0.007 in and deeper than one‑third of slab thickness must be repaired or the slab replaced; joint gaps or vertical differentials larger than the allowed ¼ in (or exceeding ½ in) indicate improper load transfer and trigger grinding, re‑grading, or replacement; grout that has not reached the specified compressive strength before traffic is opened also signals a need for further curing or repair; and any recurring damage or distress after installation requires stopping work until the underlying cause is identified and corrected. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)
