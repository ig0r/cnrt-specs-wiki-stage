AASHTO No. 1 Aggregate

Part of: Rock Capping is-subtopic-of
AASHTO No. 1 Aggregate is a topic under Rock Capping.
aashto-no-1aashto-no-1-coarse-aggregateaashto-1-aggregate
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

AASHTO No. 1 aggregate is a coarse stone or granulated blast‑furnace slag that conforms to the AASHTO No. 1 gradation limits (Section 703.2), classified as Type A or B (or at least Type C when sourced from borrow excavation), with particles no larger than 2 inches, a dry rodded density not exceeding 90 lb/ft³, and limited fines (no more than 20 % passing the No. 100 sieve) [2]. It is placed as a replacement base course after unsuitable material has been removed in rubblization or other rehabilitation methods, providing a stable, load‑bearing layer beneath asphalt overlays, new concrete slabs, or as a 1‑to‑3‑foot rock cap to strengthen weak subgrades [1]. The aggregate’s performance depends on its uniform grading, adequate cementing (or the optional blending of 20 % limestone fines when cementing is poor), and freedom from deleterious clay, silt or vegetation, which together ensure proper load distribution and durability in pavement structures.

Material purpose and applicability

AASHTO No. 1 coarse aggregate is a coarse aggregate identified by the AASHTO No. 1 specification and, in the rubblizing rehabilitation method, it is placed after unsuitable material has been removed and before an overlay is applied, serving as a replacement base course that provides a stable support layer in lieu of flexible base or concrete patches. In the Asphalt Overlay on Rubblization procedure described in the Asphalt Rehabilitation Strategies section, AASHTO No. 1 aggregate is used for base repair, providing a repaired base layer before the new asphalt overlay is placed. In the Concrete Rehabilitation Strategies section, AASHTO No. 1 aggregate is called for in the base‑repair portion of a concrete reconstruction project that follows rubblization and is placed beneath the new concrete slab to restore a uniform, supportive base before the reconstructed pavement is installed. According to the Subgrade Soils section, AASHTO No. 1 is a type of durable rock that may be used as the material for a rock‑capping layer in subgrade strengthening, and the rock cap, typically placed 1 to 3 feet deep, consists of R‑3 Rock, AASHTO No. 1, or Best Available Rock and serves to provide a strong, load‑bearing foundation that improves the support capacity of weak subgrades before the pavement structure is constructed. In the Crushed Aggregate Base Course section, AASHTO No. 1 aggregate is a coarse stone or granulated blast‑furnace slag that meets the No. 1 gradation requirements and is classified as Type A or Type B under Section 703.2; in this context it serves as the coarse material in a crushed‑aggregate base course, providing the bulk structural layer that supports and distributes loads from the surface layers. In the Class 1A and Class 1C Excavation section, AASHTO No. 1 aggregate is the granular material called for as backfill after unsuitable material below the subgrade is removed and the undercut drained, and the void is filled with this aggregate—often together with geotextile placement—to provide a stable base for the pavement structure. In the Borrow Excavation section, AASHTO No. 1 aggregate is a coarse aggregate that must meet at least Type C quality requirements and is listed as an allowable material for selected borrow excavation used as structure backfill to support the pavement system. [1][2]

Composition and characteristics

In the crushed‑aggregate base course context (Section 310—Crushed Aggregate Base Course, 310.2), AASHTO No. 1 aggregate is defined as a coarse stone or slag of Type A or Type B that conforms to the No. 1 gradation specified in Section 703.2 and it may be paired with fine material of Type A or B meeting the No. 10 stone or slag gradation. Stone whose cementing quality is unsatisfactory may be used provided limestone fines are blended at 20 % by weight. Granulated blast‑furnace slag that meets the No. 10 gradation is acceptable providing its dry rodded density does not exceed 90 lb/ft³, it is reasonably uniform and free of harmful clay, silt or vegetation, its particles are no larger than 2 inches, and no more than 20 % pass the No. 100 sieve. When the material is sourced from a borrow excavation for use as structure backfill (Section 205—Borrow Excavation, 205.2), AASHTO No. 1 is designated as a coarse aggregate that must satisfy at least the Type C quality requirements; the specification does not further prescribe mineral composition or gradation. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (p. 148), SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 209)

Variations in composition such as blending limestone fines at 20% can offset poor cementing qualities of stone while still satisfying the coarse‑aggregate requirement for a base course, and the specification also permits either stone or slag to serve as the No. 1 coarse material provided they meet Section 703.2. Using granulated blast‑furnace slag instead of natural stone is permissible when the slag meets the No. 10 gradation, remains reasonably uniform, contains no harmful clay, silt, vegetation or other objectionable substances, and satisfies density and size limits—its dry rodded density must not exceed 90 pounds per cubic foot, its particles may be up to 2 inches, and no more than 20% may pass the No. 100 sieve. These compositional, source, processing, and proportioning controls directly affect the aggregate’s uniformity, strength development, and ability to conform to the Section 310.2 material specifications. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 209)

Key material properties

The performance of AASHTO No. 1 aggregate in a crushed‑aggregate base is governed by its gradation, particle size, density and cementing characteristics. The material must be uniform and free from harmful clay, silt, vegetation or other objectionable substances, limited to a maximum size of 2 inches and not more than 20% passing the No. 100 sieve; granulated blast‑furnace slag is acceptable only if its dry rodded density does not exceed 90 lb/ft³. When stone has unsatisfactory cementing qualities, it may be used provided limestone fines in an amount of 20% are blended with the material. These physical limits and the inclusion of appropriate fines together control stability and load‑bearing capacity. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 209)

In the Crushed Aggregate Base Course (Section 310—Crushed Aggregate Base Course, 310.2 Material), AASHTO No. 1 aggregate is classified as a Type A or Type B coarse material under Section 703.2 and its suitability is verified by measuring density with the dry rodded‑density test (AASHTO T 19) to ensure it does not exceed 90 pounds per cubic foot; gradation is checked by sieve analysis so that the maximum particle size is limited to 2 inches and no more than 20 % passes the No. 100 sieve. In the Borrow Excavation (Section 205—Borrow Excavation, 205.2 Material), the same AASHTO No. 1 coarse aggregate is required to meet at least Type C quality requirements and to conform to the criteria set out in Section 703.2 Table B and Section 703.2(c) Table C, which together define how its properties are classified for use. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (p. 148), SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 209)

Behavior and mechanisms

The performance of AASHTO No. 1 coarse aggregate in a crushed‑aggregate base is governed primarily by its physical grading and density limits, its chemical cementing potential, and the mechanical uniformity of the particles. When stone lacks adequate cementing qualities it must be blended with limestone fines at 20 % to improve bonding, while acceptable granulated blast‑furnace slag may be used only if its dry rodded density does not exceed 90 lb/ft³ and the material is reasonably uniform, free of deleterious clay, silt or vegetation, with individual particles no larger than 2 inches and no more than 20 % passing the No. 100 sieve. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 209)

Durability and deterioration

In the crushed‑aggregate base course, stone that exhibits unsatisfactory cementing qualities is more prone to deterioration unless it is blended with limestone fines at an amount of 20%, which helps mitigate the weakness; similarly, granulated blast furnace slag must be uniformly free of harmful clay, silt or vegetation and its dry rodded density may not exceed 90 pounds per cubic foot, because higher density or contaminant content would increase susceptibility. Limiting the slag particles to a maximum size of 2 inches and ensuring that no more than 20% pass the No. 100 sieve also reduces the potential for degradation. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 209)

Material interactions and compatibility

For a No. 1 stone or slag base course the specification requires the aggregate to be reasonably uniform and free from harmful amounts of clay, silt, vegetation, or other objectionable substances, because such contaminants can impair performance; if the stone exhibits unsatisfactory cementing qualities it may only be used when blended with limestone fines at a 20% rate to mitigate that deficiency. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 209)

Selection, specification, and improvement

In the Asphalt Overlay on Rubblization rehabilitation strategy, AASHTO No. 1 aggregate is specified only when a base repair is required after rubblizing and seating the existing concrete pavement; the presence of that repair need therefore guides its selection [1]. When a concrete reconstruction is carried out by rubblizing and seating the existing pavement, the specification calls for a base repair with AASHTO No. 1 aggregate if a base layer is required, so the requirement for a base layer determines the use of this aggregate [1]. In rehabilitation projects where rubblizing has been performed and any unsuitable material remaining after the process must be replaced before placing an overlay, the specification requires using AASHTO No. 1 coarse aggregate rather than flexible‑base course replacement or concrete patches; thus the need to replace unsuitable material after rubblization drives the selection [1]. Within a crushed‑aggregate base course, the coarse component shall be specified as Type A or Type B material that conforms to the No. 1 grading limits defined in Section 703.2, and it may be supplied either as stone or as granulated blast‑furnace slag meeting those same gradation requirements; consequently the aggregate type (A or B) and compliance with the No. 1 size distribution per Section 703.2 constitute the selection criteria [2]. For backfill of undercuts created by removal of unsuitable material below the subgrade, if the bottom width of the undercut is less than 8 feet the work is classified as Class 1A excavation and shall be drained, a geotextile placed in accordance with Section 208.3(d), and then backfilled with AASHTO No. 1 aggregate; if the bottom width is 8 feet or more the work is treated as Class 1C excavation and the same drainage, geotextile placement, and backfilling with AASHTO No. 1 aggregate are required, so the measured bottom width determines whether AASHTO No. 1 aggregate is used for the backfill [2]. When AASHTO No. 1 aggregate is selected for a borrow‑excavation backfill, it shall be limited to the coarse‑aggregate types listed for Structure Backfill and must satisfy at least Type C quality requirements; additionally the material must conform to the provisions set out in Section 703.2 Table B and Section 703.2(c) Table C, and any required R‑3 rock lining shall follow Section 850.2(a), thus these quality, gradation, and lining specifications guide its selection [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 143–148), SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 209)

Using AASHTO No. 1 rock as a 1‑to‑3‑foot thick cap over weak subgrades is an accepted way to provide a durable base. Its effectiveness can be enhanced by placing geogrids at the subgrade‑aggregate interface or a Class 4 geotextile to separate and reinforce the layers. When the underlying soils are deficient, cement or lime stabilization—applied after a laboratory program that confirms gradation, liquid limit, plasticity, shrinkage, strength and density—is recommended to raise CBR and resilient modulus values. All subgrade improvements must be verified through the prescribed CBR, moisture‑content and in‑place density testing procedures. In SECTION 310—CRUSHED AGGREGATE BASE COURSE, blending limestone fines at 20 % with a No. 1 stone is permitted to offset its poor cementing qualities and improve bonding while remaining within the coarse‑aggregate requirement. The same section also permits substitution of granulated blast‑furnace slag for natural stone provided the slag meets the No. 10 gradation, its dry rodded density does not exceed 90 lb/ft³, it is reasonably uniform, free of harmful clay, silt or vegetation, limited to a maximum particle size of 2 inches, and no more than 20 % may pass the No. 100 sieve; these composition, density, cleanliness, and gradation controls serve as quality checks that enhance performance of the AASHTO No. 1 aggregate base. In SECTION 205—BORROW EXCAVATION, ensuring that AASHTO No. 1 coarse aggregate used for selected borrow excavation meets at least Type C quality requirements provides a baseline level of performance. When scour‑resiliency is a concern the specification permits using PennDOT 2A as an alternative; the section also authorizes other approved coarse aggregates such as AASHTO No. 3, No. 5 or No. 57 for structure backfill, offering further material options if the properties of No. 1 prove insufficient. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (p. 148), SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 209)

Performance evaluation and implications

The evaluation of AASHTO No. 1 coarse aggregate relies on laboratory test results that confirm compliance with specified density, gradation and size limits. The dry rodded density must not exceed 90 lb/ft³ as measured by AASHTO T 19, the particles may be no larger than 2 inches, and no more than 20 % of the material may pass the No. 100 sieve; these criteria constitute the laboratory evidence for acceptance. The source does not provide any field or long‑term performance evidence for this aggregate. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 209)

When a concrete pavement is rubblized because it is badly distressed, the broken pieces are rolled into the base or subbase and any unsuitable material must be replaced with AASHTO No. 1 coarse aggregate; this aggregate provides a stable, load‑bearing layer that compensates for the low support inherent in rubblizing and allows the required thick overlay to perform well over time, thereby enhancing pavement durability and reducing future maintenance needs. When subgrade soils are weak, the design may call for rock capping to strengthen the foundation; a layer of 1 to 3 feet deep composed of R‑3 Rock, AASHTO No. 1, or Best Available Rock can be placed as a durable cap, and this rock capping provides additional bearing capacity that influences the pavement structural design and helps improve long‑term performance and durability by reducing settlement and frost‑heave effects.
