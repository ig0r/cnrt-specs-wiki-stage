Paver

Part of: Lift is-subtopic-of
Paver is a topic under Lift.
paverpaving-machineasphalt-paverpaver-finisherhigh-density-asphalt-paver
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

A paver is a self‑propelled slip‑form or asphalt paving machine that deposits very dry, low‑slump roller‑compacted concrete or hot‑mix asphalt directly onto a prepared subgrade, spreading the material in a controlled width and depth while it remains plastic[1]. It incorporates built‑in compaction devices that achieve at least 85 % of maximum wet density for lifts under six inches and 90 % for thicker lifts before rollers are employed, and automatic line, grade and edge‑control systems keep alignment, limit edge slumping to fractions of an inch, and halt vibration the moment forward motion stops[2]. For specialized applications such as ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses or stone‑matrix asphalt, the paver can be fitted with a spray bar ahead of a variable‑width heated screed and a safety‑edge extrusion device that produces a wedge‑shaped edge at 26°–40°, allowing immediate rolling of the mat[1][2]

Purpose and applicability

The paver solves the problem of placing a very dry, low‑slump roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) mix onto a prepared subgrade by providing a machine that can lay the material in a controlled width and depth before rollers compact it; the achievable placement width and depth depend on the dimensions and size of the paver [1]. In addition, for RCC pavement the paver eliminates the difficulty of achieving a uniformly spread and finished surface by delivering the mixture to the required thickness, smoothness, texture, cross‑section and grade without segregation or permanent blemishes, and it provides built‑in compaction that reaches at least 85 % of maximum wet density for single lifts less than 6 inches and at least 90 % for lifts equal to or greater than 6 inches before rollers are employed [2]. For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements the slip‑form paver prevents uncontrolled lateral sag and uneven placement, maintains alignment and grade with automatic controls, and limits edge slumping to no more than 1/8‑inch between adjacent lanes or 1/4‑inch between a lane and its shoulder, thereby producing a uniform, fully consolidated slab [2]. In long‑life concrete pavement construction the slip‑form paver addresses loss of alignment and uncontrolled vibration by incorporating automatic line and grade controls, a positive interlock that stops all vibration and tamping when forward motion ceases, and a mechanical device that accurately spaces tie‑bar reinforcement; an electronic monitoring unit records vibrator frequency, clock time, station location and track speed at least every 25 feet or every 5 minutes, solving the problem of insufficient vibration tracking [2]. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses (Section 5.5) a self‑priming paver solves the problem of applying the polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane directly ahead of the screed so that the course can be laid in a single pass, sealing the pavement, reducing oxidation and weathering, which helps keep the surface resilient to fatigue and low‑temperature cracking and may also correct surface distresses such as raveling and moderate rutting [1]. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses the paver solves placement interruptions by requiring the quality‑control plan to address material delivered to the paver and its speed, with calculations that eliminate stoppages during laydown; a built‑in spray bar positioned in front of a variable‑width heated screed spreads the two layers in immediate succession, completing both operations within less than 5 seconds [2]. In pervious concrete pavement systems the paver overcomes the challenge of producing an acceptable final finish while preserving the designed void structure by striking off the material in a controlled manner with either a form‑riding paving machine, roller screed, vibrating screed or asphalt paver; when vehicular access is anticipated a paver equipped with compaction tampers ensures the finished slab meets the void content tolerance of 20 % ± 5 % and supports an infiltration rate greater than 200 inches per hour [2]. For plant‑mixed 6.3 mm thin asphalt overlay courses the paver addresses the need to place the mixture continuously at the specified laying temperature so that the quality‑control plan can be satisfied and immediate sampling for acceptance testing is possible; by delivering, spreading and compacting the mix directly from the screed it ensures the material is laid before cooling and provides a location where mixture samples can be taken without interrupting production [2]. In stone matrix asphalt wearing courses the paver equipped with a safety‑edge device solves the problem of forming a correctly shaped and compacted edge; the device confines material at the end gate and extrudes it in an adjustable wedge whose final angle must be 26 to 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope extended, allowing immediate rolling of the adjacent mat and permitting automatic transition to intersections, driveways, guiderail sections and other obstructions [2]. For concrete shoulder construction the paver prevents damage to a freshly prepared surface by restricting vehicle movement to the narrow zone directly ahead of the machine, prohibiting use of that surface for hauling; any damage must be repaired or replaced to the Representative’s satisfaction at no additional cost [2]. In hot‑mix asphalt placement the paver eliminates the difficulty of laying a continuous, uniformly thick layer that meets specified evenness, texture, width and depth without creating defects by providing self‑contained, power‑propelled units with activated screeds or strike‑off assemblies and automatic controls; it also prevents tearing, shoving, gouging, tracks, indented areas, flushing, segregation or other permanent blemishes, and requires auger extensions to be positioned within 18 inches of the end gate for fixed‑width operations [2]. For cement‑treated permeable base courses the paver solves the problem of achieving the required cross‑section, depth, profile and transverse grade across the lane without excessive thickness variations by using an approved asphalt paver or mechanical spreader equipped with a screed, plate vibrator and fully automated sensors; irregularities exceeding ½ inch must be corrected before compaction is completed [2]. In pervious asphalt pavement systems the track paver solves the problem of uniformly distributing the mixture across the lane by spreading or striking off the mix for the entire lane width and placing it in maximum 4‑inch compacted lifts; screed assemblies are adjusted to provide the indicated cross‑section depth and fully automated sensors control profile and transverse grade [2].

In Section 502—ROLLER COMPRESSED CONCRETE PAVEMENTS, when the placement area is reachable by equipment and the lift thickness falls within the ranges defined for roller‑compacted concrete, a conventional or high‑density paver equipped with compaction devices may be used; for single lifts under 6 inches the paver must achieve at least 85 % of the maximum wet density before rollers, for single lifts from 6 inches up to but not exceeding 9 inches a high‑density paver must reach at least 90 %, and for multiple‑lift placements either type of paver must provide at least 85 % per lift. If the work area cannot be accessed by a paver, alternate paving equipment must be submitted for approval. In Section 108—PERFORMANCE AND PROGRESS, when a contract calls for an asphalt paver specifying the use of a material‑transfer vehicle feeding a hopper insert to the paver’s slat conveyors and requiring continuous placement at the correct temperature, the paver must be provided and operated in accordance with those specifications; if the contract instead prescribes another method or equipment, the contractor must obtain written authority, submit technical data and a description of the proposed alternative, and discontinue its use if trial placement does not meet the contract requirements. In Section 501—PLAIN OR REINFORCED CEMENT CONCRETE PAVEMENTS, a fully energized, self‑propelled slip‑form paving machine equipped with side forms, automatic alignment and grade controls, and vibration/tamping that operates in continuous forward movement is appropriate for slip‑form concrete pavement; the paver should not be used if edge slumping cannot be kept within limits of 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes and ramps or 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders because once such slippage persists for more than 200 feet the operation must be suspended and an alternative placement method employed, and if forward movement is stopped its vibrating and tamping elements must be disengaged immediately. In Section 520—PERVIOUS CONCRETE PAVEMENT SYSTEM, where trucks or buses may access the concrete during placement a paver equipped with compaction tampers is required to achieve the proper finish; where such vehicle access is not expected the same final surface may be produced with a roller screed or vibrating screed instead. In Section 419—STONE MATRIX ASPHALT MIXTURE DESIGN, for Stone Matrix Asphalt courses at least 1.5 inches deep the paver equipped with an end‑gate confinement device is prescribed; the device must be attached to the paver and adjusted to extrude the material in a wedge shape as the mat is placed, but the safety edge requirement does not apply where a curb or sidewalk is encountered or where a guiderail face lies directly over the pavement edge, so in those situations the paver‑based safety‑edge procedure should be omitted and another treatment (or no safety edge) used instead, and a single plate strike‑off method may not be employed for the Safety Edge. In Section 413—SUPERPAVE MIXTURE DESIGN, plant‑mixed asphalt courses that require a finished surface of specified evenness and texture are appropriately placed with self‑contained, power‑propelled units equipped with activated screeds or activated strike‑off assemblies, automatic controls, heating capability, sufficient hopper capacity and uniform distribution at forward speeds that allow satisfactory placement; hydraulic or other extensions may be used only if they are fed and activated like the main screed, auger extensions must be limited to within 18 inches of the end gate, and non‑activated extensions are permitted only with Inspector‑in‑Charge approval. When work cannot be uniformly placed by a paver, or when equipment would tear, shove, gouge the mixture or cause tracks, flushing, segregation or other permanent blemishes, blade graders, drags or any prohibited equipment must not be used and an asphalt widener or another approved method should be employed instead. In Section 303—CEMENT TREATED PERMEABLE BASE COURSE, an approved asphalt paver equipped with screed, plate vibrator and fully automated sensors may be used when the contractor can apply it across the full pavement width or as much lane width as practical; if covering the entire lane is not practical a mechanical spreader (or handwork as directed) should be employed instead, but the paver alone cannot provide the required compaction because use of vibratory paver screed as sole means of attaining compaction is not allowed, so at least one pass with steel‑wheel power rollers weighing from 8 tons to 10 tons must follow paving. A paver equipped with a mechanical spreader and screed is the standard method for laying asphalt across an entire lane width, with screed assemblies adjusted to achieve the required cross‑section and depth; it is also used to construct the Safety Edge by attaching a device that confines and extrudes material at the end gate, but in locations where a mechanical spreader cannot be employed—such as tight or obstructed areas—the specification requires the mixture to be placed and screeded with suitable hand tools instead of the paver. In Section 420—PERVIOUS ASPHALT PAVEMENT SYSTEM, a track paver that complies with Section 413.3(e) should be used to spread and finish the mixture; it must place the material in compacted lifts not exceeding four inches, the screed is to be adjusted to obtain the required cross‑section and grade, and the surface should be allowed to cool to 100 °F before any subsequent layer is placed; if a track paver is unavailable or impractical, a mechanical spreader may be employed as an alternative method for spreading across the lane width. In Section 360—ASPHALT TREATED PERMEABLE BASE COURSE, a slip‑form paver referenced in Section 413.3(e) should be used to lay the mixture across the full lane width or as much of it as practical; the material is also to be placed in lifts not exceeding four inches and the screed adjusted with automated sensors to achieve the required cross‑section and profile, and this paver method is appropriate only when placement occurs above 35 °F, in dry weather, and within eight hours of mixing; if a slip‑form paver is unavailable or the specification permits, a mechanical spreader may be employed instead of the paver for the same spreading operation. Required inputs

For roller‑compacted concrete pavement (section 4.11) the paver may be a conventional or high‑density asphalt paver that places a very dry concrete mix composed of cement, aggregate and water; the mix uses a dense aggregate gradation with a maximum aggregate size less than 1 inch, may or may not include air entrainment, may contain limited admixtures, and must have a low water‑to‑cement ratio as low as 0.30 but typically 0.40, producing a no‑slump, damp‑gravel consistency [1]. The RCC application requires a prepared subgrade and subbase capable of supporting compaction and is limited to roadways with an average daily traffic ≤ 2000 and a speed limit ≤ 55 mph (or to shoulders and temporary pavements) [1]. For the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course (section 5.5) the self‑priming paver first lays a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane and then places a single lift of hot‑mix asphalt concrete; the hot‑mix must be one of three gradations—Type A, B or C—with nominal maximum aggregate sizes of 6.3 mm, 9.5 mm and 12.5 mm respectively, and the placed thickness shall be 1 to 1.5 times the diameter of the coarsest aggregate, giving a final surface thickness of ½ inch for Type A and ¾ inch for Types B and C [1]. The paver must receive an RCC mix that is free of frost, lumps or crusts and maintained within the temperature range of 50 °F to 90 °F; placement shall be stopped when ambient air temperatures fall below 40 °F and mixture‑temperature monitoring is required when temperatures exceed 85 °F so that the RCC mix does not rise above 90 °F [2]. The subgrade or subbase for RCC work must be clean, unfrozen and uniformly moist before paving, and evaporation rates may not exceed 0.20 pounds per square foot per hour unless windbreaks or fog‑misting are employed [2]. For single‑lift placements less than 6 inches the paver, whether conventional or high‑density, must achieve at least 85 % of the maximum wet density before rollers are used, and for lifts greater than or equal to 6 inches but less than 9 inches a high‑density paver equipped with compaction devices must achieve at least 90 % of the maximum wet density prior to rolling [2]. When placing hot‑mix asphalt the mixture shall be delivered through a covered material transfer vehicle that is protected under all conditions, fitted with remixing augers, and free of petroleum oils, solvents or other substances that could adversely affect the asphalt concrete; it must be supplied at the laying temperature limits prescribed in Table A (or Table B for the 6.3 mm wearing‑course) and contain no lumps of cold material [2]. The mixture shall meet the single‑sample tolerances for asphalt content and percent passing the No. 200 sieve as defined in the applicable tables, and placement may occur only when surfaces are dry and the air or surface temperature exceeds 50 °F (or, with an approved weather‑limitation exception, a minimum surface temperature of 50 °F and air temperature of 40 °F) [2]. Existing pavement shall be cleaned of loose material, joint sealer, crack filler and thermoplastic markings, tack‑coated with the specified material and allowed to break before paving [2]. Steel‑wheeled rollers weighing at least 10 tons and equipped with a watering or soapy system shall follow the paver; pneumatic wheels shall not be used, and if a direct behind‑paver sample cannot be obtained the acceptance sample may be taken from the hopper or screed [2]. For stone‑matrix asphalt courses the same delivery and temperature requirements apply, with lot‑acceptance criteria that include asphalt content and percent passing the No. 200 sieve within the tolerances of Table D, a theoretical maximum specific gravity that agrees with the contractor’s daily Gmm value within ±0.030, and a compacted density that meets the acceptance limits of Table F [2]. A safety edge shall be constructed with the same material used for the roadway course (or the paved shoulder if specified), organic material shall not be used, and the finished angle must fall between 26° and 40° measured from the pavement cross‑slope; moisture‑controlled steel‑wheel rollers shall begin rolling immediately after placement [2]. When a slip‑form paver is used for fresh concrete any reinforcement steel shall be clean of rust, dirt, oil or grease (a light powdery rust coating is permissible), and edge slump must not exceed 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders; if the paver cannot maintain those limits for a continuous 200‑foot run paving shall be suspended [2]. During curing the concrete temperature shall not rise above 160 °F and must be kept above 40 °F [2]. Pervious concrete placement requires a mix with a void structure of 20 % ± 5 % and an average hardened density that does not exceed 130 lb/ft³ with a tolerance of ± 5 lb/ft³; the test‑section thickness may be no more than 1/4 inch less than the design thickness [2]. The subgrade for pervious concrete must be undisturbed permeable soil wetted to saturation without standing water, must not be frozen, ambient air temperatures must stay above 40 °F during placement and for the following 24 hours, and rain must be absent until finishing and curing are complete; where truck or bus access is anticipated a paver equipped with compaction tampers shall be used [2]. The pervious asphalt pavement mix is laid with a track paver or mechanical spreader in compacted lifts not exceeding 4 inches; each lift must be allowed to cool to at least 100 °F before the next layer is placed, placement may only be done when air or surface temperature is 50 °F or higher, and test sections must demonstrate an average infiltration rate greater than 200 inches per hour [2]. The subgrade for pervious pavement shall have been excavated to undisturbed permeable soil without compaction and protected from heavy‑equipment traffic until the geotextile and stone bed are placed [2]. Asphalt‑treated permeable base course mix is produced by drying aggregate and heating asphalt below 320 °F so that it coats the particles with sufficient viscosity to avoid segregation; the material may not be stockpiled and must be placed within 8 hours of production [2]. Placement of ATPBC is prohibited on surfaces that are unstable, frozen, or when the air temperature is below 35 °F, and work cannot proceed during rain; the paver spreads ATPBC in lifts no thicker than four inches using a slip‑form paver or mechanical spreader [2]. Cold‑recycled asphalt base course requires an asphalt paver equipped to automatically meter liquids with a variation of not more than ± 2.0 % by weight, and the mixture must contain enough total liquid to fully blend reclaimed material, aggregate and emulsified asphalt; placement is limited to times when no freezing temperatures have occurred for at least 24 hours before paving and when ambient air temperature is 45 °F or higher and rising, with seasonal prohibitions as specified [2]. Courses whose compacted depth exceeds 6 inches shall be placed in two or more layers each between 3 and 6 inches thick; scratch or leveling courses less than 1 inch in depth or equal to or less than 110 lb/yd², mixtures placed in quantities not exceeding 500 tons continuously, and paving widths narrower than 4 feet may be used on stable, uniform bases without the usual density requirements [2]. Edge work adjacent to curbs, gutters or other abutting structures requires the wearing‑course mix to be laid slightly higher and sealed with hot asphalt (or permitted emulsified asphalt) of the class listed in Table A to form a watertight gutter at least 12 inches wide [2]. The paver must operate on a prepared surface that may be an asphaltic leveling course on rubblized concrete, a treated base course of cement or asphalt, or a No. OGS subbase; it shall travel only in the immediate area where concrete is being deposited ahead of the paver without using the surface for hauling so as not to damage it, and any damage must be repaired or replaced to the Representative’s satisfaction at no additional cost [2].

The ultra‑thin bonded wearing course (UTWC) requires a self‑priming paver that applies the polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane directly ahead of the screed and then places the hot‑mix overlay in a single lift. The aggregate permitted for the overlay is limited to nominal maximum sizes of 6.3 mm for Type A, 9.5 mm for Type B, and 12.5 mm for Type C. The finished UTWC thickness shall be one to one‑and‑a‑half times the diameter of the coarsest aggregate, resulting in a surface‑treatment thickness of ½ inch for Type A and ¾ inch for Types B and C. Prior to full production a minimum fifty‑foot long test section must be constructed with the proposed paver to verify mix consistency, placement capability, roller combination, density testing procedure, curing method and compressive strength, and the Representative shall approve the paver before use. The paver must possess sufficient weight and stability to spread and finish the RCC mixture without segregation while achieving the required thickness, smoothness, surface texture, cross‑section and grade. For single‑lift placements less than six inches the paver—whether conventional or high‑density—must be equipped with compaction devices capable of attaining at least 85 % of the maximum wet density before rollers are applied. For single‑lift placements equal to or greater than six inches but less than nine inches a high‑density paver must achieve at least 90 % of the maximum wet density before rollers are applied. When multiple lifts are used each lift shall reach a minimum of 85 % of the maximum wet density before rolling, with no single lift exceeding nine inches and a minimum lift thickness of four inches; thicker sections shall be placed in equal‑thickness multiple lifts. The asphalt paver must be supplied with a manufactured hopper insert that provides a mass flow of material directly to its slat conveyors, and the mix shall be delivered from a Material Transfer Vehicle equipped with adequate covers, remixing augers, free of petroleum oils or other contaminants, at the laying temperature specified in Table A of Section 413.2(e) and free from lumps of chilled material. If special equipment not normally used in highway construction is required, technical data shall be submitted as called for in Section 108.05(c)2. The slip‑form paving machine shall be fully energized, self‑propelled and equipped with side forms, automatic alignment and grade controls, and vibrating or tamping elements that act across the full width and depth of the concrete layer; longitudinal joints shall be constructed parallel to and within twelve inches of final pavement markings unless otherwise detailed. Edge slumping shall be tested before the concrete becomes non‑plastic with a twelve‑foot straightedge over the six‑inch zone adjacent each edge, and acceptable slump shall not exceed one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes and ramps and not more than one‑quarter inch between lanes and shoulders or ramps and shoulders; paving shall be suspended after two hundred feet if these limits cannot be maintained, with a retest after hardening using the same straightedge to confirm compliance. The QC plan must address material delivered to the paver as it relates to paver speed, and calculations supporting material production, haul‑unit capacity and paver speed shall be provided to eliminate placement stoppages during laydown. Work sequence

Prior to initiating any paving operation, the applicable specifications require a series of preparatory actions that vary by pavement type and section. Before a paver can place Roller Compacted Concrete, the project must have a prepared subgrade and subbase course that has been identified as acceptable during the design phase and is capable of supporting the compaction of the RCC pavement; these layers are treated as separate construction items, and a paving‑operation QC Plan must be prepared and reviewed by the Representative, paving may not start until that review is completed, the contractor must provide a concrete technician and a concrete field testing technician as required in Section 704.1(d), and all equipment—including mixing plant, haul trucks, the paver, compactors and water trucks—must be on site and approved by the Representative [1][2]. Under SECTION 412—SUPERPAVE MIXTURE DESIGN, CONSTRUCTION OF PLANT‑MIXED 6.3 MM THIN ASPHALT OVERLAY COURSES, a paving‑operation QC Plan prepared on Form CS‑413 must be submitted to the Representative before or at the pre‑construction conference and may not be started until the Representative has reviewed it; an asphalt preplacement meeting with the Representative must be scheduled at least two weeks before placing the mixtures to review specification, the QC Plan, sequencing, mixture acceptance, density acceptance and sample custody [2]. In SECTION 108—PERFORMANCE AND PROGRESS, CHARACTER OF WORKERS; METHODS AND EQUIPMENT, a Material Transfer Vehicle that meets the listed requirements—including adequate covers, capability of uniform continuous transfer, remixing augers and freedom from petroleum oils or other contaminants—must be provided, the paver must be equipped with a manufactured hopper insert, and the mixture must be delivered through the MTV at the laying temperature required by Section 413.2(e) Table A without any lumps of chilled material; all equipment must be furnished in the type, condition and quantity specified and maintained in good condition before use [2]. For the slip‑form paving method described in SECTION 501—PLAIN OR REINFORCED CEMENT CONCRETE PAVEMENTS, load‑transfer units must already be installed and secured at their specified locations, the fastening method for those units must have been demonstrated to and approved by the Representative, and a bond‑breaker lubricant must be applied to at least one‑half of each Type B dowel bar at least one hour before concrete is placed around the dowel assembly [2]. Under SECTION 489—ULTRA‑THIN BONDED WEARING COURSE, the QC plan must address material delivered to the paver as it relates to paver speed and provide calculations that support material production, haul units used for delivery and paver speed so that placement stoppages are eliminated during laydown [2]. Before a paver equipped with compaction tampers can be used in SECTION 520—PERVIOUS CONCRETE PAVEMENT SYSTEM, the subgrade must be excavated to undisturbed soil and scarified at least six inches where fine material has accumulated, any damaged areas must be repaired and accepted by the Representative; a detention basin must be built with a minimum two‑inch layer of fine aggregate, geotextile wrap, and coarse aggregate placed in lifts no more than eight inches and compacted as specified; forms must be set, aligned, braced and treated with form‑release agent so that tolerances and elevations are met; and the concrete mix must be produced by truck mixers operating at the manufacturer’s speed and placed within ninety minutes after the final introduction of any mix water. Only after these steps are completed may the paver be employed to deposit the concrete [2]. In SECTION 419—STONE MATRIX ASPHALT, the project must first satisfy the RPS mixture‑and‑density lot acceptance requirement and obtain approval for the Safety Edge device that will be mounted on the paver; only after those approvals are in place may the safety edge be constructed and the paver begin production [2]. For the slip‑form paving method of SECTION 530—LONG‑LIFE CONCRETE PAVEMENT, the required equipment must already be in place, including a slip‑form paver equipped with automatic line and grade controls, a positive interlock that stops all vibration when forward motion ceases, and a mechanical device that accurately spaces tie‑bars together with a non‑oscillating extrusion plate of adjustable entry angle; an electronic monitoring device must also be installed to continuously display each internal vibrator’s frequency, record clock time, station location, paver track speed and individual vibrator frequencies at least every 25 feet or every 5 minutes, with its readout visible to the operator and Representative; additionally no equipment other than sawing tools or a lightweight profiler may be run on the fresh slab, and wheels or tracks must stay more than four inches from the slab edge [2]. Before the paver can be started in concrete shoulder work (Section 658—CONCRETE SHOULDERS), the prepared surface must be protected from damage during concrete transport and deposition; equipment may travel on that surface only in the immediate paving area needed for placing concrete ahead of the paver, and any areas that become damaged must be repaired or replaced to the Representative’s satisfaction at no extra cost [2]. Before an approved asphalt paver is used for a cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303—CEMENT TREATED PERMEABLE BASE COURSE), the rubblized pavement or subbase must first be conditioned by thoroughly sprinkling water until a moist condition is achieved, applying as much water as the material readily absorbs and doing so well in advance of placement [2]. Before the track paver can be used for a pervious asphalt pavement system (Section 420—PERVIOUS ASPHALT PAVEMENT SYSTEM), the subgrade must be excavated to undisturbed soil without compaction, any damaged areas repaired, and the Representative notified for final inspection and acceptance; the detention basin must then be constructed with a fine‑aggregate base, geotextile wrap, and coarse aggregate placed in lifts and compacted as specified in Section 360.3; any required asphalt‑treated permeable base course per Section 360.3 must be installed; placement may not occur when the air or surface temperature is lower than 50 °F; existing surfaces must be conditioned by correcting irregularities, removing contaminated binder course, and applying an emulsified asphalt coating as prescribed before any wearing course is placed [2]. Before the slip‑form paver can be used to spread Asphalt Treated Permeable Base Course (Section 360—ASPHALT TREATED PERMEABLE BASE COURSE), the base surface must be stable and free of ice, the air temperature must be at least 35 °F with no rain, the aggregate must be dried as needed, the asphalt heated, and the two combined so that a film of adequate thickness is formed; the resulting mixture must be produced at a temperature below 320 °F with suitable viscosity, and it must be placed within eight hours of production because stockpiling is prohibited [2]. Before the paver can be started under the mat‑density acceptance procedure (Section 413—SUPERPAVE MIXTURE DESIGN, MAT DENSITY ACCEPTANCE), the Department must first approve the mat‑density acceptance level for the chosen acceptance method; the density of the fresh mix must then be measured with an approved nuclear or electrical impedance gauge in the presence of the Representative and the contractor’s certified asphalt field technician, an optimum‑rolling pattern must be established, documented on the required TR‑4276B or TR‑4276C form, and submitted to the Representative; only after these approvals are received may mixture placement begin [2].

The timing limits that govern critical steps of a paver are set out separately for each pavement type and construction method. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502), the second lift must be placed within 60 minutes of completing the first lift, and adjacent paving lanes must be placed within 60 minutes of one another; field density testing shall be performed as soon as possible but no later than 30 minutes after rolling is finished. In slip‑form concrete work (Section 501), the vibrating and tamping elements must be disengaged immediately when forward motion stops, any bottom‑layer concrete left uncovered for more than 1/2 hour must be removed and replaced, curing covers may be removed for sawing only for a maximum of 1/2 hour, and if an interruption in concreting exceeds 30 minutes the crew shall construct a transverse joint using a bulkhead. For pervious concrete pavement systems (Section 520), placement of the mix must occur within 90 minutes after the final introduction of any mixing water, curing shall begin within 20 minutes of discharge, and if the polyethylene curing sheet is removed the curing operation must be reinstated within 60 minutes; rollers are prohibited from bridging the newly placed slab for at least seven days or until the Representative confirms sufficient strength. In Superpave wearing‑course asphalt (Section 412), adequate time shall be allowed for the tack coat to break completely before any asphalt mixture is laid, and rolling must begin immediately after placement while the mix remains in proper condition. Stone matrix asphalt (Section 419) requires that rolling start immediately after placement and may not be delayed adjacent to a constructed Safety Edge; core samples shall be taken within one day of placing the mixture and IA samples shall be delivered to the testing point within three days of receipt, while work stoppages shorter than five calendar days do not require redefining the lot but a stoppage of five calendar days or more triggers an adjustment of lot size and sub‑lot numbers. The ultra‑thin bonded wearing course (Section 489) mandates that spreading of the flexible cementitious emulsion and the UTWC be performed consecutively within less than 5 seconds. Long‑life concrete pavement constructed by slip‑form (Section 530) requires data capture at intervals no longer than every 25 feet or every 5 minutes, and the positive interlock system shall stop all vibration and tamping elements as soon as forward motion ceases. Cement‑treated permeable base course work (Section 303) limits the interval from water addition to completion of compaction to no more than 1 hour, although a retarding admixture may extend this period to 1½ hours; curing must begin immediately after compaction and the curing compound or polyethylene cover shall remain in place for 24 hours, with an additional 24‑hour extension if temperature falls below 40 °F. Quality control and acceptance

The paver must have suitable weight and stability to spread and finish the RCC mixture without segregation, must achieve the required thickness, smoothness, surface texture, cross‑section and grade, must not tear, shove, gouge or cause tracks, indented areas, segregation or other permanent blemishes, and its compaction devices must be capable of a minimum compaction of 85 % of the maximum wet density for lifts under six inches (or each lift in multiple‑lift placement) and a minimum compaction of 90 % of the maximum wet density for single lifts between six and nine inches before roller use, as required by Section 502.3(d). The paver must be fitted with an approved safety‑edge device that confines the material at the end gate and produces a wedge whose final angle is measured each day and must fall between 26 and 40 degrees from the pavement cross‑slope extended; the mixture placed by the paver must be sampled for acceptance, requiring testing of asphalt content and the percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve against the single‑sample tolerances, and density cores taken within one day must verify percent compaction using a theoretical maximum specific gravity (Gmm) that may not differ from the contractor’s daily Gmm by more than ±0.030. For the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course construction described in Section 489.3, the paver must be inspected to confirm it includes a built‑in spray bar positioned in front of the variable‑width heated screed unit, and the inspection also verifies that the spreading of UTFCEM and the UTWC can be performed in succession within a period of less than 5 seconds. The slip‑form paver must be inspected for automatic line‑and‑grade controls, a positive interlock system that halts all vibration and tamping when the machine stops moving, and a mechanical device that accurately spaces and positions tie‑bar reinforcement while providing a non‑oscillating extrusion plate with an adjustable entry angle; the electronic monitoring unit on the paver must continuously display each internal vibrator’s operating frequency at the operator’s station, allow both manual and automatic sequencing of those frequencies, and record clock time, station location, track speed and individual vibrator frequencies at least every 25 feet of paving or at least every 5 minutes; additionally, no equipment wheels or tracks may be placed within 4 inches of the new slab edge during construction. The inspector should verify that the asphalt paver provides a self‑contained, power‑propelled unit with an activated screed (or strike‑off assembly) and automatic screed controls capable of producing the specified evenness and texture, that it is heated and can spread and finish the mixture to the indicated widths and depths, that it operates at forward speeds consistent with satisfactory placement, that its receiving hopper has sufficient capacity for uniform spreading and its distribution system places the mixture uniformly in front of the screed; the inspector must also confirm that any hydraulic or other extension types are used only if the unit feeds and activates the extension by the same method as the main screed, that auger extensions are provided to within 18 inches or less of the end gate, that non‑activated extensions are employed only when approved, and that the equipment does not tear, shove, gouge, cause tracks, indented areas, flushing, segregation or other permanent blemishes; blade graders or drags must not be used. The paver’s output must be verified by testing loose‑mix acceptance samples for asphalt content and for the percent passing the 75 mm (No. 200) sieve, and by testing a second sample to confirm the theoretical maximum specific gravity; core specimens are also examined for in‑place density; macrotexture of the finished mat is measured with PTM No. 751 and any pattern segregation is rejected when the texture depth difference exceeds 0.024 in., while flushing is declared defective when the average texture depth is ≤ 0.006 in.; the safety edge produced by the paver‑mounted device must be checked each day and must have a final slope of 26 to 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope extension. The asphalt paver used for the cold‑recycled base course must be capable of automatically metering liquids so that the variation does not exceed ± 2.0 % by weight, and it must be employed to place the CRABC mixture in accordance with Section 413,3(e)1; in addition, the paver and all related equipment shall be maintained as required by Section 108.05(c). Safety and environmental controls

Cooling or curing, protection, and handoff

For an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course placed by a self‑priming paver, the hot‑mix asphalt concrete immediately seals over the polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane, which reduces oxidation and weathering of the surface and thus protects the newly placed material; no specific cooling or curing actions are described beyond this sealing effect. When roller‑compacted concrete is laid by a paver and the field density test has been completed, the surface must be kept moist using one or more curing methods such as a water‑truck misting system, white polyethylene sheeting, or a curing compound, and these methods are to be maintained for at least 72 hours until the pavement attains a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi. If the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·hr during RCC curing, windbreaks or fog misting may be employed to prevent excess moisture loss, and haul trucks must have covers to protect the mixture during transit. For slip‑form concrete, curing materials are applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and then kept in place until the slab reaches the strength required for opening to traffic; the contractor may use any approved method but must keep the same curing system throughout unless directed otherwise, common methods including white polyethylene sheeting overlapped at least 12 inches with sealed edges, burlap‑backed sheets kept saturated, or a liquid membrane‑forming compound applied as a continuous film at about one gallon per 150 ft² while the surface is still damp. During all concrete curing operations the temperature of the concrete must not rise above 160°F and, when ambient temperatures are low, insulation or heated blankets must be used to keep the slab temperature at least 40°F, extending the cure period if it falls between 40°F and 35°F; joint areas are also covered and protected in the same manner. After pervious concrete is placed, curing must begin within twenty minutes of discharge and the surface is completely covered with polyethylene sheeting that is securely fastened at all edges and laps to resist wind, and this protective cover is kept in place for a minimum of seven days before any traffic or further work; the same seven‑day protection period also governs roller operations, which may not be allowed to bridge previously placed concrete until the representative confirms sufficient strength. For cement‑treated permeable base course, curing begins immediately after placement and the contractor either sprays a white membrane‑forming curing compound at about one gallon per 100 ft² (±10 %) or covers the surface with white polyethylene sheeting, keeping the curing material in place for 24 hours; if temperature drops below 40°F during that period the cure is extended another 24 hours, and any drop to 32°F within 72 hours requires removal and replacement at no cost to the Department. Construction traffic is prohibited for at least three days after cement‑treated permeable base course has been placed and no overlying pavement may be placed during that time; after three days only trucks and equipment needed for the next lift or adjacent work are allowed. Immediately after placing an asphalt mixture, the crew works the exposed outer edges to eliminate sharp, ragged, and open edges and repairs any edge breakdown, thereby protecting the newly placed surface; where the pavement meets curbs, gutters, or other abutting structures a seal of hot asphalt (or, when permitted, emulsified asphalt) is applied at least twelve inches wide to form a watertight joint that further shields the mat. When pervious asphalt is spread and struck off by a track paver, the fresh mix must be allowed to cool to 100°F before any subsequent layer or pavement course is placed; compaction may only begin when the surface has cooled enough to resist a 10‑ton steel‑wheeled static roller (or vibratory roller in static mode) and is limited to no more than four passes so that porosity is not reduced. After asphalt‑treated permeable base course is placed, the mat is left to cool until it reaches about 100°F before any subsequent pavement layer is laid, after which rolling with an 8‑ton to 10‑ton steel‑wheeled roller is performed as soon as the mat has cooled sufficiently to avoid shoving or lateral movement; when work stops for a full day or after more than a 30‑minute interruption, a temporary vertical bulkhead is installed to protect the fresh surface and create a straight transverse construction joint. For cold‑recycled base, exposed sections are protected from marring, distortion, and other damage by keeping construction equipment away and correcting any damage to the Representative’s satisfaction; when an emulsified asphalt stabilizing agent is used, the material must be cured for at least two weeks before a wearing or binder course is placed, or until the average moisture content is less than 2 %, and foamed‑asphalt stabilized mixes must remain in cure until the average moisture content drops below 2 %, with moisture verified by PTM No. 749 procedure and random core samples taken as required. Finally, longitudinal joints of surface courses are protected by applying a hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder sealant; the joint must be clean, dry and free of loose material before sealing, the sealant is heated and maintained between 265°F and 320°F, and it may not be placed when the air temperature is below 40°F unless the Representative permits it; application is performed with a pressure applicator that delivers a straight band approximately 4 inches wide (±1 inch) and about 1/16 inch thick (±1/32 inch), excess sealant is removed, any missed spots are resealed, and pavement markings disturbed by the sealing are replaced at no additional cost. Expected performance and maintenance

For roller‑compacted concrete pavements the paver must spread and finish the mix without tearing, shoving or gouging it, and must not produce tracks, indented areas, segregation or any permanent surface blemish; otherwise the pavement is considered distressed. The equipment also has to achieve at least 85 % of maximum wet density for lifts under six inches and 90 % for thicker lifts, and a minimum 95 % field density overall, and if these values are not met additional rollers must be used. When using the slip‑form paving method the paver’s forward motion shall remain continuous; if it stops the operator shall immediately disengage the vibrating and tamping elements, otherwise concrete may not be fully consolidated. Edge slump must not exceed 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes or lane‑to‑ramps and must not exceed 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders; if these limits cannot be maintained paving shall be suspended after 200 feet, the section evaluated and defective concrete removed and replaced at no additional cost. The safety‑edge profile produced by the paver must fall within 26 to 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope extended, and any deviation requires an immediate stop until corrected. Compaction that results in flushing, displacement, shoving, cracking or aggregate breakage is a distress that mandates halting paving work. If density results for two consecutive lots—or for a total of three lots—do not achieve the 100 % payment factor, or if asphalt content or the percent passing the 75 µm sieve fall outside their respective tolerances for the same lot sequence, paving must be stopped and corrective action taken. Damage to the prepared surface caused by the paver traveling beyond the immediate area while transporting or depositing concrete ahead of the machine shall be repaired or replaced to the Representative’s satisfaction at no additional cost. Improperly executed contraction joints, such as those marked without tacks or other approved methods or cut with saws lacking guides, blade guards, water‑cooling systems or cut‑depth control, lead to inadequate transverse or longitudinal joint formation and constitute a distress. Surface irregularities that exceed more than 1/2 inch are considered defective and must be corrected by loosening the surface mixture and removing or adding material. Over‑compacting shall not eliminate free drainage or crush the aggregate; doing so is a distress. Use of a vibratory paver screed as the sole means of attaining compaction is prohibited because it may leave the base insufficiently densified. Construction traffic is not allowed on the cement‑treated permeable base course for at least 3 days after placement, and allowing traffic before that period can damage the newly placed material. Pattern segregation becomes unacceptable when the difference in average pavement texture depth between segregated and non‑segregated areas exceeds 0.024 inch; such segregation is a distress. Flushing, defined as continuous or repeated excess asphalt on the surface, is defective when the measured average texture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 inch. Edge breakdown may occur if exposed outer edges are not worked immediately to remove sharp, ragged and open edges, leading to premature edge failure. Improper compaction that produces displacement, shoving, cracking, aggregate breakage or loose areas must be removed and replaced with fresh hot mix. The mixture placed by the paver shall be free of lumps of cold material; presence of such lumps is a failure to meet general placement requirements. [2]

For slip‑form paving covered by Section 501.3(g) preventive upkeep includes keeping the side forms, automatic alignment controls and vibrating/tamping mechanisms in good working order; operators shall monitor edge slump with a 12‑foot straightedge before the concrete loses plasticity and shall ensure that the measured drop does not exceed 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and does not exceed 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders, pausing the paver if forward movement stops and immediately disengaging the vibrating/tamping elements; if edge slump exceeds those limits over a continuous 200‑foot run the paving shall be suspended for evaluation, and after hardening any remaining slippage must be reported to the District Executive for corrective action, with defective pavement removed and replaced at no extra cost. Section 530.3(g) requires the slip‑form paver to be fitted with an electronic monitoring device that continuously displays each internal vibrator’s operating frequency on a readout visible to the operator and the Representative, and the device shall record clock time, station location, paver track speed and individual vibrator frequencies at least every 25 feet of paving or at least every 5 minutes. According to Section 419.3(1.c), the paver’s safety‑edge attachment shall be approved before construction and shall be inspected each day by measuring the extruded edge angle from the pavement cross‑slope extended; the required final shape is a slope of 26 to 40 degrees, and because the device is adjustable the operator may vary the wedge profile to keep the angle within that range as compaction rolls up the edge, but if the measured angle does not meet the 26‑to‑40‑degree requirement paving shall be stopped and corrective action taken on the paver or its attachment before production resumes. Under Section 413.3(h) daily monitoring also includes measuring the Safety Edge angle from the pavement cross‑slope extended after final compaction, confirming that it lies between 26 and 40 degrees; if it falls outside that range paving shall be stopped and corrective action taken on the paver or its safety‑edge device, and screed assemblies shall be regularly adjusted to maintain the required cross‑section and depth while the approved safety‑edge extrusion device must remain functional to confine material at the end gate. Finally, for cold‑recycled asphalt base courses covered by Section 341.3(a) the asphalt paver shall be maintained in accordance with the program of Section 108.05(c), its automatic liquid‑metering device shall be checked regularly so that weight variation does not exceed ± 2.0 % by weight, and the machine must remain capable of meeting the placement requirements set out in Section 413,3(e)1.
