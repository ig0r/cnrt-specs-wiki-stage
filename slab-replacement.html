Slab Replacement

Part of: Concrete Patching is-subtopic-of
Slab Replacement is a topic under Concrete Patching.
slab-replacementfull-depth-replacementfull-depth-replacement
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Slab replacement is a corrective‑maintenance treatment applied to rigid (concrete) pavements when an isolated slab has deteriorated to the point that its full width and depth must be removed and replaced, thereby restoring safe and efficient operation after damage has occurred [1]. The treatment is triggered by severe concrete distress such as cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third of the slab thickness (D/3), failed joints, edge spalls, corner breaks, excessive edge slumping, or transverse cracks between grout‑injection holes that cannot be remedied by localized repair [2]. The process involves lift‑out removal of the defective slab, preparation and compaction of a suitable subbase, placement of a new precast or cast‑in‑place slab meeting specified dimensional tolerances and strength requirements, and final inspection before traffic is reopened, returning the pavement to an acceptable level of service.

Purpose and treatment objectives

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, slab replacement is applied as a corrective‑maintenance treatment to rigid pavements when an isolated slab has deteriorated to the point that its full width and depth must be removed and replaced, targeting severe concrete distress such as failed joints, edge spalls, or corner breaks that threaten safety and future pavement integrity. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that slab replacement is called for when a concrete pavement slab exhibits cracking that exceeds the limits set out in Section 11, specifically cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third of the slab depth (D/3), which are to be replaced rather than repaired. The same specifications also require replacement for slabs showing manufacturing imperfections, handling damage, or construction damage that cannot be remedied by repair. Additional triggers include edge slump that exceeds the allowable tolerances of 1⁄8 in between adjacent lanes (or ramps) and 1⁄4 in between a lane (or ramp) and a shoulder after hardening, surface‑tolerance violations beyond 3⁄16 in, depth deficiencies greater than 1⁄4 in, insufficient compressive strength, curing defects, or any other defect listed in Section 501.3(t). The specifications further note that the appearance of transverse cracks between adjacent grout‑injection holes indicates that stabilization will not be effective and therefore mandates removal and replacement of the entire slab. [1][2]

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), slab replacement is classified as a corrective‑maintenance treatment for rigid pavements, and because corrective maintenance is defined as work performed in response to a deficiency that threatens safe and efficient operation and is intended to restore the pavement to an acceptable level of service, its primary objective is to restore functionality after damage has occurred [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications further state that slab‑replacement procedures are directed primarily toward repairing damage; Section 529 requires replacement of any slab whose cracks exceed 0.007 inches and are greater than one‑third of the slab depth (D/3), thereby removing the damaged concrete and restoring the pavement’s condition [2]. Section 501.3(t) adds that slabs judged defective because of construction errors, curing problems, surface‑tolerance violations, insufficient depth, poor air content, inadequate compressive strength, or other surface defects must be removed and replaced so that the new slab meets all specified performance criteria, which repairs the damaged portion and returns the pavement to an acceptable condition [2]. Likewise, Section 679.3 requires full‑depth replacement when transverse cracks develop between adjacent grout injection holes, with the aim of repairing the damaged concrete and restoring functional performance at no additional cost to the Department [2].

Distress characterization and causes

The distress that triggers slab replacement is the appearance of transverse cracks between adjacent grout injection holes; when such cracks are observed, the entire concrete slab must be replaced under Section 516, indicating a severe condition that cannot be remedied by localized stabilization. The source does not give a quantified progression rate or specific safety/operational impacts beyond the general requirement to observe normal safety rules during work. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

The distress that triggers slab replacement is identified when concrete shows defects such as edge slumping beyond the allowable limits, honeycombing, cracks or spalls before final inspection, and surface deviations caused by freezing‑thawing cycles, rain infiltration, or an improper final finish; it also includes failures to meet curing requirements or joint specifications. Under Section 501.3(t) any pavement deemed defective for these reasons must be removed and replaced at no additional cost. [2]

Applicability and treatment selection

Slab replacement is classified as a corrective‑maintenance treatment for rigid (concrete) pavements and is applied when isolated deficiencies—such as failed joints, edge spalls or corner breaks—have developed that negatively affect the safe, efficient operation of the facility; because it is a reactive measure, the treatment is used only at isolated locations where the distress is localized rather than requiring full‑depth reconstruction of the entire pavement section, and it restores functionality without increasing structural capacity [1]. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, slab replacement is appropriate when an existing slab exhibits cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third the slab thickness (D/3); in those cases the damaged slab must be removed by lift‑out without disturbing the subbase, only if the concrete to be removed can be replaced during the workday so that traffic can be restored promptly, and slabs lacking effective load transfer or undersealing shall not be exposed to traffic for more than three days [2]. The new precast unit’s mix design must account for ambient average daily temperatures and exposure conditions at the project site in order to size panels and locate expansion joints correctly [2]. The subbase beneath the replacement area must have any soft, wet, or unsuitable material removed, be rebuilt with compacted No. 2A coarse aggregate, and finished to a firm even surface within plus or minus 1/2 inch of grade and contour [2]. When transverse cracks develop between adjacent grout injection holes during slab stabilization, the entire slab shall be replaced as specified in Section 516; this work may only proceed if qualified personnel are on‑site and a crew supervisor with at least five years’ experience has been assigned, and it must be performed when daytime temperatures are not below 40 °F, the subgrade or base course is not frozen, and air temperature for deflection testing is between 40 °F and 70 °F with no spring‑thaw conditions [2]. After grout placement traffic may not be allowed until at least twelve hours have elapsed or as required by the approved field QC plan [2].

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Slab replacement is presented as a corrective‑maintenance option for rigid pavements only when the failure is confined to an isolated location, so it is unsuitable when distress is widespread or the pavement remains structurally sound but exhibits surface defects; in those cases preventive‑maintenance treatments such as partial or full‑depth concrete repairs, joint sealing, diamond grinding, or other isolated slab repairs are preferred [1]. Non‑structural enhancements that do not address load‑carrying capacity fall under minor rehabilitation rather than slab replacement [1]. Structural deficiencies that require increased load‑carrying capacity call for major rehabilitation or reconstruction instead of slab replacement [1]. The specification states that slab replacement is only called for when a crack exceeds the width of 0.007 inches and its depth is greater than one‑third of the slab thickness (D/3); in those cases the slab must be replaced at no additional cost to the Department [2]. When cracks are wider than 0.007 inches but shallower than D/3, repair according to the latest ACI 224R recommendations is required rather than replacement [2]. For cracks that are 0.007 inches wide or less, sealing with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate is prescribed instead of slab replacement [2].

Condition assessment and timing

For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, a field inspection must first identify any spalls or visible cracks and then measure each crack’s width; if a measured crack exceeds 0.007 inches the inspector should also determine its depth relative to the slab thickness D, because cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third of D trigger slab replacement, while narrower or shallower cracks are repaired according to ACI guidelines; recurring damage or distress observed during inspection may also prompt further evaluation before deciding on replacement. To decide if a concrete slab must be removed and replaced, the crew first inspects edge slumping by running a 12‑foot straightedge over the fresh surface and again after hardening, confirming that slump does not exceed 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes or 1/4 inch between lane and shoulder; next they verify surface tolerance on the hardened slab with a 12‑foot straightedge, accepting only variations of no more than 3/16 inch both parallel and perpendicular to the centerline; pavement thickness is then measured either by coring per PTM No. 606 or by non‑destructive testing per PTM No. 605, and any core that is deficient by more than 1/4 inch (or 1/2 inch for overall depth) triggers a defective condition; finally compressive strength of cylinders is tested according to PTM No. 604 and if the strength falls below 3,500 psi an investigation is required, with any failure in these inspections or tests leading to slab replacement as prescribed in Section 501.3(t). For slab stabilization projects, a visual inspection for transverse cracks between adjacent grout injection holes must be performed because the appearance of such cracks triggers full‑depth slab replacement; in addition, deflection testing in accordance with ASTM D4695 is required at each joint or crack, measuring the loaded slab corner deflection and the unloaded slab corner deflection to calculate joint efficiency, and joints or cracks that show a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more are candidates for stabilization, while if joint efficiency is below 65 % they may be patched, and movement exceeding the specified limits after two groutings leads the Representative to direct removal and replacement with a full‑depth concrete patch. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–447), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Slab Replacement is employed as a corrective maintenance treatment for rigid pavements when an isolated portion of the pavement has developed a deficiency that threatens safe, efficient operation, and the distress has progressed to the stage where it negatively impacts the pavement’s functional condition. Slab replacement shall be applied when a concrete pavement slab exhibits cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third of the slab thickness (greater than D/3); under those conditions the slab must be replaced at no additional cost to the Department. Slab replacement is also warranted whenever the pavement is judged defective under Section 501.3(t), which includes construction or sawing errors in transverse joints, failure to meet curing requirements, surface‑tolerance violations, depth deficiencies, excessive air content, compressive‑strength shortfalls, or any surface damage from freezing‑thawing, rain, improper finish, or honeycombing that cannot be repaired; at that point the defective slab must be removed and replaced. For reinforced cement concrete pavements the replacement length shall be at least 10 feet between transverse joints, and for plain cement concrete pavements it shall be at least 6 feet between transverse joints. During cement‑grout stabilization, full slab replacement is called for when transverse cracks appear between adjacent grout injection holes; in that situation the entire slab must be replaced as specified in Section 516. Additionally, if after two properly performed groutings a slab still exhibits movement beyond the allowed limits, the Representative may direct removal and replacement with a full‑depth concrete patch per Section 516. [1][2]

Materials and repair details

For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slab replacement, the slabs must be fabricated by a manufacturer whose PCPS system is listed in Bulletin 15 and whose concrete mix designs are submitted at least 28 days before production, showing trial batch data that achieve the minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and flexural strength of 650 psi. The slabs are cast with a maximum length of 15 ft (20 ft if prestressed) and a minimum length of 6 ft, include a sacrificial thickness of 1/4 in above the nominal design, have smooth bottom surfaces and top textures per Section 501.3(k), contain load‑transfer dowels and tie bars cast within ±1/8 in of the shop drawing locations with corresponding slots, grout ports, recessed lifting devices (minimum 1 in below surface) filled with cementitious grout, optional foam gaskets and slab‑setting bolt hardware as required by the manufacturer, and must meet the dimensional tolerances shown in Table A. Installation requires full‑depth saw‑cutting, lift‑out removal of existing pavement, placement of a compacted No. 2A coarse‑aggregate subbase within ±½ in of grade, fine‑grading of bedding material to a tolerance of 1/8 in over the slab length, and positioning of slabs so that transverse joint widths do not exceed ½ in (minimum ¼ in) and longitudinal joints do not exceed ¾ in, with load transfer provided by dowel slots or narrow‑mouth surface slots as detailed in the shop drawings; when the four‑bolt setting method is used, the bolts are removed once the cementitious bedding reaches a compressive strength of 50 psi or within 15 minutes after polyurethane injection. Repair provisions call for spall repair per Section 501.3(t), replacement of any slab cracked wider than 0.007 in and deeper than one‑third the slab depth, sealing of narrower cracks with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate followed by sand after about 20 minutes, and all dowel and bedding grout must attain a compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is allowed. In addition, slab replacement performed under the slip‑form paving method requires removal of the defective concrete slab with a minimum length between transverse joints of 10 ft for reinforced cement concrete or 6 ft for plain cement concrete, and the new slab must be placed full‑lane width with transverse joints built according to the Standard Drawings, salvaging or replacing any existing joint it abuts. The replacement continues longitudinally until the repaired section is not more than ¼ inch deficient in depth, as required by Section 501.3(s), and all work is performed at no additional cost to the Department. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–447)

For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529), the concrete mix must achieve a compressive strength of at least 4,925 psi and a flexural strength of at least 650 psi. The slab’s coefficient of thermal expansion shall be matched to the site’s average daily temperatures. Transverse joint widths shall be kept between ¼ inch and ½ inch, longitudinal joints shall not exceed ¾ inch, and finished vertical differentials across any joint shall not exceed ¼ inch (a maximum of ½ inch is allowable only after grinding). The subbase must be free of soft, wet or unsuitable material and compacted to within ±½ inch of grade, and the bedding surface must be leveled so that variations do not exceed ⅛ inch over a slab length. Load‑transfer dowels shall be cast with a bond breaker and grout ports must deliver bedding grout that reaches at least 2,500 psi compressive strength before traffic is allowed. For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements using the slip‑form paving method (Section 501), reinforcement placed in the new slab shall be free of rust, dirt, oil or grease; light powdery rust may remain but thicker rust must be removed. Edge slumping shall not exceed ⅛ inch between adjacent lanes and ramps, and shall not exceed ¼ inch between lanes and shoulders or ramps and shoulders. Dowel bars and load‑transfer units shall be positioned so that vertical tilt or horizontal skew does not exceed 0.25 inch at each end, longitudinal and horizontal translations shall be limited to 1 inch, and Type B dowels shall receive bond‑breaker lubricant applied at least one hour before concrete placement. The curing regime shall keep the concrete temperature below 160 °F and shall maintain a minimum compressive strength—per Table D for normal mixes or at least 1,200 psi for accelerated mixes—before traffic is allowed; any pavement that fails these limits shall be removed and replaced. For slab stabilization (Section 679), work may not begin when daytime temperatures are below 40 °F or when the subgrade or base course material is frozen, and deflection testing shall be limited to air temperatures between 40 °F and 70 °F. Joints that exhibit a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more with joint efficiency of 65 % or more must be stabilized. During grout injection upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 inch shall not be allowed, and the injection pressure may not exceed 200 psi unless directed otherwise. Mixed cement grout shall be placed within one hour of mixing and its flowability shall be measured at least twice each shift. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–447), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Treatment procedures

Under Section 529—Full‑Depth Precast/Prestressed Concrete Pavement Slabs, preparation begins with surveying the existing pavement elevations and laying out the longitudinal joints. The full‑depth concrete is then removed by making diamond saw cuts along the limits of the precast slabs and the proposed longitudinal joints and by employing the lift‑out method so that the subbase remains undisturbed. After removal, the subbase is cleaned of unsuitable material and compacted with No. 2A coarse aggregate to within ±½ inch of grade; a laser‑controlled screed subsequently creates a fine‑graded bedding surface that does not vary more than 1⁄8 inch over each slab length. The precast slabs are positioned to the line and grade shown on the working drawings within the tolerances of Section 529.3(f)6–7, then leveled by four symmetrically placed threaded setting bolts, polyurethane injection, or strongback beams while dowel bars, tie bars and bond‑breaker coating are installed in accordance with the shop drawings; joint widths are kept between ¼ inch and ½ inch transversely (≤¾ inch longitudinally) and grout ports receive rapid‑setting dowel slot grout and undersealing bedding grout. The cementitious bedding must reach at least 50 psi before setting bolts are removed (or within 15 minutes after polyurethane injection), and all dowel and bedding grouts must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is permitted; temporary shims may be used only when opening slabs prior to full grout cure. Under Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, the contractor first identifies the deficient area and marks the start point. Removal must extend at least ten feet between transverse joints for reinforced concrete or six feet for plain concrete; the defective portion is cut transversely across the full lane width and longitudinally until the remaining surface is no more than one‑quarter inch short of the required depth. New transverse joints are then installed in accordance with the standard joint methods before fresh concrete is placed using the prescribed equipment and line/grade controls, with reinforcement positioned as required, followed by strike‑off, consolidation, floating, straightedge testing and final texturing. Curing material is applied within thirty minutes after bleed water has dissipated and is retained until the slab reaches the strength needed for traffic opening per Section 501.3(q). [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–447)

For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slab replacement (Section 529), implementation depends on specialized lifting equipment such as cranes, excavators and rigging that must meet the requirements of Section 1105 and be sized for the slab weight; the work follows a defined sequence that begins with planning, pre‑fabrication and pre‑placement meetings, then surveyed full‑depth sawcutting, lift‑out removal of existing pavement, subbase preparation, bedding grading with laser‑controlled screeds, slab leveling using setting bolts, polyurethane or strongback beams, dowel and tie‑bar installation, grout placement and finally traffic opening; ambient average daily temperature is considered in the mix design to control thermal expansion, wet or soft subbase material must be removed before placement, shims are required for ungrouted slabs, any vertical drop‑off greater than 2 inches requires a temporary asphalt wedge, permanent joint differentials must not exceed 1/4 inch, and traffic may resume only after the grout reaches at least 2 500 psi compressive strength. When slab replacement is performed with the slip‑form paving method (Section 501), a fully energized self‑propelled slip‑form paving machine equipped with side forms, automatic alignment and grade controls, and vibration/tamping capability must be used; the sequencing requires coordination of mixing, delivering, placing and spreading so that the paver moves continuously and any stop triggers immediate disengagement of the vibrating and tamping elements, and paving must be suspended after 200 feet if acceptable edge slumping cannot be maintained; environmental controls mandate that concrete temperature not exceed 160°F and curing temperatures below 35°F are considered defective, rain protection is required, and traffic may not be allowed on the new slab until the required compressive strength is reached, joints are sealed or otherwise protected, and all vehicle‑code limits are observed. For slab stabilization projects that include replacement (Section 679), qualified personnel and a crew supervisor with at least five years’ experience must be present, and a field‑QC plan listing all required equipment—including a positive‑displacement cement injection pump, mixing machine, water tanker, core drill with diamond‑tip barrels, vertical movement testing devices, hoses, valves and pressure gauges—must be submitted and approved before work begins; work may not start when daytime temperatures are below 40 °F or when the subgrade or base is frozen, and deflection testing cannot be performed if air temperature is below 40 °F, exceeds 70 °F, during spring thaw, or on frozen subgrade; after grout injection the slab must remain closed to traffic for at least 12 hours—or as specified in the approved field‑QC plan and manufacturer recommendations—before reopening. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–447), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Quality and workmanship

Materials inspection includes confirming that concrete mix designs submitted at least 28 days before production contain trial‑batch data proving they meet the minimum required compressive and flexural strengths, and that load‑transfer dowels, tie bars, slots and grout ports are cast within ±1/8 inch of the locations shown on the approved shop drawings. During slab replacement the contractor must verify that reinforcement is free of rust, dirt, oil, grease or other substances that could impair bond. Preparation inspection requires checking slab dimensions against the tolerances in Table A, verifying a smooth bottom surface texture, the specified top‑surface finish, that lifting devices are recessed at least 1 inch and filled with an accepted cementitious grout, and confirming subbase grade is within ±½ inch of design and bedding material flatness does not vary more than 1/8 inch over a slab length using a straight‑edge and depth gauge. The edge slumping during slip‑form paving must not exceed 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes and ramps nor 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders, and after hardening a 12‑foot straightedge shall show no more than 3/16 inch variation parallel to the centreline. Installation inspection covers joint geometry—transverse joints no wider than ½ inch and at least ¼ inch, longitudinal joints not exceeding ¾ inch, vertical differential across any joint not exceeding ¼ inch, and that dowel‑slot and bedding grouts reach a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is allowed. Tie bars selected from the first day’s placement must be pull‑out tested once the concrete reaches 3,500 psi compressive strength using a 12,000 lb load or until slippage occurs, and macrotexture must meet a minimum mean texture depth of 0.040 inches as measured by ASTM E965. Final work inspection also looks for spalls or cracks and mandates repair according to the specified criteria, while opening to traffic requires the pavement to achieve at least 1,200 psi compressive strength for accelerated mixes and thickness checks must show no more than ½ inch deficiency before acceptance. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–447)

For SECTION 529—FULL DEPTH PRECAST/PRESTRESSED CONCRETE PAVEMENT SLABS, a slab replacement is accepted only when the fabricated slabs match the authorized shop‑drawings and fall within the tolerances listed in Table A; critical geometry such as load‑transfer dowels, tie bars and their slots must be placed within ±1/8 inch of the drawing locations, the sacrificial concrete layer must add ¼ inch to the nominal thickness, the compacted subbase surface must be graded to a plane that does not vary more than plus or minus ½ inch in grade and contour, and the bedding surface under each slab may not deviate more than 1/8 inch over the length of the slab. Joint tolerances require transverse joint widths between ¼ inch and ½ inch (minimum ¼ inch, maximum ½ inch) and longitudinal joints not to exceed ¾ inch, with vertical differentials across any joint limited to ¼ inch (allowable up to ½ inch only if diamond‑grinded back to the ¼‑inch limit). Dowel, tie‑bar and bedding grout must reach a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is permitted, and the concrete itself must achieve at least 4,925 psi compressive strength and 650 psi flexural strength. For SECTION 501—PLAIN OR REINFORCED CEMENT CONCRETE PAVEMENTS using the slip‑form paving method, a satisfactory slab replacement will be accepted when edge‑slump does not exceed 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes (and ramps) or 1/4 inch between lane and shoulder (or ramp and shoulder), when the surface after hardening does not vary more than 3/16 inch with a 12‑foot straightedge parallel to the centerline or perpendicular within a traffic lane, when longitudinal joints are no greater than 1/4 inch variation, when slab depth is no more than 1/4 inch deficient (defective if over 1/2 inch), and when the concrete attains at least the minimum required compressive strength of 1,200 psi for accelerated mixes or the strength required in Table D for normal mixes; all curing, joint‑placement and reinforcement cleanliness requirements must also be satisfied, and the removed section must be at least 10 feet between transverse joints for reinforced concrete (or 6 feet for plain concrete). [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–447)

Performance and service life

In full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, spalling and cracking are identified as the most common distresses during slab replacement; spalls are repaired according to Section 501.3(t) while cracks are evaluated by width and depth criteria such that cracks wider than 0.007 inches and greater than D/3 must be replaced, cracks wider than 0.007 inches but shallower than D/3 are repaired in accordance with ACI 224R, and cracks of 0.007 inches or less are sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate. Additional joint‑related failures include vertical edge differentials that exceed 1/4 inch across any joint, transverse joint widths that fall outside the range of a minimum of 1/4 inch to a maximum of 1/2 inch, and longitudinal joints exceeding 3/4 inch, each of which may require grinding, reseating or slab removal. These distresses are typically triggered by manufacturing imperfections, handling or construction damage, inadequate subbase preparation, insufficient bedding‑or‑grout strength, and failure to meet joint‑width or vertical‑differential tolerances during installation.

In plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements installed by the slip‑form paving method, the most frequent treatment failures observed during slab replacement comprise edge slumping beyond allowable limits, cracks or spalls that appear before final inspection, surface defects caused by freezing‑thawing cycles, rain exposure, improper finishing, honeycombing, insufficient curing, inadequate slab depth, low air content, and compressive strength below required values; each of these triggers removal and replacement at no extra cost. Edge slumping is judged unacceptable when it exceeds 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders, and any pavement that shows such excess must be corrected in accordance with Section 501.3(g)5.b. Defects in joint construction, curing, surface tolerance, depth, air content, or strength are considered defective work under Section 501.3(t), requiring removal of at least 10 feet of reinforced concrete (or 6 feet of plain concrete) and reconstruction of the transverse joints to the standard details. Monitoring and follow-up

After a slab‑replacement job the finished pavement must be inspected for vertical alignment and joint geometry before traffic is allowed. The inspector checks that the finished vertical differential across every joint does not exceed 1/4 inch, that transverse joints are between 1/4 inch and 1/2 inch wide and longitudinal joints do not exceed 3/4 inch, and that all dowel‑slot and bedding grouts have attained at least 2,500 psi compressive strength. Any spalls are repaired in accordance with Section 501.3(t) and any cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third the slab depth require full slab replacement. Ongoing monitoring continues for recurring damage, with damaged slabs inspected case‑by‑case and remedial action taken as specified. [2]

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, when a deficiency develops that negatively impacts safe, efficient operations or threatens future integrity of the pavement—such as joint failures, edge spalls, corner breaks, or other localized deterioration—the slab‑replacement area is deemed to require further action and corrective maintenance, which may include full‑width and depth slab replacement at isolated locations. In Section 529—Full Depth Precast/Prestressed Concrete Pavement Slabs of the Pennsylvania DOT Specifications, post‑replacement inspections that reveal cracks wider than 0.007 inches and whose depth exceeds one‑third of the slab thickness (D/3) must lead to replacement of the slab. The same specification states that shallower cracks that are still wider than 0.007 inches require repair in accordance with ACI 224R, while very fine cracks ≤0.007 inches are sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate, and any observed spalls also require repair. If exposed dowel or tie bars do not meet specifications when an adjacent slab is ready for placement, the slab must be removed and replaced. Joint vertical differentials greater than 1/4 inch trigger grinding of the joint, whereas differentials exceeding 1/2 inch require removal of the slab and regrading of the bedding material. Under Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, edge slumping greater than 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes (or greater than 1/4 inch between a lane and a shoulder) is identified as a defect. A surface deviation that exceeds 3/16 inch when measured with a straightedge also signals a defect. Longitudinal cracks wider than 1/8 inch, core depths that are more than 1/4 inch shallow (and especially more than 1/2 inch shallow), and concrete that has not attained the required compressive strength of at least 1,200 psi for accelerated‑strength slabs each constitute measurable defects requiring further maintenance or replacement. Section 501.3(t) further requires removal and replacement of any slab found defective in construction, curing, surface tolerance, depth, air content, compressive strength, or showing surface damage from freezing, rain, improper finish, or honeycombing. In Section 679—Slab Stabilization, the appearance after treatment of radial cracks spreading outward from grout injection holes or transverse cracks between adjacent holes mandates that work be stopped and the entire slab be replaced. Upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch during pumping indicates that additional repair is needed. Joints or cracks that still deflect 0.02 inch or more, particularly when joint efficiency falls below 65 percent, must be re‑grouted, retested, or ultimately removed and replaced with a full‑depth concrete patch.
