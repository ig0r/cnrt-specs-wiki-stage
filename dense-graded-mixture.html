Dense-Graded Mixture

Part of: Gradation is-subtopic-of
Dense-Graded Mixture is a topic under Gradation.

Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

A dense‑graded mixture is an asphalt concrete blend whose aggregate skeleton incorporates a wide range of particle sizes, producing a very dense structure with limited void space between particles[1]. It is plant‑mixed according to a volumetric asphalt mixture design developed with the Superpave Gyratory Compactor and may include prescribed additives or modifiers; when placed it forms a continuous, well‑compacted layer that provides the structural surface required for flexible pavements, serving as a wearing or binder course in the upper layers[2]. The mix’s performance is chiefly controlled by the polishing resistance of its coarse aggregate— the principal property governing surface friction—and, when a crumb‑rubber modified binder with fine particles and lower viscosity is used, it can flow into the limited voids to deliver durability comparable to polymer‑modified binders while meeting the AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) requirements[1].

Material purpose and applicability

The dense‑graded mixture is an asphalt concrete blend whose aggregate skeleton incorporates a wide range of particle sizes, producing a very dense structure with limited void space between particles. [1] It may be produced as a plant‑mixed blend that is placed on a prepared surface according to a volumetric asphalt mixture design developed with the Superpave Gyratory Compactor and can incorporate prescribed manufactured additives, modifiers, or plant process modifications. [2] By forming a continuous, well‑compacted layer, the dense‑graded mix provides the structural surface required for flexible pavements. [2] In the upper layers of a pavement it serves as a wearing or binder course, delivering a compact, stiff layer that is durable and capable of carrying traffic loads. [1][2] For projects that would normally employ polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binders in dense‑graded wearing or binder courses, crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder dense‑graded mixtures are normally selected so that their superior performance can be utilized where it matters most. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 130–131); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 258)

Composition and characteristics

A dense‑graded asphalt mixture is characterized by aggregates that span a wide range of particle sizes, producing a very dense skeleton with limited void space between individual particles[1]. In the context of crumb‑rubber‑modified asphalt binder (CRMAB) dense‑graded courses, the binder incorporates reclaimed ground tire rubber at a lower crumb‑rubber content and with a finer gradation of #30 mesh or smaller, which results in smaller particle size and lower viscosity that enables it to fill the limited voids of the dense aggregate structure[1]. The CRMAB used in such mixtures must meet the physical requirements of AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) as modified in Bulletin 25 or AASHTO M 332[1]. More generally, a dense‑graded mixture described in Section 413.1 is a plant‑mixed asphalt pavement course placed on a prepared surface and defined by a volumetric asphalt mixture design developed with the Superpave Gyratory Compactor (SGC)[2]. This mix combines the conventional asphalt binder and aggregates together with any prescribed manufactured additives, modifiers, or plant process modifications required for the project[2]. The dense‑graded character of the mixture results from this designed composition and the compaction approach, which together create a durable, tightly packed pavement structure[2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (p. 130); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 258)

Variations in the composition or source of the coarse aggregate change its polishing resistance, which is identified as the most significant material property governing surface friction; consequently, different petrographic characteristics can raise or lower the friction that a dense‑graded wearing course provides. Material properties, mix design, and construction techniques are also criteria for developing a surface with good friction values, and each approved aggregate source receives a Skid Resistance Level (SRL) based on performance in properly designed and produced dense‑graded asphalt surfaces, so selecting a different aggregate or blend directly alters the SRL designation and the expected seasonal friction cycle. When the crumb‑rubber content is reduced and the rubber particles are graded finer (#30 mesh or smaller), the resulting CRMAB binder has a smaller particle size and lower viscosity, which lets it be placed into the limited void space of a dense‑graded aggregate skeleton and still achieve performance comparable to polymer‑modified binders; these characteristics stem from producing the binder as a terminal blend—blended at a refinery or storage terminal rather than on site—and must meet the physical requirements of the AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) specification. CRMAB binders are intended for dense‑graded wearing or binder courses that would normally use a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, and their performance depends on rubber content, the possible inclusion of RAP/RAS, and the mandatory wet‑process production method which modifies the binder; using a dry process would only replace part of the aggregate. Because CRMAB mixes are a premium product, they should be limited to the upper layers where their superior performance is best utilized, and layer thickness should not exceed 2 ½ inches. The mixture is recommended for high‑traffic Interstates or Interstate look‑alikes with more than 25,000 ADT, more than 10 million ESALs over a 20‑year design life, and projects larger than 50,000 square yards; under those conditions the added rubber content can be cost‑effective. If warm‑mix asphalt is used, only a chemical warm mix additive may be utilized and foaming is not allowed. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 110–131)

Key material properties

In dense‑graded asphalt wearing courses the polishing resistance of the coarse aggregate is identified as the principal material property that controls the surface friction which must be retained under traffic and environmental conditions. The petrographic characteristics of a particular aggregate source are also related to its friction value, and observed friction values follow an annual cycle that approximates a sine curve with lower values in late summer and fall; the amplitude of this seasonal dip depends on the coarse‑aggregate characteristics and traffic volume. A dense‑graded mixture achieves its performance primarily through a physical makeup in which aggregates span a wide range of particle sizes, producing a very dense skeleton with limited void space between particles. When a crumb‑rubber modified binder is incorporated, the smaller particle size and lower viscosity are crucial because they allow the binder to flow into the tightly packed aggregate without generating excess air voids, and the binder must satisfy the physical requirements of the AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) specification as referenced in Bulletin 25 or AASHTO M 332. The stiffness of the dense‑graded mixture is another important attribute; high stiffness can render the mix unsuitable for projects that require extensive handwork or raking, and therefore it should not be used where significant handwork or raking is required. Production of the mixture must employ the wet process only, which modifies the binder chemistry through the rubber; if a warm‑mix asphalt approach is adopted, a chemical warm‑mix additive should be utilized and foaming is not allowed. Finally, the layer thickness of a dense‑graded CRMAB pavement should not exceed 2 ½ inches to maintain the intended behavior of the premium mix. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 110–131)

In section 5.8 of the Pavement Policy Manual, the principal material property used to assess surface friction of a dense‑graded mixture is the polishing resistance of its coarse aggregate, which is measured with the AASHTO T 242 test method; PennDOT then assigns each approved coarse‑aggregate source a Skid Resistance Level (SRL) based on that aggregate’s performance in properly designed and produced dense‑graded asphalt surfaces, and the SRL designation serves as the classification used when specifying the wearing course and is recorded on design approvals, typical sections, and contract proposals. [1] In section 5.17 of the same manual, a dense‑graded mixture is characterized by aggregates that span a wide range of particle sizes, producing a very dense matrix with limited void space between individual particles, and its properties are classified by requiring the crumb‑rubber modified binder to meet the physical requirements of the AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) specification as modified in Bulletin 25 or AASHTO M 332. [1] In SECTION 413 of the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, the properties of a plant‑mixed dense‑graded asphalt pavement are characterized through a volumetric mixture design generated with the Superpave Gyratory Compactor (SGC), and field samples are examined using LTS testing; acceptance is then determined by statistically evaluating those test results with Percent Within Limits (PWL) procedures. [2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 110–130); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 258)

Behavior and mechanisms

In dense‑graded asphalt wearing courses the performance is primarily controlled by physical and mechanical mechanisms rather than chemical ones. The essential physical mechanism is the surface texture, expressed as a low‑speed gradient, which must be sufficient to prevent water pressure from building at the tire/pavement interface; the polishing resistance of the coarse aggregate governs how that texture degrades under traffic. Mechanically, traffic loading causes the aggregates to polish, producing an annual friction cycle that roughly approximates a sine curve with lower values in late summer and fall, the amplitude of which depends on the aggregate characteristics and traffic volume. Mix design, material properties and construction techniques together determine whether these mechanisms maintain adequate friction throughout the pavement’s life. For dense‑graded mixtures the aggregate gradation is also a primary physical control: a wide range of particle sizes creates a very dense skeleton with limited void space, physically restricting movement of the mix. When crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder (CRMAB) is incorporated, the finer crumb rubber—smaller than #30 mesh—and the lower viscosity that results from a reduced crumb‑rubber content enable the binder to flow into and coat the tightly packed aggregate, providing the chemical‑mechanical interaction needed for durability. The CRMAB must meet the physical requirements of AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) as modified in Bulletin 25 or AASHTO M 332, giving performance comparable to polymer‑modified binders. In mixtures that use crumb rubber, chemical modification of the binder governs behavior when the wet process is employed because the rubber interacts with and alters the binder properties; physically, the same rubber can act as a partial aggregate replacement in the dry process, changing the particle structure; mechanically, the resulting stiffness of the rubber‑modified mixture limits handwork or raking during construction, and such mixtures should not be used for projects where significant handwork or raking is required. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 110–131)

Durability and deterioration

Durability concerns for dense‑graded mixtures centre on the loss of surface friction as the coarse aggregate becomes polished, which reduces the tire‑pavement grip; this polishing effect is most pronounced in late summer and fall and varies with aggregate characteristics and traffic volume. Inadequate surface texture can also allow water pressure to build up at the tire/pavement interface, further compromising safety. Consequently, the mix must retain its polishing resistance and sufficient texture under traffic and environmental conditions throughout the life of the surface. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (p. 110)

Within dense‑graded asphalt wearing courses, susceptibility to performance loss is chiefly governed by the polishing resistance of the coarse aggregate; aggregates that resist polishing help retain friction and therefore reduce deterioration, whereas aggregates with low polishing resistance allow faster loss of surface texture. The source also notes that the amplitude of seasonal friction decline—typically lowest in late summer and fall—is influenced by coarse‑aggregate characteristics and traffic volume, so higher traffic or aggregates with poorer petrographic properties increase the tendency for friction to deteriorate. Selecting an aggregate with a suitable Skid Resistance Level (SRL) designation, which reflects its inherent friction performance, further mitigates susceptibility, while using aggregates of lower SRL can exacerbate deterioration. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (p. 110)

Material interactions and compatibility

In a dense‑graded mixture the crumb‑rubber modified binder can be blended because its finer particles and reduced viscosity enable it to flow into the limited voids between the well‑graded aggregates, ensuring adequate coating and reducing the risk of stripping. This compatibility allows the CRMAB to deliver performance comparable to that of polymer‑modified binders while still satisfying the physical requirements set out in AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) as referenced in Bulletin 25 or AASHTO M 332. The CRMAB dense‑graded mixture relies on a wet‑process in which the crumb rubber chemically modifies the binder, so the binder properties are altered by the rubber rather than the rubber acting merely as aggregate replacement. The mix may also contain reclaimed asphalt pavement or reclaimed asphalt shingle material when specified in the SSP, allowing RAP/RAS to be blended with virgin binder within the dense‑graded layer. Because CRMAB binders are intended for projects that would normally use a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, they should be considered for upper wearing or binder courses where their enhanced performance is most beneficial. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 130–131)

Selection, specification, and improvement

Selection of a dense‑graded asphalt mixture is governed primarily by the polishing resistance of its coarse aggregate because that property most strongly influences surface friction. Designers must also consider the Skid Resistance Level assigned to the aggregate and select the appropriate SRL according to the anticipated average daily traffic for new or resurfaced facilities as shown in Table 5.4. Material properties, mix design, and construction techniques are cited as additional criteria that affect the ability of the wearing course to retain adequate friction throughout its service life. When a crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder is used, the binder must satisfy the physical criteria set out in AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) as amended in Bulletin 25 or AASHTO M 332. Consequently the CRMAB is formulated with a lower crumb‑rubber content and a finer gradation of #30 mesh or smaller, producing a binder of small particle size and reduced viscosity that can be placed into the limited void space of a dense‑graded aggregate skeleton. For projects that would normally employ dense‑graded wearing or binder courses with polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, CRMAB mixtures may be considered for new construction or overlays on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements provided the mixture’s stiffness does not require significant handwork or raking. Layer thickness must not exceed 2 ½ inches, RAP or RAS may be included as specified in the SSP, production must use the wet process, and any warm‑mix additive must be a chemical additive; foaming is not allowed. Use of CRMAB on Interstate‑type facilities is recommended only when average daily traffic exceeds 25,000 ADT, cumulative ESALs exceed 10 million over a 20‑year design life, and the project area is larger than 50,000 square yards, with structural layer coefficients from Table 10.6 applied for pavement design. The dense‑graded mixture is specified through a volumetric asphalt mixture design that is developed with the Superpave Gyratory Compactor. The design may incorporate prescribed manufactured additives or modifiers, or plant process modifications as required by the project specifications. Acceptance of the mix for a given project is determined by testing field samples with the LTS and then applying the Percent Within Limits statistical evaluation to those test results. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 110–131); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 258)

To improve friction in a dense‑graded wearing course the coarse aggregate shall be chosen with an SRL that matches the expected traffic and the design documents must list the selected SRL; the contractor may supply either an aggregate meeting that SRL, a higher‑rated one, or an equivalent blend, while using an excessively high SRL on non‑wearing layers shall be prohibited. A crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder (CRMAB) incorporates reclaimed ground tire rubber and is produced with a lower crumb rubber content and a finer gradation of #30 mesh or smaller, which gives the binder a smaller particle size and lower viscosity that enable it to occupy the limited void space in the densely packed aggregate skeleton. CRMAB must meet the physical requirements of AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) as modified in Bulletin 25 or AASHTO M 332. CRMAB binders should be considered for projects that would normally use dense‑graded wearing or binder courses with polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, and if warm‑mix asphalt is employed a chemical warm‑mix additive must be used because foaming is not allowed; the mixture may contain RAP or RAS material as specified in the SSP. CRMAB should be limited to projects that meet the high‑traffic thresholds of > 25,000 ADT, > 10 million ESALs (20‑year design life), and > 50,000 square yards, with layer thickness not exceeding 2 ½ inches; because CRMAB is a premium, stiffer mix it should be avoided on shoulders unless the lane and shoulder are paved in one pass and should not be used where extensive handwork or temporary lanes are required. Performance of a dense‑graded mixture can be enhanced by incorporating the prescribed manufactured additives or modifiers called for in the volumetric design, and by applying plant process modifications as outlined in the Superpave Gyratory Compactor (SGC) procedure; these measures are intended to improve durability and density. Acceptance of such improvements is verified through field sample testing with the LTS and a statistical review using Percent Within Limits (PWL) procedures, providing a quality‑control framework that confirms the mixture meets design criteria. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 110–131); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 258)

Performance evaluation and implications

Under Section 5.8 of the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), evaluation of a dense‑graded mixture includes laboratory friction testing performed according to AASHTO T 242, field observations of surface friction throughout the pavement’s service life that capture the seasonal sine‑shaped cycle indicative of aggregate polishing resistance, and long‑term performance assessment through assignment of a Skid Resistance Level (SRL) rating based on how aggregates perform in properly designed and produced dense‑graded asphalt wearing courses. [1] In Section 413 of the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification 408/2026, the dense‑graded asphalt course is first characterized in the laboratory by developing a volumetric mixture design using the Superpave Gyratory Compactor (SGC); acceptance then depends on testing field samples with the LTS and on statistical evaluation of those sample test results through Percent Within Limits (PWL) procedures. [2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (p. 110); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 258)

The dense‑graded asphalt mixture is defined by aggregates that span a wide range of particle sizes so that the resulting mixture is very dense with limited space between individual particles, which makes the pavement surface inherently durable and resistant to deformation. Because the most significant material property affecting surface friction is the polishing resistance of the coarse aggregate, the ability of a dense‑graded wearing course to maintain adequate friction over its service life depends on selecting an aggregate whose polishing resistance meets the anticipated traffic volume. The SRL designation for an aggregate is based on performance in properly designed and produced dense‑graded asphalt surfaces, and whenever an asphalt wearing course will be used the SRL designation shall be indicated on pavement design approval forms, on typical sections, and in the contract proposal. The design of inappropriately high SRL designations will be prohibited on non‑wearing courses, leveling courses, and shoulders. The CRMAB binder’s smaller particle size and lower viscosity allow its incorporation into dense‑graded aggregate that has limited void space, enabling the mix to meet AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) or AASHTO M 332 physical requirements while providing performance comparable to polymer‑modified binders. Consequently, designers must specify a CRMAB that satisfies those specifications. Because the CRMAB dense‑graded mixture’s high stiffness makes it unsuitable for projects where significant handwork or raking is required, it should be limited to mechanized placement in the upper wearing and binder courses. The mix should be considered for projects that would normally use dense‑graded wearing or binder courses with polymer modified PG 64E‑22 binder, but only when cost‑effectiveness can be demonstrated; CRMAB is a premium mixture and is typically more expensive than a conventional Superpave mixture, therefore it should only be used when it will be cost-effective. For use on Interstates and Interstate look‑alikes the thresholds are greater than 25,000 ADT, greater than 10 million ESALs over a 20‑year design life, and an area larger than 50,000 square yards. Design calculations may employ the Superpave structural layer coefficients, but layer thickness must not exceed 2 ½ inches and the mix must be produced by the wet process; it is not recommended for shoulders unless lane and shoulder are paved in one pass, nor for temporary lanes or crossovers. [1]
