Dowel Bar

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Dowel Bar is a topic under Rigid Pavement.
dowel-barsdowelsdowelleddowelled-jointscoated-dowel-bars
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Dowel bars are short steel load‑transfer devices installed in the wheel paths of rigid (Portland‑cement) concrete pavements to bridge transverse joints, active cracks, mid‑panel cracks, and deteriorated joints that would otherwise prevent adjacent slabs from sharing wheel loads [1][2]. By being smooth epoxy‑coated steel bars—typically 1.25 in or 1.5 in in diameter and 18 in long—and fully bonded along their embedded length with rapid‑set grout, polyester, epoxy or vinylester binders (with a bond‑breaker applied to at least half the bar), they create a continuous load path that limits joint faulting, keeps vertical differentials under ¼ in, and preserves ride quality [1][2]. The system’s performance is verified by limiting differential deflection to 7.5 mil after one million traffic cycles (with no more than a 2.5‑mil increase after ten million) and by requiring the surrounding grout to reach at least 2,500 psi compressive strength before traffic, thereby extending pavement service life beyond twenty years [1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Dowel bars are introduced in concrete pavements to address the problem of inadequate load transfer across transverse discontinuities such as active cracks, mid‑panel cracks, and deteriorated joints, which otherwise prevent adjacent slabs from sharing wheel loads [1][2]. The resulting lack of shared support leads to differential slab movement, faulting, loss of alignment, moisture intrusion at the joint, and premature deterioration of structural integrity, especially on heavily channeled roadways [1][2]. By providing a short steel load‑transfer unit that penetrates the joint and is securely anchored with grout or binders, dowel bars create a continuous load path so that adjacent slabs behave as a single panel [1][2]. The design requires the dowels to be positioned according to shop drawings, coated with a bond‑breaker lubricant before concrete placement, held in baskets fastened to the substrate, and grouted with rapid‑set material or epoxy, polyester, or vinylester binders to prevent movement greater than ¼ inch during construction [2][1]. Performance criteria specify that vertical differential across any joint should not exceed ¼ inch and that dynamic deflection measured by LVDT should remain below about 7.5 mil after one million traffic cycles, with no more than a 2.5‑mil increase even after ten million cycles [2]. When installed in this manner the dowel bar system limits joint faulting, maintains uniform support beneath slabs, preserves ride quality, and extends service life, particularly for pavements expected to remain in service for more than twenty years or that are subjected to slab jacking to restore settled supports [1].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–472), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

The primary functional objective is to ensure reliable load transfer across transverse joints or cracks so that the repaired slab remains aligned and structural continuity is restored under heavy wheel loads [1][2]. To achieve this, each dowel must be placed with its centre at the mid‑depth of the thinner slab and the joint must be cut directly over that centre, while the exposed portion of a coated bar is rendered bond‑free by applying a bond‑breaker lubricant to at least half its length (or by using an uncoated Type A bar) [2]. Structurally the dowel is required to be a smooth steel bar that is installed in slots cut parallel to traffic and anchored with approved binders such as epoxy, polyester or vinylester, providing a continuous bond along its entire embedded length [1]. The guides limit the bar diameter to either 1.5 in or 1.25 in with tolerances of +0.25 in/‑0.001 in and prescribe a nominal length of 18 in ±½ in, insisting that the free end be a true circle without burrs [2]. Placement tolerances demand that vertical tilt or horizontal skew at either end not exceed 0.25 in, longitudinal and horizontal translation relative to the intended joint location be limited to 1 in, and that finished vertical differentials across any joint be no greater than ¼ in [2]. The bar must be anchored in drilled holes using an anchoring material that sets within five minutes, with voids around the perimeter of each dowel limited to less than 0.5 in as verified by core inspection, and baskets or anchors must be fastened so that movement during concrete placement does not exceed ¼ in vertically or horizontally [2]. For durability the bar shall receive a minimum 8‑mil non‑abraded fusion‑bonded epoxy coating (or an equivalent corrosion‑resistant treatment), and any bar whose coating is damaged must be removed and replaced before concrete placement [2]. When service life exceeds twenty years, high‑performance dowels—preferably epoxy‑coated—are recommended to provide long‑term joint integrity and resistance to corrosion [1]. The surrounding grout or mortar must achieve at least 2 500 psi compressive strength before traffic is permitted, ensuring a robust load path and protecting the bar from moisture ingress [2]. Durability performance is further quantified by limiting differential deflection measured with LVDTs to no more than 7.5 mils after one million load cycles and allowing an increase of less than 2.5 mils after ten million cycles, in accordance with PTM No. 642 criteria [2]. Safety is achieved by preventing any movement of the dowel that could cause slab faulting or migration, thereby maintaining uniform support under traffic loads [1][2]. A quality‑control plan must include inspection of each bar after placement, replacement of damaged units, and storage of dowels for no more than thirty days without protective covering unless otherwise approved [2][1]. Serviceability is maintained through near‑complete load transfer that preserves existing aggregate interlock where hairline cracking exists, resulting in a uniform ride quality over the pavement’s design life [1]. Additional serviceability requirements include using temporary joint separators no thicker than 1/8 in and ensuring that the dowel system does not introduce excessive vertical differentials, thereby supporting smooth traffic operation [2]. Collectively these functional, structural, durability, safety and serviceability objectives ensure continuous load transfer, corrosion resistance, safe traffic operation and long‑term performance of concrete pavements equipped with dowel bars [1][2].

Scope and applicability

Dowel bars are appropriate for rigid (Portland‑cement) concrete pavements, including plain, reinforced and “Long Life” types, and they are not used on asphalt surfaces[1][2]. They may be employed in new construction where a load‑transfer joint is required—such as transverse joints, expansion‑joint replacements, patch joints, corner repairs, slab repairs with thickness changes (e.g., mainline‑to‑ramp transitions), and in multilane, widened, adjacent‑lane, shoulder, structure or slip‑form installations, whether the slabs are precast, prestressed or cast in place—as well as in rehabilitation of existing slabs that exhibit working transverse cracks, mid‑panel cracks, hairline cracks, or other joints where load transfer has become deficient but aggregate interlock remains viable[2][1]. The method is also used in full‑depth patching, slab stabilization, joint sealing, and correction of settled slabs, often together with slab jacking when settlement has reduced support[1]. Project conditions that favor dowel‑bar use include heavy, channelled traffic loads, a stable subgrade with functional drainage, and an anticipated service life for the repaired or new section of at least twenty years before any programmed replacement[1]. The technique is not applied to asphalt pavements, nor to concrete overlays or diamond‑ground surfaces[1][2]. The dowel material must be steel satisfying AASHTO M 254 bond‑breaker criteria and may be supplied as fusion‑bonded epoxy Type B with a minimum non‑abraded coating thickness of eight mil, or as mechanically galvanized, stainless‑steel, or other corrosion‑resistant uncoated forms[2]. High‑performance dowels are required to have a diameter between 1.25 and 1.5 inches (tolerance +0.25/–0.001 inch) and a length of eighteen inches, and must pass PTM No. 642 testing with differential deflection not exceeding 7.5 mil at one million cycles and no more than an additional 2.5 mil increase by ten million cycles[2]. All dowel bars must be sourced from a manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15 and must meet the applicable pavement specifications[2]. Installation involves placing smooth epoxy‑coated dowels in the wheel paths across cracks or joints and fully bonding them along their embedded length with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders; the void is then backfilled with concrete patching material[1]. When Type B epoxy‑coated bars are used, a bond‑breaker lubricant must coat at least half the length and be applied at least one hour before concrete placement[2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–472), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

Dowel bars are generally the preferred load‑transfer device, but several project conditions lead designers to select alternative methods [1]. When traffic must be reopened almost immediately after construction, accelerated (rapid‑setting) concrete patching is favored because it achieves strength quickly enough to support early loading [1]. If the required opening time is even more restrictive or if work‑zone safety mandates minimizing crew exposure, precast concrete paving systems are recommended as a substitute for dowel retrofits [1]. High‑early‑strength concrete mixes are discouraged in these situations because their tendency to develop excessive shrinkage cracking can compromise joint performance and negate the benefit of dowels [1]. An unstable supporting subgrade or a drainage system that is not functioning properly also diminishes the effectiveness of dowel bars, prompting the use of slab stabilization, jack‑up techniques, or full‑depth joint patching to restore support before any load‑transfer devices are installed [1]. When existing cracks have widened beyond hairline dimensions and the aggregate interlock has been lost, dowel bars no longer provide adequate transfer, so full‑depth concrete pavement replacement or other repair strategies become more appropriate [1]. For patches classified as Type C joints, specifications explicitly require load‑transfer units other than coated dowels; tie bars are installed and a bond‑breaker is omitted, making the tie‑bar approach the prescribed alternative [2]. Additionally, full‑depth saw cuts in Type C repairs must be limited so they do not extend into adjacent pavement where existing dowel bars could be damaged, further supporting the use of non‑dowel detailing methods [2].

Design inputs and requirements

The design and installation of dowel bars in concrete pavements are governed by a combination of state roadway construction standards and nationally recognized rigid‑pavement specifications that prescribe layout drawings, material requirements, dimensional tolerances, bonding procedures, and performance testing criteria. The Pennsylvania Roadway Construction Standards (Publication 72M) provide detailed standard drawings for retrofit installations (RC‑26M), jointed reinforced concrete pavements (RC‑21M), and jointed plain concrete pavements (RC‑27M), and they require perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars, joint spacing of 30 ft for JRCP and 15 ft for JPCP, and appropriate sealing. The accompanying Specifications in Publication 408 define the technical requirements, mandating epoxy‑coated smooth steel dowels placed in wheel paths across working transverse cracks or joints and requiring continuous bonding along the entire embedded length with an approved anchoring material such as epoxy, polyester, or vinylester binder. Across the guides, Section 705.3 of the rigid‑pavement specifications establishes the overall load‑transfer system, specifying allowable bar size, spacing, grout strength, joint geometry, and the requirement that all dowel bars be installed in accordance with approved shop drawings. Placement tolerances are limited to plus or minus one‑eighth inch from the locations shown on the drawings, with each bar positioned square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface, while vertical or horizontal tilt at either end may not exceed 0.25 inch and longitudinal or horizontal translation is limited to one inch. The portion of each dowel embedded in the slab must receive a bond‑breaker coating, and for Type B coated dowels the bond‑breaker lubricant must be applied to at least half of the bar length unless it has been pre‑applied in the shop. Drilled holes may not exceed one‑eighth inch larger than the bar diameter, and the anchoring grout must achieve an initial set within five minutes; acceptance is verified by core testing that limits total void around any dowel to 0.50 inch. Coating requirements call for a fusion‑bonded epoxy Type B coating with a minimum non‑abraded thickness of eight mils, no more than two holidays per linear foot, and mechanically galvanized bars must meet ASTM B695/B696 with a minimum thickness of 3.4 mil; stainless‑steel or other corrosion‑resistant bars must comply with the relevant ASTM and AASHTO specifications. Structural adequacy is demonstrated through PTM No. 642 testing, which limits LVDT differential deflection to no more than 7.5 mil at one million load cycles and an increase of not more than 2.5 mil after ten million cycles, and high‑performance dowels must satisfy the same limits while conforming to dimensional tolerances of 1.25 inch or 1.50 inch diameter and eighteen inches length. Installation procedures require a power‑driven self‑propelled saw to make two parallel cuts per slot, a jackhammer not exceeding thirty pounds (or fifteen pounds if pavement damage occurs), cleaning of slots within twenty‑four hours and final cleaning no later than thirty minutes before concrete placement using at least one hundred psi oil‑free compressed air, and the use of non‑metallic chairs providing a minimum half‑inch clearance together with preformed cellular polystyrene joint material and expansion caps of one‑and‑a‑half inches with a quarter‑inch clearance. Project constraints limit dowel‑bar retrofits to locations subjected to heavily channeled loads that produce joint faulting or to hairline cracks where aggregate interlock still exists, and when retrofits are combined with slab‑jacking the procedures of Publication 408 Section 681 apply. High‑performance dowels are recommended for repaired segments expected to remain in service for more than twenty years, and only one dowel type may be used on a Long Life Concrete project unless an exception is granted; on‑site storage is limited to thirty days without special approval, and any bar showing corrosion, coating damage, or visible defects must be replaced prior to concreting. [1][2]

Design methods and criteria

The design and installation of dowel‑bar load‑transfer systems in concrete pavements are governed primarily by procedural guidelines, prescribed testing protocols, and material standards rather than explicit analytical equations [1][2]. Load transfer is achieved by placing a dowel bar at every patch joint or transverse crack and anchoring it with an epoxy, polyester, or vinyl‑ester binder that secures the full embedded length of the bar [1][2]. The selection of coated dowels depends on joint spacing: jointed reinforced concrete pavement (RC‑21M) uses 30 ft joint intervals while jointed plain concrete pavement (RC‑27M) employs 15‑ft intervals, both requiring perpendicular joints and appropriate seals; high‑performance dowels are recommended when the repaired section is expected to remain in service for more than twenty years [1]. All dowel bars must conform to AASHTO M 254 together with ASTM A513 and AASHTO T 253, possessing a fusion‑bonded epoxy coating of at least eight mils non‑abraded thickness and being sized either 1.5 in or 1.25 in in diameter (tolerance +0.25 in/–0.001 in) with an overall length of 18 in ±0.5 in as shown on standard drawing RC‑20M; any alternate shape must be demonstrated equivalent [2]. Prior to concrete placement, the locations for dowel bars and load‑transfer units are marked on the pavement and secured to the substrate in accordance with the standard drawings so that movement during paving does not exceed a quarter‑inch vertically or horizontally, as required by the quality‑control plan [2]. The joint preparation begins with a power‑driven self‑propelled saw that makes two parallel cuts at mid‑depth, providing three slots per wheel path centered over the joint; concrete between the cuts is removed with a jackhammer not exceeding thirty pounds in weight (or fifteen pounds if damage occurs), and slot surfaces are promptly cleaned with compressed air of at least one hundred psi free of oil, moisture, or contaminants [1][2]. For post‑cast installation, Type B dowel bars receive a bond‑breaker lubricant applied by spray or gloved hand to at least half their length at least one hour before concrete placement; holes are drilled full depth with a diameter no more than one‑eighth inch larger than the bar, and each dowel is rotated three to five complete turns during insertion to purge air [2]. Dowel bars are seated on plastic chairs that maintain a minimum half‑inch gap from all slot surfaces, and vertical tilt or horizontal skew of each end relative to the dowel midpoint is limited to a quarter‑inch displacement while longitudinal translation before concrete placement may not exceed one inch [2][1]. After placement, grout surrounding the dowels must achieve a compressive strength of at least 2,500 psi before traffic is allowed, and core verification requires that the summed voids around any examined dowel be less than half an inch; the slot is then filled with epoxy bonding compound followed by concrete patching material which is vibrated for consolidation, finished to match the existing surface, cured, and finally sawed and sealed as required [2][1]. Structural adequacy of the load‑transfer system is demonstrated through PTM No. 642 testing, limiting Linear Variable Differential Transformer (LVDT) differential deflection to no more than 7.5 mils after one million loading cycles and permitting an increase of only 2.5 mils by ten million cycles; high‑performance dowels not listed in Bulletin 15 must be accompanied by full PTM No. 642 test reports, shop drawings, and a load‑transfer unit sample capable of holding at least six bars with pre‑applied bond‑breaker [2]. Documentation of the anchoring system, test results, and LTU samples is submitted to the Representative for verification at least forty‑five days before paving, and only one dowel type may be used on a long‑life concrete project unless special approval is obtained; onsite storage of dowels is limited to thirty days unless protected as specified [2]. Measurement and payment practices record six dowel bars for each joint or crack treated, reflecting the standard practice for both retrofit and new construction applications [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–472), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

Dowel bars must provide full load transfer across transverse joints, which requires that each bar be securely anchored to the existing slab with approved bonding agents such as epoxy, polyester or vinylester and that the dowel be bonded along its entire embedded length. The design specifications for jointed pavements (RC‑21M for reinforced concrete and RC‑27M for plain concrete) prescribe coated dowel bars at a spacing of 30 ft between joints, with perpendicular joints and appropriate seals. When the repaired section is expected to remain in service for more than twenty years before a planned replacement, high‑performance dowel bars are recommended. Fabrication and installation must follow approved shop drawings; each bar shall be placed square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface within a tolerance of plus or minus one‑eighth inch. The exposed portion of every dowel is required to receive a bond‑breaker coating before grout placement; for Type B epoxy‑coated bars this lubricant must cover at least half the length and be applied at least one hour prior to concrete placement, whereas Type A bars require no shop‑applied bond breaker. Installation holes may not exceed one‑eighth inch larger than the bar diameter, and narrow‑mouth dowel slots are approximately one inch wide at the slab surface tapering to about three inches below mid‑slab depth. Joint width limits are enforced at a quarter to one‑half inch for transverse joints and up to three‑quarters inch for longitudinal joints to assure proper load transfer. The anchoring material used to embed the bars must achieve its initial set within five minutes and be placed without voids; acceptance is granted only when core samples show peripheral voids totalling less than half an inch on any one core. Bars are positioned at slab mid‑depth on non‑metallic chairs that create a minimum half‑inch gap between the dowel cap and all slot surfaces, with a 1½‑inch expansion cap leaving a quarter‑inch clearance to the bottom of the cap. Geometric tolerances require vertical tilt or horizontal skew at either end not to exceed a quarter inch relative to the bar’s midpoint, while longitudinal and horizontal translations from the intended joint location are limited to one inch each. Dimensional requirements specify true‑circular free ends without burrs, diameters of 1.5 in or 1.25 in (allowable +0.25 in/–0.001 in) and lengths of 18 in ±½ in. Type B epoxy coatings must have a minimum non‑abraded thickness of eight mils, contain no more than two holidays per foot, and any repairs are limited to two percent of the surface area with patch coverage not exceeding five percent; mechanically galvanized bars must be at least 3.4 mil thick and stainless‑steel bars must conform to ASTM A955/A955M. Performance testing according to PTM 642 establishes a reliability threshold: differential deflection between loaded and unloaded joint faces may not exceed 7.5 mil after one million load cycles, and the increase in deflection must be no greater than 2.5 mil by ten million cycles. Prior to paving each dowel‑bar type must be approved through formal acceptance testing; contractors supplying high‑performance bars not listed in Bulletin 15 must provide complete PTM 642 test reports, shop drawings and a laboratory test unit containing at least six dowels with pre‑applied lubricant, while bars listed in Bulletin 15 require a similar sample with at least three dowels. Only one dowel‑bar type may be used on a long‑life concrete project unless an exception is granted. Dowel bars must be stored no longer than thirty days on site (or protected if stored longer), kept free of corrosion, uncoated spots or contaminants, and must exhibit uniform appearance throughout the batch. Dowel‑basket assemblies are to be securely anchored so that vertical or horizontal movement does not exceed a quarter inch; the type, location, number and length of anchors are determined by field conditions and documented in an approved quality‑control plan. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–472), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

Materials and mixture design

Dowel bars for concrete pavements shall be steel members that conform to the applicable ASTM and AASHTO specifications. They may be supplied as Type A coated bars, which require no bond‑breaker lubricant, or as Type B fusion‑bonded epoxy‑coated bars whose coating must have a non‑abraded thickness of at least eight mils, with holiday defects limited to two per linear foot and any rust or damage repaired before use. When a mechanically galvanized bar is specified, the zinc layer shall be no less than 3.4 mil (equivalent to 2 oz/ft²). Stainless‑steel or other corrosion‑resistant bars must meet ASTM A955/A955M or AASHTO M334 Level 3 requirements. Bar dimensions are limited to diameters of 1.25 in or 1.50 in, toleranced at +0.25 in and –0.001 in, with a length of 18 in ±½ in. All bars must be clean and free of rust, oil, grease, dirt, or any foreign material that could impair the concrete bond; any bar whose coating is damaged shall be removed and replaced prior to placement. The dowel surface shall be epoxy‑coated and smooth to facilitate load transfer, and the bar must be anchored to the surrounding concrete by a continuous bond along its full embedded length using an anchoring binder such as epoxy, polyester or vinylester resin. For Type B bars, at least one‑half of the bar length shall receive a bond‑breaker lubricant applied no later than one hour before concrete placement unless the lubricant has already been applied in the shop. The slot for the dowel is first lined with a ¼‑in thick preformed cellular polystyrene piece cut to match the slot cross‑section, and an epoxy bonding compound, used according to the manufacturer’s instructions, secures the bar. A non‑metallic expansion cap 1½ in high is placed on each bar leaving a ¼‑in clearance from the dowel end, and the cap is supported by plastic or other non‑metallic chairs that maintain at least a ½‑in clearance to all slot faces. An approved anchoring material formulated to achieve its initial set within five minutes shall be injected into the hole before bar insertion, rotated three to five times to purge air, troweled flush, and a data sheet for this material shall be provided. When a patch replaces an existing expansion joint, a ¾‑in expansion‑joint filler is installed in the adjacent joint. No special mixture proportions are prescribed for the dowel bar itself; the slots are filled with a conventional concrete patching mix cured according to normal practice. High‑performance dowel bars should be selected when the repaired portion is expected to remain in service for more than twenty years, and such bars must satisfy structural adequacy testing that limits LVDT differential deflection to no more than 7.5 mil at one million load cycles and an increase of no more than 2.5 mil after ten million cycles. Structural details and interfaces

Dowel bars are required wherever mid‑panel cracks or transverse joints in concrete pavements lack a load‑transfer device, and they serve as the primary means of transferring wheel loads across those locations [1]. For retrofit applications the bars are smooth steel that have been epoxy‑coated and are fully bonded along their entire embedded length with an approved anchoring material such as epoxy, polyester or vinylester binder [1][2]. The installation calls for three mid‑depth slots to be cut in each wheel path; each slot is produced by two parallel saw cuts that run parallel to the roadway centreline and are positioned within the wheel‑load zone, with six dowel bars placed—one per slot—for every affected joint or crack [1][2]. In full‑depth reconstruction of jointed pavements perpendicular transverse joints must be provided at regular intervals, spaced at 30 ft for reinforced‑concrete sections (RC‑21M) and at 15 ft for plain‑concrete sections (RC‑27M), each incorporating coated dowel bars and appropriate sealants to maintain effective load transfer [1]. The transverse joint is saw‑cut so that its centre coincides with the dowel bar, and the opened width of the joint is limited to between one‑quarter and one‑half inch along its entire length; longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters inch in width [2]. For continuous paving sections longer than sixty‑five feet, longitudinal tie bars are installed in those joints to supplement load transfer [2]. Each dowel slot is drilled no more than one‑eighth inch larger than the bar diameter and is centered over the crack or joint; the steel dowels have nominal diameters of 1.25 in or 1.5 in with a tolerance of +0.25 in/‑0.001 in and a length of 18 in ±½ in [2]. The bars are supplied with a Type B fusion‑bonded epoxy coating that is at least eight mils thick, free of burrs, has a true circular end, and limits holidays to two per linear foot [2]. Unless the shop has already applied a bond‑breaker, a lubricant is sprayed on at least one‑half of each bar’s length an hour before placement to ensure proper bonding [2]. A 1½‑in non‑metallic expansion cap that provides a quarter‑inch clearance beneath the cap is fitted to each dowel and the assembly is seated in a plastic or other non‑metallic chair that maintains a minimum half‑inch clearance on all slot surfaces, including the end, sides and bottom [2]. GFRP dowels are secured with clips while tubular steel bars receive epoxy‑bonded caps, and an approved anchoring material that sets within five minutes is used to embed the assembly, troweled flush so that voids around any core do not exceed half an inch [2]. The completed load‑transfer unit (LTU) is anchored in a basket that is fastened so that vertical tilt or horizontal skew does not exceed 0.25 in and longitudinal or horizontal translation does not exceed one inch; the baskets themselves may not move more than a quarter inch after fastening, and this condition must be demonstrated to the project representative each day before paving [2]. After the sub‑base and bedding layer are placed, precast slabs are set, the dowel bars are inserted into the pre‑formed slots, and the slots are patched with grout that is pumped in and must achieve at least 2 500 psi compressive strength before traffic is allowed, with a minimum of four grout ports per slab and bedding material not exceeding one‑half inch for fine‑grained fill or two inches for cement‑treated fill [2]. Only a single dowel type may be used on a project unless special approval is obtained; the bars must be stored no longer than thirty days on site and kept away from uncoated reinforcement to protect the coating integrity [2]. A quality‑control plan governs anchoring, alignment tolerances, coating condition, and verification testing of an LTU sample containing at least six dowels (or three high‑performance dowels) that must be submitted for review at least forty‑five days before paving operations commence [2].

Dowel bars are installed at transverse joints and other pavement interfaces to provide reliable load transfer between concrete slabs and adjacent features, and they must be fully bonded along their entire embedded length with a suitable binder such as epoxy, polyester, or vinylester [1][2]. The layout of the dowels follows the joint spacing dictated by pavement type; for jointed reinforced‑concrete pavements bars are placed at thirty‑foot intervals while jointed plain‑concrete pavements use fifteen‑foot intervals and new joints are aligned with existing ones to preserve continuity [1]. When an existing interface exhibits inadequate load transfer, a retrofit is performed by cutting parallel slots across the wheel path, inserting epoxy‑coated smooth steel dowel bars, and backfilling the slots with concrete patching material that is cured to reestablish continuous load transfer while preserving aggregate interlock for hairline cracks [1]. Dowel bar locations are set according to shop drawings with a positional tolerance of plus or minus one‑eighth inch, required to be square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface [2]. The embedded portion may be low‑bond (Type A) requiring no additional treatment, or high‑bond (Type B) which must receive a bond‑breaker lubricant on at least half its length at least one hour before concrete placement; when an epoxy coating is used it shall be fusion‑bonded with a minimum non‑abraded thickness of eight mils and limited to no more than two holidays per linear foot, with any damaged or rusted areas repaired so that repairs do not exceed two percent of the bar surface and patching does not cover more than five percent [2]. Slots for dowels are cut at mid‑depth (or at the thinner slab where thickness changes) by a full‑depth saw, cleaned to remove all loose material within twenty‑four hours and given a final cleaning no later than thirty minutes before concrete placement; drilled holes may be no larger than one‑eighth inch beyond the dowel diameter [2]. Non‑metallic chairs or plastic spacers provide at least a half‑inch clearance between the bar cap and slot surfaces, and alignment limits require vertical tilt or horizontal skew of no more than a quarter‑inch at each end with longitudinal translation not to exceed one inch [2]. The grout or anchoring material placed in the slots must be pumpable and achieve a compressive strength of at least 2,500 psi before traffic is permitted [2]. During installation the dowel is rotated three to five full turns while being inserted into fresh grout to purge air, and core testing verifies that voids around each bar do not exceed half an inch [2]. Transverse joint gaps are controlled to a minimum of one‑quarter inch and a maximum of one‑half inch, while longitudinal joints may be up to three‑quarters inch wide; expansion joint material of three‑quarters inch is installed adjacent to existing joints, and when dowel bars intersect shoulders, structures or other pavement features the ends of all transverse joints that meet the bar are sealed to prevent mortar intrusion and preserve load transfer across layers [2]. If fine‑grained granular bedding is used beneath the slab its thickness shall not exceed one‑half inch; cement‑treated bedding may be up to two inches provided it meets the specified strength criteria [2]. Dowel baskets are anchored to the substrate so that movement during concrete placement does not exceed a quarter‑inch in any direction, with a quality‑control plan defining anchor type, location, number and length based on field conditions; anchorage is demonstrated for approval before paving each day [2]. After concrete placement the repaired area is finished to match the existing pavement profile, and the joint is sawed and sealed as required [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

The design phase requires confirming that the underlying subgrade is stable and that the existing drainage system is functioning, because dowel‑bar retrofits are only pursued when those conditions are met [1]. To protect the load‑transfer function from moisture, chlorides and other incompressible materials, the installation plan must include measures to keep such agents out of the concrete structure [1]. Moisture‑control is further achieved by limiting voids around the bars, which is accomplished by anchoring the dowels properly and applying a bond‑breaker lubricant to at least half of the length of Type B coated bars unless the lubricant was already applied in the shop [2]. When work leaves a joint open during rain, an outlet should be excavated through the shoulder at the lowest point of the patch so that water can drain away from the work area and not infiltrate the joint [2].

Verification and expected performance

The completed dowel‑bar system is verified through a coordinated sequence of documentation review, dimensional inspection, material certification, installation tolerances, anchoring checks, and performance testing. Prior to any concrete placement the contractor must submit full PTM 642 test reports, shop drawings and an LTU sample containing at least six bars with bond‑breaker lubricant already applied; these documents are reviewed and formally approved by the Representative at least forty‑five days in advance, and no paving may commence until that approval is received. The dowel bars themselves must conform to the specified size limits—diameter of 1.5 in or 1.25 in with a +0.25 in/–0.001 in tolerance and length of 18 in ±½ in—and be coated with a Type B fusion‑bonded epoxy at least eight mils thick, containing no more than two holidays per linear foot as detected by a 67.5‑V detector, while any rust or surface defect must be repaired so that damaged areas do not exceed two percent of the bar surface per foot.

During placement each dowel and tie bar is set square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface within ±1⁄8 in of the locations shown on the shop drawings. A bond‑breaker lubricant is applied to every embedded portion at least one hour before concrete placement, and the slot is cleaned and dried in accordance with the required time limits. The bar rests on non‑metallic chairs that provide a minimum half‑inch clearance, and its ends may not be displaced more than 0.25 in from the midpoint; longitudinal and horizontal translations are limited to one inch each, while vertical or horizontal skew relative to the pavement plane is also restricted to 0.25 in. Expansion caps of 1½ in must have a quarter‑inch gap to the cap bottom, and the concrete patching material is placed, vibrated and finished so that the dowel remains stationary and the surface profile matches the existing pavement before the joint is sawed, sealed and opened to traffic.

Joint geometry is checked to ensure transverse joint widths are between ¼ in and ½ in along their entire length and longitudinal joints do not exceed ¾ in. The finished vertical differential across any joint must be no greater than ¼ in before the next slab is set, and after placement the dowel slots and bedding chambers are grouted; the grout must reach a compressive strength of at least 2 500 psi prior to traffic opening.

Anchoring performance is verified on the first day of dowel‑bar anchoring by randomly selecting three locations, coring a 4‑in diameter hole at least three inches from the joint face, and measuring the void perimeter around each bar; acceptance requires that the summed voids be less than 0.50 in for each core. If any core exceeds this limit, a new patch is constructed offset two feet from the deficient joint at no additional cost.

A quality‑control plan for anchoring dowel‑basket assemblies must be provided, and the baskets are fastened to the substrate so that vertical or horizontal movement does not exceed ¼ in; the fastening method is demonstrated and approved by the Representative before concrete placement each day. Throughout storage and handling the bars may remain on site no longer than thirty days unless protected and approved, must appear uniform, free of corrosion, uncoated spots or contaminants, and any damaged units identified after placement are replaced. Only one dowel‑bar type is permitted for Long Life Concrete unless a waiver is granted. Collectively these inspections and tests confirm that the installed dowel‑bar system meets all compliance, constructability, performance, and project‑requirement criteria. [2]

The dowel bar is expected to provide reliable load transfer across concrete pavement joints throughout its service life, thereby preventing slab deflection and the development of joint‑related distress such as faulting and loss of ride quality. The repaired section may be opened to traffic only after the surrounding concrete attains a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi, confirming that the material can adequately support loads. Performance is demonstrated by two complementary verification procedures. First, random core inspections are conducted; the void space measured around each dowel must not exceed 0.5 in in any core and cylinder compression tests must verify the required 1,000‑psi strength. Second, structural adequacy testing performed according to PTM No. 642 uses Linear Variable Differential Transformers to record the differential deflection between the loaded and unloaded sides of a joint. The test requires that the LVDT‑measured differential be no greater than 7.5 mil after one million load cycles and that any increase in this differential not exceed 2.5 mil after ten million cycles. Satisfying these criteria confirms that the dowel bar maintains effective load transfer, limits joint movement, resists distress, and preserves ride quality over the pavement’s intended lifespan.
