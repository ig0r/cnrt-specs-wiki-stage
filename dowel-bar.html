Dowel Bar

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Dowel Bar is a topic under Rigid Pavement.
dowel-barsdowelsdowelleddowelled-jointscoated-dowel-bars
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-28

Dowel bars are short steel members—typically 18 in long with a nominal diameter of 1.25–1.5 in—installed across transverse joints or cracks in rigid Portland‑cement concrete pavements to provide a reliable load‑transfer mechanism[1][2]. By being fully bonded along their embedded length with fast‑setting epoxy, polyester, or vinylester binders and protected by a minimum eight‑mil Type B fusion‑bonded epoxy (or equivalent corrosion‑resistant coating), they tie adjacent slabs together so that wheel loads are shared and differential slab movement is limited to strict deflection limits[1][2]. Performance verification through PTM No. 642 testing requires joint differential deflection not to exceed 7.5 mil after one million load cycles and no more than an additional 2.5 mil after ten million cycles, thereby preserving slab alignment, ride quality, and long‑term durability for service lives often exceeding twenty years[1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

The primary engineering problem addressed by dowel bars is the inability of adjacent concrete slabs to share wheel loads when transverse cracks, mid‑panel cracks, or deteriorated joints interrupt load transfer. This lack of load sharing produces differential slab movement, faulting, loss of alignment, moisture intrusion and accelerated structural deterioration, especially under heavily channeled traffic. Consequently the performance need is to provide a reliable load‑transfer mechanism that ties adjacent slabs together, preserving uniform support across the joint and maintaining pavement ride quality and service life. Dowel bars are short steel members installed through the joint and anchored with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders, or mechanically secured so that movement of the bar does not exceed 0.25 in during placement. The design requirement is that the differential deflection between the loaded and unloaded sides of a joint remain within strict limits—no greater than 7.5 mil after one million load cycles and an increase of no more than 2.5 mil after ten million cycles—to ensure that repeated traffic loading does not exceed allowable joint movement. By meeting these criteria the dowel bar retrofit restores load transfer, reestablishes slab alignment, prevents faulting and cracking, and extends the pavement’s service life, particularly on roadways expected to remain in service for more than twenty years. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–462), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–673)

The essential functional objective of a dowel bar system is to provide reliable load transfer across transverse joints or cracks, especially in heavily trafficked wheel paths, while also preventing joint deflection and moisture intrusion that could degrade the pavement structure [1][2]. Structurally, the bar must be a smooth steel member installed in slots cut parallel to traffic and anchored with approved binders such as epoxy, polyester, or vinylester, creating a continuous bond along its entire embedded length; the installation tolerances are strict, requiring vertical differentials across any joint not to exceed one‑quarter inch, transverse joint gaps between one‑quarter and one‑half inch, and total voids around the dowel of no more than 0.50 inch per verification core [1][2]. Alignment criteria further limit tilt or skew at each end of the bar to a maximum of 0.25 inch relative to its midpoint, longitudinal translation before concrete placement to one inch, and any vertical or horizontal movement during concrete placement to a quarter‑inch, with a bond‑breaker lubricant applied to at least half of the dowel length and the exposed portion left free of concrete bonding to preserve load transfer capability [2]. The system must also satisfy cyclic deflection limits, restricting differential deflection under dynamic loading to no more than 7.5 mil after one million cycles and allowing an increase of no greater than 2.5 mil after ten million cycles, thereby ensuring long‑term structural performance [2]. For durability, high‑performance dowel bars—preferably epoxy‑coated—are recommended for pavements expected to remain in service for more than twenty years, with the coating required to be a Type B fusion‑bonded epoxy of at least eight mils thickness, free of burrs, and limited to no more than two holidays per linear foot; any visible rust, corrosion deposits, uncoated spots, or staining must be removed and the bar replaced before concreting [1][2]. The anchoring material surrounding each dowel must achieve an initial set within five minutes, and the grout placed around the bar must reach a compressive strength of at least 2 500 psi prior to opening the pavement to traffic; storage on‑site is limited to thirty days unless protective covering is provided, with special rules applying to stainless‑steel bars [2]. Safety objectives are met through rigorous inspection procedures that verify uniform appearance, absence of coating damage, and proper anchoring basket installation, with any damaged or displaced dowels replaced before concrete placement and a quality‑control plan demonstrated to the Representative each day to prevent unsafe slab movement or cracking [1][2]. Serviceability is maintained by preserving near‑complete load transfer and aggregate interlock where hairline cracks exist, keeping joint alignment within specified tolerances throughout the pavement’s life, ensuring smooth operation of the joint, protecting the structure from moisture ingress, and delivering a uniform ride quality over the design period [1][2].

Scope and applicability

Dowel bars are employed wherever a rigid cement‑concrete pavement requires load transfer across a transverse joint, crack or expansion joint, and the technique is limited to Portland‑cement concrete surfaces—including plain, reinforced and Long Life types—rather than asphalt pavements [1][2]. The approach is appropriate for new construction of precast concrete slab systems, multilane slip‑form slabs with full‑width joints, prestressed concrete slabs, and other rigid pavement installations that specify load‑transfer units in the contract documents [2]. It is also suitable for rehabilitation work such as full‑depth patching, slab stabilization, joint sealing, correction of settled slabs (often combined with slab jacking), widening of existing concrete lanes, addition of shoulders or structures, corner repairs, and intermittent patch repairs on precast systems [1][2]. Project conditions that favor dowel‑bar retrofits include heavily channelled traffic loads, a stable subgrade with functional drainage, and an anticipated service life of the repaired section of at least twenty years before any programmed replacement [1]. The method is indicated when existing slabs exhibit working transverse cracks, mid‑panel cracks, hairline cracks, or other joints where load transfer has become deficient but aggregate interlock remains viable [1]. Steel dowels are specified with a diameter of 1.25 to 1.5 in (tolerance +0.25 in, –0.001 in) and an approximate length of 18 in (±½ in), and the guides accept a range of bar types including epoxy‑coated steel per ASTM A775, coated steel per ASTM A513, mechanically galvanized steel, stainless‑steel dowels per ASTM A955/A955M, uncoated corrosion‑resistant steel meeting AASHTO M 334 Level 3, high‑performance dowels listed in Bulletin 15, and approved non‑metallic alternatives such as GFRP or zinc‑alloy sleeves [2]. For Type B epoxy coatings the minimum non‑abraded thickness must be eight mils, and a bond‑breaker lubricant is required on at least one‑half of the bar length applied at least one hour before concrete placement; Type A coating is also permitted without this requirement [2]. Installation practices call for the dowel centre to lie at slab mid‑depth (or at the mid‑depth of the thinner slab where thickness varies), slots cut and cleaned within thirty minutes prior to concrete placement, three slots per wheel path, use of non‑metallic expansion caps with a quarter‑inch clearance, chairs providing at least a half‑inch clearance, and grout that attains the specified compressive strength [2]. For Long Life Concrete projects only a single dowel type may be used unless a waiver is granted, a full load‑transfer unit sample containing at least three bars with pre‑applied bond‑breaker lubricant must be submitted forty‑five days before paving, and on‑site storage of dowels is limited to thirty days without special protection [2]. By meeting these material specifications and installation requirements, dowel bars provide the necessary load transfer to maintain slab alignment under traffic loads and ensure the intended performance of both new and rehabilitated rigid pavements [1][2].

When a project demands an exceptionally rapid reopening of traffic or when work‑zone safety constraints impose a minimum construction time, the guides recommend accelerated (rapid‑setting) concrete patching as the preferred load‑transfer method, and if those constraints are especially stringent they advise using precast concrete paving systems instead of dowel retrofits. The use of high early‑strength concrete is discouraged because its tendency toward excessive shrinkage cracking can undermine joint performance, leading designers to select alternative concrete mixes or repair techniques. Unstable subgrade conditions or a non‑functional existing drainage system also diminish the effectiveness of dowel bars, prompting the adoption of slab stabilization or jacking measures, or in some cases full‑depth joint patching, rather than relying on dowels. When cracks have widened beyond hairline dimensions and aggregate interlock has been lost, dowel bars no longer provide adequate load transfer, making other repair approaches more suitable. If a repair cannot be grouted immediately, the guides allow narrow‑mouth surface dowel slots to remain in place and be patched later rather than installing conventional dowel bars. For continuous precast sections exceeding 65 feet, tie bars are required along longitudinal joints instead of transverse dowels. When the finished vertical differential across any joint exceeds one‑quarter inch but does not exceed one‑half inch, diamond grinding of the slab surface is permitted as an alternative to readjusting dowel placement. Construction details that would jeopardize existing dowels—such as full‑depth saw cuts extending into an adjacent slab—or specifications calling for a Type C patch shift the design toward tie bars and omit a bond breaker, thereby relying on other load‑transfer measures. Finally, if dowel bar baskets cannot be secured so that they move no more than one‑quarter inch vertically or horizontally, or if field conditions prevent establishing an approved anchoring plan, paving must be suspended and another design solution adopted. Design inputs and requirements

The dowel‑bar design must incorporate site temperature and exposure conditions for thermal expansion, concrete mix designs that meet the required compressive and flexural strengths, the specified grout materials and bond‑breaker coating for the bars, geotechnical data on subbase quality and removal of unsuitable material to achieve the design subgrade elevation, and existing‑condition surveys such as elevations of adjoining pavement, core locations, and joint geometry; traffic loading requirements are not explicitly called out in the specification. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

The design and installation of dowel bars in concrete pavements are governed by a combination of state roadway construction standards and national specifications that prescribe layout drawings, material requirements, performance testing, and project‑specific constraints. State documents such as the Roadway Construction Standards (Publication 72M) provide standard detail sheets—RC‑26M for retrofits, RC‑21M for jointed reinforced concrete pavements, and RC‑27M for jointed plain concrete pavements—and require perpendicular joints, coated dowels, joint spacing of 30 ft for JRCP and 15 ft for JPCP together with appropriate sealing. Complementary technical specifications are found in Publication 408, where Section 527 mandates epoxy‑coated smooth steel dowels placed in wheel paths across working transverse cracks or joints, and Sections 516 and 519 require continuous bonding of each dowel along its entire embedded length with an approved anchoring material such as epoxy, polyester, or vinylester binder. Nationally, all dowel bars must be fabricated to meet the material criteria of ASTM A513, while galvanized bars must conform to ASTM B695/B696 with a minimum coating thickness of 3.4 mil (2 oz/ft²), stainless‑steel bars must satisfy ASTM A955/A955M, and epoxy‑coated bars must be treated in accordance with ASTM A775 and receive a fusion‑bonded Type B coating of at least 8 mil non‑abraded thickness containing no more than two holidays per linear foot. When an epoxy coating is specified, the bond‑breaker lubricant must be applied to at least one‑half of the dowel length unless a shop‑applied bond breaker already exists; Type A bars do not require lubricant. Structural adequacy of the dowel system is demonstrated through testing to PTM No. 642, which limits differential deflection measured with LVDTs to 7.5 mil at one million load cycles and permits an increase of no more than 2.5 mil by ten million cycles; certification of compliance must be submitted under Section 106.03(b) together with shop drawings and a long‑term unit sample at least forty‑five days before paving. The anchoring material used to bond the dowel must set within five minutes and be capable of limiting movement of the assembled dowel to no more than a quarter‑inch in any direction, and a quality‑control plan for the anchoring system must be approved by the project Representative. Placement tolerances are defined in the construction specifications, requiring dowel locations shown on approved shop or standard drawings to be staked prior to paving and positioned within plus‑or‑minus one‑eighth inch of the indicated points, with each end allowed a maximum tilt or skew of 0.25 inch relative to the bar midpoint and longitudinal or horizontal translation limited to one inch; the finished vertical differential across any joint may not exceed one‑quarter inch. Slots for dowel insertion are cut either as bottom‑slot or surface‑slot configurations meeting the dimensions prescribed in Section 7.a, using a power‑driven self‑propelled saw to produce parallel cuts and a jackhammer not exceeding thirty pounds (or fifteen pounds if damage occurs) to remove concrete between cuts; slots must be cleaned within thirty minutes before concrete placement with oil‑free air at a pressure of at least one hundred psi. Holes drilled for dowel insertion are full‑depth, may be no more than one‑eighth inch larger in diameter than the bar, and must be aligned with frame‑mounted equipment to achieve the required positional accuracy. The grout used to embed transverse dowels must attain a compressive strength of at least 2 500 psi before traffic is permitted, ensuring that load transfer performance meets the design intent. Performance criteria across all guides call for effective load transfer across cracks and joints, with high‑performance dowels recommended when the repaired segment is expected to remain in service for more than twenty years. Project constraints limit dowel bar retrofits to locations subjected to heavily channeled loads that produce joint faulting or to hairline cracks where aggregate interlock still exists, and prohibit the use of more than one dowel type on a Long Life Concrete pavement unless an exception is granted. On‑site storage of dowel bars is restricted to thirty days without special approval; stored bars must be kept under conditions that allow adequate air circulation or be protected with covering for longer periods, and any bar with damaged coating must be removed and replaced before concreting. The total perimeter voids around each dowel may not exceed 0.5 inch per core, and exceeding anchoring or void limits triggers additional inspection and reconstruction at no extra cost to the owner. When dowel bar retrofits are combined with slab‑jacking to correct settled slabs, the procedures outlined in Section 681 of Publication 408 apply, integrating the load‑transfer objectives with pavement restoration activities. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–462), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–673)

Design methods and criteria

Design of dowel‑bar systems is directed by procedural guidelines rather than explicit analytical equations, emphasizing performance criteria and construction tolerances [1][2]. Load transfer is achieved by installing a dowel bar across every patch joint or transverse crack, with the bar anchored in the existing concrete using an epoxy, polyester, or vinyl‑ester binder [1][2]. For new pavement, load‑transfer dowels and tie bars are cast into precast slabs at locations shown on shop drawings, positioned square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface within ±1/8 inch of the specified coordinates [2]. The portion of each dowel that will be embedded in the slab is coated with a bond‑breaker; Type B‑coated bars receive lubricant over at least half their length and must cure for one hour before concrete placement [2]. When retrofitting existing pavement, epoxy‑coated smooth dowels are placed in wheel paths across cracks or joints that lack dowels, using a power‑driven self‑propelled saw to cut two parallel slots per dowel location, creating three slots per wheel path aligned with the roadway centreline [1]. After cleaning the slots, the dowels are positioned and the voids are filled with rapid‑setting grout or concrete patching material that is later cured; excess material is troweled flush with the surrounding slab [1][2]. The standard practice records six dowel bars for each joint or crack treated in retrofit work, while new construction typically provides one dowel per transverse joint at the prescribed spacing [1]. Joint spacing dictates the use of coated dowels: jointed reinforced concrete pavement (RC‑21M) employs 30‑ft joint intervals, and jointed plain concrete pavement (RC‑27M) uses 15‑ft intervals, both with perpendicular joints and appropriate seals [1]. Alignment tolerances for installed dowels require that vertical tilt or horizontal skew of each end relative to the bar midpoint not exceed 0.25 inch, and longitudinal or horizontal translation be limited to 1 inch; holes drilled in existing pavement may be no larger than one‑eighth inch beyond the bar diameter, and fixed frames are used to maintain alignment during insertion [2]. Each dowel is rotated three to five full turns as it is placed to purge air, after which rapid‑setting grout—pumped through designated ports—is used to fill both the dowel slots and bedding chambers; the grout mix must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 2 500 psi before traffic may be permitted [2]. All reinforcement steel, dowels, and longitudinal joint ties receive epoxy coating in accordance with ASTM A775, with Type B coatings required to have a non‑abraded thickness of at least 8 mil per AASHTO M‑254 [2]. Structural adequacy is verified by PTM No. 642 testing, which limits LVDT differential deflection under dynamic and static loading to no more than 7.5 mil after one million cycles and restricts any increase to 2.5 mil after ten million cycles [2]. Joint gaps are controlled so that transverse joints range from ¼ inch to ½ inch and longitudinal joints do not exceed ¾ inch, while bedding material beneath slabs is limited to a maximum thickness of one‑half inch for fine‑grained granular bedding [2]. Field acceptance includes coring anchored dowels and confirming that peripheral voids total less than 0.50 inch per core, and anchoring quality‑control plans prevent movement greater than 0.25 inch in any direction during concrete placement [2]. When the repaired section is expected to remain in service for more than twenty years before programmed replacement, high‑performance dowel bars are recommended [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–462), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–673)

Dowel bars in concrete pavements must achieve full load transfer across transverse joints, which requires that each bar be securely anchored to the existing slab with approved bonding agents such as epoxy, polyester or vinylester and that the dowel be bonded along its entire embedded length. The design specifications for jointed pavements prescribe coated dowel bars placed at the prescribed joint spacings—30 ft for reinforced concrete (RC‑21M) and similarly for plain concrete (RC‑27M)—and recommend high‑performance dowels when the repaired section is expected to remain in service more than twenty years before a programmed replacement. Installation must follow the approved shop drawings, with each bar positioned square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface within a tolerance of plus or minus one‑eighth inch. The exposed portion of every dowel is required to receive a continuous fusion‑bonded epoxy bond‑breaker coating of at least eight mils non‑abraded thickness (Type B) before concrete placement so that the grout does not adhere to the steel, and no more than two holidays per linear foot may be detected with a 67.5‑volt holiday detector. Joint gaps surrounding the dowels are limited to between one‑quarter and one‑half inch to assure proper load transfer and alignment. The performance of the load‑transfer unit must satisfy Section 705.3, meaning that a load‑transfer test shall demonstrate a maximum LVDT differential deflection of no more than 7.5 mils after one million loading cycles and an increase of no more than 2.5 mils by ten million cycles on both approach and leave sides of the joint. Physical dimensions are constrained to a nominal diameter of 1.5 inches (or 1.25 inches with a tolerance of +0.25 / –0.001 inch) and a length of 18 inches plus or minus one‑half inch as shown on standard drawing RC‑20M. Material grades must meet ASTM specifications such as A955 Grade 60 or higher for solid steel dowels, A513 with a minimum yield strength of 60 ksi, or an equivalent high‑performance alternative, and the bond‑breaker material shall conform to AASHTO M 254 Type A or B depending on required bond strength. Installation holes may be no larger than one‑eighth inch beyond the coated bar diameter and must be drilled in accordance with the alignment tolerances of the standard drawings, while a clearance tube of at least one inch is placed over the lubricated end of each coated dowel. Bond‑breaker lubricant shall be applied at least one hour before concrete placement covering a minimum of half the length of Type B dowels unless pre‑lubricated in the shop, and the anchoring grout must set within five minutes, contain no voids, and be accepted only when perimeter voids measured on any core are less than 0.50 inch. Geometric tolerances for placement require that vertical tilt or horizontal skew at either end of a bar not exceed a quarter‑inch relative to the midpoint, while longitudinal and transverse translations are limited to one inch each. Each dowel must be fitted with a non‑metallic expansion cap 1½ inches long leaving a quarter‑inch gap from the tip, and it must rest on plastic or other non‑metallic chairs that provide at least a half‑inch clearance to all slot surfaces. Coating integrity is mandatory; any bar showing damaged corrosion protection must be replaced, and all reinforcement steel, dowel bars, and longitudinal joint ties shall be epoxy‑coated in accordance with ASTM A775 and the provisions of Section 1002.1, with high‑performance dowels additionally satisfying the requirements of Section 530 when indicated on contract drawings. Reliability is verified through a load‑transfer unit test package that includes full PTM No. 642 reports and shop drawings submitted at least forty‑five days before paving; the LTU sample must contain a minimum of six high‑performance dowels (or three when listed in Bulletin 15) with bond‑breaker lubricant pre‑applied, and paving may not commence until the Representative signs off on passing results. On‑site storage of dowel bars is limited to thirty days unless a protective cover and approval are obtained, and only one dowel‑bar type may be used on a Long Life Concrete project unless a special waiver is granted; bars must appear uniform, free of corrosion, uncoated spots, acid or flux residues, and must not contact other uncoated steel. Dowel‑basket assemblies must be anchored with a documented quality‑control plan so that vertical or horizontal movement during concrete placement does not exceed a quarter‑inch, shipping wires are to remain in place unless magnetic tomography verification is employed, and the baskets must be secured with anchors that maintain correct position and alignment throughout placement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–462), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–673)

Structural section and dimensions

When dowel bars are used, their placement must meet strict alignment tolerances: each end of the bar may be tilted or skewed no more than a quarter‑inch from the midpoint, and the bar’s longitudinal and horizontal positions may not deviate more than one inch from the intended joint line; the required vertical depth is dictated by the standard drawings. No other dimensional requirements for pavement, base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab, shoulder, edge‑support or reinforcement are specified in this section. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–367)

In practice the dowel‑bar pavement is built on a compacted No 2A aggregate base. The concrete slab is poured one‑quarter inch thicker than the nominal design depth; this extra thickness functions as a sacrificial layer and is omitted from structural calculations. Joint geometry follows the shop drawings: transverse joints are provided with a width between one‑quarter and one‑half inch, while longitudinal joints are limited to three‑quarters inch. When dowel‑bar panels are laid out, the panel dimensions are constrained so that the longer side does not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the aspect ratio of length to width is kept at or below 1.5; longitudinal joints are positioned away from wheel paths. The screeding equipment, calibrated with laser control, grades the bedding material and establishes the required cross‑slope within the specified tolerance for each slab. Transitions are placed at the limits of the concrete overlay; where an existing treated base or concrete underlies the transition a geotextile debonding layer is installed to isolate the new pavement. Dowel‑bar assemblies are anchored to the substrate in a manner that restricts vertical and horizontal movement to no more than one‑quarter inch, with the type, number and length of anchors selected according to field conditions. Tie bars are incorporated along longitudinal joints for continuous sections longer than sixty‑five feet as shown on the shop drawings, providing continuity without impeding joint movement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–462)

Materials and mixture design

Dowel bars specified for concrete pavements shall be fabricated from steel and provided with a smooth epoxy‑coated surface to protect the metal and facilitate load transfer. The specification must identify the steel grade and reference an applicable standard such as ASTM A513 or AASHTO T253 for coated bars, ASTM B695/B696 for mechanically galvanized bars, ASTM A955/A955M for stainless‑steel bars, or AASHTO M334 Level 3 for uncoated corrosion‑resistant steel. The coating system shall be described in detail; a Type B fusion‑bonded epoxy coating must have a non‑abraded thickness of at least eight mils (or may consist of a double layer totaling twenty to sixty mils provided it is free of holidays), while a galvanized coat must not be less than 3.4 mil (2 oz/ft²). For Type B coated bars the specification shall require application of a bond‑breaker lubricant to at least one‑half of the bar length unless the bar is supplied pre‑lubricated, whereas Type A coated bars do not require lubricant. Physical dimensions are limited to diameters of either 1.5 in or 1.25 in with tolerances of +0.25 in and –0.001 in, and lengths of 18 in ±½ in; the ends must be true circles free of burrs. All dowel bars must be clean steel free of rust, dirt, oil, grease or any contaminant that could impair bonding to concrete, and the corrosion‑protective coating must remain intact. The anchoring material used to bond the dowel to the surrounding concrete shall provide a continuous bond along the full embedded length and may be an epoxy, polyester or vinylester resin that sets within five minutes of application, with a data sheet supplied for the product. When high‑performance dowels are required—such as when the repaired portion is expected to remain in service more than twenty years—the bars must satisfy the performance criteria set out in Section 530 and be tested according to PTM No. 642, demonstrating a maximum LVDT differential deflection of 7.5 mil after one million load cycles and no more than an additional 2.5 mil after ten million cycles. The spacing of coated dowel bars shall follow the design standards that call for placement at thirty‑foot intervals on jointed plain concrete pavements and fifteen‑foot intervals on jointed reinforced concrete pavements, with joints oriented perpendicular to traffic flow. Associated expansion‑joint filler shall be installed around each bar, using a three‑quarter‑inch material for general patches and a one‑half‑inch material for corner repairs, together with a quarter‑inch thick preformed cellular polystyrene piece to serve as a temporary joint. Non‑metallic expansion caps of one‑and‑a‑half inches shall be positioned with a quarter‑inch clearance from the bar end, and plastic or other non‑metallic chairs must provide at least a half‑inch clearance on all slot faces. The drilled holes for dowel insertion may not exceed one‑eighth inch larger than the bar diameter, and the bar should be rotated three to five times during placement to purge air voids. After the dowel is set, the transverse slot shall be filled with a manufacturer‑approved grout or concrete patching material capable of being pumped into the slot and achieving a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is allowed; no additional mixture proportion requirements are imposed beyond this conventional patching mix. [1][2]

To satisfy the combined structural, durability, constructability and performance objectives for dowel‑bar load transfer systems, the designer follows a coordinated material‑selection and mixture‑proportioning protocol that begins with the bar itself. The bar is specified as a short steel member 18 in long whose free end must be a true circle without burrs and whose nominal diameter is either 1.25 in or 1.5 in, toleranced to +0.25 in and –0.001 in in accordance with drawing RC‑20M. The steel may be plain carbon, galvanized, stainless‑steel or a high‑performance alloy, each meeting the applicable ASTM requirements for strength and corrosion resistance. Every bar receives a fusion‑bonded epoxy coating of Type B; the non‑abraded portion of the coating must be at least 8 mil thick and free of holidays, with any surface rust repaired before placement. The coated bar is required to pass PTM No. 642 structural adequacy testing, demonstrating that the LVDT differential deflection does not exceed 7.5 mil after one million load cycles and that the increase in deflection over ten million cycles is limited to an additional 2.5 mil. The concrete matrix surrounding the dowel bars must be proportioned to complement the bar performance. Aggregate grading is controlled so that thin, elongated coarse aggregate retained on a 3/8‑in sieve does not exceed 20 % of the total, maintaining a 3:1 ratio as prescribed by ASTM D4791. Cement content for both slip‑form and fixed‑form placements is capped at 611 lb per cubic yard; water‑cement ratios are limited to 0.42 for slip‑form and 0.45 for fixed‑form applications, thereby providing adequate workability while limiting permeability. High‑early‑strength cement concrete is expressly excluded from long‑life concrete paving because of its adverse effect on durability. The grout that fills the transverse dowel slots and any tie‑bar openings follows the manufacturer’s mix design, must be pumpable under field conditions, and is required to achieve a compressive strength of at least 2 500 psi before traffic loading. Likewise, cement‑treated bedding material placed beneath the bar must reach a minimum of 500 psi prior to opening to traffic. To verify that these strengths are attainable, a trial batch of each mix—grout and bedding—is produced under anticipated field conditions at least twenty‑eight days before slab placement and tested in accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications. All reinforcement steel, including dowel bars and longitudinal joint ties, is coated with epoxy per ASTM A775, and any high‑performance dowel bar system must also satisfy the provisions of Section 530. By adhering to these dimensional tolerances, coating requirements, aggregate limits, cement factors, water‑cement ratios, strength thresholds for grout and bedding, and by using approved test reports and storage practices (bars stored no longer than thirty days unless protected), the selected dowel‑bar system achieves the required structural capacity, long‑term durability, ease of construction, and overall pavement performance. Structural details and interfaces

Dowel bars are installed in every transverse joint of concrete pavement slabs and also retrofitted across mid‑panel cracks or joints that lack a load‑transfer device [1][2]. The transverse joint width is limited to a minimum of one‑quarter inch and a maximum of one‑half inch along its entire length, while longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters inch [2]. In full‑depth reconstruction the pavement is divided by perpendicular transverse joints spaced at thirty feet for reinforced‑concrete sections and fifteen feet for plain‑concrete sections; each of these joints incorporates coated dowel bars together with appropriate sealants [1]. The dowel bar itself is a smooth steel bar typically 1.25 inches in diameter (allowable +0.25 in, –0.001 in) or 1.5 inches in diameter and eighteen inches long, with an overall length tolerance of plus or minus one‑half inch [2]. Bars are supplied either with a fusion‑bonded epoxy Type B coating at least eight mils thick in accordance with ASTM A775, or as galvanized, stainless‑steel, or other corrosion‑resistant steel meeting the applicable specifications; when a Type B coating is used a bond‑breaker lubricant must be applied to at least half of the bar length unless it has already been applied in the shop [2]. For retrofit work the dowel bars are epoxy‑coated smooth steel and are placed fully bonded along their entire embedded length using an epoxy, polyester or vinylester binder that fills the slot before it is backfilled with concrete patching material and cured [1]. Each wheel path receives three parallel slots cut by two saw cuts each, positioned within the wheel‑load zone; six dowel bars—one per slot—are installed and later covered with a concrete patch [1]. The dowel bar is positioned in a load‑transfer unit that is staked and anchored before paving so that tilt at either end does not exceed 0.25 inch and longitudinal or horizontal translation does not exceed one inch [2]. Plastic or non‑metallic chairs provide a minimum half‑inch clearance to all slot surfaces, and a quarter‑inch thick preformed cellular polystyrene (or expansion joint material for wider cracks) lines the mid‑depth of the retrofit slot; non‑metallic expansion caps one‑and‑a‑half inches high are fitted with a quarter‑inch clearance from the dowel end [2]. After placement, grout or binder pumped into the slots must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 2 500 psi before traffic is permitted [1][2]. Longitudinal tie bars are installed in longitudinal joints to keep slabs aligned and share loads, but they are prohibited within twenty‑four inches of a dowelled contraction joint [2]. Dowel bars and tie bars are embedded together with the reinforcing steel that is cast into each precast unit, and the precast slabs are fabricated complete with concrete, reinforcement, dowel bars, tie bars, and quality‑control documentation [2]. Storage of dowel bars at the jobsite must not exceed thirty days without approval, they must be protected from corrosion, never allowed to contact uncoated reinforcement, and any bar showing visible corrosion or damage must be replaced after placement inspection [1][2]. Shipping wires may remain in place unless magnetic tomography verification is used to confirm proper placement [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Before any dowel‑bar retrofit is undertaken the design must confirm that the underlying subgrade is stable and that the existing drainage system is functioning properly, because a compromised foundation or poor drainage would allow water to accumulate and degrade load transfer [1]. The design also calls for measures that keep water, chlorides and other incompressible materials from entering the concrete structure, thereby limiting moisture‑related deterioration under varying climate conditions [1]. Each dowel bar is coated with a bond‑breaker lubricant at least one hour prior to placement of fresh concrete, and any portion of the bar that remains exposed after placement is left bondless as specified, which creates a barrier against moisture penetration [2]. The bars are then anchored in place with a fast‑setting material that reaches its initial set within five minutes, providing rapid development of strength while maintaining the protective coating [2]. During installation the void space surrounding each bar is limited to less than one‑half inch, a tolerance intended to prevent water from infiltrating the joint and compromising the load‑transfer function [2]. If rain occurs while a pavement patch containing dowel bars is open, an outlet is excavated at the lowest point of the patch through the shoulder so that water can be drained away from the joint area, further protecting the assembly from excess moisture [2].

The dowel bar system must satisfy a comprehensive set of accessibility, safety, geometric, and user‑performance criteria. Geometrically, each bar is required to be cast square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface within ±1/8 in of the locations shown on the shop drawings, with its free end formed as a true circle free of burrs [2]. The bar diameter shall be either 1.5 in or 1.25 in, toleranced +0.25 in / –0.001 in, and the length 18 in ±½ in [2]. Placement tolerances limit vertical tilt or horizontal skew at each end to no more than 0.25 in relative to the bar midpoint, longitudinal translation along the joint line to 1 in, and horizontal translation across the joint also to 1 in [2]. Joint geometry must maintain transverse gaps between one‑quarter and one‑half inch and vertical differentials not exceeding one‑quarter inch, while a clearance of at least ¼ in is required between a non‑metallic expansion cap and the dowel end and half‑inch from all slot surfaces [2]. The bars are required to be coated with a Type B fusion‑bonded epoxy coating of minimum 8 mil thickness, with no more than two holidays per linear foot; any rust, visible defects or damaged coating must be repaired (patching limited to five percent of the bar surface) or the bar replaced before concrete placement [2]. For long‑term service exceeding twenty years, a high‑performance dowel bar is recommended to maintain safety and ride quality for road users [1]. The coating is protected by applying a bond‑breaker lubricant to at least one‑half of the length of Type B coated bars, sprayed or hand‑applied without brushes at least one hour prior to concrete placement, and the lubricated end must be protected by a tube providing a minimum one‑inch clearance [2]. Installation requires drilling holes no larger than 1/8 in beyond the bar diameter, with an allowable offset of up to ±1 in to avoid existing reinforcement, and the coated dowel is embedded using an approved anchoring material that reaches initial set within five minutes; the material is injected before insertion, the bar rotated three‑to‑five times to purge air, excess concrete troweled flush, and voids around the perimeter kept under 0.50 in on verification cores [2]. Anchors and baskets must hold the dowel in position with vertical or horizontal movement limited to ¼ in during concrete placement, and a daily quality‑control plan for anchoring must be demonstrated to the Representative [2]. Accessibility and safety of installation equipment are addressed by requiring a power‑driven self‑propelled saw capable of making two parallel cuts per slot, while jackhammers are limited to 30 lb (or 15 lb if damage occurs) to protect workers and surrounding pavement [2]. Slot cleaning shall be completed within twenty‑four hours before concrete placement and the final blast performed with at least 100 psi oil‑free air to ensure a clean joint surface [2]. Documentation requirements include PTM No. 642 test reports, shop drawings and an LTU sample submitted at least forty‑five days prior to paving; only one dowel type may be used unless an exception is granted, and bars must not remain on site longer than thirty days unless protected by opaque covering [2]. Finally, all dowel bars must appear uniform, free of corrosion, uncoated spots, acid, flux or black stains, and any bar damaged during placement must be inspected and replaced before concrete placement to preserve the structural integrity and ride quality for users [2][1].

Verification and expected performance

The completed dowel‑bar system is verified through a sequence of checks that confirm compliance with the design documents, constructability requirements, performance criteria, and overall project consistency. First, the bars are inspected to ensure they have been cast exactly as shown on the approved shop drawings, with each bar square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface and positioned within a plus‑or‑minus one‑eighth inch tolerance of the indicated locations. The embedded portion must be coated with bond‑breaker material, and all required slots and grout ports must be present at the correct sizes. After placement, any dowel or tie bar that remains exposed beyond the allowed setting period is removed and replaced before adjacent slabs are set, and vertical differentials across joints are confirmed to stay within a quarter‑inch limit. The free ends of each bar are examined for true circular shape without burrs, and a fusion‑bonded epoxy Type B coating of at least eight mils non‑abraded thickness is applied; holiday detection must not exceed two per linear foot. Any rust or surface damage is repaired within the allowable limits.

Dimensional tolerances are then checked: tilt or skew at either end may not exceed a quarter‑inch relative to the midpoint, longitudinal and horizontal translation from the intended joint location may not exceed one inch, and vertical depth must match the drawings. Bond‑breaker lubricant for Type B bars is applied at least one hour before concrete placement, chairs provide a minimum half‑inch clearance, expansion caps are set with the required quarter‑inch gap, and slots are cleaned of all loose material within twenty‑four hours and again within thirty minutes prior to concrete placement, remaining free of debris. The fastening method for anchoring dowel baskets is demonstrated to the Representative before each pour; anchors must hold the assemblies so that movement does not exceed a quarter‑inch in any direction during placement.

Performance compliance is documented by submitting full PTM No. 642 test reports, shop drawings, and an LTU sample containing the required number of bars (six for non‑Bulletin 15 or three for Bulletin 15) with bond‑breaker lubricant pre‑applied at least forty‑five days before any concrete placement. The Representative must approve all documentation before paving can commence. Structural adequacy testing must show a maximum LVDT differential deflection of no more than 7.5 mils at one million load cycles and an increase of no more than 2.5 mils at ten million cycles for both approach and leave sides, while bond‑breaker criteria per AASHTO M254 are satisfied and the required certification is obtained. Epoxy coating of all reinforcement steel, dowel bars, and longitudinal joint ties must follow ASTM A775 as specified, and grout mix designs are verified by a trial batch at least twenty‑eight days before installation with independent laboratory results confirming a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is allowed. All test results, mix designs, and compliance documentation are submitted to the Representative for final acceptance. Any bar that fails any of these checks—whether dimensional, coating, anchoring, or performance—is classified as defective and must be removed and replaced at no cost to the Department, ensuring that the installed dowel‑bar system meets every contractual requirement.
