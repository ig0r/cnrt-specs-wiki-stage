Material purpose and applicability

Asphalt Concrete (HMA) is the hot‑mix, plant‑mixed asphalt‑bound mixture that forms the wearing surface of a pavement; any pavement possessing such a wearing layer is classified as an asphalt concrete surface [1][2]. It consists of selected asphalt material combined with fine aggregate, coarse aggregate, filler when required, and an asphalt binder that meets the Standard Specification for Performance‑Graded Asphalt Binder, AASHTO M 332; the aggregates are sized, uniformly graded and proportioned in accordance with a joint mix formula or volumetric mix design, and the binder provides cohesion to bind the mineral particles into a durable, smooth riding surface [2]. This wearing layer constitutes the topmost part of the pavement system and is evaluated for design items such as drainage, base repair, leveling courses, binder‑course thickness, surface friction, and shoulder condition [1]. In rehabilitation contexts the asphalt concrete serves as the flexible overlay material applied to existing pavements to correct structural deficiencies, surface deterioration, or poor rideability, thereby providing a new riding surface and supplying the additional structural strength required by design [1]. When used in an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course, the hot‑mix asphalt concrete is placed in a single lift over a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane; this thin HMA layer seals the pavement, reducing oxidation and weathering, which helps the pavement remain resilient to fatigue and low‑temperature cracking and may also correct surface distresses such as raveling and moderate rutting, while simultaneously acting as the topmost layer that shields underlying structures [1][2]. Within a hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt treatment the mixture placed on the existing pavement serves as a relatively short‑term interim wearing surface that can remediate rutting and other surface distress, improve the pavement’s appearance and overall performance, and thereby prolong service life until a permanent surface course is applied [1]. In unbonded Portland cement concrete overlay designs the asphalt concrete is used as an interlayer separating the existing concrete pavement from the new concrete overlay, typically specified as a nominal depth of 1 inch of a Superpave Wearing Course with a 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix [1]. For pavements covered by the Section 496 sixty‑month warranty, the asphalt concrete is selected and proportioned to satisfy the warranty performance criteria, employing aggregate having the specified SRL designation [2]. During construction the mixture is spread, screeded and compacted across the full lane width—or as much lane width as practical—to create a finished surface that carries traffic, provides a smooth riding plane, protects the pavement edges, and, when placed adjacent to flush curbs, gutters or other abutting structures, is positioned uniformly higher so that after compaction the surface sits slightly above the edge [2].

In an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course, a hot‑mix asphalt concrete is applied in one lift over a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane, with the final thickness being 1/2 inch for Type A or 3/4 inch for Types B and C. The pavement policy manual notes that there are no traffic restrictions for this paver‑placed surface treatment. For the ultra‑thin bonded wearing‑course layer of flexible pavements, the specifications require a plant‑mixed asphalt concrete containing fine aggregate, coarse aggregate, filler and asphalt binder that meets the JMF and table requirements. Fly ash may be used as filler only when the design traffic is less than 3 million ESALs; if the design traffic is greater than or equal to 3 million ESALs another filler or material must be selected. Crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder dense‑graded mixtures are recommended for the upper layers of the pavement, specifically wearing and binder courses that would otherwise employ a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder. These CRMAB mixtures are appropriate for high‑volume Interstate‑type facilities that exceed more than 25,000 ADT, more than 10 million ESALs over a 20‑year design life, and cover areas larger than 50,000 square yards. The premium CRMAB mix should be avoided in lower layers, in shoulders unless the lane and shoulder are paved together, on temporary lanes or crossovers, and wherever significant handwork or raking is required because of its stiffness; in those situations a conventional Superpave mixture or another material is preferred. Cost‑effectiveness considerations also favor selecting a standard Superpave mix rather than the more expensive CRMAB when the premium performance is not justified. [1][2]

Composition and characteristics

Hot‑mix asphalt concrete (HMA) is a composite pavement material whose basic constituents are mineral aggregate—both fine and coarse—obtained from approved suppliers, and an asphalt binder that must meet performance‑graded specifications evaluated by the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery test (AASHTO M 332) [2]. The aggregate may be virgin or may include reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP), reclaimed asphalt material (RAM) or recycled asphalt shingles (RAS); when RAP is used, at least 5 % of cold‑milled or crushed asphalt mixture shall be incorporated, and RAS may be added up to 5 % by weight provided the shredded material passes a 9.5 mm sieve [2]. A mineral filler system may be employed, but fly ash is excluded when the design traffic equals or exceeds three million Equivalent Single Axle Loads (ESALs) [2]. The binder, aggregates and any filler are stored separately in dedicated stockpiles, tanks or silos before blending, and additives or modifiers such as warm‑mix technologies, anti‑strip agents, stabilizing fibers or cellulose fibers may be introduced according to the manufacturer’s specifications; when a WMA stabilizing agent is used the minimum VMA may be reduced to 16.5 % [2].

The proportions of these constituents are established by a volumetric mix design prepared with the Superpave Gyratory Compactor and recorded in a Job Mix Formula that specifies percent by weight, bulk specific gravity, absorption, gradation on an FHWA 0.45 power‑type chart, sand equivalency, angularity and the volumetric indices VMA, Voids in Total Mixture and Voids Filled with Asphalt [2]. Aggregate gradation is limited by the percent passing the No. 200 (75 µm) sieve and must satisfy the primary control‑sieve requirement; for ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses additional limits apply to the percent passing the No. 8 (2.36 mm) and ¼‑inch (6.3 mm) sieves [2]. The asphalt content is expressed as a percentage by weight, and the mixture must achieve at least 95 % particle coating; draindown testing according to AASHTO T 305 with a one‑hour reading shall be passed [2]. Physical characteristics of the produced mix are controlled through several limits: apparent moisture may not exceed 0.5 %, and both aggregates and the completed mixture shall be free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture as specified in Section 413.2(e)1.d.1 [2]; air voids, bulk specific gravity and VMA are verified on compacted specimens using standardized grading, gyration and density tests (AASHTO T209, T312) [2]. When anti‑strip additives are required for moisture susceptibility they shall be blended at not less than 0.25 % of the asphalt by weight or hydrated lime may be used; if stabilizer fibers are needed they shall be dosed between 0.2 % and 0.4 % of the total mix [2].

Placement requirements dictate that the hot mix be delivered and placed at the laying temperatures prescribed in Table A, must be free of cold lumps, and the finished product shall be shipped within two hours of plant mixing to preserve its hot‑mix condition [2]. Courses whose compacted depth exceeds six inches shall be constructed in two or more layers of approximately equal depth, with no layer less than three inches or more than six inches; a safety edge formed from the same material shall have a depth of at least 1.5 inches but not exceed five inches total [2]. Macro‑texture limits state that a difference greater than 0.024 inch between average texture depths measured in segregated versus non‑segregated areas is unacceptable, and an average texture depth of 0.006 inch or less indicates flushing; both conditions are classified as defective pavement [2].

Specialized mix types follow additional constituent and composition rules. Pervious asphalt pavement systems require a 9.5 mm wearing course produced from uniformly graded mineral aggregate combined with an asphalt cement binder of class PG 64H‑22 or PG 64E‑22, while a 19.0 mm binder course uses PG 64S‑22; polymer additives or ground tire rubber may be employed to achieve the required grade and RAP content shall not exceed ten percent of the mixture [2]. Ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses incorporate fine aggregate supplied as manufactured sand, coarse aggregate from an approved source and a filler that must not be fly ash when design traffic is three million ESALs or greater; acceptance is based on measured asphalt content by weight and gradation limits for the 75 µm, 2.36 mm and 6.3 mm sieves as defined in Table E [2]. In addition, the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course consists of a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane that is immediately overlaid with an ultra‑thin layer of hot‑mix asphalt concrete placed in a single lift; the HMA layer may be produced with one of three aggregate gradations—Type A, B or C—with nominal maximum aggregate sizes of 6.3 mm, 9.5 mm and 12.5 mm respectively, and its final thickness is 1 to 1.5 times the diameter of the coarsest aggregate, resulting in a finished surface treatment that is ½ inch thick for Type A and ¾ inch thick for Types B and C [1].

Cold‑recycled asphalt concrete mixtures are composed of reclaimed asphalt pavement combined with virgin coarse aggregate (such as No. 8, 57, or 67) and an emulsified asphalt binder that is often polymer‑modified to boost early strength; the mix exhibits a higher void content than hot‑mix HMA, requiring a wearing surface, and its structural strength is lower when first constructed but increases as the mixture cures over several weeks, so a minimum of one week of curing under low‑humidity high‑temperature conditions is required before placing the wearing course; design specifications limit the compacted thickness to a minimum of 3 inches and a maximum of 5 inches [1].

The gap‑graded wearing course that uses asphalt rubber employs an asphalt rubber binder blended with a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder by the wet process before mixing with mineral aggregates, may contain reclaimed asphalt pavement or reclaimed asphalt shingle material, and must be produced using the wet process; its layer thickness should not exceed 2½ inches [1].

The dense‑graded course that uses a crumb‑rubber modified binder consists of a dense‑graded mineral aggregate skeleton bound together by a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 asphalt binder into which crumb rubber is blended in the wet process, may also incorporate reclaimed asphalt pavement or reclaimed asphalt shingle material as specified, and because the rubber‑enhanced binder makes the mix relatively stiff it should not be used on projects that require significant handwork or raking; its layer thickness is limited to no more than 2½ inches [1].

FB‑1 wearing and binder courses are cold mixes that combine mineral aggregate with asphalt cement, typically a softer binder than PG 64S‑22, giving the mix a high void content and resulting in a highly flexible surface [1]; FB‑2 mixes use either PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement and must be produced at a stationary plant, so they are hot‑mixed but retain the same aggregate‑asphalt composition, and because of the high void content a structural coefficient of 0.20 must be used when FB courses are treated as overlays in a deflection‑based design [1].

Seal‑coat applications of HMA use coarse aggregate that has been pre‑coated with an asphalt binder; the residual binder content shall be 0.6 % to 1.2 % by weight of the mix when asphalt cement is employed or 0.4 % to 1.0 % when other asphalt materials are used, and the pre‑coating must produce a free‑flowing aggregate that forms a thin, brownish translucent film covering at least 90 % of the visible surface area; after ten minutes of dry sieving the percent passing on a 75 µm sieve shall not exceed 0.5 %; production temperatures for seal‑coat mixes shall not exceed 350 °F, and when cut‑back asphalts are used the aggregate temperature is limited to 150 °F [2].

Variations in the proportion of asphalt binder and aggregate gradation are reflected in the Percent Within Limits (PWL) metric, whose standard‑deviation component directly influences the graduated pay factors applied to asphalt content, sieve‑passing percentages, and pavement in‑place density; contractors must achieve consistent density results near the middle of the target field density range (e.g., 92.0 % to 98.5 % for Standard and RPS construction of Wearing Courses) to maximize payments, and mixes that deviate toward lower densities can trigger reduced pay factors when PWL values fall at or near the bottom of the density range [1]. In the measurement and payment provisions, changes in asphalt content or aggregate gradation that alter the percent passing the No. 200 sieve, the percent passing the No. 8 sieve, or the in‑place density directly affect the Percent Within Limits values used for payment; when PWL is ≥ 90 the pay factor for a parameter follows PF_X = 100 + 0.4(PWLX‑90), and when PWL is between 50 and 90 the factor follows PF_X = 70 + 0.75(PWLX‑50); if the payment factors for asphalt content, the No. 200 sieve, and the No. 8 sieve are all 85 % or if a density core yields a PWL less than 50, the pavement is considered defective [2]. FB‑1 mixes are cold‑placed, have a high void content and use asphalt that is softer than PG 64S‑22, making them highly flexible and suitable for low‑volume roads with existing flexible structures; switching to the FB‑2 specification requires hot mixing at a stationary plant and permits stiffer binders such as PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22, which increases mix stiffness and mandates hot placement, and designers must apply a structural coefficient of 0.20 for FB courses in deflection‑based overlay designs, reflecting their lower load‑bearing capacity compared with Superpave materials; the allowable traffic level changes from independent use on roadways having an ADT ≤ 1,500 to requiring a combined binder and wearing course when ADT exceeds 1,500 [1]. The Ultra‑thin Bonded Wearing Course is offered in three nominal maximum aggregate sizes—6.3 mm for Type A, 9.5 mm for Type B, and 12.5 mm for Type C—and the selected aggregate size determines the lift thickness because the final layer is placed at 1 to 1.5 times the diameter of the coarsest aggregate, yielding a ½‑inch surface for Type A and a ¾‑inch surface for Types B and C; the resulting UTWC seals the pavement, reducing oxidation and weathering, which helps maintain resilience to fatigue and low‑temperature cracking while also allowing correction of surface distresses such as raveling and moderate rutting, and an expected service life of 8 to 10 years [1]. Using a 6.3 mm nominal maximum aggregate size (Type A) yields the lightest‑duty mix with a fine surface texture that performs well in urban and suburban settings with light truck traffic but is not recommended for highways that are borderline candidates for preventive maintenance; raising the nominal maximum to 9.5 mm (Type B) produces a mix durable enough for moderate‑to‑heavy traffic and truck traffic on moderate‑speed highways, and selecting the 12.5 mm size (Type C) creates the most heavy‑duty mix suitable for any application, especially high‑speed, high‑traffic situations where moderate rutting is expected [1]. Within the Superpave system, when the estimated ESAL range exceeds 10 million, a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder is specified for wearing courses to avoid rutting, whereas lower traffic volumes (0.3 to < 3.0 million ESALs) may permit less severe binders and even allow an ESAL range of < 0.3 million for wearing and binder layers; incorporating aramid fibers can prevent or delay crack initiation and propagation, but the benefit depends heavily on how well the fibers are distributed in the mix [1]. All ARBC materials are limited to designs with less than 0.3 million ESALs, have design air voids of 2.5 % and a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25 mm, providing the bottom of the pavement with the highest tensile strength and improving resistance to bottom‑up cracking and moisture damage [1]. A 6.3 mm thin asphalt overlay can be placed in thin lifts, provides a longer service life and better ride quality than emulsified alternatives, but it must use PG 64E‑22 binder for all Standard Superpave wearing course applications; the 9.5 mm Fine Grade mix cannot use that binder and is intended for thinner applications, and the choice of aggregate size (25 mm, 19 mm, 12.5 mm or 9.5 mm) determines the minimum depth needed to achieve the required cross‑section [1]. When an interlayer is incorporated, its composition must be compatible with the asphalt matrix and remain thermally stable and physically durable so that it does not lose properties over time, ensuring a strong internal bond and resistance to moisture intrusion; a too‑heavy tack coat may cause slippage at higher temperatures, while asphalt that is too cold or applied at an insufficient rate can fail to adhere properly, and if the existing surface has been milled rough a leveling course may be required before the interlayer is placed; on projects with grades steeper than 5 % the mix should be proportioned to improve compaction and shear‑rut stability [1]. Cold‑recycled mixtures start with lower structural strength that only reaches acceptable levels after a minimum of one week of curing under low humidity high temperature, so composition and processing time directly control early load capacity; unlike hot asphalt mixes these mixes are less resistant to abrasion by traffic and therefore require a wearing surface, with the compacted depth limited to a minimum of 3 inches and a maximum of 5 inches; if reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) is a fine‑graded or wearing‑course material virgin coarse aggregate (No. 8, 57, or 67) must be incorporated to meet design specifications, showing how source material influences mix stability; polymer‑modified emulsions provide improved initial strength of the recycled mix and are recommended where traffic volumes exceed 3,000 two‑lane AADT, high truck percentages, early or late season recycling, or quick stabilities are desired [1]. In full‑depth reclaimed sections the introduction of calcium or magnesium chloride into the HMA mix increases strength because the salts act as particle cementing agents and also lower the freezing temperature of the material, thereby improving resistance to freeze‑thaw damage; the practice calls for a minimum 35 % solution applied at a rate of 0.1–0.15 gal per square yard for each inch of reclaimed depth, and adding emulsified asphalt as a stabilizer similarly modifies the composition, providing better protection against water‑related distress and greater fatigue resistance under traffic loading [1]. When an asphalt‑rubber binder is used in a gap‑graded wearing course the mixture becomes stiffer, which means it should not be employed on projects that require significant handwork or raking; this stiffness results from the required wet process in which the crumb rubber modifier (CRM) is blended with the asphalt binder before aggregate addition, and if a warm‑mix additive is incorporated a chemical additive must be used and foaming is not permitted, altering workability and temperature sensitivity while the mix may contain reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) or reclaimed asphalt shingle (RAS) material as specified in the SSP without changing its premium classification [1]. For dense‑graded courses that use a crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder (CRMAB) the wet process again modifies the binder properties, leading to increased mix stiffness and higher cost relative to conventional Superpave mixtures; consequently CRMAB is recommended for upper pavement layers such as wearing or binder courses where its superior performance can be utilized, but it should not be placed in lower layers, shoulders (unless lane and shoulder are paved together), or projects requiring significant handwork because of the mixture’s stiffness, and the layer thickness for CRMAB should not exceed 2 ½ inches; when warm‑mix asphalt is employed a chemical warm‑mix additive must be used while foaming remains prohibited, and the mix may also contain RAP or RAS material as specified in the SSP [1]. In construction, variations in the mix’s composition—particularly asphalt content and aggregate gradation measured by the percent passing the No. 200 sieve—directly influence whether a pavement is accepted because the mixture must stay within the single‑sample or multiple‑sample tolerances; repeated out‑of‑tolerance results trigger a production stop until the cause is corrected [2]. Non‑uniform distribution of coarse and fine aggregates, a composition or processing issue, can produce pattern segregation that becomes defective when the difference in average pavement texture depth between segregated and sound areas exceeds 0.024 inch, and excess asphalt, a proportioning problem, leads to flushing that is defined as a surface macrotexture depth less than or equal to 0.006 inch and is also deemed defective [2]. Variations in aggregate moisture, binder content, and reclaimed material proportions are tightly controlled because they directly influence the mixture’s volumetric properties; if the water absorption of a coarse aggregate determined according to AASHTO T 85 exceeds 2.0 % the mix must be sampled according to PTM No. 1 and its apparent moisture content must be kept at or below 0.5 %, otherwise it is non‑conforming [2]. If the measured specific gravity of the mixture changes by 0.030 or more from the previous test or from the Job Mix Formula, immediate notification is required [2]. The total amount of RAM and RAP may not exceed 15 % of the mixture by mass unless SRL documentation is provided; any use of WMA additives or anti‑strip agents must follow a documented QC plan to ensure that the blend does not alter the prescribed performance limits [2]. The proportioning system must meter stabilizer fibers within ±10 % of the target weight, and deviations beyond this tolerance can cause uneven fiber distribution, increasing draindown and leading to rejection of stored material that does not match the quality of freshly discharged mix [2]. When a change in source or grade of the asphalt material occurs, a new mix design is required; such changes can alter air voids, VMA and moisture resistance, so moisture susceptibility analysis must be performed and draindown testing repeated [2]. When a WMA stabilizing additive is used the specification may allow the minimum VMA to be lowered to 16.5 %, but the mixture still must achieve at least 95.0 % coated particles and meet all tolerances; failure of single‑sample or multiple‑sample tests triggers corrective actions before production can continue [2]. Mixture must be produced from a fully automated plant listed in Bulletin 41, and both aggregates and the completed mixture must be free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture; any change in source or composition of aggregates can introduce contaminants that jeopardize compliance with asphalt content, gradation, density and air‑void limits [2]. In slab‑stabilization grout the asphalt is heated to a temperature of 380 °F to 450 °F; any batch heated to 500 °F or more will be rejected [2].

Key material properties

The high void content of FB surface courses contributes to flexibility, while a design air‑void content of 2.5 % is specified for Asphalt Rich Base Course mixes to provide the greatest tensile strength at the bottom of the pavement and improve resistance to bottom‑up cracking and moisture damage [1]. The nominal maximum aggregate size governs the duty level of ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, with a 6.3 mm mix (Type A) yielding the lightest‑duty surface, a 9.5 mm mix (Type B) providing durability for moderate to heavy traffic, and a 12.5 mm mix (Type C) being the most heavy‑duty option recommended for high‑speed, high‑traffic situations and for applications with moderate rutting [1]. Superpave asphalt concrete performance hinges on its ability to resist permanent deformation (rutting) and low‑temperature thermal cracking, and hot‑in‑place recycled mixtures are required to exhibit rutting severity of less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth of less than 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and raveling or weathering severity that does not exceed a medium rating; minor corrugation, shoving, and slippage are acceptable provided their average depth is less than 1½ inches, and the overlay thickness must be at least 1½ inches greater than the milling depth to ensure adequate support [1]. Ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses are placed in a single lift with a final thickness equal to 1 to 1.5 times the diameter of the coarsest aggregate, resulting in a finished thickness of ½ inch for Type A and ¾ inch for Types B and C, while standard Superpave 6.3 mm wearing courses require a PG 64E‑22 binder to supply the necessary mechanical strength and durability [1]. The interlayer material must be chemically compatible with the asphalt to form a strong internal adhesive or mechanical bond, must be thermally stable and physically durable so its properties do not break down during paving or over time, and proper application temperature is required for waterproofing performance and to prevent debonding; excessive tack coat thickness may cause slippage at higher temperatures and adhesion failure can occur if the asphalt is too cold [1]. On interlayer projects where grades are steeper than 5 % the asphalt mixes should be proportioned to help with compaction and shear‑rut stability [1]. Cold‑recycled base courses attain adequate structural strength only after a minimum of one week of curing under favorable weather conditions (low humidity–high temperature); because these mixes have higher void content they are less resistant to abrasion by traffic and therefore require a compacted depth between 3 inches and 5 inches [1]. Polymer‑modified emulsions are used in cold‑recycled mixes to enhance the initial chemical binding and thus improve early strength, while calcium chloride and magnesium chloride additives provide particle cementing and lower the freezing temperature of the reclaimed material, improving resistance to freeze‑thaw cycles [1]. Aramid fibers incorporated into the mix provide strong tensile and heat‑resistance properties that can further delay crack initiation and propagation [1]. Asphalt stabilization offers several advantages including improved resistance to water‑related damage and enhanced fatigue resistance to loading [1]. The binder for plant‑mixed HMA must meet a specified Performance‑Grade such as PG 64S‑22, PG 64V‑22, PG 64E‑22, PG 58S‑28 or PG 58V‑28 in accordance with AASHTO M 332 and be evaluated with the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery test; thin‑asphalt overlays are governed primarily by the binder’s specified performance grade and its visco‑elastic response as measured by the MSCR test, so any warm‑mix additive or modifier blended at the refinery or plant must still enable the binder to meet that same performance grade [2]. Moisture susceptibility is addressed by requiring at least 95.0 % of particles to be coated to ensure binder adhesion, a minimum anti‑strip additive or hydrated lime dosage of not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt to achieve at least 90 % coating, and draindown limits that satisfy AASHTO T 305; when a warm‑mix additive is used the minimum VMA may be lowered to 16.5 % [2]. Placement of HMA mixtures is prohibited when the air or surface temperature is 40 °F or lower or when surfaces are wet, and the contractor must maintain a proper and uniform placement temperature throughout paving; if ambient temperatures fall below 50 °F between October 1 and April 30, insulation, double‑walled or heated trucks shall be used to preserve mix heat, while the mix must be placed at the laying temperatures listed in Table A for the type and class of material used and the asphalt production temperature range for normal paving and any special conditions shall be observed [2]. The apparent moisture content of the mix must not exceed 0.5 %, coarse‑aggregate water absorption may not exceed 3.0 % for Type A and 3.5 % for Type B, natural coarse aggregates with absorption less than 2 % are considered freeze‑thaw resistant, and if absorption exceeds 2 % a sodium‑sulfate loss of less than 16 % or an AASHTO T 103 loss after 25 cycles not exceeding 7.0 % is required [2]. Macrotexture shall remain uniform because a texture‑depth difference greater than 0.024 inch between segregated and non‑segregated zones is unacceptable, while an average texture depth of 0.006 inch or less indicates flushing and is deemed defective; density acceptance limits must be achieved so that roller marks are eliminated, and density verification shall be performed by pavement cores or, where cores are not feasible, by an optimum‑rolling pattern [2]. Uncompacted void content, sand equivalent value, maximum abrasion percent, allowable percentages of thin or elongated pieces, and a minimum percentage of fractured faces must conform to the limits in AASHTO M 323 Table 6, and steel‑slag stockpiles must have an expansion limit of less than 0.50 % while the maximum glassy particle content in asphalt wearing courses shall not exceed 10 % [2]. Recycled asphalt pavement may be used at least 5 % (up to a maximum of 10 %) of the mixture, and when a stabilizer is required cellulose fiber content shall be between 0.2 % and 0.4 % by weight of the total mix; fly ash is prohibited when design traffic is greater than or equal to 3 million ESALs [2]. The theoretical maximum specific gravity (Gmm) shall be used to verify that the compacted mix attains the required density, and bulk specific gravities of aggregate and binder, Voids in Mineral Aggregate (VMA), Voids in Total Mixture (VTM) and Voids Filled with Asphalt (VFA) shall be expressed as percentages in accordance with the mix design; when a warm‑mix additive is employed the minimum VMA may be reduced to 16.5 % [2]. Moisture sensitivity shall be evaluated through tensile strength data, Tensile Strength Ratio or Hamburg Wheel Tracking results, and combined aggregate consensus properties such as sand equivalency, fine and coarse aggregate angularity, and flat‑and‑elongated pieces must conform to the limits specified in the job mix formula [2]. For AR‑GG wearing courses and crumb‑rubber‑modified dense‑graded courses the mixture stiffness arising from a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder blended with crumb rubber makes the mix unsuitable for projects requiring significant handwork or raking, and the layer thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches; when warm‑mix asphalt is employed a chemical warm‑mix additive should be utilized (foaming is not allowed) [1].

Type A mixes are considered the lightest duty mix suitable for urban and suburban applications with light truck traffic but not recommended for highways that are borderline candidates for preventive maintenance; Type B mixes are durable enough to handle moderate to heavy traffic on highways with moderate speeds and may also be used in lighter‑duty situations if a slightly thicker lift is desired or more surface distress is present; Type C mixes are the most heavy‑duty and are recommended for high‑speed, high‑traffic applications and for conditions with moderate rutting [1]. Cold‑recycled asphalt mixtures are characterized by a structural strength that is lower when first placed and increases as the mixture cures over several weeks, requiring a minimum one‑week curing period under low humidity and high temperature before a wearing course can be applied; they are less resistant to abrasion than hot‑mix asphalt, have higher void content making a wearing surface imperative, must be compacted to a depth between 3 inches and 5 inches, and are limited to projects that do not exceed heavy truck traffic of 200 or more daily ESALs, with polymer‑modified emulsions recommended when traffic volumes exceed 3,000 two‑lane AADT [1]. Hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt mixtures are characterized by quantitative distress limits: rutting severity should be less than 1½ inch, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth should be less than 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, raveling or weathering should not exceed the medium rating, minor corrugation, shoving, and slippage are acceptable if their average depth is less than 1½ inches, bleeding may be present only in isolated areas, and the asphalt pavement thickness must be at least 1½ inches greater than the milling depth; typical layer thickness for HIR applications is 1½ inches (range 1 to 2 inches) [1]. In FB surface courses the physical mechanism of high void content creates an open structure while the chemical softness of binders that are softer than PG 64S‑22 keeps the mix highly flexible, and mechanically this flexibility is expressed by a low structural coefficient of 0.20 together with the requirement for hot mixing when PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 binders are used [1]. Full‑depth reclamation relies on chemical cementation from calcium or magnesium chloride additives that bond particles and increase strength, a physical freezing‑point depression that improves resistance to freeze‑thaw cycles, and emulsified asphalt that chemically creates a water‑resistant film and mechanically enhances fatigue resistance under traffic loads [1]. Asphalt rubber gap‑graded wearing courses are governed by their physical stiffness, which limits handwork or raking and caps layer thickness at not more than 2½ inches; chemically the mixture must be produced using the wet process where the CRM is blended with the binder before aggregate addition, and when warm‑mix asphalt is used a chemical warm‑mix additive is required while foaming is prohibited; mechanically suitability is tied to traffic loading criteria of greater than 25 000 ADT, more than 10 million ESALs over a 20‑year design life, and an area exceeding 50 000 square yards [1]. For plant‑mixed hot‑mix asphalt concrete the behavior is governed primarily by thermal, compositional and mechanical mechanisms; the mix must be delivered and placed at the laying temperatures specified in Table A for the type and class of material used, and any material below the minimum temperature or with a deficiency or excess of asphalt content may not be added to the mat, thereby controlling viscosity and binder proportion [2]. Rollers shall be equipped with a watering or soapy system that prevents material from sticking to the rollers, pneumatic‑tire rollers are prohibited, and vibratory rollers may be used only if it can be demonstrated that no breaking of aggregate or flushing of asphalt binder results, with monitoring required to stop vibration when such damage occurs [2]. Immediate compaction with steel‑wheeled rollers must achieve the density‑acceptance criteria and eliminate all roller marks while avoiding displacement, shoving, cracking, aggregate breakage or flushing of the binder; adjustment of roller speed, pattern, size and, where approved, amplitude and frequency is required to meet these mechanical requirements, and if the average texture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 inch the pavement is considered flushed and defective, indicating that surface flushing also influences performance [2]. In stone‑matrix asphalt the behavior of hot‑mix asphalt concrete is governed by the rheological properties of its binder that must satisfy the Performance‑Graded Asphalt Binder criteria as demonstrated by the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery test; when a Warm Mix Asphalt technology additive or modifier is incorporated it must be approved, listed in Bulletin 15, used at a documented dosage, and the final blend must still meet the specified performance‑grade requirements for the asphalt binder [2]. The mixture composition is further controlled chemically by maintaining the specified asphalt content and the aggregate gradation expressed as the percent passing the 75 µm sieve so that binder‑aggregate interaction remains within tolerances, and physically by managing moisture on steel‑wheel rollers—keeping them moist and clean without excess water—to prevent the mixture from adhering to the wheels [2]. In slab‑stabilization work the hot‑asphalt grout behavior is governed by its temperature, injection pressure and resulting slab lift; the asphalt shall be heated to a temperature of 380 °F to 450 °F before pumping and any material heated to 500 °F or more shall be rejected, it shall then be pumped at a pressure of 25 psi to 45 psi providing the mechanical force that drives the grout beneath the pavement, and upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed [2]. The physical characteristics of coarse aggregate control performance through hardness, durability, particle shape, absorption capacity, abrasion loss and resistance to freeze‑thaw cycling; absorption shall not exceed 3.0 % for Type A use (or 3.5 % for Type B), aggregates with absorption greater than 2 % are accepted only if their sodium‑sulfate soundness is less than 16 % or their freeze‑thaw loss after 25 cycles does not exceed 7.0 %, steel slag stockpiles must exhibit total expansion below 0.50 % after curing, and aggregates shall be free from reactive minerals such as chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide [2]. In pervious HMA the behavior is governed primarily by binder chemistry and the interaction between binder, additives and aggregate structure; PG‑64 binders may be modified with polymer additives or ground tire rubber to achieve required temperature performance, while moisture resistance relies on achieving at least 90 % coating of aggregates and incorporating an anti‑strip additive not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt (hydrated lime may serve a similar role); mechanical stability is provided by cellulose fibers used as a pervious asphalt stabilizer, whose dosage must remain within 0.2 % to 0.4 % by weight of the total mix, and the overall mixture design limits reclaimed asphalt pavement to a maximum of 10 % of the blend [2]. In cold‑recycled asphalt base work behavior is governed primarily by how water and additives are introduced and mixed with reclaimed asphalt; adequate total liquids shall be maintained to ensure thorough blending, and the foamed‑asphalt system must accurately and uniformly inject water into hot binder so that the resulting foam together with RAP and water meets the mix design, equipment capable of automatically metering liquids with a variation of not more than + 2.0 % by weight shall be used, and for emulsified asphalt additive a positive‑displacement pump capable of accurately metering flow down to 4 gallons per minute shall be employed, thereby controlling the chemical foaming reaction and physical moisture content that affect compaction and final density [2].

Durability and deterioration

Durability of asphalt concrete pavements is compromised when water cannot be effectively removed from the surface or subsurface, allowing moisture to infiltrate and promote stripping of the binder from the aggregate as well as freeze‑thaw induced cracking and potholing [1]. Structural capacity deficiencies in underlying base areas also reduce durability because inadequate support leads to loss of subgrade support and accelerates pavement deterioration [1]. Traffic loads applied to intermediate layers or cold longitudinal paving joints contribute to poor performance by inducing fatigue and reflective cracking in the overlay [1]. Insufficient shoulder support allows edge stresses to develop, accelerating damage at pavement edges [1]. Edge breakdown is further aggravated when the exposed outer edges are left sharp or ragged after placement; immediate work on these edges is required to eliminate an unfinished appearance and reduce edge deterioration [2]. When surface friction is insufficient, safety is reduced and accelerated wear may occur [1]. Stripping of the asphalt and surface deformation such as rutting are identified as common damage processes that can be addressed by a thin‑bonded concrete overlay only when underlying support and drainage conditions are satisfactory; otherwise bonded overlays are discouraged because they may compromise HMA performance [1]. FB‑1 and FB‑2 wearing courses have a high void content that makes them vulnerable to damage, so a seal coat or surface treatment must be placed on the FB surface after a minimum of 3 months of warm‑weather traffic densification; if the underlying pavement is structurally sound and the FB surface remains satisfactory, the seal coat may be postponed for up to 3 years, whereas a FB Modified surface may forego a seal coat for at least 4 years [1]. The ultra‑thin bonded wearing course (UTWC) seals the surface, reducing oxidation and weathering that would otherwise diminish fatigue resistance and promote low‑temperature cracking in the underlying hot‑mix asphalt concrete; nevertheless surface distresses such as raveling and moderate rutting can still develop if those protective effects are insufficient [1]. Superpave mix design seeks to limit permanent deformation (rutting) and thermal cracking (low‑temperature cracking) by selecting an appropriate performance grade binder and ESAL range, and a polymer‑modified binder is required when the estimated 20‑year design life exceeds ten million ESALs in order to avoid rutting [1]. Incorporating aramid fibers into an asphalt mix may prevent or delay crack initiation and propagation, although the benefit depends heavily on uniform fiber distribution [1]. For base layers thicker than six inches, the Asphalt Rich Base Course is limited to designs with less than 0.3 million ESALs and a design air void of 2.5 % in order to achieve high tensile strength at the bottom of the pavement and promote better resistance to bottom‑up cracking and moisture damage during service life [1]. Reflective cracking commonly occurs when new asphalt overlays are placed over old, cracked roadways, and water intrusion into the structural section can cause stripping, localized failures, crack propagation, and deterioration caused by freeze‑thaw cycles; age oxidation may lead to alligator cracking across the surface, while other distresses such as rutting, bleeding, and raveling are not normally addressed with interlayers [1]. The durability of an HMA interlayer can be compromised by temperature‑related adhesion problems and improper tack coat application; if the asphalt is too cold or the application rate insufficient, bonding may fail, whereas an overly heavy tack coat can cause slippage at higher temperatures, and high ambient heat or overspray can produce fabric pickup and bleed‑through [1]. Proper waterproofing requires correct material temperature and adequate placement, a leveling course may be required after rough milling, and on grades steeper than 5 % the asphalt mixes should be proportioned to aid compaction and shear‑rut stability [1]. Cold‑recycled asphalt base mixtures exhibit lower structural strength at placement that improves only after at least one week of curing under favorable weather; their higher void content makes a wearing surface essential, they are less resistant to abrasion by traffic which limits use on high‑ADT roads, and fatigue cracks, transverse thermal cracks, reflection cracks and raveling are observed in such pavements [1]. Stabilization with calcium or magnesium chloride lowers the material’s freezing point, enhancing resistance to freeze‑thaw effects, while emulsified asphalt stabilization improves resistance to water‑related damage and provides better fatigue resistance under loading [1]. For hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) mixtures, rutting severity shall be less than 1 ½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth shall be less than 1 inch with an average crack width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, raveling or weathering severity shall not exceed the medium rating, the surface may be rough and pitted with loose particles, and minor corrugation, shoving or slippage may be present provided the average depth is under 1 ½ inches; the presence of rubber or fiber materials in the top 2 inches, mastic patches larger than 5 square feet, or extensive crack sealant or over‑banded rubberized joint seal renders a pavement unsuitable for HIR application [1]. Pattern segregation becomes a durability defect when the measured macro‑texture difference between segregated and non‑segregated zones exceeds 0.024 inch, at which point paving must be suspended and the affected lane removed [2]. Flushing is considered defective when the average macro‑texture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 inch, requiring replacement of the wearing course [2]. Inadequate compaction can produce displacement, shoving, cracking, aggregate breakage, or loose areas with excess or deficient binder; these conditions must be eliminated by compacting the mixture while it is in proper condition and adjusting roller speed, amplitude, frequency, pattern, and roller size [2]. Cores shall be taken within one day after placing the mixture and density for two consecutive lots or a total of three lots must meet the density payment factor percentage of 100; otherwise paving must stop [2]. Apparent moisture content in the mix shall not exceed 0.5 % and sampling is required when coarse‑aggregate water absorption, determined according to AASHTO T 85, exceeds 2.0 %; a compatible heat‑stable amine‑based liquid anti‑strip or alternate additive shall be used together with a moisture susceptibility analysis as specified in Section 413.2(e)1 [2]. Moisture‑susceptibility evaluation is also required whenever a warm‑mix asphalt (WMA) additive, modifier, or process is employed [2]. For pervious HMA mixes the chief durability concern is moisture‑induced stripping; an anti‑strip additive or hydrated lime shall be used when testing shows the mix does not meet a minimum of 90 % coating in ASTM D3625, and the specification limits recycled asphalt pavement to a maximum of 10 % of the mixture [2]. If moisture susceptibility cannot be ruled out, a pervious asphalt stabilizer may be required with the stabilizer dosage limited to between 0.2 % and 0.4 % by weight of the total mix [2]. Placement is prohibited on wet surfaces or when the air or surface temperature is 50 °F or lower; an exception for a 6.3 mm wearing course strictly enforces a minimum surface temperature of 50 °F and an air temperature of 40 °F, while the specifications also forbid paving when the air or surface temperature is 40 °F or lower [2]. The cold‑recycled asphalt base course may not be placed from September 1 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0, 2‑0, 3‑0, 4‑0, 9‑0, and 10‑0 and from October 1 to April 30 in all other districts; with written approval it may only be placed when no freezing temperatures have occurred for 24 hours and the ambient air temperature is at least 45 °F and rising [2]. A depth deficiency of one‑half inch or more relative to the indicated compacted depth renders the pavement defective [2]. Steel‑wheeled rollers weighing at least 10 tons shall be used; pneumatic wheeled rollers are prohibited, and vibratory rollers may be employed only if it can be demonstrated that no breaking of aggregate or flushing of asphalt binder results, with continuous monitoring of cores for aggregate breakage and immediate discontinuation of vibration when such damage is observed [2]. For Stone Matrix Asphalt, moisture‑induced stripping and binder draindown are primary durability concerns; a moisture susceptibility analysis shall be performed and any WMA additive may not reduce the mixture’s moisture resistance, draindown testing according to AASHTO T 305 with a 1‑hour reading verifies that binder loss stays within limits, and the mix must achieve at least 95.0 % coated particles [2]. When a WMA stabilizing agent is used, the minimum VMA may be lowered to 16.5 %, but the moisture resistance requirement remains unchanged [2]. Recycled Asphalt Pavement (RAP) and Recycled Asphalt Shingles (RAS) must be kept free of foreign material and segregation to prevent premature deterioration [2]. Fly ash shall not be used as filler when the design traffic is greater than or equal to three million Equivalent Single Axle Loads (ESALs) [2]. Coarse aggregate shall consist of hard, tough, durable, uncoated inert particles reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation, and deleterious substances; for all Type A use the maximum allowable absorption determined according to AASHTO T 85 is 3.0 % (and 3.5 % for Type B) [2]. Freeze‑thaw resistance criteria require a total expansion of less than 0.50 % for steel slag and an aggregate loss not exceeding 25 % [2]. Soundness testing with five cycles of sodium‑sulfate immersion and drying, along with abrasion limits and restrictions on glassy particles, metallic iron, clay lumps, and fines, is required to ensure proper coating by the binder [2]. The hot mixture shall be shipped within 2 hours of plant mixing to avoid loss of workability and quality degradation [2]. During asphalt grout stabilization the material must be heated only between 380 °F and 450 °F; any asphalt heated to 500 °F or more is rejected, the lift of pavement caused by injection may not exceed 0.05 inch, and after grouting the surface must remain closed to traffic for at least 30 minutes to allow the asphalt to set and avoid premature loading [2]. Within the 60‑month warranty framework durability of Asphalt Concrete is tied to its resistance to moisture damage and to maintaining proper volumetric void structure; the mix design shall include Moisture Sensitivity Data such as tensile strength ratio or Hamburg wheel‑tracking results, and the reported values for Voids in Mineral Aggregate (VMA), Voids in Total Mixture (VTM), and Voids Filled with Asphalt must stay within the specified tolerances [2]. An HMA seal coat depends on achieving at least 90 % coating of the visible aggregate surface; the pre‑coating shall contain between 0.6 % and 1.2 % residual asphalt binder (or 0.4 % to 1.0 % when other asphalt materials are used), mixing temperatures must not exceed 350 °F, and if cut‑back asphalts are employed aggregate temperatures must not exceed 150 °F; when damp aggregate is used with cut‑backs the pre‑coated material must be sufficiently cured before placement to prevent moisture pickup, and uncoated fines passing a No. 200 sieve may not exceed 0.5 % [2]. The specifications also require that asphalt content and the percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve remain within the single‑sample or multiple‑sample tolerances in Table D; any deviation causes the material to be classified as defective [2].

In the general guidance (section 2.1 GENERAL), susceptibility of asphalt concrete to deterioration rises when traffic loads are applied to intermediate pavement layers or cold longitudinal paving joints, while effective removal of surface and subsurface water, provision of adequate structural thickness in the binder course, repair of a sound base, enhancement of surface friction, and maintenance of shoulder structures that supply lateral support reduce deterioration. In the rehabilitation guidance (section 4.9 REHABILITATION), susceptibility increases when stripping of the asphalt, significant structural deterioration, or unstable support such as a weak subgrade are present, and when drainage problems are not addressed; providing adequate subdrainage and ensuring a stable, well‑drained subgrade in pavements that are in fair to excellent condition reduces the likelihood of further distress. In the flexible base surface course guidance (section 5.3 FB SURFACE COURSES), the high void content and use of binders softer than PG 64S‑22 make FB‑1 and FB‑2 mixes highly flexible but also require a seal coat or surface treatment to protect the surface; a minimum of three months of warm‑weather traffic densification is recommended before applying such a seal coat, although when the underlying pavement is sound the application can be postponed up to three years, and FB Modified surfaces may avoid a seal coat for at least four years. For roadways with an average daily traffic greater than 1 500 vehicles, a combination of binder and wearing courses must be used rather than an independent FB wearing course. In the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course guidance (section 5.5 ULTRA‑THIN BONDED WEARING COURSE), exposure to oxidation and weathering increases susceptibility to fatigue and low‑temperature cracking, whereas sealing the surface with a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane reduces oxidation and weathering, thereby lowering the risk of those distresses and also correcting raveling and moderate rutting; the applied thickness is ½ inch for Type A and ¾ inch for Types B and C. In selecting the aggregate size within the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course (section 5.5 ULTRA‑THIN BONDED WEARING COURSE > C), mixes with larger nominal maximum aggregate sizes are less susceptible to deterioration under demanding traffic conditions; a 6.3 mm mix (Type A) is the lightest‑duty blend and is not recommended for highways that are borderline candidates for preventive maintenance, a 9.5 mm mix (Type B) offers durability sufficient for moderate to heavy traffic and truck loads on moderate‑speed highways, and a 12.5 mm mix (Type C), the most heavy‑duty mix, is especially recommended for high‑speed, high‑traffic applications and for moderate rutting. In the Superpave paving course guidance (section 5.9 SUPERPAVE PAVING COURSES), high traffic loading expressed as ESALs greater than 10 million increases the risk of permanent deformation (rutting) and therefore a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder should be specified to mitigate that susceptibility; incorporating aramid fibers may reduce crack initiation and propagation when a uniform fiber distribution is achieved, and using an Asphalt Rich Base Course with less than 0.3 million ESAL mix designs and 2.5 % air voids provides the highest tensile strength at the pavement bottom, improving resistance to bottom‑up cracking and moisture damage. In the overlay guidance (section 5.12 OVERLAYS), susceptibility of HMA to deterioration is reduced when the overlay is placed on a structurally sound, well‑drained surface and provided with at least the minimum 1 ½‑inch thickness required for thin Superpave wearing courses; overlays thinner than 1 ½ inches or placement over poorly drained pavements increase the risk of cracking and moisture‑related failure, while adding an asphalt paving interlayer that acts as a moisture barrier and stress absorber further mitigates reflective cracking and water intrusion, and using the specified PG 64E‑22 binder for 6.3 mm wearing courses also helps limit deterioration. In the interlayer installation guidance (section 5.12 OVERLAYS > b), susceptibility of an HMA interlayer to deterioration is heightened when ambient or pavement temperatures are high and the tack coat or asphalt material is applied too heavily, which can cause slippage and bleed‑through; applying the material at the proper temperature and with a correct rate helps maintain waterproofing and prevents debonding, while cold asphalt during placement reduces adhesion and may require an increased application rate or a leveling course to restore bond strength; on grades steeper than 5 % the mix must be proportioned to improve compaction and shear‑rut stability, thereby reducing deterioration on steep slopes. In the cold‑recycled asphalt base course guidance (section 5.13 COLD RECYCLED ASPHALT BASE COURSES > C), structural strength is lower when first constructed and increases as the mixture cures over several weeks, so a minimum of one week of curing under low humidity high temperature is required before the wearing course is applied, making early‑age material more vulnerable; their higher void content also makes them less resistant to abrasion by traffic, therefore a wearing surface is imperative to limit deterioration. Projects carrying significantly heavy truck traffic (i.e., 200 or more daily ESALs) should not be selected for cold recycling and poor drainage conditions further increase risk; traffic volumes over 3 000 two‑lane AADT, a high percentage of truck traffic, and early or late season recycling can impair curing unless polymer‑modified emulsions are used to provide improved initial strength, and adding virgin coarse aggregate when the RAP is fine‑graded helps meet design requirements and improves performance. In full depth reclamation techniques (section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES), adding calcium or magnesium chloride at a minimum 35 % solution applied at 0.1–0.15 gal/yd² per inch of depth reclaimed can increase particle cementation, lower the freezing point, and generally improve resistance to freeze‑thaw deterioration; incorporating emulsified asphalt as a stabilizer enhances the mix’s ability to resist water‑related damage and provides better fatigue resistance under traffic loading, thereby reducing overall susceptibility to deterioration. Material interactions and compatibility

In Superpave wearing courses where the estimated traffic loading exceeds ten million equivalent single axle loads (ESALs), the district should specify a polymer‑modified binder of grade PG 64E‑22; the polymer component is intended to interact with the aggregate matrix so as to mitigate rutting. Optional fiber‑modified mixes in the same course may include aramid fibers because their high tensile strength and heat resistance may delay crack initiation and propagation, but this benefit is highly dependent on achieving a uniform distribution of the fibers throughout the binder‑aggregate skeleton, reflecting a mechanical interaction between the fibers and the surrounding mix. Asphalt Rich Base Course (ARBC) designs are limited to less than 0.3 million ESALs (50 gyrations) with design air voids of 2.5 %; this coordination of aggregate packing and binder content is intended to provide the greatest tensile strength at the bottom of the pavement section and to improve resistance to bottom‑up cracking and moisture‑damage. For overlay applications a sound adhesive bond between new hot‑mix asphalt (HMA) and the underlying surface is required; when an existing asphalt layer is milled over cement or brick, a minimum of 1 ½ inches of the original asphalt should be retained to preserve structural integrity and to ensure adequate bonding. Any interlayer placed between the old and new HMA must be compatible with the asphalt so that it can create a strong internal adhesive or mechanical bond within the asphalt matrix; incompatibility may permit water intrusion, which is a major cause of stripping—the separation of binder from aggregate in the hot mix. Moisture‑blocking interlayers such as geotextile fabrics or composite reinforcing grids provide a barrier to surface water, and their effectiveness is governed by the binder application rate associated with the specific interlayer system. In full‑depth reclamation (FDR), calcium chloride or magnesium chloride must be applied as at least a 35 % solution at a rate of 0.1–0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of depth reclaimed; this creates particle cementing that raises strength and lowers the material’s freezing temperature, thereby improving resistance to freeze‑thaw cycles. Emulsified asphalt further binds with the reclaimed mix, increasing resistance to water‑related damage and fatigue, and it functions well together with granular materials, cement, and lime. Asphalt rubber gap‑graded (AR‑GG) mixtures are produced by first blending crumb‑rubber modifier with the asphalt binder in a wet process; this ensures that the rubber is fully incorporated before the binder contacts the aggregates, governing how the binder interacts with the aggregate matrix. When a warm‑mix approach is employed for AR‑GG mixtures, a chemical additive must be used and foaming is prohibited, further controlling the interaction between binder and aggregates. Crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder dense‑graded (CRMAB) mixtures also use a wet process in which the rubber chemically alters the binder properties rather than acting merely as an aggregate replacement; CRMAB binders are considered compatible with polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binders that would otherwise be used for dense‑graded wearing or binder courses. If a warm‑mix additive is selected for CRMAB mixtures, the specification requires a chemical additive and prohibits foaming, thereby dictating the binder‑aggregate interaction. Hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) mixtures are sensitive to certain existing additives; if rubber has been incorporated in the pavement, the HIR process is not acceptable, and similarly, if fiber materials are present within the top two inches of the pavement, the HIR treatment cannot be applied. When paving fabric has been used on the project site, it must be located at least 1 ½ inches below the specified HIR cut depth to allow the existing binder‑aggregate system to interact successfully with the recycling operation. In unbonded Portland cement concrete (PCC) overlays, asphalt concrete serves as a separating medium between existing concrete slabs; a nominal depth of one inch of a Superpave Wearing Course, 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix, is specified to act as that interlayer, preventing direct bonding of the concrete layers while providing uniform support and drainage. If joint faulting exceeds ½ inch, the design calls for placing a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch), 9.5 mm mix at the joint or for milling/grinding the joints to reduce faulting, demonstrating how the asphalt layer must accommodate larger joint movements. When the water absorption of a coarse aggregate determined by AASHTO T‑85 exceeds 2.0 percent, the mixture shall be sampled in accordance with PTM No. 1 at the frequency specified in the producer quality‑control plan and the apparent moisture content of the final mix shall not exceed 0.5 percent; a compatible heat‑stable amine‑based liquid anti‑strip additive or an alternate compatible anti‑strip additive shall be used. For asphalt wearing courses the total combination of reclaimed asphalt material (RAM) and reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) shall be limited to a maximum of 15 percent by mass unless documentation of the SRL designation of the coarse aggregate in the RAM and RAP materials is provided. The aggregates and the completed mixture must be free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture as specified in Section 413.2(e)1.d.1. When fibers or other stabilizers are employed they shall be introduced through specialized equipment that can accurately proportion and meter the material by weight for each batch (or continuously for drum plants); fiber may not be fed through cold‑feed bins or RAP bins, and if any evidence of fiber appears in the bag‑house or wet washer fines the liquid asphalt binder line and/or the fiber line shall be relocated so that the fiber is captured by the liquid asphalt spray. Aggregates supplied shall meet Superpave quality limits and, when a higher SRL is required, the contractor may blend two aggregates in a 50 percent‑by‑mass ratio to achieve the specified SRL. At least 5 percent RAP consisting of cold‑milled or crushed asphalt must be included and processed so that the final mix meets the same requirements as new mixtures, while RAS may be used up to 5 percent by mass only if shredded and screened so that 100 percent passes a 9.5 mm sieve and blended with fine aggregate at a 50 percent‑by‑mass ratio. Modifiers such as warm‑mix asphalt (WMA) additives or other polymer/rubber/fiber anti‑strip agents are introduced after the joint mix formula is approved and are employed solely for moisture susceptibility testing to verify compatibility without altering the volumetric design; fly ash may not be used as filler when the design traffic is greater than or equal to 3 million ESALs. The asphalt‑aggregate bond shall be verified by a moisture susceptibility analysis and by measuring the degree of particle coating, which must reach at least 95.0 percent coated particles so that the mix can be handled, placed and compacted without stripping the binder from the aggregate; any WMA additive or modifier must not impair this moisture resistance, and when a WMA stabilizing agent is used the minimum voids in mineral aggregate may be lowered to 16.5 percent as an allowable adjustment. Only binders and additives listed in Bulletin 15 may be used and any other material, including unapproved rejuvenators or anti‑strip agents, must not be incorporated into the HMA mixture. For mixes containing 5 to 15 percent RAP or up to 5 percent RAS the binder shall meet the performance‑graded requirements of AASHTO M 332 and be supplied with quality‑control certification; mixtures that contain more than 15 percent RAP—or both 5 percent RAS and at least 5 percent RAP—are subject to laboratory testing services evaluation of the reclaimed binder so that the appropriate grade of virgin binder can be selected for the final mix. An anti‑strip additive shall be blended with the asphalt cement before it enters the mix and must be used at a dosage not less than 0.25 percent by weight of the asphalt; the additive must be compatible with both the aggregate and the supplied binder, moisture susceptibility shall be verified by testing for at least 90 percent coating, RAP content shall not exceed 10 percent of the mixture, polymer additives or ground‑tire rubber are permitted to achieve the required PG grade indicating such modifiers are considered compatible with the binder. When a pervious asphalt stabilizer is needed cellulose fibers shall be added at a rate between 0.2 percent and 0.4 percent by weight of the total mix. Equipment capable of mixing reclaimed material and asphalt into a homogeneous mixture without stripping the asphalt material shall be provided; water shall be added to the RAP by a calibrated meter as necessary to aid in mixing and compaction, and all liquid metering shall stay within a variation of not more than +2.0 percent by weight of liquids. The batch plant shall be equipped to weigh the RAP and/or RAS before it enters the pug mill; feed bins and conveyors shall be designed to keep the recycled material from segregating or sticking, both virgin aggregate and RAM shall be dried and then heated so that after addition of RAP and/or RAS the completed mixture reaches a temperature within the limits set in Table A for the class and type of material used, and all aggregates must be free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture before mixing. For a virgin design or mixes that include up to 10 percent RAP, the asphalt material must satisfy the performance‑graded binder requirements defined by the MSCR test and any RAP binder is assumed compatible with the virgin binder provided the overall mixture conforms to those specifications. If a warm‑mix technology is employed, the additive or modifier shall be supplied already blended at the refinery or terminal, shall originate from an approved manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15, and its dosage rate, blending method, and quality‑control testing must be documented in the producer’s QC plan; the final blend shall satisfy the required performance grade and the bill of lading shall reflect the additive name, source, and dosage rate to ensure traceability. In a Superpave asphalt‑rich base course (ARBC) mixture, recycled asphalt pavement may be incorporated only up to 20 percent by weight and manufacturer waste recycled asphalt shingles are prohibited from use in these mixes. For an asphalt‑treated permeable base course the joint mix formula specifies an initial target asphalt content of 2.5 percent by weight; the asphalt content may be adjusted within the range given in Table A until the aggregate is uniformly coated and no observable runoff of excess asphalt occurs, thereby ensuring adequate adhesion between binder and mineral aggregates and preventing stripping. Under the 60‑month warranty requirements, the mix design must explicitly account for binder‑aggregate interaction by incorporating the aggregate’s percent absorption and bulk specific gravity, reporting volumetric properties such as VMA and VFA, and providing moisture‑sensitivity data (tensile strength ratio or Hamburg wheel tracking) to evaluate adhesion and potential stripping; any change in material source that could affect these interactions must be reported in writing to the DME/DMM before use. During production of a pre‑coated aggregate seal coat, the asphalt binder shall form a thin brownish translucent film that covers at least 90 percent of the visible aggregate surface; the residual binder shall be between 0.6 percent and 1.2 percent by weight of the mix for asphalt cement, whereas other asphalt materials may be applied with a residual binder between 0.4 percent and 1.0 percent, and if coverage is less than 90 percent the fraction of uncoated material passing the No. 200 sieve must not exceed 0.5 percent. The Pavement Policy Manual notes that in rehabilitation projects moisture‑related stripping is identified as a compatibility risk for asphalt or composite pavements when a bonded concrete overlay is contemplated, and such pavements should not receive a bonded JPCP overlay if they exhibit significant structural deterioration, unstable support, stripping of the asphalt, or unresolved drainage issues because any condition that compromises adhesion creates a debonding risk and may require use of an unbonded overlay. For overlays, the same manual states that moisture intrusion into the pavement structure can cause stripping of the asphalt‑aggregate bond and other distress, therefore any interlayer placed beneath an HMA overlay must be compatible to provide a strong internal adhesive or mechanical bond, as incompatibility may lead to debonding between the new asphalt layer and an underlying concrete or brick base, which would require removal of the entire existing overlay. The manual further requires that the interlayer be thermally stable and physically durable so that it does not break down under paving temperatures, otherwise temperature‑related performance loss can occur, and it stresses that proper temperature and application of the asphalt material are required for waterproofing performance and to prevent debonding; if the temperature is too low or the application rate insufficient, the layer may fail to adhere, while an overly heavy tack coat can cause slippage at higher temperatures. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require that any hot‑mix asphalt containing an anti‑strip additive be evaluated for moisture susceptibility by following Bulletin 27 analysis and draindown testing, and that at least ninety‑five percent of aggregate particles remain coated; if this threshold is not met, plant mixing time shall be increased. Unburned fuel oil is banned in both the mixture and the aggregates, and aggregates must be free of excess moisture before entering the pug mill; hauling vehicles must be tightly sealed and contain no petroleum oils, solvents or other contaminating materials. Finally, Section 496 mandates submission of a moisture‑sensitivity assessment—either tensile strength ratio data or Hamburg wheel‑tracking results—with the mix design, acknowledging stripping due to moisture as an inherent risk. Influence of production and placement

The FB‑1 wearing and binder materials are cold mixes that are normally mixed and placed by a mobile plant, resulting in high void content that makes the surface highly flexible and therefore they are recommended for low‑volume roads. The FB‑2 mixes must be produced at a stationary mixing plant and, when a PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 binder is used, the specification requires that the material be mixed hot and placed hot, which influences its stiffness and durability. Plant‑mixing operations for Superpave mixtures (section 413) must be carried out on a fully automated plant and adjusted so that the finished mix falls within the temperature range prescribed in Table A for the material class, thereby keeping the hot mix inside the laying‑temperature limits; any material delivered outside those limits is prohibited. The same requirement applies to the 6.3 mm thin overlay (section 412), where plant mixing must deliver the mix at the laying temperature specified in Table B to avoid cold lumps. For Stone Matrix Asphalt (section 419) the plant also must be fully automated, keep aggregates free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture, and meter any WMA additive or stabilizer accurately so that a uniform blend is obtained. Seal‑coat pre‑coated aggregate (section 471) is mixed at a temperature not exceeding 350 °F (or 150 °F when cut‑back asphalts are used) to maintain the required residual binder range. During haulage the mix must retain the proper and uniform placement temperature, and Section 413 requires tightly sealed trucks equipped with insulation, double‑walled or heated bodies when the air temperature is below 50 °F from October 1 to April 30, with temperature checks through a designated hole in the truck. Cold‑recycled asphalt base (section 342) must be transported in clean, tightly sealed vehicles to protect it from contamination. Proper application temperature of the asphalt material is required for the waterproofing function to perform and to prevent debonding; if the mix is too cold it may fail to adhere and an increased application rate may be needed. Conversely, applying a tack coat or asphalt that is too heavy when temperatures are high could result in slippage problems. Placement may not be performed on wet surfaces. Section 413 prohibits placement when the air or surface temperature is 40 °F or lower; sections 412 and 419 prohibit placement when the air or surface temperature is 50 °F or lower, although section 412 allows an exception in which a surface temperature of 50 °F combined with an air temperature of 40 °F may be approved. Section 342 restricts placement to dates outside the specified winter windows; when an exception is granted the ambient air temperature must be 45 °F and rising after no freezing temperatures have occurred for 24 hours. For interlayer projects where grades are steeper than five percent, the asphalt mixes should be proportioned to help with compaction and shear‑rut stability. Compaction must begin immediately after placement. Sections 412, 419 and 58 all require steel‑wheeled rollers; section 412 calls for an adequate number of rollers each weighing a minimum of 10 tons, while section 419 specifies a minimum of three such rollers. Rollers are to be kept moist but not wet and clean without excess water so that the mix does not adhere to the wheels. Roller speed, pattern, size and, where approved, amplitude and frequency of vibratory rollers are adjusted to eliminate displacement, shoving, cracking, aggregate breakage and binder flushing while achieving the density‑acceptance requirements and eliminating all roller marks. Finishing practices include immediate edge work after spreading, constructing layers no less than 3 inches and no more than 6 inches in compacted depth for courses over 6 inches, and avoiding addition of material that is segregated, below the minimum temperature or has an asphalt‑content deficiency or excess. Use of a Material Transfer Vehicle and minimization of hand work help maintain the optimum rolling pattern for density measurement. If the average texture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 inches the pavement is considered flushed and defective. Cold‑recycled asphalt base mixtures exhibit lower structural strength when first constructed, but the strength increases as the mixture cures over several weeks; consequently a minimum of one week of curing under favorable weather conditions (low humidity high temperature) is required before the wearing course can be placed. FB Modified surfaces may not require a seal coat for at least four years, reflecting the effect of curing time on performance. Cooling and curing controls are required for seal‑coat applications; the pre‑coated aggregate must be stocked no higher than 4 feet, allowed sufficient cure time before use, cover at least 90 % of the visible surface area and limit the percent passing a No. 200 sieve to 0.5 %. For cold‑recycled asphalt base courses, compaction, finishing and curing follow the procedures outlined in Section 341.3, which together control density, surface tolerance and long‑term performance. Construction‑related variations that dominate the long‑term performance of HMA are identified in several specifications. For overlay projects the existing pavement must be stable (non‑movement under the roller) and properly leveled, sealed and patched before the new HMA layer is placed (source: Pavement Policy Manual, section 5.12 OVERLAYS) [1]. A leveling course thinner than the required minimum 1 inch is considered inadequate, and combining a binder course with a leveling course is discouraged because it makes quality control of either layer difficult (section 5.12) [1]. On Interstates a Superpave wearing‑course overlay less than 1 ½ inches thick may be placed only with prior approval (section 5.12) [1]. When milling over a concrete or brick base, it is preferred to leave at least 1 ½ inches of existing asphalt so that structural integrity and bonding are retained (section 5.12) [1]. Omission of an appropriate interlayer in areas prone to reflective cracking permits moisture intrusion and accelerated distress (section 5.12) [1]. An overly thick tack coat may cause slippage problems at higher temperatures (section 5.12) [1]. If the asphalt is too cold or applied at an insufficient rate, interlayer debonding can occur (section 5.12) [1]. Rough milled surfaces often require a leveling course to achieve proper compaction (section 5.12) [1]. On slopes greater than 5 % the mix must be proportioned to provide adequate compaction and shear‑rut stability (section 5.12) [1].

For cold‑recycled asphalt base courses low initial structural strength and high void content arising from insufficient curing and inadequate compaction dominate long‑term performance; a minimum of one week of curing under favorable weather (low humidity, high temperature) is required before the wearing course is placed (section C. Characteristics of Cold Recycled Mixtures) [1]. Because of the higher voids, a protective wearing surface is imperative (section C. Characteristics of Cold Recycled Mixtures) [1]. The compacted thickness must fall between 3 inches and 5 inches (section C. Characteristics of Cold Recycled Mixtures) [1]. When the recycled material is fine‑graded, adding virgin coarse aggregate No. 8, 57, or 67 is required to meet design specifications (section C. Characteristics of Cold Recycled Mixtures) [1]. If traffic exceeds 3,000 two‑lane AADT or truck traffic is high, polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended to offset the initial weakness (section C. Characteristics of Cold Recycled Mixtures) [1].

In hot‑in‑place recycled projects surface distresses that exceed specified limits most critically affect long‑term performance; rutting severity shall be less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth less than 1.0 inch, average crack width not to exceed the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, raveling or weathering must not exceed a medium rating, minor corrugation, shoving and slippage average depth less than 1½ inches, and only limited bleeding is acceptable (section C. Characteristics of Hot‑In‑Place Recycled Mixtures) [1]. The asphalt layer must be at least 1½ inches thicker than the milling depth and any paving fabric must lie a minimum of 1½ inches below the HIR cut depth (section C. Characteristics of Hot‑In‑Place Recycled Mixtures) [1]. Presence of rubber or fiber in the top 2 inches, mastic patches larger than 5 sq ft, or extensive crack sealant renders the pavement unsuitable (section C. Characteristics of Hot‑In‑Place Recycled Mixtures) [1].

For unbonded PCC overlays joint faulting greater than ¼ inch calls for milling of the joint when a geotextile interlayer is specified; if faulting exceeds ½ inch a Superpave scratch wearing course (9.5 mm) must be placed or the joints milled/grinded to reduce faulting (section UNBONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT) [1]. The design also requires a nominal depth of 1 inch for a 9.5 mm fine‑graded Superpave wearing course, and deviating from this thickness can affect durability (section UNBONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT) [1].

During spreading and finishing (section 413.3) any material that is segregated, below the minimum temperature, or has a deficient or excess asphalt content creates zones prone to premature cracking (source: DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATION Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS) [2]. Pattern segregation is considered unacceptable when the difference in average pavement texture depth between non‑segregated and segregated areas exceeds 0.024 inch, and a flushed surface is declared defective if the measured texture depth is ≤ 0.006 inch (section 413.3) [2]. Material storage and handling (section 413.2) must keep apparent moisture content at or below 0.5 % and coarse‑aggregate water absorption not greater than 2.0 %; a variation of the theoretical maximum specific gravity of 0.030 or more requires immediate notification (section 413.2) [2]. Deviations in asphalt content, gradation, or sieve‑passing percentages beyond the tolerances listed in Table A, as well as air‑voids or VMA values outside Table B, are treated as corrective actions because they affect stiffness, moisture susceptibility and durability (section 413.2) [2]. The pavement is considered defective when the payment factor for asphalt content, percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve and percent passing the 2.36 mm (No. 8) sieve are all ≤ 85 %, or when density cores produce a percent‑within‑limits below 50 % (section 413.4) [2].

For thin 6.3 mm overlays (section 412.3) aggregate breakage or binder flushing caused by improper roller vibration, pattern segregation, insufficient compaction density, or mixture content outside the single‑sample tolerances for asphalt content and percent passing the 75 µm sieve are deemed defective; steel‑wheeled rollers weighing at least 10 tons must be used, pneumatic rollers are prohibited, and vibratory rollers may be employed only if it can be demonstrated that no aggregate breakage or flushing occurs, with cores monitored on every lot and vibration stopped when either defect is observed (section 412.3) [2].

In stone‑matrix asphalt (section 419.3) long‑term performance is most sensitive to placing the mix on wet surfaces or when air or surface temperature falls to 50 °F or lower, to storing hot mix for more than two hours before placement, to using pneumatic‑wheeled rollers or vibratory rolling without proven absence of aggregate breakage or binder flushing, to uneven distribution of stabilizer fibers, and to excess moisture or unburned fuel oil in aggregates; these conditions create localized voids that accelerate deterioration (section 419.3) [2].

Aggregate quality controls (section 413.2) limit coarse‑aggregate abrasion to the maximum percent specified in Bulletin 27, cap thin‑and‑elongated pieces at their prescribed maxima, require a minimum percentage of crushed fragments with fractured faces, and mandate fine‑aggregate void content and sand equivalent values given in AASHTO M 323 Table 6; any RAP or RAS must be incorporated at least 5 % by mass, remain free of foreign material, and be blended with minimal segregation (section 413.2) [2].

During asphalt grout stabilization (section 679.3) the hot mix must be heated to a temperature between 380 °F and 450 °F—material heated to 500 °F or more is rejected—and pumped at pressures of 25 psi to 45 psi through vertical holes not larger than 1½ in.; upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch during lift is prohibited because it can cause premature distress (section 679.3) [2].

For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses (section 489.2) long‑term performance is most strongly affected when construction introduces deviations in asphalt binder content or fine‑aggregate gradation, specifically the percent passing the 75 µm sieve; any consecutive violations of the Joint Mix Formula tolerances for these values must be corrected immediately or production suspended (section 489.2) [2].

In permeable asphalt base courses (section 360.2) long‑term performance is sensitive to variations that disturb mix proportions and coating quality; the aggregate must be uniformly coated with asphalt, no runoff of excess asphalt may be observed, and the actual asphalt content may not deviate more than 0.8 % from the Joint Mix Formula (section 360.2) [2].

During production of pre‑coated aggregate (section 471.2) inadequate binder film coverage and excessive fine uncoated particles are dominant defects; at least 90 % of the visible aggregate surface must be covered by a thin, brownish, translucent film, and if less than this is achieved the percent passing the 75 µm sieve after ten minutes of dry sieving must not exceed 0.5 %; mixing temperature shall not exceed 350 °F and aggregate temperature shall not exceed 150 °F when cut‑back asphalts are used (section 471.2) [2].

In manual asphalt patching (section 450.3) long‑term performance depends on thorough cleaning of the area, complete drying before tack coating, application of tack coat to bottom and vertical surfaces, binder lifts not exceeding 3 inches in uncompacted depth and a top wearing course limited to 1½ inches, and adequate compaction with a steel‑wheeled roller or other approved equipment; failure to meet these steps can lead to premature rutting or cracking (section 450.3) [2].

For cold‑recycled asphalt base courses (section 342.3) the metering of liquid additives must stay within a variation of not more than + 2.0 % by weight, and placement depth must be controlled so that any deviation is less than ½ inch from the indicated compacted depth; a deficiency of ½ inch or more renders the pavement defective (section 342.3) [2].

Under Superpave construction (section 413.3) mixtures shall not be placed when surfaces are wet or when air or surface temperature is 40 °F or lower, uniform placement temperature must be maintained during hauling, courses placed at compacted depths less than 1.5 inches require density verification by pavement cores, segregation or excess moisture in RAP/RAS must be prevented, a Material Transfer Vehicle is required when paving exceeds 1,500 ft, and extended‑season work must follow an approved plan to avoid defects such as transverse cracking or potholing (section 413.3) [2].

Safety edge construction (section 419.3) requires the completed angle to be between 26 and 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope, and density must remain at the 100 % payment factor for two consecutive lots (or three total); asphalt content and percent passing the 75 µm sieve must stay within their single‑sample tolerances for the same period, otherwise paving operations shall be stopped (section 419.3) [2].

Selection, specification, and improvement

The selection of an HMA surface begins by evaluating functional needs such as improving drainage, repairing failed base areas to restore structural capacity, adding binder‑course thickness when additional structural capacity is required, and enhancing surface friction or shoulder condition where needed. For projects that use the Superpave Restricted Performance Specification, the mix must be placed on a normal‑size lot and the wearing and binder course thicknesses must satisfy the minimum values given in Table 9.5, while the existing pavement must be stable, properly leveled, sealed and patched before resurfacing. In rehabilitation overlays the decision to use an HMA overlay is driven primarily by the condition of the existing pavement and the amount of structural capacity that must be added; the required overlay thickness equals the additional pavement needed to achieve the target structural strength calculated with the AASHTO Design Method. Asphalt overlays are acceptable for all existing pavement conditions provided that surface‑preparation steps such as falling‑weight‑deflectometer testing, full‑depth patching, slab stabilization or jacking and adequate subdrainage are addressed. For low‑volume roads with highly flexible structures the high‑void soft‑binder FB‑1 mix is recommended, whereas when average daily traffic exceeds 1 500 ADT a combination of binder and wearing courses must be used; FB‑1 may be mixed and placed by a mobile plant while FB‑2 requires a stationary mixing plant, and only PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement is allowed in the mix design. Because of the high void content, a seal coat or surface treatment must be placed on the FB surface after a minimum of three months of warm‑weather traffic densification, although it may be postponed up to three years if the underlying pavement is sound. A structural coefficient of 0.20 shall be used for FB courses when designing FB overlays. When specifying an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course the engineer selects Type A, Type B or Type C based on nominal maximum aggregate size—6.3 mm for Type A, 9.5 mm for Type B and 12.5 mm for Type C—and the treatment shall be 1/2 inch for Type A and 3/4 inch for Types B and C, corresponding to a final thickness of one to one‑and‑a‑half times the diameter of the coarsest aggregate; Type A is suited to urban or suburban applications with light truck traffic, Type B to moderate to heavy traffic and highway speeds, and Type C to high‑speed, high‑traffic applications. Selection of a Superpave mixture under the performance‑based approach is first guided by the project’s climatic and traffic environment expressed through a 20‑year design life ESAL count placed into one of five ranges (< 0.3 million, 0.3 to < 3.0 million, 3.0 to < 10.0 million, 10.0 to < 30.0 million, or ≥ 30.0 million); the chosen PG‑binder and that ESAL range together dictate whether a standard binder or a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder is required, with polymer modification recommended when the range exceeds 10 million ESALs. Fiber reinforcement may be specified only on mixes using PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22 or PG 64E‑22 binders and only on eligible wearing, binder or base layers as listed. When the total base course thickness exceeds 6 inches an Asphalt Rich Base Course must be used for the bottom 3 to 6 inches, employing a mix design of < 0.3 million ESALs with a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25.0 mm. A thin Superpave wearing‑course overlay may be used only when the existing surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than 2 % requiring patching, and both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded; the overlay thickness must meet a minimum of 1½ inches unless prior approval is obtained. For an overlay interlayer the selection is guided by the condition of the existing pavement, the type of overlay and the placement temperature because these factors can impact placement and performance; if the existing surface has been milled rough a leveling course may be required before the interlayer is placed, and on projects where grades are steeper than 5 % the asphalt mixes should be proportioned to help with compaction and shear‑rut stability. For cold‑recycled base courses selection is driven by traffic loading, existing pavement condition, drainage and construction logistics; projects carrying significantly heavy truck traffic (i.e., 200 or more daily ESALs) should not be selected, the existing road must have at least 2 inches of asphalt material to achieve a minimum thickness of 3 inches after adding reclaimed or virgin aggregate, poor drainage disqualifies a candidate, and only projects where traffic can be controlled or detoured during base construction and curing period are acceptable. When traffic volumes exceed 3 000 two‑lane AADT, when there is a high percentage of truck traffic, during early or late season recycling, or when rapid strength development is needed, polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended to improve initial mix stability. Asphalt rubber gap‑graded mixtures should be considered for projects that would normally use a gap‑graded wearing or binder course with a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder and may be used for new construction or overlays on asphalt, concrete or composite pavements; they should not be used where significant handwork or raking is required because of mixture stiffness, and because the mix is premium it should only be used when cost‑effective. For AR‑GG wearing courses layer thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches, the wet‑mix process must use the wet method (CRM blended with binder before aggregate), a chemical warm‑mix additive shall be utilized if a warm‑mix process is employed (foaming is not allowed), and pavement markings should have the wet film thickness increased by 5 mils for gap‑graded pavements. AR‑GG may be considered on Interstates and Interstate look‑alikes that exceed > 25 000 ADT, > 10 million ESALs (20‑year design life) and > 50 000 square yards; it must use the Superpave structural layer coefficients from Table 10.6, should not be used in shoulders unless lane and shoulder are paved in one pass, and should not be used in temporary lanes or crossovers. Crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder dense‑graded mixtures should be considered for projects that would normally use dense‑graded wearing or binder courses with a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder and may be used for new construction or overlays on asphalt, concrete or composite pavements; they also should not be used where significant handwork or raking is required, should not be used in shoulders unless lane and shoulder are paved in one pass, layer thickness must not exceed 2½ inches, and the mix is premium so it should only be specified when cost‑effective. For Interstates and Interstate look‑alikes CRMAB may be specified when average daily traffic exceeds 25 000 ADT, cumulative ESALs exceed 10 million over a 20‑year design life, and the project area exceeds 50 000 square yards; placement is recommended in the upper layers of the pavement section where superior performance is best utilized. Hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt selection begins with an assessment of existing pavement, base, subbase and subgrade, sampling and testing, plus review of construction, maintenance history and traffic data; candidate projects must have surface‑only distresses, rutting severity less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth less than 1.0 inch with average crack width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, raveling/weathering severity not exceeding the medium rating, and asphalt pavement thickness must be at least 1½ inches more than the milling depth. Pavements containing rubber or fiber in the top 2 inches, durable markings, mastic patches greater than 5 square feet, extensive crack sealant or overbanded rubberized joint seal are not acceptable for HIR. For an unbonded concrete overlay of PCC pavement selection of asphalt concrete depends on pavement condition and amount of joint faulting; if fracturing of the existing concrete is preferred the “AC over Fractured PCC” module sets thickness, the interlayer normally uses a Superpave Wearing Course with a nominal depth of 1 inch and a 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix, whereas when joint faulting exceeds ½ inch a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix is placed at the joint or joints are milled/grinded to reduce faulting; for geotextile interlayers joints with faulting greater than ¼ inch are milled. The mix must be produced and placed at the laying temperatures listed in Table A for its type and class, and any material delivered outside those temperature limits may not be incorporated. Layer thicknesses are limited to between 3 inches and 6 inches per compacted layer; courses deeper than 6 inches must be built in two or more equal layers with no layer less than 3 inches or more than 6 inches. A Safety Edge shall be constructed with a depth of at least 1.5 inches but not greater than 5 inches and an edge‑seal width of minimum 12 inches adjacent to curbs. The apparent moisture content of the mix may not exceed 0.5 %. Asphalt‑content and gradation each must fall within the limits of Table A after a minimum of three test results, and the theoretical maximum specific gravity value shall not vary 0.030 or more from the previous test or from the Joint Mix Formula value without immediate notification to the DME/DMM. The total RAM and RAP content may not exceed 15 % of the mixture by mass unless SRL documentation is provided; any Warm‑Mix Asphalt technology or anti‑strip additive must be approved per Bulletin 15 and incorporated according to the QC plan. RAP material proposed for use shall contain at least 5 % RAP consisting of cold‑milled or crushed asphalt mixture, and manufacturer‑waste recycled asphalt shingles may be used only up to 5 % of the total mix mass with certification; fly ash is prohibited when the design traffic is greater than or equal to 3 million ESALs. All binder material shall conform to the performance‑graded requirements defined by the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery test per AASHTO M 332 (as revised in Bulletin 25) and be obtained from a source listed in Bulletin 15. For an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course the aggregate must come from approved suppliers listed in Bulletin 14, satisfy the limits set out in Table A and Table B, filler may not be fly ash when design traffic is ≥ 3 million ESALs, and each batch is accepted only when certification shows asphalt content by mass and the percentages passing the 75 µm (No. 200), 2.36 mm (No. 8) and 6.3 mm sieves fall within those tolerances. Placement of a 6.3 mm wearing‑course mixture is prohibited when the air or surface temperature is 50°F or lower and paving may not be performed from October 1 to March 31 in designated districts unless an exception is granted; any approved exception also requires a minimum surface temperature of 50°F and an air temperature of 40°F. Equipment must include steel‑wheeled rollers each weighing at least 10 tons; moisture‑controlled roller wheels must be used, and pneumatic or vibratory rollers may not be employed unless aggregate breakage can be demonstrably avoided. Mixture acceptance is governed by the asphalt‑content and percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve tolerances set out in Table B for individual samples, and density must satisfy non‑movement and optimum‑rolling‑pattern conditions verified with approved nuclear or electrical gauges. For Stone Matrix Asphalt courses a safety edge must be provided when the SMA course depth reaches 1.5 inches or greater, and the extruded wedge must achieve a final angle between 26° and 40° measured from the pavement cross‑slope. Seal‑coat mixes require a pre‑coating of 0.6 % to 1.2 % residual binder when asphalt cement is used and 0.4 % to 1.0 % for other asphalt materials; mixing temperature shall not exceed 350°F and aggregate temperatures should not exceed 150°F when cut‑back asphalts are employed. For all mixes the binder must meet the Standard Specifications for Performance‑Graded Asphalt Binder using the MSCR test and be obtained from a supplier listed in Bulletin 15; when Warm‑Mix Asphalt technology is used an approved additive or modifier must be blended either at the refinery/terminal or at the plant, and the producer’s QC plan must detail the additive name, dosage rates, blending method, testing procedures, corrective actions and handling information. Asphalt rubber binders should be considered for projects that would normally use gap‑graded wearing or binder courses with polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder; when Warm Mix Asphalt (WMA) is employed a chemical warm‑mix additive must be utilized while foaming is not allowed, and the mixture shall be produced by the wet process in which crumb rubber modifier is blended with the asphalt binder before the binder is mixed with aggregates; layer thickness should not exceed 2½ inches, and pavement markings having the wet film thickness increased by five mils [1]. Wet‑process crumb‑rubber modified binders may also be used for dense‑graded wearing or binder courses, are most effective in the upper layers of new construction, may contain RAP or RAS as specified in the SSP, shall likewise not exceed a layer thickness of 2½ inches, and are recommended only for projects with average daily traffic greater than 25 000, estimated ESALs greater than ten million over a twenty‑year design life, and an area greater than 50 000 sq yd; they should not be used on shoulders unless the lane and shoulder are paved in one pass [1]. The hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) process can modify existing HMA by adding a rejuvenator sized from core samples, milling before recycling to treat deeper cracks, and placing a recycled layer typically 1½ inches thick but ranging from one to two inches; when combined with stone matrix asphalt or open‑graded friction courses the HIR layer can remediate rutting, improve surface appearance, and prolong service life [1]. Acceptance of an HIR project requires rutting severity less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth less than 1.0 inch with average crack width not exceeding ½ inch, existing asphalt at least 1½ inches thicker than the milling depth, avoidance of rubber or fiber materials in the top two inches, and removal of large mastic patches and durable markings [1]. In unbonded Portland cement concrete overlays a Superpave wearing course of 9.5‑mm fine‑graded mix at a nominal depth of one inch may be specified; if joint faulting exceeds ½ inch a Superpave wearing‑course (Scratch) 9.5‑mm mix should be placed at the joint or the joints milled/grinded to reduce faulting, and a geotextile interlayer may be used instead of asphalt concrete to provide separation while ensuring proper drainage [1]. Hot‑mix asphalt performance can be enhanced by adhering to the placement and finishing requirements in Section 413.3; the mixture shall be placed at the temperatures listed in Table A and any material below those limits must be rejected [2]. When a course exceeds six inches in compacted depth it shall be constructed in two or more layers of approximately equal depth, with no layer less than three inches or more than six inches in compacted depth [2]. A safety edge shall be formed with a depth of at least 1½ inches and shall not be placed where the total depth exceeds five inches [2]. Adjacent to flush curbs the wearing course shall be sealed with a minimum twelve‑inch strip of hot asphalt, or, if approved by the Inspector‑in‑Charge, with emulsified asphalt consisting of PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT [2]. Section 413.2 requires that the total combination of reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) and reclaimed aggregate material (RAM) not exceed fifteen percent of the mixture by mass unless SRL documentation is provided, and the apparent moisture content of the blend shall not exceed 0.5 %; a compatible heat‑stable amine‑based liquid anti‑strip agent or a compatible alternate anti‑strip additive shall be used [2]. Approved Warm Mix Asphalt (WMA) technologies, including additives, modifiers, or processes listed in Bulletin 15, may be incorporated into the mixture, and when moisture susceptibility is evaluated only the WMA technology additive, modifier, or process may be used during mix‑design testing [2]. At least five percent RAP consisting of cold‑milled or crushed asphalt mixture may be added provided that a producer’s QC plan controls composition and segregation, and up to five percent reclaimed asphalt shingle (RAS) by mass may replace fine aggregate after shredding and screening so that all material passes a 9.5‑mm sieve; different felt types shall be handled separately [2]. Fly ash shall not be used when the design traffic is greater than or equal to three million ESALs [2]. Lot acceptance is tied to percent‑within‑limits values: when PWLD, PWLAC, PWL200 and PWLPCS are each greater than or equal to 50 the Department will determine the pay factor for each PWL as specified; if either PWLD or PWLAC is less than 50 the lot is defective, and the contractor may request a written retest if the asphalt‑content pay factor or the density pay factor falls below 80.00, provided the request is submitted within three weeks of the original test results [2]. Section 412.3 mandates that a paving‑operation QC Plan be prepared and approved before work begins, that placement shall not occur when surfaces are wet or when the air or surface temperature is 50 °F or lower (any exception limited to a minimum surface temperature of 50 °F and an air temperature of 40 °F), and that steel‑wheeled rollers weighing at least ten tons each, equipped with watering or soapy systems, shall be used while pneumatic rollers are prohibited [2]. All existing vertical surfaces that will contact the asphalt mixture shall be painted with a uniform coating of either emulsified asphalt consisting of PennDOT Material Class TACK, NTT/CNTT, or hot binder applied in two or more applications [2]. Plant modifications to introduce WMA technology additives, modifiers, or foaming processes must provide proportioning devices interlocked with the plant system and controlled to ±10 % of the weight of fibers used as stabilizer; fibers shall be stored in a dry environment and shipped within two hours of plant mixing, and when the WMA additive serves as the stabilizing agent the minimum voids in mineral aggregate (VMA) may be lowered to 16.5 % when approved by the DME/DMM [2]. The finished mixture shall achieve a particle‑coating degree of at least 95.0 % as measured by AASHTO T 195, and draindown testing according to AASHTO T 305 (1‑hour reading) must remain within specified tolerances [2]. Coarse aggregate shall be hard, tough, durable, and essentially free of deleterious substances; for all wearing courses at least 85 % of the particles must be crushed with a minimum of two fracture faces, and alternative aggregates such as blast‑furnace slag or steel slag may be used provided traceability is demonstrated and, for steel slag, total expansion determined according to PTM No. 130 is less than 0.50 %; lightweight aggregate that meets an absorption factor not exceeding 2.5 % and satisfies freeze‑thaw resistance criteria may be employed, and recycled concrete may serve as a 2A aggregate under the same quality limits [2]. Under Section 413.2 reclaimed asphalt pavement may be incorporated at five to fifteen percent RAP or five percent RAS, and when the RAP content exceeds fifteen percent (or when both five percent RAS and at least five percent RAP are used) the LTS will evaluate the required binder grade; all binders shall meet Performance‑Graded requirements verified by the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery test (AASHTO M 332) and be sourced from a Bulletin 15 listed supplier with documented QC testing, certification and signed bill of lading for each batch [2]. For cold‑recycled asphalt base courses the reclaimed material shall be screened until ninety‑five percent passes a two‑inch sieve; the mix shall use a Performance‑Graded binder such as PG 64S‑22, PG 64V‑22, PG 64E‑22, PG 58S‑28 or PG 58V‑28 in accordance with AASHTO M 332, and stabilization is achieved with emulsified asphalt (e.g., MS‑2, CMS‑2, SS‑1) or foamed asphalt as prescribed by the approved mix design, with Portland cement added where specified; the plant must meter liquids so that variation does not exceed plus 2.0 % by weight, emulsified asphalt shall be delivered by a positive displacement pump accurate to four gallons per minute, calibrated meters shall control water addition, and continuous quality control is provided by automatic shut‑off interlocks, flow‑rate meters recording additive quantities, and depth verification testing one location per three thousand square yards with removal of any section that is deficient one‑half inch or more from the target compacted depth [2]. In pervious asphalt pavement systems polymer‑modified binders such as Class PG 64H‑22, PG 64E‑22 or PG 64S‑22 may be used and approved polymer additives or ground tire rubber are permitted to achieve the required PG grade; reclaimed asphalt pavement may be incorporated but shall not exceed ten percent of the mixture, and moisture susceptibility can be reduced by blending a heat‑stable anti‑strip additive at a dosage not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt, provided it is compatible with the aggregate and binder, and when AASHTO T 305 testing indicates additional stability, cellulose fibers may be added as a pervious asphalt stabilizer at a rate between 0.2 % and 0.4 % by weight of the total mix [2]. Manual asphalt patching requires a clean, dry substrate and a tack coat applied to the bottom and vertical surfaces before hot‑mix placement; edges should be sealed with emulsified asphalt, Class TACK or NTT, or the same PG binder used in the pavement course such as PG 64S‑22, binder lifts shall not exceed three inches uncompacted depth and the wearing course shall be capped at a topmost one‑and‑a‑half inch layer, each lift being compacted with approved equipment such as a steel‑wheeled roller, and each patch shall have an area of at least one square foot [2]. Extended‑season paving may be performed only with a written request and an approved Extended‑Season Paving Plan detailing plant and field quality controls; during such periods density acceptance shall be by pavement cores for mixes placed at the minimum compacted depths, and a Material Transfer Vehicle shall be used whenever the paving length exceeds 1 500 linear feet; hauling of the mix shall use tightly sealed trucks with covers, maintain uniform placement temperature, and provide insulation or a heated truck body when the air temperature is below fifty degrees Fahrenheit from October 1 to April 30 [2]. Mixture acceptance shall be demonstrated through certification of asphalt content and a density check using an optimum‑rolling pattern; a lot is deemed defective when the payment factor for both the asphalt content and the percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve each reach eighty‑five percent, which triggers a retest requiring new cores taken twenty‑four inches longitudinally from the original sample, rinsed, backfilled with matching mix and supported by control‑chart data [2]. Stone Matrix Asphalt wearing‑course mixes may be submitted for consideration; their Joint Mix Formulations shall be established by a volumetric mix design using the Superpave Gyratory Compactor and documented on the TR‑448A form at least seven calendar days before use, with certified quality‑control results—including moisture‑sensitivity data such as tensile strength ratio or Hamburg Wheel Tracking—provided to the Representative within forty‑eight hours of material sampling; the base binder shall satisfy Performance‑Graded requirements by the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery test and up to ten percent RAP may be incorporated, and when Warm Mix Asphalt technology additives or modifiers are employed they may be blended at the asphalt material supplier’s refinery or terminal with the manufacturer name, additive type, dosage rate and blending method recorded in a Producer QC Plan and reflected on the bill of lading, while plant modifications required for WMA additives, modifiers or foaming processes shall achieve a uniform blend; for recycled mixtures the plant must measure RAP/RAS mass, prevent segregation, dry virgin aggregate and heat it such that after adding RAP/RAS the completed mix falls within the temperature range specified in Table A while remaining free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture [2]. Stone Matrix Asphalt performance can be further enhanced by constructing a safety edge on all outside edges and shoulders at least one‑and‑a‑half inches deep, using the same mix as the roadway and a paver‑mounted device to produce a wedge shape with a final angle of twenty‑six to forty degrees, verified each day before compaction resumes [2]. For slab stabilization the asphalt grout shall be heated to a temperature between 380 °F and 450 °F before pumping; any mix heated to five hundred degrees Fahrenheit or more shall be rejected, pumping pressure shall be limited to twenty‑five to forty‑five pounds per square inch, and upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed [2].

Performance evaluation and implications

Evaluation of asphalt concrete mixes incorporates laboratory acceptance testing, on‑site field verification, and long‑term performance monitoring. The Pavement Policy Manual specifies that the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course is evaluated on its expected service life of eight to ten years and on its ability to seal the pavement, thereby reducing oxidation and weathering of the surface. For Superpave paving courses the manual adds advanced performance testing and performance prediction models, with design ESALs calculated using a 20‑year design life and placed into one of five ESAL ranges that drive binder selection and mixture proportions. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses are evaluated through both laboratory and field observations that track strength development and durability, requiring a minimum of one week of curing under favorable weather conditions (low humidity high temperature) before a wearing course can be placed because the structural strength is lower initially and increases as the mixture cures over several weeks. Projects with 200 or more daily ESALs are excluded from this treatment, and polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended when traffic volumes exceed three thousand two‑lane AADT to achieve quicker early‑stage stability. Hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt mixtures are evaluated through field observations and limited laboratory sampling that include assessment of existing pavement layers, sampling and testing of the pavement, and review of construction, maintenance history and traffic data. The field criteria for candidate pavements require rutting severity less than 1 ½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth less than 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, raveling/weathering severity not to exceed the medium rating, and minor corrugation, shoving or slippage with an average depth less than 1 ½ inches. In the Pennsylvania specifications, the Laboratory Testing Services conducts acceptance testing on loose mixture samples using PTM No. 757 or PTM No. 702 Modified Method D to determine asphalt binder content and the percent passing the 75‑µm (No. 200) sieve, and a separate Gmm verification sample is tested in accordance with AASHTO T209 to establish theoretical maximum specific gravity. The same laboratory performs binder performance grading with the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery test specified in AASHTO M 332, determines uncompacted void content by AASHTO T304 Method A (or Bulletin 14), measures sand equivalent per AASHTO T176, and checks aggregate abrasion against the maximum percent limits in Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A while controlling thin‑and‑elongated pieces and crushed fragments to the percentages set out in AASHTO M 323 Table 6. Moisture susceptibility testing must produce tensile strength ratio data for inclusion in the Job Mix Formula, and stone matrix asphalt mixes require additional moisture susceptibility analysis per Bulletin 27, draindown testing according to AASHTO T305 with a one‑hour reading, and determination of particle coating by AASHTO T195 where at least 95.0 % of particles must be coated. Volumetric properties such as bulk specific gravity, air voids and VMA are measured on compacted specimens prepared to AASHTO T312 and compared with the tolerances in Tables D and E, while Superpave Gyratory Compactor specimens provide further quality‑control data. For mixes containing more than fifteen percent reclaimed asphalt pavement or a combination of five percent recycled shingles and at least five percent RAP, long‑term strength testing is performed to evaluate the binder present in the reclaimed materials and to determine the required grade class. Field density verification is achieved with an optimum‑rolling pattern and core sampling; a pavement is deemed defective when the payment factors for asphalt content, the 75‑µm sieve, and the 2.36‑mm (No. 8) sieve are all 85 % or less, and any failing lot must be retested using the same laboratory methods and coring procedures. Field quality‑control documentation is required through a paving operation QC plan on Form CS‑413, with density acceptance by pavement cores reported within 24 hours of paving completion, and a manual survey conducted in accordance with Publication 336 evaluates fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond/delamination to determine final acceptance. Long‑term performance is monitored through a 60‑month warranty bond; moisture‑sensitivity data such as tensile strength ratio or Hamburg Wheel Tracking results are retained for the warranty period, and final annual performance surveys trigger remediation if required. Designers must evaluate drainage to improve removal of surface and subsurface water, determine binder‑course thickness needed to increase structural capacity, and consider surface‑friction needs when extra structure is not required (section 2.1 GENERAL etc.)[1]. Construction specifications require that existing overlays be stable, properly leveled, sealed and patched before new HMA is placed, and contractors must achieve consistent density results near the middle of the target field density range (92.0 % to 98.5 %) to avoid reduced pay factors[1]. The flexibility of asphalt concrete permits its use as a flexible overlay on any existing pavement condition; the required thickness of an asphalt overlay is defined as the amount of pavement needed to achieve the structural strength prescribed by the AASHTO design method, and during construction the overlay must be sawed and sealed over all underlying transverse joints and patched joints to ensure proper bonding and limit reflective cracking[1]. High void content and softer binders in FB‑1 and FB‑2 mixes make them highly flexible, which is why they are recommended for low‑volume roads that have highly flexible existing pavement structures; this flexibility also means a seal coat or surface treatment must be placed on the FB surface after at least three months of warm‑weather traffic densification (or can be postponed up to three years if the underlying pavement is sound), while an FB Modified surface may go without such treatment for at least four years[1]. Because deflection‑based design programs assume a Superpave overlay, the depth of an FB overlay must be adjusted using a structural coefficient of 0.20[1]. The ultra‑thin bonded wearing course (UTWC) uses a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane topped with a hot‑mix asphalt concrete layer whose final thickness is set at 1 to 1.5 times the diameter of the coarsest aggregate, resulting in a paver‑placed surface treatment that is only ½ inch thick for Type A and ¾ inch for Types B and C; the UTWC seals the pavement, reducing oxidation and weathering, which allows the pavement to remain resilient to fatigue and low‑temperature cracking, and surface distresses such as raveling and moderate rutting may also be corrected[1]. The nominal maximum aggregate size of an HMA wearing course governs its duty level: a 6.3 mm mix (Type A) provides a fine surface texture suited to urban or suburban roads with light truck traffic but is not recommended for highways that are borderline candidates for preventive maintenance; a 9.5 mm mix (Type B) offers durability sufficient for moderate‑to‑heavy traffic and highway speeds, and can also be applied in lighter duty settings when a slightly thicker lift or greater surface distress is present; the 12.5 mm mix (Type C), being the most heavy‑duty, may be used for any traffic condition and is especially recommended for high‑speed, high‑traffic applications where moderate rutting is expected[1]. The Superpave system tailors HMA mix design to climate and traffic by requiring selection of a Performance Grade binder and a 20‑year design life ESAL range; when the estimated ESAL range exceeds 10 million, a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder is specified for wearing or binder courses to avoid rutting, while lower traffic levels (0.3 to < 3.0 million ESALs) may permit less severe binders unless regional cracking concerns exist[1]. Incorporating aramid fibers in an Asphalt Fiber Modified Course can prevent or delay crack initiation and propagation, provided the fibers are well distributed, but it does not change skid resistance, required design thickness, or pavement life and maintenance cycles[1]. An Asphalt Rich Base Course, limited to < 0.3 million ESAL mix designs with design air voids of 2.5 % and a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25.0 mm, supplies the highest tensile strength at the bottom of the pavement section, thereby improving resistance to bottom‑up cracking and moisture damage throughout service life[1]. For non‑Interstate projects a Superpave Wearing Course thinner than 1½ inches may be placed only when the existing asphalt surface is structurally sound, less than 2 % of the pavement requires patching, and both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded, which ensures that the thin layer can carry traffic loads without premature failure[1]. Using the specified PG 64E‑22 binder for standard 6.3 mm wearing courses provides the required mix properties at the minimum depth, while an interlayer that is compatible with the asphalt creates a strong internal adhesive or mechanical bond, acts as a moisture barrier and stress‑absorbing layer, and therefore mitigates reflective cracking and water intrusion, extending service life and allowing thinner overlays to be milled without sacrificing structural integrity[1]. The ability of an HMA interlayer to waterproof and stay bonded depends on applying the mix at the proper temperature; if the asphalt is too cold the layer may not adhere, whereas a tack coat that is too heavy can result in slippage problems at higher temperatures[1]. When existing pavement has been milled rough, a leveling course may be required before placing the interlayer to ensure smooth bonding, and for overlays on grades steeper than 5 % the asphalt mixes should be proportioned to help with compaction and shear‑rut stability[1]. Cold‑recycled mixtures exhibit lower structural strength when first constructed and gain strength as the mixture cures over several weeks; a minimum of one week of curing under favorable weather conditions (low humidity, high temperature) is required before placing the wearing course, which dictates early performance[1]. Because these mixes are less resistant to abrasion by traffic and have higher void content, a wearing surface is essential for durability, and they are unsuitable for high‑traffic roads unless traffic can be detoured until sufficient strength develops; design must respect a minimum compacted depth of 3 inches and a maximum of 5 inches, and projects with heavy truck traffic (200 or more daily ESALs) should not be selected for this treatment[1]. Polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended when traffic exceeds 3,000 two‑lane AADT or rapid early strength is needed[1]. Calcium chloride and magnesium chloride additives result in some gain in strength due to particle cementing, and these chemicals also lower the freezing temperature of the reclaimed material, improving resistance to freeze‑thaw cycles; generally calcium chloride is applied using a minimum 35 % solution at a rate of 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each 1 inch of depth reclaimed[1]. Asphalt stabilization offers several advantages including improved resistance to water‑related damage and improved fatigue resistance to loading, which influences mix design choices and construction practices for durable reclaimed sections[1]. AR‑GG mixtures should not be used for projects where significant handwork or raking is required due to the stiffness of the mixture, but they may be employed on Interstates and Interstate look‑alikes with > 25,000 ADT, > 10 million ESALs (20‑year design life), and > 50,000 square yards; layer thickness should not exceed 2½ inches and the wet process is required to blend the CRM before mixing with aggregates, ensuring proper binder integration[1]. When used in warm‑mix asphalt a chemical warm‑mix additive should be utilized (foaming is not allowed), and pavement markings need a wet film thickness increased by 5 mils for gap‑graded pavements[1]. Crumb rubber modified asphalt binder dense‑graded (CRMAB) mixtures should be considered for projects that would normally use dense‑graded wearing or binder courses with polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder; they also should not be used where significant handwork or raking is required, and for new construction it is recommended that CRMAB be placed in the upper layers of the pavement section (wearing and binder courses) where its superior performance is best utilized, with layer thickness not exceeding 2½ inches[1]. The hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) asphalt mixture can remediate rutting and distress from deteriorated pavements, recover and improve pavement performance, improve appearance, and prolong the pavement service life, but it functions only as a short‑term interim wearing surface that must be followed by a permanent surface before the construction season ends; designers may specify the typical 1½‑inch layer (range 1 to 2 inches) and must also provide at least an additional 1½‑inch thickness of existing asphalt beneath the HIR cut to avoid thin layers that could compromise durability, traffic can be reopened as soon as the surface cools to the required temperature, and project selection requires rutting severity < 1½ inches, crack depth < 1.0 inch, and average crack width ≤ ½ inch, ensuring that only surface‑level distresses are treated[1]. In unbonded PCC overlays asphalt concrete is used as the separating layer, with a nominal depth of 1 inch of a Superpave Wearing Course, 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix providing uniform support across the existing concrete slab; if joint faulting exceeds ¼‑inch the design may call for a geotextile interlayer, but if faulting exceeds ½‑inch the asphalt concrete must be placed as a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch), 9.5 mm mix at the joint or the joints must be milled/grinded to reduce faulting, ensuring proper load transfer and durability of the overlay[1]. Coarse aggregate required for all wearing courses must be hard, tough and essentially free of deleterious substances such as reactive chert or gypsum; at least 85 % of the particles must be crushed with a minimum of two fracture faces, and absorption shall not exceed 3.0 % for Type A aggregates and 3.5 % for Type B aggregates[2]. The binder grade shall be established by the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery test in accordance with AASHTO M 332, permitting reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) at a rate of 5‑15 % without additional evaluation while limiting the total combination of RAP and recycled asphalt material (RAM) to 15 % by mass unless SRL documentation is provided; mixes containing more than 15 % RAP or both at least 5 % RAP and 5 % recycled asphalt shingles must be submitted for laboratory binder evaluation, and fly ash may not be used when design traffic equals or exceeds 3 million ESALs[2]. The mixture must be produced within strict volumetric limits – apparent moisture content shall not exceed 0.5 %, asphalt content must stay within the tolerances of Table A/B/E, VMA shall be at least 16.5 % when a warm‑mix additive is used, coated aggregate particles shall be at least 95 %, and a one‑hour draindown test per AASHTO T 305 must be passed – because these parameters control air voids, load‑bearing capacity and resistance to cracking[2]. Percent‑within‑limits values for in‑place density, asphalt content, the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve and the primary control sieve must each be at least 50; any value below 50 classifies the lot as defective and triggers corrective actions that affect payment factors, while a combination of 85 % for asphalt content and both sieves also results in a defective classification[2]. Placement temperature limits are imposed – asphalt mixtures shall not be placed when surfaces are wet or when air or surface temperature is 50 °F or lower; an exception to the weather‑limitation dates requires a minimum surface temperature of 50 °F and an air temperature of 40 °F[2]. The ultra‑thin bonded wearing course must be mixed at temperatures not exceeding 350 °F (or 150 °F for cut‑back asphalts), and asphalt grout used in stabilization must be heated between 380 °F and 450 °F, with any material above 500 °F rejected; pump pressure shall be maintained between 25 and 45 psi and upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed[2]. Layer thickness control requires that any course deeper than six inches in compacted depth be constructed in two or more layers each between three and six inches, while a safety edge shall have a depth of at least 1.5 inches but not exceed five inches[2]. Compaction must be achieved with an adequate number of steel‑wheeled rollers each weighing a minimum of ten tons – at least three rollers are required – using an optimum rolling pattern that eliminates roller marks, prevents flushing of binder, shoving, cracking or aggregate breakage, and meets density acceptance criteria measured with approved nuclear or impedance gauges; the compacted density shall be within ±0.030 of the contractor’s daily Gmm value[2]. Edge sealing with a minimum twelve‑inch wide hot asphalt (or approved emulsified asphalt) creates a watertight joint that reduces edge breakdown and long‑term maintenance needs[2]. For pervious asphalt systems the binder grade may be PG 64H‑22, PG 64E‑22 or PG 64S‑22, optionally polymer‑modified, with RAP limited to ten percent of the mixture; at least ninety percent coating in ASTM D3625 testing and a minimum anti‑strip additive dosage of 0.25 % by weight are required when such additives are used[2]. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses shall employ performance‑graded binders such as PG 64S‑22, PG 64V‑22, PG 64E‑22, PG 58S‑28 or PG 58V‑28, be placed only after no freezing temperatures for twenty‑four hours and when ambient temperature is at least 45 °F and rising, and any layer whose compacted depth deviates by one‑half inch or more from the target shall be deemed defective[2]. Potholes or abrupt depressions three inches deep or less must be patched with an asphalt wearing course placed after a cured tack coat; each patch shall have a minimum area of one square foot, binder lifts shall not exceed three inches in uncompacted depth and the final wearing layer shall be confined to the topmost 1.5 inches[2]. Pre‑coating steps for seal coats require residual asphalt binder between 0.6 % and 1.2 % (or four to ten tenths percent when other asphalt materials are used), mixing temperatures not exceeding 350 °F, film coverage of at least ninety percent of the visible aggregate surface, stockpiling height limited to four feet, and uncoated fine material passing a No. 200 sieve not exceeding 0.5 %; these controls maintain seal‑coat durability and facilitate future maintenance[2]. Compliance with the Job Mix Formula, certification testing within Table E tolerances, submission of certified quality‑control results within forty‑eight hours of sampling, and adherence to warranty performance criteria together link material behavior directly to construction quality, long‑term pavement serviceability and subsequent maintenance requirements[2].
