Corner Break

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Corner Break is a topic under Distress.
corner-breakcorner-breaks
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Corner Break is a concrete‑slab distress that appears as cracks intersecting transverse joints at the slab corners, indicating that the slab has lost underlying support due to voids formed by pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement.[1] These voids permit excessive deflection and stress in the corner area, producing faulting, diagonal cracking and visible breakage while water infiltration through poorly sealed joints accelerates the process.[1] The prescribed remedy—slab stabilization—fills the voids with cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing or high‑density polyurethane foam to re‑establish full support, reduce deflection, and halt further corner cracking, thereby restoring the pavement’s structural functionality.[1]

Purpose and treatment objectives

Corner Break is a distress that signals loss of support beneath concrete slabs, typically caused by voids that develop near transverse joints and cracks at the slab corners due to pumping action, subgrade consolidation, and settlement. These voids allow excessive slab deflection and stress, producing cracks that intersect the joints at the corners. Slab stabilization treatments address this problem by filling the underlying voids with grout materials (such as cement, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane) to restore full support and reduce further corner cracking. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

The purpose of treating a corner break through slab stabilization is to restore the pavement’s structural functionality by filling voids beneath the slab, thereby re‑establishing full support and reducing deflections that lead to further cracking. By restoring this support, the treatment reduces the likelihood of future pumping, faulting, and additional slab damage, effectively bringing the slab back to a functional condition rather than merely slowing deterioration or extending service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Distress characterization and causes

Corner Break occurs when voids form beneath the concrete slab because of pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement, especially near transverse joints at the slab corners. The resulting loss of support allows excessive deflection and stress in the corner area, producing cracks that intersect the joints. Water that infiltrates through joints or poor drainage accelerates the pumping process, further weakening the support. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Condition assessment and timing

To decide if a corner‑break treatment is needed, a field inspection should look for the loss‑of‑support signs listed in the guidelines: transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks on the lane or shoulder, small depressions or blow holes at transverse joints or cracks, visible corner breaks themselves, and overall slab settlement. When any of these conditions are observed at a slab corner, it indicates that voids may be present beneath the slab and that stabilization treatment should be considered. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Corner Break is treated when it serves as a sign that the slab has lost support beneath its base. The presence of a corner break indicates voids have caused excessive deflection and stress in the concrete, triggering loss‑of‑support conditions that warrant slab stabilization to fill those voids and restore structural integrity. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Materials and repair details

Corner breaks are repaired by slab‑stabilization, which fills the voids beneath the concrete at the affected corner. The voids may be filled with a cement grout mixture (about one cubic foot per hole, roughly a quarter bag of cement), hot asphalt undersealing (30–50 gal per hole), or a high‑density polyurethane foam (approximately 0.4 lb per square foot). After placement the slab must remain closed to traffic until the material cures—up to twelve hours for cement grout and at least thirty minutes for asphalt or polyurethane—and joint and crack sealing should be applied concurrently to limit future water infiltration. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Corner breaks develop when pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or settlement generate voids beneath the slab, especially near transverse joints at outside corners, leading to loss of support and excessive deflection. The effectiveness of stabilization depends on existing conditions such as transverse joint faulting, fines near joints, small depressions, slab settlement, and overall loss of slab support. Selecting a grout material that matches site constraints is critical: cement grout must be allowed to cure before traffic, hot‑asphalt requires cooling and safety measures, while high‑density polyurethane’s low viscosity allows deep penetration and it forms a water‑ and chemical‑resistant closed‑cell foam with long‑term durability. Proper joint and crack sealing and functional subsurface drains are also required to limit water ingress and maintain the restored support. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Treatment procedures

To treat a corner‑break the slab is first inspected, joints and cracks are sealed, and appropriate traffic control is installed; holes are then drilled at the affected transverse joint or crack just before stabilization work begins. The chosen grout—cement‑based mix, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane—is pumped into each hole following the specified pattern, and the pavement remains closed until the material cures (about 12 hours for cement grout or a minimum of 30 minutes for asphalt or polyurethane). Once the grout has hardened, traffic is reopened and normal drainage conditions are maintained. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Implementing corner‑break repairs requires drilling holes at the affected joints or cracks and pumping a grout material—cement slurry, hot liquid asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane—beneath the slab; the work must be sequenced so that drilling occurs immediately before stabilization and both crews share the same temporary traffic control setup, with operations limited to one lane at a time. Safety equipment is needed when handling hot asphalt, and after injection the pavement cannot be reopened to traffic until the grout has set (approximately 12 hours for cement or about 30 minutes for asphalt or polyurethane). Successful repair also depends on sealing joints and cracks and maintaining subsurface drainage to limit water infiltration that drives pumping. Although stabilization restores support and reduces deflection, it does not increase the slab’s design capacity. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Quality and workmanship

Inspection for corner break should verify that the slab support is intact by looking for transverse joint faulting, fines or blow‑holes near joints, visible corner breaks and any slab settlement. The materials used to stabilize voids—cement grout, hot asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane—must be checked for proper mix and quantity before pumping, and the preparation step requires drilling holes according to the prescribed pattern and sealing all joints and cracks. Installation is accepted only when the grout has fully cured (at least 12 hours for cement, a minimum of 30 minutes for asphalt or polyurethane) and traffic is not reopened until that time. After work is completed, a visual confirmation that voids are filled, surface depressions are eliminated, and drainage remains functional confirms compliance. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Performance and service life

Corner break signals a loss of slab support caused by voids beneath the concrete, which leads to excessive deflection and stress that degrade pavement condition, limit functionality, and shorten durability and service life. Stabilizing the slab by injecting grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane fills those voids, restores full support, and reduces deflections, thereby improving structural integrity and slowing further cracking. Although stabilization does not increase the original design capacity or fully eliminate faulting, it mitigates the progression of corner breaks and can extend the expected service life of the slab. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Corner break is typically accompanied by loss‑of‑support symptoms such as transverse joint faulting, diagonal cracking and ultimately slab breakup; the corner break itself is one of the primary distresses observed. These failures arise because pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement generate voids beneath the concrete, especially near transverse joints and cracks at the slab corners, allowing water to infiltrate and increase deflection stresses. The resulting excessive slab deflections produce joint faulting and diagonal cracking that can propagate into a full corner break, while inadequate joint sealing or poor drainage accelerates the development of these distresses. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Monitoring and follow-up

Additional work is warranted when the treated corner continues to show signs of lost slab support such as renewed transverse‑joint faulting, accumulation of fines along joints or cracks on either lane or shoulder, the appearance of small depressions (blow holes) at joints, recurring corner breaks, or measurable slab settlement. These observable distress patterns signal that voids remain or have re‑formed beneath the slab, meaning the previous stabilization has not fully restored support and further repair or rehabilitation is required. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)
