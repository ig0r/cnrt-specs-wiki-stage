Geogrid

Part of: Pavement Structure is-subtopic-of
Geogrid is a topic under Pavement Structure.
geogridgeogrids
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Geogrid is a polymeric geosynthetic consisting of a regular network of integrally connected longitudinal and transverse tensile ribs that create large apertures capable of mechanically interlocking with surrounding soil, rock or aggregate particles[1][2]. It is installed at the subgrade‑aggregate interface to reinforce soft or weak pavement subgrades, providing stabilization, confinement, load‑distribution pads and improved bearing capacity that extend pavement service life[1][2]. Geogrids are supplied as rolls of net‑like material classified as Class 1 uniaxial or Classes 2/3 biaxial, fabricated from polypropylene for stabilization or polyester for reinforcement, and must meet a minimum tensile strength of 2,000 lb/ft in each direction while being protected from direct sunlight, UV radiation, temperatures above 140 °F and debris to preserve durability[2].

Material purpose and applicability

In the Pavement Policy Manual, section 2.9 Experimental Pavement Construction defines a geogrid as a type of geosynthetic material applied to reinforce soft subgrade soil and aggregate layers, thereby adding structure to the pavement; when employed for soil reinforcement it is treated as an experimental construction item unless it falls within PennDOT’s standard uses. Section 6.2 Subgrade Soils further describes the geogrid as a net‑like geosynthetic with apertures large enough for surrounding soil, rock or aggregate particles to interlock, and it may be used at the interface between the subgrade and overlying aggregate layers to stabilize weak pavement subgrades, functioning primarily as reinforcement. In Section 738—GEOGRIDS of the Specifications, a geogrid is described as a polymeric biaxial geosynthetic composed of a regular network of integrally connected longitudinal and transverse tensile ribs that create apertures large enough to interlock mechanically with surrounding soil or aggregate. When placed in the subgrade or subbase it provides stabilization, confinement, load‑distribution pads and minor slope reinforcement, thereby improving bearing capacity and extending pavement life. Polypropylene biaxial grids are required for stabilization (confinement) applications, whereas polyester geogrids are required for reinforcement applications such as load distribution pads or slope support, and the specification calls for a tensile strength not less than 2,000 lb/ft in either direction. In Section 224—GEOSYNTHETIC STABILIZED BRIDGE APPROACH the geogrid is identified as a biaxial Class 2, Type A geosynthetic that serves as the stabilization geosynthetic in a Geosynthetic Stabilized Bridge Approach; it is laid over the prepared subgrade and slope face to reinforce the fill, after which a Class 4, Type A geotextile is placed behind the geogrid for protection before backfilling in compacted layers to create a stable embankment. [1][2]

Composition and characteristics

A geogrid is a net‑like geosynthetic that consists of intersecting ribs forming apertures large enough to interlock with surrounding unbound materials such as soil, rock, and aggregate; it is placed at the subgrade‑aggregate interface to reinforce and stabilize weak pavement subgrades. Geogrids are supplied as identified rolls of synthetic grid material that fall into Class 1 uniaxial or Class 2/3 biaxial types. They must be manufactured from polymeric coated polyester (PET), high‑density polyethylene (HDPE) or polypropylene (PP) and the resin may not contain any post‑consumer recycled material. The grids consist of a regular network of integrally connected longitudinal and transverse polymer tensile elements whose aperture geometry is sufficient to permit mechanical interlock with the surrounding material; the ribs are stiff and are joined by integrally formed or welded connections. For Class 1 uniaxial geogrid the grid must be placed in the direction of primary reinforcement (machine or roll direction) and the maximum allowable vertical spacing is 2 feet, while adjacent layers must overlap at least 3 inches. The performance of a Class 1 uniaxial geogrid is quantified by an ultimate tensile strength expressed as a minimum average roll value from twelve months of manufacturer testing; a creep reduction factor based on the ratio of ultimate to creep‑limiting strength and reduction factors for installation damage are calculated as TU/Tdam and shall never be less than 1.3, durability factors are applied, and the allowable long‑term tensile strength may not exceed the maximum allowable strain of 5 percent for a 100‑year design life. For Class 2/3 biaxial geogrids the grid provides reinforcement in both directions; the maximum allowable vertical spacing is 12 inches and edges must overlap a minimum of 1 foot. Class 2 biaxial geogrids are manufactured from either polypropylene (PP) for stabilization or polyester (PET) for reinforcement, and the tensile strength requirement specifies a TU not less than 2,000 lb/ft in either the machine‑direction or cross‑direction. The grid must be laid flat, free of wrinkles or stretching, and must be protected from direct sunlight, UV rays, temperatures greater than 140°F, mud, dirt, dust and debris as required by ASTM D4873. [1][2]

Variations in the polymer composition directly dictate the intended function: polypropylene biaxial grids are required for stabilization or confinement, whereas polyester geogrids are required for reinforcement such as load‑distribution pads or slope reinforcement. The manufacturing process also influences the product class; Class 2 Type A and B biaxial geogrids are fabricated with stiff ribs whose connections may be integrally formed or welded, yet both must satisfy the tensile strength requirement of at least 2,000 lb/ft in either the MD or XD direction. Proper handling and storage further affect durability, because the material must be protected from direct sunlight, ultra‑violet rays, temperature greater than 140F, mud, dirt, dust, and debris. [2]

Key material properties

Geogrids function primarily as reinforcement by providing a net‑like structure whose apertures are sufficiently large to interlock with surrounding unbound materials such as soil, rock and aggregate; their performance therefore depends on this aperture geometry and the overall quality of the product as defined by the applicable specifications [1]. The physical configuration required by the specifications is a regular network of integrally connected longitudinal and transverse polymer tensile elements whose aperture geometry permits mechanical interlock with the surrounding material, and the grid must be placed flat and free of wrinkles or stretching while being anchored as required; it also must retain its integrity by being free of defects such as tears, punctures, flaws or any deterioration that could arise during manufacture, transport, storage or installation [2]. For uniaxial reinforcement in mechanically stabilized earth (MSE) wall applications the maximum allowable vertical spacing may not exceed 2 feet, whereas for biaxial reinforcement the maximum vertical spacing may not exceed 12 inches; overlap lengths must be at least 3 inches in the primary direction or at least 1 foot when overlapping both machine and cross‑machine directions, and the material must be protected from mud, dirt, dust and debris [2]. Mechanically, a Class 2 biaxial geogrid must provide a minimum tensile strength (TU) of not less than 2,000 lb/ft in either the machine‑direction or transverse direction, while for Class 1 uniaxial products the ultimate tensile strength serves as a baseline average roll value; creep behavior is expressed by the creep reduction factor (RFCR), calculated as the ratio of ultimate to creep‑limiting strength, and installation damage is accounted for with an installation damage reduction factor (RFID) that must never be less than 1.3 [2]. The allowable tensile strength (TL) is derived from TU divided by the combined reduction factor, and strain is limited to a maximum of 5 percent for a 100‑year design life when applying the combined reduction factor [2]. Chemically and thermally, the geogrid must be shielded from direct sunlight, ultraviolet radiation and temperatures greater than 140°F during handling, storage and service to avoid UV‑induced degradation or thermal softening, and it must be kept away from debris that could damage the material [2].

[2] For Classes 2 and 3 biaxial geogrids the specification limits the maximum vertical spacing to 12 inches and prescribes minimum overlap lengths as required for each class. [2] Within the biaxial category Class 2 geogrids are divided into Types A, B or C based on material (polypropylene for stabilization, polyester for reinforcement), rib stiffness and whether the longitudinal and transverse tensile elements are integrally formed or welded. Behavior and mechanisms

Under sustained tensile loading the Class 1 uni‑axial geogrid is characterized by its ultimate tensile strength (TU) which is reduced for long‑term service by three separate factors: a creep reduction factor (RFCR), an installation damage reduction factor (RFID) that must never be less than 1.3, and a durability reduction factor (RFD). The product of these three gives the combined reduction factor (RFOV); allowable tensile strength TL is obtained as TU divided by RFOV or the 5 % elongation strength T5, whichever is lower, and TL must meet or exceed the required design tensile strength Td. For a 100‑year design life the geogrid may be strained no more than 5 % when the combined reduction factor is applied. [2]

[2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 720–722)

The behavior of a geogrid in pavement applications is governed primarily by its physical characteristics, namely the net‑like structure with apertures sized to interlock with surrounding unbound soils, rock or aggregate, which provides a physical lock and enables load distribution across weak subgrade. [1] In the Pennsylvania specifications, the same aperture geometry is described as a regular network of longitudinal and transverse polymer tensile elements whose size permits mechanical interlock with the surrounding material. [2] Chemical control is introduced through the selection of polymer type, with polypropylene grids specified for stabilization (confinement) applications and polyester grids for reinforcement, each having inherent resistance to degradation when protected from sunlight, ultraviolet rays and temperatures greater than 140 °F. [2] The backfill material that contacts the geogrid must be free of organic matter and other deleterious substances, possess an angle of internal friction not less than 34 degrees, a maximum plasticity index of 3, and a pH between 5.0 and 9.0, thereby limiting swelling and polymer degradation. [2] Mechanical performance requirements include a minimum tensile capacity of 2,000 lb/ft in both the machine‑direction and cross‑direction for Class 2 biaxial geogrids, with the ultimate tensile strength serving as the baseline capacity. [2] For Class 1 uni‑axial geogrids, long‑term tensile performance is limited by a combined reduction factor composed of creep reduction, installation‑damage reduction and durability reduction; the installation‑damage reduction factor must never be less than 1.3 and the allowable strain is capped at 5 percent for a 100‑year design life when the combined reduction factor is applied. [2] In Section 221 the geogrid installation requires overlaps be no less than 3 inches (or one foot where required). [2] In Section 224 the required overlap is at least 12 inches, and splicing is prohibited. [2] The backfill must be placed after the geogrid has been covered with an eight‑inch loose lift, traffic or equipment may not be allowed on the grid until this cover is provided, and the maximum allowable drop height of backfill is three feet. [2] Vertical spacing between successive layers of geogrid shall be maintained at two feet for Class 1 uni‑axial grids and twelve inches for biaxial classes. [2] Aggregate placement over an exposed geogrid must be non‑destructive, such as hand placement, light rain from an excavator bucket, or gradual blading, to avoid puncture or tear of the material. [2]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 190–204), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 720–723)

Durability and deterioration

Geogrids are susceptible to several durability concerns that can arise during handling, storage, installation and service. Exposure to direct sunlight, ultraviolet radiation, or temperatures greater than 140 °F must be avoided because these conditions contribute to material deterioration; the same protection is required against contact with mud, dirt, dust and debris. The specification also requires compliance at a minimum with ASTM D4873 for identification, storage and handling of geogrid. Mechanical damage such as tears, punctures, wrinkles, stretching or deformation can occur if the grid is mishandled, placed under traffic before being covered, subjected to a backfill drop height exceeding 3 feet, or crossed by tracked equipment making sudden turns; any geogrid showing such defects must be rejected and replaced. Handling on‑site must also avoid dropping aggregate directly onto the exposed sheet and vehicle traffic is not permitted until at least 6 inches of loose backfill has been placed, both actions being capable of mechanically injuring the grid. Small damaged zones under 3 square feet may be patched with matching material, but damage exceeding that size requires full removal and replacement. Geogrids installed in MSE walls must be protected from backfill that contains organic matter or other deleterious substances because such material can compromise the reinforcement; the backfill shall have a maximum plasticity index of 3 and a pH between 5.0 and 9.0 to avoid chemical attack, and materials such as clay lumps, friable particles, coal, coke, metallurgical slag or cinders are prohibited. Degradation and damage considerations are incorporated through reduction factors; the creep reduction factor (RFCR) is defined as the ratio of ultimate strength to creep‑limiting strength, an installation damage reduction factor (RFID) is applied for handling effects and must never be less than 1.3, and a durability reduction factor (RFD) is taken from the minimum value in Table B unless a higher project‑specific or product‑specific value is justified. The allowable tensile strength is obtained by dividing the ultimate tensile strength by the combined reduction factor (RFCR × RFID × RFD), and the strain limit is capped at 5 percent for a 100‑year design life. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 190–204), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 720–723)

Geogrid susceptibility to deterioration is increased by several exposure conditions and material interactions that are defined in the various sections of the report. In Section 221—Extensible Reinforcement for MSE Walls, Construction (Section 221.3), geogrid becomes more susceptible when it is exposed to direct sunlight or ultraviolet radiation, temperatures greater than 140 °F, and contamination by mud, dirt, dust, or debris, and when it suffers mechanical damage such as traffic or equipment contact before being covered with at least an eight‑inch lift of backfill, when backfill drop heights exceed the maximum of three feet, or when deformation results from tracked equipment making sudden turns. The same section (Section 221.2 Material) states that durability is reduced when the backfill contains organic matter, clay lumps, friable particles, coal, coke, metallurgical slag or cinders, or when the fines have a plasticity index exceeding three, a pH outside the range of 5.0 to 9.0, or an internal friction angle less than thirty‑four degrees; using natural backfill free of those deleterious substances and providing backfill that meets the limits of PI ≤ 3, pH 5.0–9.0, and internal friction angle ≥ 34° helps to limit deterioration. Section 738—Geogrids, Class 2 Biaxial Geogrids (Section 738.3) controls susceptibility by mandating protection from direct sunlight, ultraviolet rays, temperatures greater than 140 °F, and mud, dirt, dust, and debris, and it requires proper handling and storage in accordance with ASTM D4873 to maintain the specified tensile properties. Section 738.1 Polymer Requirements specifies that geogrids must be manufactured from polymeric coated polyester (PET), high‑density polyethylene (HDPE) or polypropylene (PP) polymers and expressly prohibits any post‑consumer recycled material in the polymer resins, thereby defining a material characteristic that influences susceptibility to deterioration. Finally, Section 224—Geosynthetic Stabilized Bridge Approach, Construction (Section 224.3) notes that geogrid is more likely to deteriorate when it already contains defects such as tears, punctures, flaws, or prior deterioration from manufacture, transportation, storage, or installation, and when it is exposed to temperatures greater than 140 °F, direct sunlight, or debris that can damage the material; susceptibility is reduced by keeping geosynthetic materials protected from those conditions, storing them elevated off the ground surface, and rejecting any damaged pieces for replacement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 190–204), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 720–723)

Material interactions and compatibility

Geogrids may be used at the interface between subgrade and aggregate layers to stabilize weak pavement subgrades, with their net‑like apertured structure allowing interlocking with surrounding unbound materials such as soil, rock, and aggregate, thereby functioning primarily as reinforcement of the subgrade [1]. The interaction is therefore a mechanical one—providing load distribution and stability through physical interlock rather than chemical bonding with cement, binders, or other reactive components [1]. In Class 2 biaxial geogrids the polymer ribs are spaced to create apertures that mechanically interlock with the surrounding soil or subbase material, providing confinement for stabilization when polypropylene is used and reinforcement for load‑distribution pads or slope support when polyester is employed; this physical interlock is the primary way the geogrid interacts with adjacent granular media and the specification does not address chemical or cementitious interactions such as binder‑aggregate reactivity or paste demand [2]. For extensible reinforcement of MSE walls, the backfill must be natural, free of organic matter and other deleterious substances, and meet the Type C coarse‑aggregate or better criteria while excluding clay lumps, friable particles, coal, coke, metallurgical slag, and cinders [2]. The fines content is required to be determined by a wash test, and the material must have a maximum plasticity index of 3, a pH between 5.0 and 9.0, and an angle of internal friction not less than 34 degrees, ensuring adequate shear strength and drainage for effective load transfer through the geogrid [2]. In a Geosynthetic Stabilized Bridge Approach, the Class 2 Type A biaxial geogrid is installed directly on the prepared subgrade and must be placed before the continuous strip of Class 4 Type A geotextile that backs the slope face; the geogrid must lie flat, be pulled tight and anchored until backfill is placed [2]. Aggregate may not be dumped onto the exposed geosynthetic – it must be introduced in a non‑destructive manner such as hand placement, light rain from an excavator bucket, or gradual blading, to avoid wrinkling or damage during interaction with the coarse aggregate [2]. Vehicles are prohibited on the geogrid until at least 6 inches of loose backfill cover it, ensuring that interaction with the coarse aggregate does not cause wrinkling or damage [2].

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 190–204), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 722–723)

For geogrid installations, the backfill must be free of organic matter and other deleterious substances because such contaminants can impair reinforcement performance. The fill also must contain no clay lumps, friable particles, coal, coke, metallurgical slag or cinders, which could affect drainage or chemically interact with the grid. Moreover, the backfill’s plasticity index may not exceed 3 and its pH must stay between 5.0 and 9.0 to prevent excessive swelling or aggressive chemical attack that might compromise the geogrid. [2]

Influence of production and placement

Within the requirements of Section 221—Extensible Reinforcement for MSE Walls, correct placement of the geogrid is critical; the grid must be laid flat, free of wrinkles, and anchored where needed, with overlaps that meet the minimum requirements (at least 3 inches for uniaxial and 1 foot in both directions for biaxial) and vertical spacing not exceeding the limits set for each class (2 feet for Class 1 uniaxial and 12 inches for Class 2/3 biaxial). The geogrid may not be subjected to traffic or equipment until it is covered by an initial 8‑inch loose backfill lift, after which backfill must be placed with a drop height no greater than 3 feet and compacted as specified; improper compaction or excessive drop heights can deform or damage the grid and reduce its reinforcing effectiveness, and the source does not address mixing, finishing, or curing practices for geogrid. In Section 224—Geosynthetic Stabilized Bridge Approach, the performance of the geogrid depends on careful placement and compaction rather than mixing or curing; the geosynthetic must be laid flat, pulled tight and anchored before backfill, positioned within ±½ inch of the design elevation, and direct dumping of aggregate onto it is prohibited. The required lift must be placed gently by hand, lightly raining from an excavator bucket, or gradual blading to avoid damage, and backfill is then compacted in two 6‑inch layers, building a stepped fill while preserving the integrity of the geogrid; the surface is graded without wrinkling the material, ensuring that the geogrid remains continuous and undistorted and thereby providing the intended reinforcement and load distribution. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 190–204)

Long‑term performance of a geogrid is most affected by construction‑related defects such as tears, punctures, flaws or any deterioration that occurs during manufacture, transport, storage or installation, and these defective materials shall be rejected; the grid must be placed flat and free of wrinkles, stretching and inadequate anchoring, and it shall remain free of traffic or equipment until it has been covered with one lift (8‑inch loose layer) of backfill, while the backfill drop height must not exceed 3 feet. Overlap requirements for this application are no less than 3 inches for uniaxial grids and a minimum of 1 foot for biaxial grids, with maximum spacings of 2 feet and 12 inches respectively. The most critical variations for Class 2 biaxial geogrids are damage to the polymer ribs or contamination of the apertures, which can be caused by exposure to direct sunlight, ultra‑violet rays, temperature greater than 140 °F, mud, dirt, dust or debris during handling, and a regular network of integrally connected longitudinal and transverse tensile elements with aperture geometry sufficient for mechanical interlock must be provided. Construction‑induced damage such as exposure to temperatures greater than 140 °F, direct sunlight or debris that can create tears, punctures, flaws or deterioration during manufacture, transportation, storage or installation shall be prevented and any geosynthetic with such defects must be rejected; splicing of any geosynthetic, including seams or connections, is prohibited, a minimum 12‑inch overlap (or at least 4 feet from a wrapped edge before an overlap) shall be provided, the material shall be placed within ±½‑inch of the design elevation, and it must be protected from direct truck dumping, wrinkling during grading and vehicle traffic until at least 6 inches of loose backfill is placed. For Class 1 uniaxial geogrids, reductions in tensile capacity caused by installation damage are reflected in an Installation Damage Reduction Factor that must never be less than 1.3; durability loss and creep are accounted for by the Durability Reduction Factor and Creep Reduction Factor respectively, the ultimate tensile strength shall be based on a minimum average roll value from twelve months of quality‑control testing, and the allowable strain is limited to 5 percent for a 100‑year design life. The backfill placed behind the geogrid must be free of organic matter and other deleterious substances, shall not contain clay lumps, friable particles, coal, coke, metallurgical slag or cinders, the fine‑fraction plasticity index shall not exceed 3, the pH shall be between 5.0 and 9.0, and the angle of internal friction shall be not less than 34 degrees. Selection, specification, and improvement

Selection of a geogrid is guided by the design requirements for stabilizing weak subgrades at the subgrade‑aggregate interface and must be based on the properties, design specifications, product data and construction procedures documented in Publication 408 Sections 221 and 738, Publication 35 (Bulletin 15), and the manufacturer’s published technical information. Factors that favor choosing a geogrid include its ease and speed of installation, the broader range of available types, brands and quality, and the reduced material cost resulting from strong market competition. The first step in selecting a geogrid is to identify the reinforcement class required for the project; the class dictates which polymer type and grid configuration are permissible and ensures that the geogrid will be compatible with the backfill prescribed for that class. For MSE wall applications the class must be identified and the geogrid chosen in accordance with Section 738, while for a geosynthetic‑stabilized bridge approach the specification requires a Class 2, Type A biaxial geogrid as defined in Section 738.3. All geogrids must be listed in Bulletin 15 and manufactured from one of the approved polymers—polymeric coated polyester (PET), high‑density polyethylene (HDPE) or polypropylene (PP)—in accordance with the indicated class and type, and the resin may not contain any post‑consumer recycled content. When the intended function is subgrade or subbase stabilization the specification calls for a polypropylene biaxial grid, whereas reinforcement functions such as load‑distribution pads or slope support require a polyester biaxial grid. The selected grid must present a regular network of longitudinal and transverse polymer tensile elements with aperture geometry sufficient to allow mechanical interlock with the surrounding material. For biaxial grids the tensile‑strength requirement is an ultimate tensile strength (TU) not less than 2,000 lb/ft in either the machine direction or cross direction where required, and the grid must conform to the property limits listed in Table C, be sampled according to ASTM D4354, and be identified, stored and protected in accordance with ASTM D4873. For Class 1 uniaxial geogrids the designer must first verify that the ultimate tensile strength meets the minimum average roll value derived from twelve months of manufacturer testing; the creep reduction factor (RFCR) is calculated from the ratio of ultimate to creep‑limiting strength, and an installation damage reduction factor (RFID) that for coarse backfill cannot be less than 1.3 must be applied together with a durability reduction factor (RFD). The allowable long‑term tensile strength (TL) is obtained by dividing TU by the combined reduction factor (RFCR × RFID × RFD); TL must not fall below the required design tensile strength (Td) and the strain may not exceed 5 % for a 100‑year design life. For uniaxial grids used in MSE walls the grid must be placed in the primary reinforcement direction with a maximum vertical spacing of 2 feet, and adjacent layers must be overlapped at least 3 inches. For biaxial grids the vertical spacing may not exceed 12 inches and edges must overlap a minimum of 1 foot in both directions after a Class 4 Type A geotextile is laid on a smooth, debris‑free subgrade. Backfill may be dropped no more than 3 feet onto the grid and must be covered with at least an eight‑inch loose lift before traffic. Overlap between adjacent strips of stabilization geosynthetic and the backing geotextile must be a minimum of 12 inches, and placement shall be within ±½ inch of plan elevation. Each roll must be clearly identified with product designation, manufacturer name, lot number, material quantity, model designation, manufacturing plant and lot number; rolls must be stored away from direct sunlight, ultraviolet rays, temperatures greater than 140°F, mud, dirt, dust and debris. Any geosynthetic showing defects must be rejected, and repairs are permitted only for damaged areas under 3 sq ft using a patch that extends at least 3 feet beyond the damage. [1][2]

To improve geogrid performance designers are advised to select higher‑quality geogrids from the expanding range of types and brands and to follow the detailed requirements set out in Publication 408 (Sections 221 and 738) together with Publication 35, using manufacturer‑provided data to define material properties, design specifications, product selection and construction procedures as a quality‑control measure[1]. When reinforcement or separation is required but a geogrid may be unsuitable, a Class 4 geotextile—Type A for layer separation or Type C for combined separation and lateral restraint—can serve as an alternative material[1], and additional subgrade strengthening options such as cement or lime stabilization or rock capping modeled with CBR = 20 or a structural coefficient of 0.12 may be employed to mitigate weak subgrades[1].

Section 221 of the specifications mandates that each roll be clearly identified in accordance with ASTM D4873 and protected from direct sunlight, ultraviolet radiation, temperatures greater than 140°F, mud, dirt, dust and debris; any geogrid exhibiting defects, tears, punctures, flaws, deterioration or damage incurred during manufacture, transportation, storage or installation must be rejected and replaced at no cost[2]. During placement the grid shall lie flat, free of wrinkles, with overlaps not less than three inches (and at least one foot on edges for biaxial types), vertical spacing limited to two feet for uniaxial grids and twelve inches for biaxial grids; it may not be loaded until covered by an eight‑inch loose backfill lift, the backfill drop height shall not exceed three feet, and tracked equipment should avoid sudden turns that could deform or damage the grid[2].

For Class 2 biaxial geogrids the specification improves performance by requiring a polymer type matched to function: polypropylene grids are required for stabilization and confinement applications, whereas polyester grids are required for reinforcement such as load‑distribution pads or slope support; the design calls for stiff ribs with connections that are either integrally formed or welded and an aperture geometry large enough to lock mechanically with surrounding material[2]. For Class 1 uniaxial geogrids tighter quality control can be achieved by computing the minimum average roll value (MARV) as the twelve‑month average ultimate tensile strength minus two standard deviations, and designers may specify creep, installation‑damage and durability reduction factors that exceed the Table B minima; the combined reduction factor shall never be less than 1.3 for RFID, the maximum allowable strain is five percent for a 100‑year design life, and the minimum reduction factor for durability (RFD) indicated in Table B should be used unless project‑specific conditions or a specific geogrid indicate a higher value[2].

Geogrids must be manufactured from polymeric‑coated polyester (PET), high‑density polyethylene (HDPE) or polypropylene (PP) as listed in Bulletin 15 and detailed in Table A, and the use of any post‑consumer recycled material in the polymer resin is prohibited, providing an additional quality‑control measure[2]. For Class 1 installations the surrounding backfill shall meet strict quality criteria: it shall be Type C coarse aggregate or better, free of clay lumps, friable particles, coal, coke, metallurgical slag or cinders; fines content shall be verified by a wash test per PTM No. 100 in addition to PTM No. 616, the plasticity index shall not exceed three, the pH shall remain between 5.0 and 9.0, and the angle of internal friction for material finer than a No. 8 sieve shall be at least thirty‑four degrees; acceptable alternatives for Class 2 and Class 3 geogrids include AASHTO No. 57 coarse aggregate or PennDOT No. 2A coarse aggregate[2].

In a geosynthetic‑stabilized bridge approach (Section 224) performance of the geogrid can be further enhanced by protecting it from temperatures greater than 140°F, shielding it from direct sunlight and debris, and storing it elevated; any geosynthetic showing defects, tears, punctures, flaws, deterioration or damage incurred during manufacture, transportation, storage or installation must be rejected and replaced at no additional cost[2]. When a Class 2 Type A biaxial geogrid is used as the stabilization layer, backing it with a full‑width Class 4 Type A geotextile on the slope face adds stability, all layers shall overlap adjacent strips by at least twelve inches and be placed within ±½ inch of the design elevation; small damaged zones under three square feet may be repaired with a patch of the same material extending at least three feet beyond the defect, whereas larger damaged areas require complete removal and replacement[2].

Performance evaluation and implications

Evaluation of Class 1 uniaxial geogrids relies on laboratory tensile testing to obtain a minimum average roll value (MARV) for ultimate tensile strength (TU) using ASTM D6637 Method B, and on standardized creep tests per AASHTO R 69, ASTM D5262 and ASTM D6992 to calculate the creep reduction factor (RFCR). Installation damage is quantified with an installation‑damage reduction factor (RFID) derived from AASHTO R 69, which must never be less than 1.3, while durability is addressed through a durability reduction factor (RFD) taken from Table B or project‑specific data. The long‑term allowable tensile strength (TL) is then computed as TU divided by the combined reduction factor (RFCR × RFID × RFD), or limited to the MARV at 5 % elongation, whichever is lower, and must satisfy a maximum strain of 5 % for a 100‑year design life. [2]

In PennDOT projects geogrids are permitted primarily to reinforce soft subgrade soil and aggregate layers, thereby adding structural capacity to the pavement; when a geogrid is employed for any other soil‑reinforcement purpose it is treated as an experimental construction item, so its design parameters must be established on a project‑by‑project basis using FHWA guidelines and related technical references, and the use must be coordinated through the BOCM Materials Division. The presence of geogrid influences pavement design by requiring specific reinforcement specifications and impacts construction planning and approval procedures. Geogrids are installed at the subgrade‑aggregate interface where their net‑like structure, with apertures large enough to interlock with surrounding soil, rock or aggregate, provides primary reinforcement that stabilizes weak pavement subgrades and improves load distribution. Because they can be placed quickly and benefit from a growing variety of high‑quality, competitively priced products, geogrids simplify construction while contributing to longer‑lasting pavements; their selection and design must follow the specifications outlined in Publication 408 (Sections 221 and 738) and related product data. Section 221—Extensible reinforcement for MSE walls requires the geogrid to be placed flat, free of wrinkles, and protected from direct sunlight, ultraviolet rays, temperatures greater than 140 °F, mud, dirt, dust, and debris; any defect or damage requires replacement because compromised material would reduce reinforcement effectiveness. For uniaxial grids the vertical spacing may not exceed 2 feet and adjacent layers must overlap at least 3 inches, while biaxial grids are limited to a 12‑inch vertical spacing with a minimum one‑foot edge overlap, ensuring continuous load transfer across the base course. The grid cannot be loaded until it is covered by an eight‑inch lift of backfill and the backfill drop height must not exceed three feet, preventing deformation that would impair its tensile behavior. Section 738—Geogrids specifies that polypropylene biaxial grids are required for confinement applications and polyester geogrids are required for reinforcement such as load‑distribution pads or slope reinforcement, so material choice directly governs the stabilizing function of the pavement structure. The geogrid must consist of a regular network of longitudinal and transverse polymer tensile elements with aperture geometry sufficient to allow mechanical interlock with the surrounding material, which promotes uniform load distribution and enhances base course performance. Tensile strength requirements include a TU not less than 2,000 lb/ft in either the MD or XD direction for the applicable class, ensuring that the grid can sustain traffic loads without excessive deformation, contributing to durability and design reliability. Handling and storage must protect the geogrid from direct sunlight, ultraviolet rays, temperatures greater than 140 °F, mud, dirt, dust, and debris because exposure to these conditions could degrade its mechanical properties and affect long‑term maintenance needs. Section 224—Geosynthetic stabilized bridge approach mandates that the geogrid be kept below 140 °F, shielded from direct sunlight and debris, and any material showing tears or punctures must be rejected, ensuring the reinforcement retains its tensile strength throughout service. Design calls for continuous sheets with at least a 12‑inch overlap and no splicing; installation tolerances require the geogrid to lie within ±½ inch of the specified elevation and to remain flat and taut until backfill is placed, and vehicles are prohibited until a minimum of six inches of loose fill covers it, preventing deformation that could reduce load distribution. If damage occurs, patches using the same geosynthetic must extend at least three feet beyond the defect for areas under three square feet, whereas larger damaged zones must be fully removed and replaced, preserving long‑term performance.
