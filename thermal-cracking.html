Thermal Cracking

Part of: Cracking is-subtopic-of
Thermal Cracking is a topic under Cracking.
thermal-crackinglow-temperature-crackinglow-temperature-crackingthermal-cracksthermal-cracking-low-temperature-cracking
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Thermal cracking, also referred to as shrinkage cracking, is a distress that develops in asphalt pavements when temperature variations make the material brittle during cold weather, producing visible fracture lines on the surface[1]. It appears as a regular pattern of relatively evenly spaced cracks whose spacing does not exceed 15 feet, and it is distinguished from moisture‑related, support‑loss, random or alligator cracking because it forms regardless of drainage conditions[1]. Although proper drainage does not slow the formation of these cracks, water can infiltrate along them and cause adjacent moisture‑associated damage that reduces ride quality[1].

Description and identification

Thermal cracking, referred to in the source as shrinkage cracking, is a distress that develops when temperature variations make the asphalt pavement brittle during cold weather. The resulting cracks are visible on the pavement surface as fracture lines where the material has split. While proper drainage does not slow the formation of these thermal cracks, it can limit moisture‑related damage adjacent to them. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59)

In section 2.3 DRAINAGE, thermal cracking in asphalt pavements is recognized as shrinkage cracks that appear during cold weather when temperature fluctuations make the pavement brittle; it is distinguished from moisture‑related or support‑loss cracks because the cracks develop regardless of drainage conditions, although poor drainage may cause additional moisture damage adjacent to the thermal crack. In section 2.9 EXPERIMENTAL PAVEMENT CONSTRUCTION, thermal cracking is recognized by a regular pattern of relatively evenly spaced cracks whose spacing does not exceed 15 feet, and it is distinguished from random or alligator cracking because the pavement surface must not exhibit excessive cracking or extensive alligator cracks when thermal cracks are present, with the spacing limit and the absence of severe interlocking crack patterns providing the basis for differentiation. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–63)

Where it occurs

Thermal cracking is reported as a distress of asphalt pavement, arising from temperature fluctuations and cold‑weather brittleness that cause shrinkage cracks; concrete pavements are not mentioned in connection with this distress. In the context of stress‑absorbing membrane (SAM) and SAM interlayer applications, thermal cracking is considered only on flexible pavements with flexible bases, and the spacing between thermal cracks may not exceed 15 feet. The phenomenon is specifically addressed for Superpave asphalt paving courses—including wearing, binder, and base layers—where the mixture design process is intended to resist low‑temperature (thermal) cracking as part of overall performance criteria. Thermal cracking is also listed among the typical surface distresses affecting existing asphalt streets, intersections, and road sections that are candidates for thin bonded Portland cement concrete overlays. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–63), Ch. 5 (pp. 111–113), Ch. 10 (p. 186)

Causes and mechanisms

According to the drainage subsection, thermal cracking—referred to as shrinkage cracks—is primarily a climate‑related distress caused by temperature variations and the brittleness of asphalt in cold weather; it is not attributed to traffic loading, moisture or drainage conditions, nor to design or construction deficiencies. The text also notes that while proper drainage does not slow the formation of thermal cracks, it can limit moisture‑associated damage adjacent to those cracks. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59)

Thermal cracking, described as shrinkage cracks, develops when temperature variations cause the asphalt to contract and the pavement becomes brittle during cold weather, allowing those contractions to open cracks; repeated thermal cycles allow the cracks to propagate further. The condition that accelerates this distress is low temperatures combined with temperature fluctuations that increase brittleness, while moisture does not retard the cracking itself but can cause additional damage adjacent to the crack if drainage is inadequate. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59)

Effects on performance

When shrinkage or thermal cracks develop in an asphalt pavement they do not stop at the line; water can infiltrate along the opening and cause moisture‑associated damage next to the crack, which shows up as surface roughness and a noticeable loss of ride quality if the drainage is inadequate. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59)

Repairs and treatments

Thermal cracking can be mitigated through several treatments. In experimental pavement construction, stress‑absorbing membrane (SAM) and stress‑absorbing membrane interlayer (SAMI) are described as flexible, full‑width seals that accommodate crack movement; they may be used only on flexible pavements with flexible bases, the pavement surface must not exhibit excessive cracking or extensive alligator cracks for SAMs, and the distance between thermal cracks cannot be more than 15 feet (section 2.9). Within the Superpave paving‑course specifications, selection of an appropriate performance‑grade binder together with the 20‑year design‑life ESAL range is intended to resist thermal cracking; districts should consider changing the ESAL range when asphalt cracking is a problem in the region (section 5.9). Asphalt fiber‑modified courses may prevent or delay crack initiation and propagation and are permitted for optional use on eligible wearing, binder, and base courses when the mix uses PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22, or PG 64E‑22 binders (Subsection D). An asphalt‑rich base course is required for all new and reconstructed pavements whose total base thickness exceeds 6 inches, providing the highest tensile strength at the bottom of the pavement to promote better resistance to bottom‑up cracking (Subsection E) (section 5.9). For overlay solutions, a thin bonded concrete overlay (Thin BCOA) is cited as appropriate for asphalt streets, intersections and roads that exhibit typical distresses including thermal cracking, and may be used as an alternative to traditional milling and asphalt‑concrete overlay when those distresses are present (section 10.8). Conventional bonded concrete overlay (Conventional BCOA) also addresses thermal cracking and is indicated where a significant increase in structural capacity or pavement life extension is desired without resorting to thick asphalt overlays or full reconstruction (section 10.8). [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 62–63), Ch. 5 (pp. 111–113), Ch. 10 (p. 186)

In Chapter 2, Section 2.9 of the source, SAM or SAMI applications are allowed only on flexible pavements that do not exhibit excessive cracking or extensive alligator cracks and where the spacing between thermal cracks does not exceed 15 feet; if either of those conditions is present the treatment is ruled out. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 62–63)

Prevention in design and construction

In the experimental pavement construction guidance (section 2.9), a stress‑absorbing membrane (SAM) or stress‑absorbing membrane interlayer (SAMI) is recommended to mitigate thermal cracking by limiting the spacing of thermal cracks; the measure is intended to reduce crack propagation when the distance between thermal cracks does not exceed 15 feet, may be applied only on flexible pavements with flexible bases, and requires that the pavement surface not exhibit excessive cracking or extensive alligator cracks before a SAM or SAMI is used. The Superpave paving course specifications (section 5.9) state that the volumetric asphalt mixture design—implemented by specifying a performance‑grade binder and calculating the 20‑year design‑life ESAL range for the project—produces a mix that is able to resist low‑temperature (thermal) cracking; this applies whenever the project’s binder grade and ESALs are defined as required in the Superpave paving course specifications. In addition, asphalt fiber‑modified courses that incorporate aramid fibers may prevent or delay crack initiation and propagation, including thermal cracks, when they are used on eligible wearing, binder or base courses with PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22 or PG 64E‑22 binders as listed in the FMC guidance. The bonded concrete overlay provisions (section 10.8) identify thin bonded concrete overlay (Thin BCOA) as a design measure that can reduce thermal cracking by serving as an alternative to traditional milling and asphalt overlay on asphalt streets, intersections, or roads that exhibit typical distresses such as rutting, shoving, slippage, and thermal cracking; it may be used when the concrete thickness is no less than 2.5 inches and no more than 4 inches. Conventional bonded concrete overlay (Conventional BCOA) is also listed as appropriate for the same situations because it includes all applications shown for thin BCOA, and additionally may be selected where significant improvements in structural capacity or pavement life extension are desired without thick asphalt overlays or reconstruction. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 62–63), Ch. 5 (pp. 111–113), Ch. 10 (p. 186)

The use of a stress‑absorbing membrane (SAM) or stress‑absorbing membrane interlayer (SAMI) can mitigate thermal cracking by sealing and accommodating movement across existing cracks, but it should be used only on flexible pavements with flexible bases whose surface does not exhibit excessive cracking or extensive alligator cracks, and the distance between thermal cracks cannot be more than 15 feet; the SAMI must be placed directly on the existing pavement surface with any leveling courses applied over it. In Superpave paving‑course construction, properly designing the mixture to resist permanent deformation (rutting) and thermal cracking (low temperature cracking) is required by using the appropriate PG‑binder together with the ESAL range derived for a 20‑year design life for all Superpave courses where binder and ESALs are specified. Additionally, when the estimated ESAL range is 0.3 to < 3.0 million ESALs, the District may specify an ESAL range of < 0.3 million ESALs only for the wearing and binder layers if asphalt cracking is a problem in the region, the average truck speed exceeds 30 miles per hour, and rutting is not a concern, thereby targeting thermal cracking under those conditions. The optional Asphalt Fiber Modified Course that incorporates aramid fibers may prevent or delay crack initiation and propagation on eligible courses that use PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22, or PG 64E‑22 binders, providing a material requirement aimed at thermal cracking wherever those binders are selected. Finally, a thin bonded concrete overlay (Thin BCOA) with a nominal concrete thickness of no less than 2.5 inches and no more than 4 inches may be appropriate as an alternative to traditional milling and asphalt overlay for asphalt streets, intersections, or roads that exhibit typical distresses, including thermal cracking, thus addressing the distress when such typical distress is present. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 62–63), Ch. 5 (pp. 111–113), Ch. 10 (p. 186)

Specification limits and acceptance

For stress‑absorbing membrane (SAM) or SAMI experimental applications, the specification requires that the distance between thermal cracks cannot be more than 15 feet; compliance is determined by measuring crack spacing on the existing pavement surface and this limit is set in Chapter 2 Project Considerations, Subsection 2.9 Experimental Pavement Construction. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 62–63)
