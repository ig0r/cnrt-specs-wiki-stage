AASHTO 1993 Design Guide

Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

The AASHTO 1993 Design Guide provides a mechanistic‑empirical methodology that ensures pavements are structurally adequate, cost‑effective and meet prescribed serviceability and minimum thickness requirements over their design life [1]. It is applied to all new construction, reconstruction or rehabilitation projects exceeding specified length thresholds (500 ft on divided roadways, 1 000 ft on undivided roadways, or bridge approaches greater than 1 000 ft) and must be executed with the AASHTOWare® DARWin® software, incorporating traffic loading, subgrade CBR‑derived resilient modulus, reliability levels, overall standard deviation and drainage coefficients [1]. The procedure mandates achieving at least the Terminal Serviceability Index for the roadway’s Maintenance Functional Classification—adjusted for frost‑heave effects where applicable—and requires overlay designs that provide a minimum eight‑year and maximum twenty‑year structural service life when ride quality or structural adequacy must be improved [1].

Purpose and design objectives

The AASHTO 1993 Design Guide is intended to provide a methodology that ensures pavements are structurally adequate and cost‑effective for the projected traffic loads over their design life while satisfying prescribed minimum thickness standards and serviceability requirements. For PennDOT projects it must be applied whenever new construction, reconstruction or rehabilitation exceeds 500 ft continuous length on divided roadways, 1 000 ft on undivided roadways, or bridge approaches greater than 1 000 ft, and a redesign is required if the design year differs by more than 3 years from that used in the approved pavement design and Life‑Cycle Cost Analysis. Where existing pavement thickness is less than the minimum, the new pavement must meet the minimum thickness criteria. The guide also addresses weak or variable subgrade conditions and frost‑heave effects; any CBR value greater than 10 must be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and no CBR value greater than 20 may be used in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation, and soils highly susceptible to frost heave are recommended to be removed and replaced with a non‑frost‑susceptible layer thick enough to insulate the roadbed. Designs must achieve at least the Terminal Serviceability Index appropriate to the road’s Maintenance Functional Classification, with the next higher TSI value permissible when traffic volume is significantly high for the functional classification. For full‑depth flexible pavements the required terminal serviceability value from Table 6.3 is increased by a calculated frost‑heave loss derived from frost‑heave rate, maximum potential loss and probability using the figures in Appendix G; this loss is applied because the design software does not calculate it directly. Overlay designs are mandated for projects that need improved ride quality, restored structural capacity or higher surface friction, and each overlay must be sized to provide a minimum eight‑year and a maximum twenty‑year structural service life based on current ADT, projected growth and truck percentages used to compute design ESALs. The procedure incorporates a reliability percentage that accounts for variation in traffic prediction and performance prediction, and an overall standard deviation is required to capture all error and variability; the value shall be 0.35 for rigid pavement designs, 0.45 for flexible pavements, asphalt overlays of fractured PCC and flexible‑over‑flexible overlays, and 0.40 when flexible pavement is placed over a rigid base. For rigid pavement design the inputs include 18‑kip ESALs over the initial period, initial and terminal serviceability levels, concrete modulus of rupture, slab elastic modulus, effective subgrade k‑value, reliability level, overall standard deviation, load transfer coefficient and drainage coefficient, with a drainage coefficient C_d = 1.0 for unbound aggregate only, C_d = 1.1 when a treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system provides positive drainage, and C_d = 1.0 for rehabilitation projects where no significant drainage improvements are planned. For full‑depth flexible pavement design the methodology accommodates the specified traffic loading (for example 18‑kip ESALs over the initial performance period) while meeting target serviceability levels and reliability criteria, requiring input values such as initial serviceability, terminal serviceability, reliability level, overall standard deviation, roadbed soil resilient modulus and number of construction stages. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 134–152), Ch. 8 (pp. 160–163), Ch. 9 (pp. 168–169), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177)

The AASHTO 1993 Guide requires that pavement designs be based on the lowest subgrade CBR values so that the resulting structure is adequate for all areas, because using unjustified high resilient‑modulus (Mr) values may result in inadequate pavement thickness and premature failure of the pavement. All CBR values greater than 10 for use in pavement designs shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, and in no case will a CBR value greater than 20 be allowed to be used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation, thereby meeting safety and durability criteria. For soils highly susceptible to frost heave, it is recommended that they be removed and replaced with a layer of non‑frost‑susceptible material thick enough to insulate the roadbed soil from frost penetration, preserving serviceability by preventing differential heaving, loss of bearing support, and freeze/thaw damage. The design must achieve an initial serviceability level that estimates the Present Serviceability Index (PSI) immediately after construction and must meet at least the Terminal Serviceability Index (TSI) prescribed for its Maintenance Functional Classification; when traffic volumes are significantly high for the functional class, a higher TSI may be used. The MFC definitions together with their initial and terminal serviceability values are supplied in Table 6.4 and guide selection of these targets. For full‑depth flexible pavements, the required terminal serviceability index is taken from Table 6.3 and must be increased by the loss of serviceability calculated for frost heave using the procedures in Appendix G; the resulting target—such as 3.75 in the Interstate example—becomes the serviceability that the structure must attain at the end of its design life. An overlay design is required when a project is intended to improve ride quality, when the existing pavement is suspected to be structurally inadequate, or when low friction values are present; each overlay shall be designed for a structural design life of at least a minimum 8‑year and not exceed a maximum of 20 years. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–151), Ch. 9 (pp. 168–169), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177)

Scope and applicability

The 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Guide is applied to all new construction, reconstruction, and rehabilitation projects that exceed the length thresholds—more than 500 continuous feet on divided roadways or more than 1,000 continuous feet on undivided roadways—and to bridge‑approach work where paving on both sides of the structure exceeds 1,000 feet. For short pavement sections that do not meet those length criteria the proposed pavement must match the existing pavement structure where it meets minimum thickness requirements; a DARWin analysis is required only when the existing composite pavement exceeds those minima or contains elements that cannot be replicated. For PennDOT Oversight projects a rigid and a flexible design alternative should be considered. The guide covers rigid‑pavement structures such as Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement or Roller Compacted Concrete placed on a subgrade‑subbase‑base system, typically with an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base or Cement Treated Permeable Base and a draining subbase. Designs employ Class AA normal‑weight Portland cement concrete, using a mean elastic modulus of 4,000,000 pounds per square inch (corresponding to a mean compressive strength of 4,925 pounds per square inch unless local historical data show a significantly different mean compressive strength). Required inputs include 18‑kip ESALs for the initial performance period, initial and terminal serviceability indices, 28‑day mean modulus of rupture and elastic modulus, effective k‑value, reliability level, overall standard deviation (So = 0.35), load transfer coefficient and drainage coefficient. The drainage coefficient is C_d = 1.0 for rigid pavements constructed on unbound aggregate only; it is C_d = 1.1 for new construction or reconstruction projects where a subbase of treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate is combined with an underdrain system and positive drainage; rehabilitation projects that have shown substandard existing drainage and where no major drainage improvements are planned again use C_d = 1.0. The guide also applies to full‑depth flexible pavement structures. Designs require 18‑kip ESALs, initial and terminal serviceability levels, reliability level, overall standard deviation (So = 0.45 for flexible pavements, asphalt overlays on fractured PCC, and flexible overlays over flexible pavements; So = 0.40 when a flexible layer is placed over a rigid base), roadbed soil resilient modulus and the number of construction stages. The terminal serviceability value is taken from Table 6.3 and the loss of serviceability due to frost heave must be calculated; the frost‑heave rate must be taken from Table 9.1 or Table 9.2 according to subgrade soil type, and separate designs are required when markedly different soils occur. The frost‑heave probability used in Figure G.8 should be selected from the recommended range of 25%–75% for all flexible designs, and maximum potential serviceability loss is obtained by converting the Design Freezing Index to a frost‑penetration depth and reading Figure G.7 for FAIR drainage quality. Overlay projects may be designed with the guide when the purpose is to improve ride, address suspected structural inadequacy, or remedy low friction values; each overlay design targets a minimum 8‑year structural design life and a maximum of 20 years. The guide supports overlays on both asphalt concrete and Portland cement concrete pavements, including AC over AC, AC over fractured PCC slab, AC over PCC slab, AC over AC/PCC composite, bonded or unbonded PCC overlays of PCC, AC/PCC composite, or AC pavements, provided the appropriate traffic data (average daily traffic, growth, truck percentage and distribution) are available. When asphalt is placed over cement concrete or brick for the first time a minimum of 2 1/2 inches of binder material is required, with wearing‑course thicknesses taken from the chapter tables. Falling Weight Deflectometer back‑calculation may be used for the listed pavement situations and Dynamic Cone Penetration testing is limited to roadways with MFC = B, C, D & E. Frost‑heave considerations are required for full‑depth flexible pavements, and subgrade strengthening would be a cost‑effective solution whether the existing subgrade value is below or above a CBR of 5, especially if the pavement quantity is at least 25,000 SY and on a Traffic Pattern Group of 1 to 4. For reconstruction projects the guide directs that gradation, moisture content, density and CBR data for the existing subgrade or base be obtained before design. Designs must employ at least the terminal serviceability value given in the Maintenance Functional Classification table, though the next higher TSI value may be used when traffic volume is significantly high for the functional classification of the highway. Under section 6.1, if a project’s pavement length is less than or equal to 500 continuous feet on divided roadways or less than or equal to 1,000 continuous feet on undivided roadways, the design may forgo the full AASHTO 1993 procedure and simply match the existing structure provided it meets the minimum thickness requirements; however, when the existing composite pavement well‑exceeds the minimum thickness or contains elements that are not reasonable to replicate such as crack‑and‑seat, a DARWin pavement design analysis must be performed when directed by the District Pavement Management Engineer to achieve a structurally sufficient and cost‑effective solution, and for PennDOT Oversight projects the guidance suggests considering one rigid and one flexible design alternative. In section 6.2, when isolated low‑CBR zones are identified the guide advises improving those subgrade areas rather than applying a uniform AASHTO design, and if laboratory CBR results appear unrepresentative or exhibit a wide spread the designer must select the controlling (lowest) soil type or obtain additional testing before proceeding; when large portions of the project have markedly different soils and CBR values it may be permissible to divide the corridor into a limited number of subsections with separate pavement designs, provided distinct break points exist and drainage can be accommodated, while no CBR value greater than 20 will be allowed in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation and any value above 10 requires chief geotechnical engineer approval, so projects with such values must consider alternative design or more conservative assumptions, and back‑calculated resilient modulus from FWD is only permissible for specific pavement situations (full‑depth asphalt sections, certain overlays, etc.) and DCP conversion to Mr is limited to roadways with MFC = B, C, D & E, indicating that when these conditions are not met a different design approach should be employed. Finally, as stated in section 10.1, when an overlay involves a bonded concrete layer on top of existing asphalt pavement—or on an AC/PCC composite—the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures are considered inadequate because they do not account for the bonding effect, and the bonded concrete overlay mechanistic‑empirical (BCOA‑ME) procedure is preferred, with thicknesses to be determined using that BCOA‑ME method. Design inputs and requirements

The AASHTO 1993 design process, as applied by PennDOT, requires that the submittal begin with a cover letter that identifies the project’s location by MPMS number, county, state route and segment/offset and includes a location map showing the beginning and ending segments. Traffic information must be supplied as a summary table of average daily traffic and truck percentage for each design, followed by the full traffic breakdown together with its source and date. Material data are reflected in the structural design calculations and supporting DARWin printouts. Environmental considerations are recorded by noting the environmental (or design field view) approval date in the cover letter. Geotechnical input must include a CBR summary table, all Falling Weight Deflectometer data or soils test results, and a frost‑heave probability signed by the geotechnical engineer. Existing‑condition information requires the pavement history for the project limits obtained from RMS. For rigid‑pavement design the guide also requires a set of inputs that capture traffic loading, serviceability targets, concrete material properties, subgrade stiffness, reliability criteria, statistical variability, load‑transfer capability and drainage effectiveness. The required traffic input is 18‑kip ESALs over the initial performance period. Serviceability targets consist of an initial serviceability and a terminal serviceability. Concrete material properties must include the 28‑day mean PCC modulus of rupture and the 28‑day mean elastic modulus of slab. Subgrade stiffness is represented by the mean effective k‑value. The design also calls for a reliability level, an overall standard deviation, a load transfer coefficient (J) and an overall drainage coefficient (Cd). [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 134–136), Ch. 8 (p. 160)

The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation requires that every pavement design be performed in accordance with the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures as amended by the department and executed with the AASHTOWare® DARWin® 3.01 software. A DARWin analysis is mandatory when the continuous pavement length exceeds more than 500 ft on divided roadways, more than 1,000 ft on undivided roadways, or when bridge approaches total more than 1,000 ft; if the design year changes by more than three years a new design and Life‑Cycle Cost Analysis must be performed. For sections that do not trigger a DARWin analysis the proposed pavement must meet the minimum thickness criteria described in Tables 8.3 and 9.4, and when an existing structure exceeds those minima but cannot be replicated a DARWin analysis is required by the District Pavement Management Engineer. All CBR values greater than ten shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, and no CBR value greater than twenty may be used in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation. Isolated low‑CBR zones must be improved in coordination with the District Geotechnical Engineer and detailed in the PS&E specifications. Back‑calculated resilient modulus from Falling Weight Deflectometer data is permissible only for full‑depth asphalt sections, thin concrete overlays, ASR‑affected concrete, pre‑1945 concrete, or directly on subgrade/subbase; the back‑calculated value must be adjusted with a correction factor of 0.25 and verified if it exceeds fifteen thousand psi. Dynamic Cone Penetration testing is limited to roadways whose MFC is B, C, D, or E, and standard penetration test conversions to CBR are not allowed; all borrow material shall conform to the minimum specified resilient modulus. Frost‑susceptible soils should be removed or isolated with a non‑frost‑susceptible granular layer, and subgrade strengthening using cement or lime stabilization is considered when the existing subgrade CBR is below five for projects of at least twenty‑five thousand square yards on Traffic Pattern Group 1‑4. Geogrid or geotextile applications must follow Publication 408 specifications (Sections 221, 210.3(b), 212) and the approved construction materials bulletin. The design method requires an estimate of the Present Serviceability Index immediately after construction, with PSI values ranging from zero to five; new pavement, reconstructed pavement and overlay designs shall be performed using at least the terminal serviceability value given for the roadway’s Maintenance Functional Classification, and a higher TSI may be selected when traffic volumes are significantly high for that functional class. The governing performance requirement for reliability must be selected from the ranges listed in Table 6.5 by functional classification; these ranges are deliberately more restrictive than those in the original guide, and designers should utilize the upper end of the range whenever traffic predictions are less certain. An Overall Standard Deviation (S₀) is required to capture all error and variability in design and construction inputs: for rigid‑pavement designs S₀ shall be 0.35; for flexible pavements, asphalt overlays on fractured PCC pavement and flexible overlays on flexible pavements S₀ shall be 0.45; when a flexible pavement is placed over a rigid pavement S₀ shall be 0.40. Rigid‑pavement designs governed by the 1993 AASHTO Procedures must supply to DARWin an 18‑kip ESAL count for the initial performance period, target initial and terminal serviceability values, the 28‑day mean PCC modulus of rupture and slab elastic modulus, the mean effective k‑value, a specified reliability level, the appropriate overall standard deviation, the load‑transfer coefficient (J), and the overall drainage coefficient (C_d). The concrete elastic modulus is governed by the American Concrete Institute’s recommended correlation; designers should use a mean Ec of 4,000,000 psi for Class AA concrete, which corresponds to a mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi unless district historical data indicate a significantly different mean strength. For rigid pavements constructed on unbound aggregate only the guide specifies a drainage coefficient of C_d = 1.0; for new construction or reconstruction where a treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate is combined with an underdrain system and positive drainage the guide specifies C_d = 1.1; for rehabilitation projects without significant drainage improvements the guide specifies C_d = 1.0, the value used in the AASHO Road Test representing fair drainage. Full‑depth flexible pavement designs must be based on the terminal serviceability index listed in Table 6.3 and then reduced by the loss of serviceability caused by frost heave as prescribed in Appendix G, Section G.2; the frost‑heave loss is computed from three inputs: a frost‑heave rate that should be selected from Table 9.1 or Table 9.2 according to the subgrade soil type, the maximum potential serviceability loss derived from the design freezing index and Figure G.7 after determining frost‑penetration depth, and an estimated frost‑heave probability that the guide recommends be taken in the range of 25 %–75 %; these values together determine the adjusted terminal serviceability that governs the pavement structure design. Any project intended to improve ride quality, address suspected structural inadequacy, or remedy low friction values requires a pavement overlay design. All such overlay designs shall be prepared for a minimum structural design life of eight years and a maximum of twenty years while using adequate and accurate traffic information as the basis for design; when necessary traffic data must be obtained from the Bureau of Planning and Research and Form D‑4332 shall be completed and submitted to PDAU for concurrence and FHWA approval. When asphalt is placed over cement concrete or brick pavements a minimum of 2½ inches of binder material is required, with the wearing‑course thickness following table‑specified minima; however, in parking lanes the wearing course may be tapered to a depth of one inch at the curb provided compaction and joint sealing are carefully addressed. [1]

Design methods and criteria

The 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Guide is applied through the 1993 AASHTO pavement design procedures, which for Pennsylvania projects must be executed with the AASHTOWare® DARWin® 3.01 software and are described in Chapters 6 through 10 that specify the required input values for all Department of Transportation designs. Under the subgrade treatment the resilient modulus M_r is obtained from a California Bearing Ratio (CBR) using the Heukelom and Klomp correlation; normally M_r =1500 × CBR, but the guide directs multiplication by 1,000 instead of 1,500 when applying that equation, and CBR values greater than 20 are prohibited because they would produce an M_r exceeding 30,000 psi. When field measurements are available the resilient modulus may be back‑calculated from Falling Weight Deflectometer data with a correction factor C = 0.25 and conversion of dynamic k to static by dividing by two; any back‑calculated M_r above 15,000 psi must be corroborated by additional testing before use, and Dynamic Cone Penetration test results may also be converted to M_r via the CBR relationship. Serviceability predictions are based on a Present Serviceability Index (PSI) that is derived from measured pavement roughness, cracks, patches and rut depth; the initial serviceability level is taken as an estimate of PSI immediately after construction. The Terminal Serviceability Index (TSI) defines the condition at which a pavement is no longer adequate, and new pavement, reconstructed pavement and overlay designs shall be performed using at least the terminal serviceability value given in the appropriate table, with the next higher TSI value permissible when traffic volume for the functional classification is significantly high. The design incorporates a reliability percentage that accounts for variability in both traffic prediction and performance prediction; Table 6.5 provides reliability ranges by Functional Classification for all Department pavements and designers are instructed to utilize the upper end of the range when the traffic prediction is less certain. For rigid‑pavement designs the required inputs include 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) over the initial performance period, the initial and terminal serviceability indices, the 28‑day mean modulus of rupture and elastic modulus of the concrete slab, the mean effective k‑value, the selected reliability level, the overall standard deviation, the load transfer coefficient J, and the overall drainage coefficient C_d. Concrete elastic modulus is estimated with the ACI correlation Ec = 57,000 (f′c)^{0.5}, where f′c is the 28‑day compressive strength in psi; the guide adopts a mean Ec of 4,000,000 psi for Class AA concrete, which corresponds to a mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi unless district data indicate otherwise. The drainage coefficient reflects drainage quality: C_d = 1.0 is used for rigid pavements constructed on unbound aggregate only, C_d = 1.1 is prescribed for new construction or reconstruction projects that employ a treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system and positive drainage, and the AASHO Road Test condition of C_d = 1.0 corresponds to a rating of fair. For full‑depth flexible pavements the terminal serviceability value from Table 6.3 is increased by the loss of serviceability due to frost heave; that loss is estimated using three factors—the frost‑heave rate selected from tables according to subgrade soil type, the maximum potential serviceability loss derived from the depth of frost penetration obtained via the Design Freezing Index and associated figure, and a frost‑heave probability applied in the recommended range of 25 %–75 %; the calculation may be performed with Figure G.8 or with an available Frost Heave spreadsheet tool. Overlay designs are carried out using the same 1993 AASHTO procedures as implemented in DARWin for seven specified overlay types; when a bonded concrete overlay is placed over asphalt or composite pavement the thicknesses shall be determined with the bonded‑concrete‑overlay‑of‑asphalt mechanistic‑empirical (BCOA‑ME) procedure developed at the University of Pittsburgh. The design must use traffic inputs—including current average daily traffic, projected growth, truck percentage and distribution—to compute the design daily 18‑kip ESALs in accordance with Chapter 7, and each overlay shall be designed for a minimum structural design life of eight years and may not exceed a maximum of twenty years. Minimum material thicknesses are prescribed; for example, at least 2½ inches of binder is required when first overlaying cement‑concrete or brick pavements, and the chapter’s tables define suitable asphalt materials and allowable layer ranges based on ADT. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 134–152), Ch. 8 (pp. 160–163), Ch. 9 (pp. 168–169), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177)

The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation requires that all pavement designs be completed according to the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures as amended and that the AASHTOWare® DARWin® 3.01 software be used. A design analysis must be performed whenever the continuous paving length exceeds 500 ft on divided roadways, 1,000 ft on undivided roadways, or when bridge‑approach paving on both sides totals more than 1,000 ft; if the design year employed in an approved design differs by more than three years from the current project design year, a new analysis and any required life‑cycle cost analysis shall be performed. For projects that do not require a DARWin analysis, the proposed pavement must meet or exceed the minimum thicknesses shown in Tables 8.3 and 9.4, although composite sections that substantially exceed those minima remain subject to analysis at the District Engineer’s direction. Preservation projects must be let within fifteen months of the Pavement Type Selection approval date; moderate‑complexity projects must obtain design approval within one year after the Design Field View (but no later than six months before the Final Design Office Meeting), and most‑complex projects must obtain approval within two years after the Design Field View (again at least six months before that meeting). Any CBR value greater than ten for use in pavement designs shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, and no CBR value greater than twenty may be used in the Heukelom‑and‑Klomp correlation, which limits the subgrade resilient modulus to a maximum of 30,000 psi. When back‑calculated subgrade resilient modulus values exceed fifteen thousand pounds per square inch, they must be verified by another form of testing prior to use; CBR values above twenty may be employed only with extreme caution. Dynamic cone penetrometer testing is limited to roadways whose MFC classification is B, C, D or E, and soils highly susceptible to frost heave should normally be removed and replaced rather than increasing pavement thickness. Subgrade strengthening by rock capping assumes a CBR of twenty, while lime or cement stabilization uses a structural coefficient of 0.12 together with the project‑specific CBR. The design method assumes an initial serviceability level that estimates the present serviceability index immediately after construction; PSI values range from zero to five. Designs for new pavement, reconstruction or overlay shall be performed using at least the terminal serviceability value specified for the roadway’s Maintenance Functional Classification, and a higher terminal serviceability index may be used when traffic volumes are significantly high for that functional class. Reliability must be expressed as a percentage that reflects the variability and uncertainty of both traffic and performance predictions so that the pavement will last through the analysis period. The Department applies reliability ranges by Functional Classification that are more restrictive than those in the original guide, and the upper end of the applicable range should be used when the traffic forecast is less certain. An overall standard deviation is required to capture all error and variability in design and construction inputs; a value of 0.35 should be used for rigid pavement designs, a value of 0.45 for flexible pavements, asphalt overlays on fractured PCC pavement, and flexible overlays on flexible pavements, and a value of 0.40 is prescribed for flexible pavement placed over rigid pavement. For rigid‑pavement design the mean concrete elastic modulus is assumed to be 4 × 10⁶ psi for Class AA concrete, which corresponds to a mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi; unless district historical data show mean compressive strengths significantly different from 4,925 psi, designers should use an Ec value of 4 × 10⁶ psi in all rigid pavement designs. Full‑depth flexible pavement design starts with the terminal serviceability index given in Table 6.3 and adds the loss of serviceability caused by frost heave obtained from Appendix G; the frost‑heave rate should be taken from Table 9.1 or Table 9.2 according to subgrade soil type, using an average when rates are close but performing separate loss calculations and designs when soils differ markedly. The maximum potential serviceability loss is found by determining the frost‑penetration depth from the Design Freezing Index and reading the corresponding loss for FAIR drainage quality; a frost‑heave probability between twenty‑five percent and seventy‑five percent should be selected by the District Geotechnical Engineer. An overlay design is required whenever a project seeks to improve ride quality, addresses suspected structural inadequacy, or targets low‑friction conditions, and each such design shall be prepared for a minimum eight‑year structural design life and a maximum of twenty years. The design must be based on current average daily traffic, projected traffic growth, and truck percentage (including a truck distribution unless the road is a collector or local), with the daily 18‑kip ESALs determined in accordance with Chapter 7. When asphalt is placed over cement‑concrete or brick pavement for the first time, at least two and one‑half inches of binder material must be provided and the wearing course shall meet the minimum thickness specified for the selected material; an exception permits tapering the wearing course to a one‑inch depth at the curb within a parking lane, provided compaction and joint sealing are carefully addressed. Seven overlay types are recognized under the 1993 AASHTO procedures, and bonded concrete overlays of asphalt or composite pavements must be designed using the BCOA‑ME mechanistic‑empirical method because the standard AASHTO procedure does not account for bonding. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 134–152), Ch. 8 (p. 161), Ch. 9 (pp. 168–169), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177)

Materials and mixture design

The 1993 AASHTO Guide requires that the pavement design specify a minimum acceptable roadbed soil resilient modulus together with the corresponding California Bearing Ratio values; any CBR greater than 10 must be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and no CBR exceeding 20 may be used in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation. For frost‑susceptible subgrades the guide calls for removal or replacement with a non‑frost‑susceptible granular layer, while rock cuts shall be treated with a rock capping of R‑3 Rock, AASHTO No. 1, or best available rock placed 1 to 3 ft deep. When subgrade strengthening is considered, cement or lime stabilization shall be designed based on laboratory tests that must include soil gradation, liquid and plastic limits, shrinkage limit (AASHTO T92), unconfined compressive strength, dry density, sulfate, chloride and organic content, and for lime also pH. Geogrids may be used at the subgrade‑aggregate interface and geotextiles of Class 4 Type A shall be used for layer separation on all completed subgrades (minimum 8 ft lane width) and Class 4 Type C where CBR is less than three. In rigid pavement design the guide requires that the structure be defined by its constituent layers—subgrade, a subbase consisting of an aggregate layer placed on the subgrade to provide drainage and a platform for subsequent layers, a base normally constructed of Asphalt Treated Permeable Base or Cement Treated Permeable Base material, and a surface that shall be Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement or Roller Compacted Concrete. For design of the concrete slab the guide also mandates specification of a set of material properties and performance inputs, including 18‑kip ESALs over the initial performance period, initial and terminal serviceability indices, the 28‑day mean PCC modulus of rupture, the 28‑day mean elastic modulus of the slab, the mean effective k‑value, the reliability level, the overall standard deviation, the load transfer coefficient (J), and the overall drainage coefficient (Cd). For rigid pavement designs under Chapter 8 Section 8.5 the guide requires specifying the concrete elastic modulus, Ec, as a material property of normal‑weight Portland cement concrete; the recommended correlation from the American Concrete Institute is Ec = 57,000 (fc′)^0.5 where fc′ is the compressive strength in psi. When Class AA concrete with a 28‑day design compressive strength of 3500 psi is used, the mean elastic modulus employed in the 1993 procedures shall be 4,000,000 psi, which corresponds to a mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi, and unless district historical data show significantly different mean strengths the designer should use an Ec value of 4 × 10^6 psi in all rigid pavement designs. The overall drainage coefficient (Cd) shall be specified according to the pavement’s material configuration and drainage provisions: for rigid pavements constructed on unbound aggregate only Cd is set to 1.0; for new construction or reconstruction projects that use a subbase of treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system and positive drainage Cd is set to 1.1; and for rehabilitation projects where existing drainage is judged substandard and no significant improvements are planned Cd reverts to 1.0. In overlay designs the guide requires that only asphalt materials—leveling, wearing, and base courses—be used in accordance with their suitability tables and that each layer respect the thickness limits shown in Table 10.4; when an existing cement‑concrete or brick pavement is overlaid with asphalt for the first time, or when previous asphalt overlays have been completely removed, a minimum of 2 ½ inches of binder material shall be provided, and the wearing course shall have the minimum thickness indicated for the material to be used. Table 10.5 also mandates that the pavement‑course thickness be matched with the appropriate Superpave mixture size for both scratch and leveling types. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 8 (pp. 160–163), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177)

An overlay design is required whenever ride quality must be improved or the existing pavement is suspected to be structurally inadequate, and each design shall be based on a minimum 8‑year structural design life and a maximum of 20 years. The selection of asphalt materials and mixture thicknesses follows the suitability tables that match leveling, wearing, or base courses to the highway’s average daily traffic, and the layer thickness may be used within the minimum and maximum limits given in Table 10.4; for a first‑time asphalt overlay on cement concrete or brick pavement at least 2 ½ inches of binder material shall be placed, while the wearing course shall have the minimum thickness indicated for that material, with an optional taper to 1 inch at curb reveal only within parking lanes. The appropriate overlay type—such as AC over AC, AC over fractured PCC slab, or bonded concrete overlay—is then chosen and thicknesses for these overlays shall be determined using the BCOA‑ME mechanistic‑empirical design procedure. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177)

Structural details and interfaces

The 1993 AASHTO Guide may be used to reinforce the subgrade‑aggregate interface with geogrids, and it specifies that Class 4 Type A geotextile fabric is to be used on all completed subgrades across the full lane width while Class 4 Type C fabric is to be used when the subgrade CBR is less than three, providing separation and lateral restraint. Granular transition material should be used where a new pavement structure will abut an existing pavement of a different total depth. Subgrade strengthening techniques such as rock capping or cement/lime stabilization are also addressed, but specific joint or load‑transfer system details are not prescribed in this portion of the guide. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

In the subgrade‑aggregate interface the guide calls for reinforcement by placing geogrids and for separation of subgrade from subbase by using Class 4 geotextiles. Type A fabric must be installed across the full lane width (8 feet or greater) to keep each material distinct, while Type C fabric is required where the subgrade CBR is less than three to provide both separation and lateral restraint. Frost‑susceptible soils should be removed and replaced with a sufficiently thick non‑frost‑susceptible layer, and base drains may be installed at interfaces to mitigate differential heave. In rock‑cut sections lateral benches identified in the soil survey shall be retained where rock meets soil, and subgrade strengthening such as rock capping uses 1 to 3 feet of R‑3 or AASHTO No. 1 rock with a geotextile separator to achieve a design CBR of 20. When an overlay is designed over cement‑concrete or brick pavements the guide requires a minimum of 2½ inches of binder material before any wearing course, and the wearing course must meet the minimum thickness indicated for its material; if a parking lane calls for curb reveal the wearing course may be tapered to a 1‑inch depth at the curb but compaction and sealing of the pavement/curb joint must receive careful attention. For overlays that rely on bonding between a new concrete layer and an existing asphalt or composite pavement the standard AASHTO procedures are not used; thicknesses shall be determined with the bonded concrete overlay mechanistic‑empirical design procedure (BCOA‑ME). [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177)

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

The AASHTO 1993 design process incorporates drainage by requiring that each typical section drawing display all edge drains and any widening. Moisture‑related climate effects are considered through the geotechnical report, which must contain a Frost Heave Probability as defined in Section 9.3.3. Subgrade soils are evaluated and low‑CBR zones are improved; any CBR value greater than 10 for use in pavement designs shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and no CBR exceeding 20 is permitted in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation. When large areas of a project have significantly different soil types and CBR values, the design may be divided into subsections provided that drainage beneath newly constructed pavements with different thicknesses can be effectively accommodated. It is recommended that soils highly susceptible to frost heave be removed and replaced with a layer of non‑frost‑susceptible material thick enough to insulate the roadbed soil from frost penetration. A stronger base course or pavement base drains should be incorporated to mitigate differential heaving caused by moisture and freeze‑thaw cycles. Subgrade strengthening techniques such as cement or lime stabilization, rock capping, geogrids, or geotextiles may be employed when the existing subgrade CBR is below 5, with design values of CBR = 20 for rock capping and a structural coefficient = 0.12 for stabilized subgrades specified to ensure adequate performance under moisture and climate effects. In rigid‑pavement design a subbase layer, an aggregate layer placed on the subgrade to provide drainage, is specified and a functional drainage system is required as a key ingredient to long‑term performance. The overall drainage coefficient Cd must be supplied to the DARWin software. In full‑depth flexible pavement design the terminal serviceability index is increased by the loss of serviceability due to frost heave; the frost‑heave rate should be determined from Table 9.1 or Table 9.2 according to the subgrade soil type. A recommended range for frost heave probability (Pf) of 25% – 75% is used in all flexible designs. Serviceability loss is obtained by drawing a horizontal line through the point where the vertical line crosses the FAIR drainage quality curve, thereby explicitly incorporating drainage quality, moisture‑related frost penetration depth, and regional climate into the pavement structure design. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 134–150), Ch. 8 (p. 160), Ch. 9 (pp. 168–169)

The guide must be applied to any new construction, reconstruction or rehabilitation where the paved length exceeds 500 continuous feet on divided roadways or 1,000 continuous feet on undivided roadways, which constitutes the geometric trigger for a DARWin analysis. In addition, each design submission is required to provide traffic information, specifically a summary of average daily traffic and truck percentage, as part of the user‑related data. The section does not specify any separate accessibility or safety criteria. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 134–136)

Verification and expected performance

The finished AASHTO 1993 pavement design must be packaged and submitted to Central Office where it is compared against a Pavement ME analysis performed by Central Office, ensuring the design follows the 1993 AASHTO procedures and meets departmental amendments. The submission package must contain a cover letter identifying project specifics, Form D‑4332, typical section drawings, location map, full DARWin printouts, all structural calculations, traffic summaries, geotechnical data (including CBR or FWD results), temporary pavement plans, and the Life‑Cycle Cost Analysis, which together allow reviewers to verify compliance with standards, constructability details, predicted performance, and consistency with the project’s requirements before final approval. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 134–136)

The AASHTO 1993 design method expects a pavement’s performance to be expressed through a Present Serviceability Index that is estimated immediately after construction and tracked until it reaches the Terminal Serviceability Index defined for the road’s Maintenance Functional Classification. The PSI is quantified on a 0‑to‑5 scale using objective measurements of roughness, cracks, patches and rut depth, and the TSI represents the minimum ride quality at which the pavement is no longer adequate. Serviceability is monitored with non‑destructive testing; friction values are obtained with a Single Locked Wheel Skid Tester, riding quality is measured with high‑speed laser profilers, and structural capacity is assessed with a falling weight deflectometer, providing the data needed to calculate PSI and verify that the pavement meets or exceeds the required TSI for its functional class. For full‑depth flexible pavements the guide defines expected performance in terms of a target terminal serviceability index selected from Table 6.3 and then reduced by the loss of serviceability attributable to frost heave, which is computed from Appendix G charts using the frost‑heave rate, the maximum potential serviceability loss derived from frost penetration depth, and a frost‑heave probability that typically ranges from 25 % to 75 %; the calculated loss is read off the ΔPSIFH axis of Figure G.8 and subtracted from the base terminal index (for example, a base value of 3.0 for an Interstate may be adjusted to a final target such as 3.75). In addition, any overlay that is designed to improve ride or address structural inadequacy must be provisioned for a minimum eight‑year structural design life and may extend up to a maximum of twenty years, establishing an explicit service‑life performance outcome for the overlay. [1]
