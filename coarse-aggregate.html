Coarse Aggregate

Part of: Mix Design is-subtopic-of
Coarse Aggregate is a topic under Mix Design.
coarse-aggregate
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Coarse aggregate is the larger mineral component incorporated into pavement mixtures, ranging from the particles retained on a No. 4 sieve in asphalt wearing courses to stone, gravel or slag up to AASHTO No. 8 used in Portland‑cement concrete structures[1][2]. It provides the load‑bearing skeleton that gives structural integrity to concrete pavements and, through its hardness and polishing resistance, controls the amount of material removed during grinding and the ability of an asphalt surface to retain skid resistance—a property expressed by a Skid Resistance Level (SRL) designation[1][2]. The aggregate must be hard, tough, durable, uncoated inert particles that are reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation and deleterious minerals, with limits on water absorption, flat‑or‑elongated pieces and crushed‑fragment content to ensure proper interlock and long‑term performance[2].

Material purpose and applicability

Coarse aggregate is the larger mineral component incorporated into pavement mixtures, such as the larger particles mixed with cement and natural sand in Portland‑cement concrete pavements and the particles retained on the No. 4 sieve that form the coarse fraction of asphalt mixes [1][2]. In Portland‑cement concrete pavements, the coarse aggregate together with cement, fine aggregate and water forms the bulk material that constitutes the structural component of the pavement, and its hardness and tendency to polish affect the amount of material that must be removed during diamond grinding or grooving, thereby influencing cost of surface‑treatment operations as well as resulting friction and durability [1]. In asphalt wearing courses, the coarse aggregate’s polishing resistance is the primary property controlling surface friction, so selecting a source with an appropriate Skid Resistance Level (SRL) designation ensures that adequate tire‑pavement friction is developed and retained throughout service life [1]. For Superpave 6.3 mm thin‑asphalt overlays, the coarse aggregate consists of particles retained on the No. 4 sieve and must satisfy a specified SRL rating, abrasion‑loss limits, a minimum percentage of crushed fragments, and flat‑and‑elongated particle contents that may not exceed 10 percent for a 1:5 ratio or 20 percent for a 1:3 ratio; these requirements ensure the aggregate contributes to the structural integrity and friction performance of the wearing course [2]. In cement concrete described in Section 704.1, coarse aggregate refers to stone, gravel, or slag classified as Type A with a maximum size of AASHTO No. 467, No. 57, No. 67, or No. 8, and together with Portland cement, fine aggregate, water and admixtures it forms the structural material of the pavement [2]. When applied as an asphalt seal coat (Section 470.3), coarse aggregate is spread as a dry or damp top layer at a rate of 15 lb/yd² to 25 lb/yd², rolled within five minutes so that at least 50 percent of the stones become embedded, thereby providing a protective surface for the seal coat [2]. In polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing (Section 483.2), coarse aggregate supplied from approved sources and meeting Table B quality requirements is sized and uniformly graded with fine aggregate and filler to achieve the prescribed final gradation and SRL, forming the bulk of the aggregate skeleton that contributes to structural integrity and surface performance [2]. Within a crushed‑aggregate base course (Section 310.3(b)), coarse material is placed over an initial fine‑material bed, spread uniformly and compacted to create a load‑bearing skeleton; after thorough compaction fine material is spread to fill voids and produce a monolithic surface ready for the surface course, thus providing the bulk material that forms the structural layer beneath the surface courses [2]. For seal coats made with reclaimed asphalt pavement (Section 473.2), coarse aggregate is obtained by crushing, screening and grading RAP free of deleterious debris to meet gradation limits, thereby supplying the required particle‑size skeleton in the emulsified‑asphalt seal‑coat and enabling the intended performance [2]. In pervious asphalt pavement systems (Section 420.2), coarse aggregate classified as Type A for Asphalt JMF or as AASHTO No. 3 (optionally No. 57) for detention basins is incorporated to meet specified limits on abrasion, thin and elongated pieces, and crushed fragments, fulfilling the material requirements of those systems and providing a load‑bearing skeleton that supports traffic while maintaining void space for drainage [2]. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses (Section 489.2(a)), coarse aggregate from an approved source meeting Table B is combined with fine aggregate, filler and binder to produce a plant‑mixed asphalt concrete wearing layer; its presence provides the coarse fraction of the aggregate skeleton that helps achieve the specified mixture composition and pavement surface performance [2]. In pervious concrete pavement construction (Section 520.3), coarse aggregate is installed in 8‑inch maximum lifts and compacted according to Section 360.3, providing a stable load‑bearing layer that supports the overlying concrete while preserving the intended void structure for drainage [2]. For asphalt surface‑treatment mixes (Section 480.2), coarse aggregate identified as Type A, No. 8 and No. 67 must be provided so that the mix attains at least the SRL designation indicated in the Contract Item; a blend of two aggregates may be used if the blend has an SRL equal to or better than specified, thereby ensuring the surface‑treatment achieves the required friction performance [2]. In bonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545.2), coarse aggregate consists of stone, gravel, or slag identified as Type A and classified as No. 57, No. 67, or No. 8; it must be durable and free from slate texture or cleavage planes, is washed during production, and is incorporated into the cement‑concrete mix to provide the bulk material required for the concrete structure [2]. For pipe underdrain and pavement base drain backfill (Section 610.2), coarse aggregate classified as Type C or better may include gravel without a required percentage of crushed fragments and is used as backfill material, with No. 57 specified for Type I backfill and No. 8 for Type II backfill, thereby providing structural support for the drainage system [2]. In the geosynthetic‑stabilized bridge approach (Section 224.3), PennDOT No. 2A coarse aggregate is placed as backfill behind the stabilization geosynthetics in two 6‑inch compacted layers that fill each interval between geosynthetic sheets up to the next layer or subgrade, thereby providing structural support and a stable base for the pavement system [2]. During the first application of an asphalt surface treatment (Section 480.3), No. 67 coarse aggregate is spread at 25–45 lb per square yard with a mechanical spreader, rolled as specified and any loose material swept away, creating a protective top layer for the treatment [2]. During the second application of an asphalt surface treatment (Section 480.3), No. 8 coarse aggregate is spread at 15–30 lb per square yard with a mechanical spreader, back‑rolled until the aggregate adheres to the emulsified asphalt, forming the wearing surface of the pavement [2]. In construction of a crushed‑aggregate base course type DG (Section 312.3), coarse material is measured separately from fine material and proportioned into the mix; by feeding these coarse particles into the mixer in proper proportions and keeping them stockpiled to prevent segregation, they become a key component of the structural base layer, providing bulk and enabling the mixture to be compacted to the required density for a stable pavement foundation [2].

Composition and characteristics

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, coarse aggregate in asphalt wearing courses is defined primarily by its polishing resistance, which is identified as the most significant material property influencing surface friction; its petrographic properties can be related to a Skid Resistance Level (SRL) that reflects performance in properly designed dense‑graded asphalt surfaces and in AASHTO T 242 friction tests. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications describe coarse aggregate as hard, tough, durable, uncoated inert particles that are reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation, and deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica, and hydrated iron oxide. Acceptable sources include durable stone free from slate texture or cleavage planes; durable gravel whose crushed portion must contain at least two fracture faces for asphalt wearing courses (or three faces for open‑graded subbase); air‑cooled blast‑furnace slag having a unit weight not less than 70 lb/ft³; steel slag that passes an expansion test of less than 0.50 %; granulated slag limited to a maximum size of 50 mm and no more than 20 % passing the 150‑µm sieve; lightweight aggregates meeting AASHTO M 195 soundness and abrasion limits with an absorption factor not exceeding 2.5 %; and recycled concrete used only as 2A aggregate. For Type A coarse aggregate the maximum water absorption determined according to AASHTO T 85 is 3.0 % (the restriction does not apply to dredged river gravel in portland‑cement concrete); for Type B the maximum allowable absorption is 3.5 %; and in cement‑concrete mixes designated as Type A, absorption must not exceed 3.0 % for gravels and 2.0 % for all other aggregates. In Superpave mixtures the aggregate must be a Type A material per Section 703.2; its abrasion content may not exceed the maximum percent specified in Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A, and it must possess at least the SRL designation required for the intended wearing course. Flat or elongated particles are limited to a maximum of 10 % when evaluated on material retained on the No. 4 sieve using a 1:5 length‑to‑thickness ratio, and to a maximum of 20 % when the ratio is 1:3; additionally, particles retained on the No. 4 sieve must not exceed a length‑to‑thickness ratio of 5:1. The aggregate must also contain at least the minimum percentage of crushed fragments prescribed in AASHTO M 323 Table 6 for material retained on the No. 4 sieve, and for asphalt wearing courses a minimum of 85 % of the coarse particles must be crushed with at least two fracture faces. Production testing requires an average coefficient of uniformity of at least 4.0, with each individual sample required to be no less than 3.5; glassy particles are limited to a maximum of 4 % for cement‑concrete use and to a maximum of 10 % for other uses, and limits are also imposed on coal, coke, metallic iron, clay lumps and shale as identified by visual or petrographic analysis. Additional tests assess soundness loss after five sulfate immersion‑drying cycles, specific gravity on a saturated‑surface‑dry basis as listed in Bulletin 14, bulk density, voids, and the presence of friable particles. For lightweight cement concrete the coarse aggregate must achieve an equilibrium density between 110 lb/ft³ and 117 lb/ft³ as measured by ASTM C567; its bulk specific gravity is recorded on a saturated‑surface‑dry basis (Bulletin 14). In base‑course applications the coarse aggregate is placed over a fine‑material bedding, must be well‑graded so that particles interlock, spread uniformly across the full width, and compacted in layers no thinner than four inches and no thicker than eight inches (or five to ten inches when vibratory methods are used) until a dense monolithic mass is achieved. For seal‑coat applications the coarse aggregate is produced by crushing, screening and grading reclaimed asphalt pavement so that it is free of deleterious construction debris and meets the gradation requirements of Table A; any clumps or oversized particles retained on a 12.5‑mm sieve must be reprocessed before shipment, and documentation must confirm that the unprocessed RAP originated from a source using only Bulletin 14‑approved aggregates. In the asphalt‑treated permeable base course the coarse aggregate shall be Type A; when crushed gravel is used the mix must contain at least 75 % crushed particles and each particle must exhibit a minimum of three fracture faces. The pervious asphalt pavement system requires the coarse aggregate for the asphalt JMF to be Type A and to satisfy quality limits set out in the referenced tables, including the maximum abrasion percentage from Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A, the maximum proportion of thin or elongated pieces defined in AASHTO M 323 Table 6, and at least the minimum percentage of crushed fragments also defined in that table; for detention‑basin applications the material may be AASHTO No. 3 topped with AASHTO No. 57 as a choker/leveling coarse, or solely AASHTO No. 57. For extensible reinforcement of MSE walls the backfill coarse aggregate must be a natural material meeting the quality requirements of Type C coarse aggregate or better, free of clay lumps, friable particles, coal, coke, metallurgical slag or cinders, and is specified as AASHTO No. 8 (or No. 57 or PennDOT No. 2A) with a maximum plasticity index of 3, a pH between 5.0 and 9.0, and an angle of internal friction not less than 34° for the portion that passes the No. 8 sieve; the coarse aggregate itself is exempt from angle‑of‑friction testing. In the pervious concrete pavement system the coarse aggregate shall be AASHTO No. 8, Type A for concrete, or for detention basins either AASHTO No. 3 topped with AASHTO No. 57 as a choker and leveling course, or AASHTO No. 57 only. Concrete pavement partial‑depth repair specifies coarse aggregate as Type A, No. 8; when an aggregate extender is required for rapid‑set concrete patching material that does not already contain one, only AASHTO No. 8 coarse aggregate from a Bulletin 14‑approved supplier may be used, and it must have an expansion index less than 0.10. For asphalt concrete pavement with a 60‑month warranty the wearing‑course coarse aggregate shall be sourced from a Bulletin 14 listed supplier and meet the required SRL designation; its composition is documented by the producer’s material code/class, percent in mix, bulk specific gravity and percent absorption, and it is characterized through combined consensus properties that include coarse‑aggregate angularity and flat or elongated pieces, with the gradation of the coarse fraction following an FHWA 0.45 power‑type curve plotted on the joint mix formula. Full‑depth reclamation allows the coarse aggregate to be supplied by an approved aggregate producer (Bulletin 14) or reclaimed material that meets the size required by the mix design, such as AASHTO No. 57; it may be classified as Type A, B, or C, which define its gradation and quality categories, and consists of larger mineral particles that provide the structural skeleton of the pavement mix. Within earthwork classifications coarse aggregate is described as natural or synthetic mineral aggregates that satisfy particular sieve‑size proportions: Granular Material Type 1 contains greater than 70 % passing the 3/8‑inch sieve (less than 30 % retained) and less than 20 % passing the No. 200 sieve; Granular Material Type 2 contains less than or equal to 70 % passing the 3/8‑inch sieve (greater than or equal 30 % retained) and also less than 20 % passing the No. 200 sieve; both types may include AASHTO Nos. 8 or 57, PennDOT Nos. 2A or OGS meeting the requirements of Section 703.2/3. The asphalt seal‑coat requires coarse aggregate to be Type A or No. 8, and on roadways carrying fewer than 1,000 ADT it may be No. 89 as defined in Section 703.2; the material must conform to the gradation listed in Table A and to the SRL designated in the contract item, be sourced from a supplier listed in Bulletin 14, and any blend used shall consist of fifty percent by weight of each of the two aggregates provided the blended SRL is equal to or better than that specified. The cement‑treated permeable base course limits coarse aggregate to Type A or Type B material meeting AASHTO No. 67 or No. 57 specifications and may be supplied as blast‑furnace slag or crushed gravel; fine aggregate is not used, the amount passing a No. 200 sieve must not exceed two percent, and when crushed gravel is selected at least seventy‑five percent of the particles shall be fractured with a minimum of two fracture faces. The bonded concrete overlay permits coarse aggregate for Class AA cement concrete to be Type A material identified as No. 57, No. 67, or No. 8 and must consist of stone, gravel, or slag particles; stone shall be durable and free from slate texture or cleavage planes, gravel particles shall also be durable, both materials shall be washed thoroughly during production, and blast‑furnace slag aggregate is not permitted for cement concrete paving applications. The pipe underdrain and pavement base drain specifies coarse aggregate that meets the requirements of Type C or better according to Section 703.2; unlike finer aggregates, no minimum percentage of crushed fragments is required when gravel is used, and the gradation shall be No. 57 for Type I backfill and No. 8 for Type II backfill. The crushed‑aggregate base course may be supplied as fine material (No. 10) or coarse material (No. 1) and must be classified as Type A or Type B stone or slag per Section 703.2; when stone with poor cementing qualities is employed it may be blended with limestone fines up to twenty percent of the mix, and granulated blast‑furnace slag is acceptable provided it conforms to the No. 10 gradation, has a dry rodded density not greater than ninety pounds per cubic foot, is reasonably uniform, free of harmful clay, silt, vegetation or other objectionable substances, limited to a maximum particle size of two inches, and no more than twenty percent passes the No. 100 sieve. For flowable backfill coarse aggregate is defined as Type A, B, or C material meeting the AASHTO 10 requirements of Section 703.2, must be obtained from a source listed in Bulletin 14, and may contain no more than ten percent of particles that pass a No. 200 sieve. Variations in the mineral composition and source rock of coarse aggregate change its hardness, which directly influences the amount of material that must be removed during diamond grinding and therefore the grinding cost; aggregates that are polishing‑susceptible also lose surface friction more rapidly over the annual cycle, making their polishing resistance the most significant material property affecting surface friction [1]. PennDOT assigns each approved quarry source a Skid Resistance Level (SRL) based on how its aggregate performs in dense‑graded asphalt surfaces, and designers must select an SRL appropriate for the expected traffic volume; selecting a source with a higher SRL or blending aggregates to meet the required SRL directly affects the pavement’s ability to retain adequate friction throughout its service life [1]. Coarse aggregate must be obtained from producers listed in Bulletin 14 and its SRL rating shall meet the specification, and when a single source cannot achieve the required SRL the contractor may blend two aggregates in equal mass (50 % by weight of each) to obtain an SRL designation equal to or better than specified [2]. The permissible abrasion loss is limited by the maximum percent loss given in Bulletin 27, Chapter 2A, Table 6A, and flat‑and‑elongated particles shall not exceed the maximum percent shown in ASTM D4791 for material retained on the No. 4 sieve—maximum 10 % for a 1:5 ratio and maximum 20 % for a 1:3 ratio [2]. For all asphalt wearing courses, unless otherwise specified, at least 85 % of the particles must be crushed with a minimum of two fracture faces; for open‑graded subbase gravel the requirement is at least 75 % crushed particles with a minimum of three fracture faces [2]. Gravels shall not exceed 3.0 % absorption and all other aggregates shall not exceed 2.0 % absorption; Type A aggregate may not exceed an absorption of 3.0 % while Type B may not exceed 3.5 % [2]. When the mineral composition or quarry changes, potential alkali‑silica reaction is evaluated by field performance history, petrographic examination and expansion testing per ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303, and if an aggregate shows a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % it may be used only with cement‑supplementary cementitious combinations that reduce measured expansion to less than 0.1 %, while any aggregate exceeding 0.30 % expansion shall be rejected [2]. The coefficient of uniformity must have a minimum average of 4.0 for the overall material and at least 3.5 for each individual sample, otherwise the lot is rejected; the maximum percentage of glassy particles allowed is 4 % for coarse aggregate used in cement concrete and 10 % for other uses [2]. If the water absorption of a coarse aggregate determined according to AASHTO T 85 exceeds 2.0 %, the mixture shall be sampled in accordance with PTM No. 1 at the frequency specified in the producer QC plan and the apparent moisture content of the produced mix may not exceed 0.5 % [2]. The bulk specific gravity (saturated, surface‑dry basis) listed in Bulletin 14 is used for proportioning concrete and therefore influences mix proportions and resulting strength; batch weight shall be adjusted to compensate for free water on the aggregate based on tests of representative stockpile samples [2]. Aggregates shall be stored dry or only slightly damp, excessive free moisture shall be avoided, each size and type shall be kept in a separate stockpile, uniformity of moisture and gradation shall be tightly controlled, and aggregate temperature shall not be less than 40 °F before and during batching; lightweight aggregates must be stored in a stable, uniform, saturated condition greater than SSD to ensure proper moisture condition [2]. Any change in source or blend composition triggers a requirement to submit a revised Joint Mix Formula to the DME/DMM before field use and, for seal‑coat applications, a new seal‑coat design shall be submitted if the source or gradation changes [2]. Proportioning together with asphalt content must keep the mixture within the composition tolerances of Table A and the volumetric property tolerances of Table B; deviations can cause the mix to fall outside allowable air‑voids, VMA or other performance limits and require corrective actions [2]. Higher absorption aggregates may be permitted only if mix‑design specimens meet 300 freeze‑thaw cycles according to ASTM C666 with less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80 [2]. Key material properties

Hardness of coarse aggregate is a key mechanical property because it directly controls the amount of material that must be removed during diamond grinding, grooving or NGCS, influencing both cost and ability to achieve desired surface texture. For pavements constructed with polishing‑susceptible aggregate, susceptibility to polishing becomes critical; aggregates that polish slowly retain surface texture and thus maintain adequate tire‑pavement grip throughout traffic and environmental exposure. The most significant material property affecting surface friction is the polishing resistance of the coarse aggregate, and NGCS may be employed where the aggregate used in the concrete is prone to polishing. Petrographic characteristics of the aggregate source are linked to friction performance because they determine how the material behaves under polishing and therefore influence the assigned Skid Resistance Level. The Superpave 6.3 mm thin asphalt overlay design limits the aggregate’s abrasion loss to the maximum percent specified in Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A, ensuring that the particles resist wear under traffic loading. Shape requirements restrict flat or elongated particles to no more than 10 % for a 1:5 aggregate ratio and no more than 20 % for a 1:3 ratio as defined by ASTM D4791 on material retained on the No. 4 sieve, while a minimum percentage of crushed fragments with fractured faces must be met in accordance with AASHTO M 323 Table 6 to provide interlock. Coarse particles are required to be hard, tough, durable, and uncoated inert material that is reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation, and deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica, and hydrated iron oxide. Water absorption shall not exceed 3.0 % for Type A use and 3.5 % for Type B use as determined by AASHTO T 85, while lightweight aggregate is limited to an Aggregate Absorption Factor of 2.5 %. When blast‑furnace slag is used its density must be at least 70 lb/ft³ and shall not exceed a rodded density of 80 lb/ft³, and the total expansion of steel slag must be less than 0.50 % as measured by PTM No. 130. Freeze‑thaw durability is limited to a decrease in dynamic modulus of no more than 60 % after 300 cycles, providing a thermal performance criterion for the aggregate in cold climates. Production testing must determine soundness loss after five sodium‑sulfate cycles according to PTM No. 510 and abrasion loss according to AASHTO T 96, addressing chemical resistance to sulfate attack and mechanical wear. During rolling the aggregate layer must withstand a contact pressure of 40 psi to 50 psi without being turned over, indicating that mechanical stability under load is essential for performance. For pervious asphalt pavement systems the same abrasion‑loss limit from Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A applies, flat‑or‑elongated particle limits follow AASHTO M 323 Table 6, and a minimum percentage of crushed fragments is also required. In permeable base‑course applications the coarse aggregate shall be Type A, and when crushed gravel is used at least 75 % of the particles must be crushed with a minimum of three fracture faces; the mix also calls for aggregate containing at least 3 % free moisture before lime treatment. Extensible reinforcement backfill requires that the material have a maximum plasticity index of 3, a pH between 5.0 and 9.0, and that the portion finer than the No. 8 sieve exhibit an angle of internal friction of not less than 34 degrees; although coarse aggregate is exempt from this test, the backfill must be free of clay lumps, friable particles, coal and coke. For concrete pavement partial‑depth repair the coarse aggregate shall achieve a minimum durability factor of 80 % at 300 cycles and an expansion index less than 0.10 when AASHTO No. 8 aggregate is specified. Under the 60‑month warranty for asphalt concrete pavement the coarse‑aggregate portion is characterized by its bulk specific gravity, percent absorption, voids in mineral aggregate expressed as a percent by volume, and combined aggregate consensus properties such as sand equivalency, fine‑aggregate angularity, coarse‑aggregate angularity, and flat & elongated pieces, all of which must conform to the FHWA 0.45 power‑type gradation chart. If water absorption determined according to AASHTO T 85 exceeds 2.0 %, the mixture shall be sampled under the producer QC plan, and the apparent moisture content in the produced mix shall not exceed 0.5 %. Placement and compaction of embankment and fill require a particle‑size distribution of either greater than 70 % passing the 3/8‑inch sieve with less than 30 % retained, or for Type 2 less than or equal to 70 % passing that sieve with at least 30 % retained; in both cases less than 20 % may pass a No. 200 sieve, and the material must be reasonably free of organic matter, coal, carbonaceous materials, or other objectionable matter. Cement‑treated permeable base courses limit fine material to a maximum of 2 % passing the No. 200 sieve, and when crushed gravel is used at least 75 % of the particles must have two fracture faces to provide the necessary angular shape for interlock. Bonded concrete overlays require durable stone that is free from slate texture or cleavage planes and durable washed gravel particles; aggregates showing a 14‑day expansion greater than 0.30 % are rejected, while those with expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % may be used only when an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material combination reduces the effective expansion to less than 0.1 %. In crushed‑aggregate base courses granulated blast‑furnace slag is acceptable provided its density does not exceed 90 lb/ft³, particles may be no larger than 2 inches and no more than 20 % may pass the No. 100 sieve; the material should be reasonably uniform and free from harmful amounts of clay, silt, vegetation, or other objectionable substances, and stone with unsatisfactory cementing qualities may be used only if limestone fines are blended at a rate of 20 %. Flowable backfill specifies that coarse aggregate shall be Type A, B, or C material meeting AASHTO 10 specifications, and the gradation requirement limits the amount passing the No. 200 sieve to no more than 10 % of the total. For crushed‑aggregate base course type DG, aggregates must be stored and handled so that segregation and degradation are prevented, thereby preserving their mechanical integrity during compaction; when the material is too coarse for standard density methods, compaction is judged by its non‑movement under the prescribed equipment, and moisture is chemically controlled by adding water and, where directed, calcium chloride to achieve the required level while excluding frost or frozen pieces. Placement shall not occur on a frozen surface when air temperature is below 40 °F or when freezing temperatures are forecast within the next 24 hours, reflecting the importance of thermal conditions for aggregate performance. Behavior and mechanisms

Coarse aggregate is described as hard, tough and durable inert particles that must be reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation and deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide, which gives it the capacity to resist loading without degradation. For gravel used in Type A applications the maximum allowable absorption determined according to AASHTO T 85 is 3.0% (with a higher 3.5% limit for Type B), and lightweight aggregate must not exceed an Aggregate Absorption Factor of 2.5%. In seal‑coat work the specification calls for dry or damp aggregates while explicitly avoiding excessive free moisture, and emulsified asphalt may only be applied when air, surface and aggregate temperatures are above 60 °F and rain or freezing is not expected within 24 hours. After placement a single layer of coarse aggregate is spread at a rate of 15 to 25 pounds per square yard and must be rolled within five minutes using pneumatic‑tire rollers at a speed less than or equal to ten miles per hour with a contact pressure from 40 to 50 pounds per square inch to prevent the stones from turning over; the surface is then protected from vehicular loads until adequate stability, adhesion and curing are achieved. In crushed‑aggregate base courses each layer must be compacted to not less than 100 % of maximum density so that no movement occurs under the prescribed compaction equipment, providing the load‑bearing stability required for pavement service. Moisture during construction is kept within the proper range, and if needed calcium chloride is blended before water addition at a rate of seven pounds per ton for Type 1 or 5.7 pounds per ton for Type 2. Placement is prohibited on frozen surfaces, when air temperature falls below 40 °F, or when freezing temperatures are forecast within the next 24 hours, thereby preventing degradation of bonding or density caused by temperature fluctuations. For steel‑slag stockpiles the total expansion measured according to PTM No. 130 must be less than 0.50%, and lightweight aggregate must meet a freeze‑thaw resistance requirement that limits the decrease of dynamic modulus after 300 cycles to a maximum of 60%. Chemical durability is ensured by requiring petrographic analysis and, for certain by‑product slags, chemical testing per DEP General Permit WMGR144 before use; laboratory soundness is judged by the percentage loss after five immersion‑drying cycles in a sodium sulfate solution (PTM No. 510), deleterious shale content is assessed after four wetting‑drying cycles (PTM No. 519), and resistance to mechanical loading is indicated by the abrasion test AASHTO T 96 and friable particle loss (PTM No. 620). The specification also requires a minimum average coefficient of uniformity of 4.0, with individual samples not less than 3.5, to ensure gradation needed for stable performance. During Superpave mixture construction the coarse aggregate must retain its integrity while the mixture is compacted in proper condition; roller speed, amplitude, frequency, pattern and size are adjusted to eliminate displacement, shoving, cracking and aggregate breakage, steel‑wheel rollers are kept moist without excess water, and the mix is placed at temperatures within the limits of Table A. Unacceptable pattern segregation is identified when the macrotexture depth difference exceeds 0.024 inch, and flushing is flagged when average texture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 inch. [2]

In the Pavement Policy Manual the behavior of coarse aggregate during diamond grinding or nongrooving concrete surfacing (NGCS) is said to be governed by its physical hardness, which determines how much material must be removed, and by its mechanical tendency to polish, which influences skid‑resistance and the effectiveness of grooving for macro‑texture; aggregates that are prone to polishing may be treated with NGCS [1]. The same manual further states that in asphalt wearing courses the principal control on coarse aggregate is its physical resistance to polishing under traffic, because this resistance dictates the ability of the surface to generate and retain friction, a characteristic that is closely linked to the petrographic makeup of the mineral composition which governs how rapidly particles become smooth [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania specifications require that the mechanical durability of coarse aggregate used in Superpave mixtures be limited by an abrasion loss not to exceed the maximum percent loss specified in Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A, thereby controlling the amount of material lost under repeated loading [2]. Shape characteristics are also controlled mechanically: flat and elongated particles retained on the No. 4 sieve may not exceed ten percent for a 1:5 aggregate‑to‑asphalt ratio or twenty percent for a 1:3 ratio as measured by ASTM D4791, and a minimum percentage of crushed fragments with fractured faces must be present on the No. 4 sieve according to AASHTO M 323 Table 6, both provisions governing interlock and void structure [2]. Bulk specific gravity on a saturated‑surface‑dry basis is required for cement‑concrete applications and is used to compute the aggregate’s contribution to mix proportions, ensuring that the water‑cement ratio remains as intended [2]. Chemical mechanisms are addressed through requirements that coarse aggregate be free of deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica, and hydrated iron oxide, and that any potentially alkali‑silica reactive material be evaluated by petrographic classification, expansion testing (ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303), and, if reactive, mitigated according to the prescribed measures; aggregates exhibiting a 14‑day expansion greater than 0.30 percent are rejected, while those with expansion between 0.10 percent and 0.30 percent may be used only with an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material combination that reduces expansion to less than 0.1 percent [2]. Mechanical shear resistance for backfill in MSE walls is governed by an internal friction angle not less than thirty‑four degrees for material finer than the No. 8 sieve, while chemical stability is limited by a pH range of five point zero to nine point zero; the aggregate must also be free of organic matter, clay lumps, friable particles, coal, coke, metallurgical slag, or cinders because such substances would degrade durability [2]. The specifications further prescribe mechanical actions during placement and compaction: layers in crushed‑aggregate base courses must be placed within thickness limits (four to eight inches with rollers alone or five to ten inches with vibration) and compacted to at least one hundred percent of maximum density as defined by PTM No. 106 Method B; rolling speeds, amplitudes, frequencies, patterns and roller sizes are to be adjusted to avoid displacement, shoving, cracking and aggregate breakage, thereby controlling the physical integrity of the coarse aggregate during construction [2]. Finally, storage requirements emphasize physical mechanisms: moisture and gradation must remain uniform, aggregates must be stored at temperatures not below forty °F to prevent frost, segregated or contaminated material must be excluded, and separate stockpiles for each size and type are mandated, all to preserve the intended physical properties of the coarse aggregate prior to use [2].

Durability and deterioration

In concrete pavements where diamond grinding or grooving is performed, the coarse aggregate’s tendency to polish leads to surface‑texture deterioration that reduces skid resistance and may require longitudinal diamond grooving or next‑generation surfacing to retain friction; the hardness of the coarse aggregate also determines how much material must be removed during grinding, influencing maintenance cost. In asphalt wearing courses, surface friction depends heavily on the polishing resistance of the coarse aggregate, so loss of texture through polishing causes a decline in skid resistance that is especially low in late summer and fall and varies with the aggregate’s petrographic characteristics and traffic volume; initial friction measurements are not indicative of future performance. The Superpave mixture design for plant‑mixed 6.3 mm thin asphalt overlay courses limits coarse‑aggregate abrasion loss to the maximum percent loss specified in Bulletin 27, Chapter 2A, Table 6A, and restricts flat and elongated particles to no more than 10 percent for a 1:5 shape ratio or 20 percent for a 1:3 ratio on material retained on the No. 4 sieve, while requiring at least the minimum percent of crushed fragments specified in AASHTO M 323 Table 6 for fractured faces; the aggregate must consist of hard, tough, durable, uncoated inert particles reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation and deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide. For steel‑slag coarse aggregate used in these mixes, the total expansion measured according to PTM No. 130 must be less than 0.50 percent, and stockpiles that exceed this limit shall be cured under moist conditions before reuse. Lightweight coarse aggregates are limited to an Aggregate Absorption Factor of 2.5 percent and shall exhibit a decrease in dynamic modulus of no more than 60 percent after 300 freeze‑thaw cycles. Coarse‑aggregate Type A is limited to a maximum water absorption of 3.0 percent for gravels and 2.0 percent for all other aggregates; higher absorption may be permitted only when mix specimens meet 300 freeze‑thaw cycles per ASTM C666 with less than 0.04 percent dilation or a durability factor greater than 80. In seal‑coat construction, aggregates with excessive free moisture must be avoided because they can impair adhesion; if less than 50 percent of the aggregate becomes embedded in the emulsified asphalt, the Representative may require a fog seal, and rolling must begin within five minutes of applying the emulsified asphalt, be limited to speeds not exceeding 10 miles per hour, and use a contact pressure between 40 and 50 pounds per square inch to prevent turning over the aggregate. Coarse aggregate may suffer durability problems when it is reactive and participates in alkali‑silica reaction; reactivity is assessed through field performance history, petrographic examination and expansion testing per ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303, and if either coarse or fine aggregate is reactive mitigation is required, with new sources initially listed at an expansion of 0.11 percent (R1) pending confirmation. Aggregates must be kept free from segregation and contamination because pattern segregation that causes a macrotexture depth difference exceeding 0.024 inch is unacceptable; aggregates that become segregated or mixed with earth or foreign material shall not be used, water‑spray systems should be employed as needed to maintain moisture control, and aggregates must be stored at temperatures not less than 40 °F before and during batching to eliminate frost. For seal‑coat applications using reclaimed asphalt pavement, the aggregate must be free of clumps and oversized particles retained on the 12.5 mm sieve; any material remaining in an uncovered stockpile longer than one month or in a covered stockpile longer than three months shall be reprocessed, and gradation sampling is required on the first day of production and at least once every 1 000 tons. In asphalt‑treated permeable base courses, stripping potential is reduced by adding hydrated lime to the aggregate at not less than 1 percent by weight of total dry aggregate, or by using an anti‑stripping additive at not less than 0.25 percent by weight of the asphalt; when crushed gravel is specified, at least 75 percent of the particles must be crushed with a minimum of three fracture faces to promote interlock and resist degradation. In pervious asphalt pavement systems, durability concerns are addressed by limiting abrasion, thin or elongated pieces, and requiring crushed fragments as described for Superpave, while compaction is limited to no more than four passes with smooth steel‑wheeled static rollers and pneumatic‑tire rollers are prohibited to preserve the open, free‑draining structure. When coarse aggregate is used as backfill for extensible reinforcement of MSE walls, it must be free of organic matter and other deleterious substances, contain no clay lumps, friable particles, coal, coke, metallurgical slag or cinders, have a plasticity index not exceeding 3, a pH between 5.0 and 9.0, and an angle of internal friction of at least 34 degrees to reduce susceptibility to chemical attack, swelling, or loss of shear resistance. In pervious concrete systems, the aggregate must be screened for reactivity because reactive aggregates can initiate alkali‑silica reaction, which requires remediation as prescribed in Section 704.1(g). Rapid‑set concrete patching material that incorporates coarse aggregate must achieve a minimum durability factor of 80 percent at 300 cycles in ASTM C666 Procedure B, and any AASHTO No. 8 coarse aggregate used as an extender shall have an expansion index less than 0.10 to limit expansion‑induced damage. In bonded concrete overlays, the coarse aggregate must be durable stone free from slate texture or cleavage planes and durable gravel particles, both washed thoroughly; blast furnace slag aggregate is not allowed, and aggregates that exhibit alkali‑reactivity are limited to those showing a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 percent and 0.30 percent only when paired with an approved cement‑supplementary material combination that reduces the expansion to less than 0.1 percent, while any aggregate with a 14‑day expansion greater than 0.30 percent must be rejected. For crushed‑aggregate base courses, aggregates must be stockpiled to prevent segregation and degradation, frost or frozen pieces must not be placed into the mixture, and the mix may not be laid on a frozen surface when air temperature is below 40 °F or freezing temperatures are forecast within the next 24 hours; each layer must be compacted to at least 100 percent of maximum density and surface irregularities exceeding ½‑inch must be corrected immediately. Excessive asphalt covering the surface, known as flushing, is a durability concern; if the average texture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 inch the wearing course is considered flushed and must be replaced. Improper compaction can also damage aggregate, so roller adjustments are required to eliminate displacement, shoving, cracking, and aggregate breakage. [1][2]

Aggregates that are identified as polishing‑susceptible tend to raise the probability of surface deterioration such as loss of skid resistance, while aggregates with greater hardness reduce material removal during grinding operations and therefore are less prone to rapid wear. The most significant material property influencing surface friction is the polishing resistance of the coarse aggregate; higher polishing resistance makes the aggregate less susceptible to loss of friction and slower to deteriorate, whereas lower polishing resistance increases susceptibility. Susceptibility also rises under exposure conditions that produce low friction values—typically in late summer and fall—and when traffic volume is high, because both factors amplify the effect of coarse‑aggregate characteristics on surface performance [1].

For the 6.3 mm Superpave wearing course (Section 412) durability of coarse aggregate is governed by abrasion resistance, particle shape and fractured‑face content; aggregates that exceed the maximum percent loss allowed for abrasion or contain more than the permitted flat and elongated particles—no more than 10 % for a 1:5 gradation and no more than 20 % for a 1:3 gradation—are considered more susceptible to deterioration, whereas meeting the minimum crushed‑fragment percentage required by AASHTO M 323 and satisfying the specified SRL rating helps reduce that susceptibility [2]. In the Superpave mixture design described in Section 413, coarse aggregate becomes more prone to deterioration when its abrasion content exceeds the maximum percent allowed, when flat or elongated particles exceed their permitted maximum, or when the proportion of fractured faces falls below the required minimum; complying with these limits therefore reduces susceptibility, and providing aggregate that meets at least the specified SRL designation—by using a single source or an approved 50 %/50 % blend—also helps limit deterioration [2].

Section 703 states that coarse aggregate containing clay, silt, vegetation or deleterious minerals such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide is more prone to deterioration because these substances can cause inconsistent performance; for gravel used in Type A applications the susceptibility rises when absorption exceeds the maximum allowable 3.0 % determined by AASHTO T 85, while steel‑slag aggregate becomes unacceptable if its total expansion measured by PTM No. 130 is greater than 0.50 %; lightweight aggregate must keep its Aggregate Absorption Factor below 2.5 % and meet a freeze‑thaw resistance limit of no more than a 60 % decrease in dynamic modulus after 300 cycles, otherwise exposure to freezing and thawing can accelerate deterioration [2]. Section 704 requires that coarse‑aggregate absorption shall not exceed 3.0 % for gravels and 2.0 % for all other aggregates; exceeding those limits is only permitted when the concrete mix can demonstrate resistance to freeze‑thaw damage by passing 300 cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation or achieving a durability factor greater than 80, thereby reducing its susceptibility [2]. The risk of alkali‑silica reaction described in Section 704.1(g)3 is driven primarily by the aggregate’s inherent reactivity; aggregates identified as potentially reactive must be used only with the cement or cement‑SCM combinations specified and require mitigation as outlined, whereas non‑reactive aggregates do not trigger these requirements, thereby reducing their deterioration risk [2].

During seal‑coat construction (Section 470), coarse aggregate is less likely to deteriorate when it is dry or only damp and free moisture is avoided, and when the operation is performed at air, surface, and aggregate temperatures above 60 °F; excessive free moisture in the aggregate raises susceptibility, and applying emulsified asphalt while rain is imminent or when freezing temperatures are expected within 24 hours must be avoided, with shaded areas potentially requiring longer set times to prevent increased vulnerability [2]. Proper storage practices (Section 106.05) reduce susceptibility by keeping each size and type in a dedicated stockpile to prevent segregation or mixing with earth or foreign material, storing the material on a properly constructed base of at least 4 inches depth of asphalt concrete or Class C concrete, maintaining uniform moisture through water sprays, and ensuring that during cool weather the aggregate temperature stays not less than 40 °F, which eliminates frost‑related deterioration [2].

Production testing limits (Section 703.2) indicate that coarse aggregate becomes more prone to deterioration when a high percentage loss is observed in the soundness test after five immersion‑drying cycles, when abrasion loss exceeds its limit, when the thin‑to‑thick particle ratio is greater than 5:1, or when the coefficient of uniformity falls below 3.5 for any sample; elevated amounts of fines finer than the 75 µm sieve, crushed fragments, deleterious shale identified after four wetting‑drying cycles, friable particles, coal, coke, glassy particles above the permitted 4 % (for cement concrete) or 10 % (for other uses), metallic iron (unless waived for asphalt mixtures), and clay lumps also increase susceptibility, while aggregates that meet the minimum average coefficient of uniformity of 4.0 and stay within the specified limits for soundness, abrasion, fines, glassy particles and other deleterious constituents are less likely to deteriorate [2].

Seal‑coat coarse aggregate reclaimed from RAP (Section 473) becomes more susceptible when clumps or oversized particles remain on the 12.5 mm sieve—a condition that tends to develop if the material is stored in uncovered stockpiles for more than one month after production or in covered stockpiles for more than three months—requiring reprocessing to remove the clumped or oversized material, while keeping the aggregate free of deleterious construction debris and meeting the specified gradation reduces susceptibility [2].

Crushed gravel (Section 360) must contain a minimum of 75 % crushed particles with at least three fracture faces; treating the dry aggregate with hydrated lime of at least 1 % by weight before asphalt cement is added, or alternatively using a compatible heat‑stable anti‑stripping additive as recommended by the manufacturer, reduces stripping potential and thus lowers susceptibility [2].

In pervious asphalt systems (Section 420 material) coarse aggregate becomes more prone to deterioration when its abrasion content exceeds the maximum percent defined in Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A, or when thin and elongated pieces exceed the maximum percent set by AASHTO M 323 Table 6; maintaining at least the minimum percent of crushed fragments required by AASHTO M 323 Table 6 for fractured faces helps reduce susceptibility [2]. During construction (Section 420) the susceptibility increases when compaction exceeds the limit of four roller passes, because over‑compacting can crush the aggregate and eliminate its free‑draining nature; keeping compaction within this limit preserves porosity, using smooth steel‑wheeled rollers rather than pneumatic tire rollers avoids unnecessary damage to the aggregate surface, and mixtures must not be placed when air or surface temperature is lower than 50 °F; preventing storage of equipment, materials or soils on the pervious beds during construction eliminates additional mechanical wear that could accelerate aggregate breakdown [2].

Section 221 specifies that coarse aggregate containing organic matter, clay lumps, friable particles, coal, coke, metallurgical slag or cinders is more prone to deterioration, whereas aggregate free of those deleterious substances is less susceptible; the specification also limits the plasticity index to a maximum of 3 and requires a pH between 5.0 and 9.0, with values outside these ranges increasing vulnerability, while the finer fraction of the backfill must exhibit an angle of internal friction of not less than 34 degrees, lower values indicating greater risk of degradation [2].

Section 520 requires mitigation for chemically reactive aggregates that are prone to alkali‑silica reaction, thereby reducing their susceptibility, and it calls for protective measures during the first seven days of cure to lessen deterioration caused by exposure to freezing temperatures in early curing periods [2]. Section 525 reduces susceptibility by requiring a durability factor of at least 80 % after 300 cycles (ASTM C666, procedure B) and by limiting the expansion index of any AASHTO No. 8 coarse aggregate used as an extender to less than 0.10; aggregates that do not meet these thresholds would be more prone to degradation [2]. Section 413 flags a coarse aggregate that shows water absorption above the threshold of 2.0 % (as measured by AASHTO T 85) as more susceptible to deterioration, and it prescribes keeping the apparent moisture content of the mixture at or below 0.5 % to mitigate that susceptibility [2].

Section 545 states that coarse aggregate consisting of durable stone free from slate texture or cleavage planes and durable gravel particles that have been thoroughly washed reduces deterioration, whereas blast‑furnace slag aggregate is prohibited because it would increase susceptibility; reactive aggregates may be employed only when their 14‑day expansion lies between 0.10 % and 0.30 % and an approved cement‑supplementary blend can limit the mitigated expansion to less than 0.1 %, while any aggregate showing a 14‑day expansion greater than 0.30 % must be rejected as more prone to deterioration [2].

Section 310 indicates that coarse aggregate exhibiting unsatisfactory cementing qualities is more prone to deterioration, but its susceptibility can be reduced when limestone fines are blended at 20 % of the material; granulated blast‑furnace slag is acceptable provided its dry rodded density does not exceed 90 lb/ft³, whereas a density greater than 90 lb/ft³ raises concern; harmful amounts of clay, silt, vegetation or other objectionable substances also increase risk, and this is mitigated by using uniformly clean slag with a maximum size of 2 inches and limiting fines to no more than 20 % passing the No. 100 sieve [2].

Section 312 (type DG) indicates that susceptibility rises when the mix contains frost or frozen pieces, when aggregates are allowed to segregate or degrade during stockpiling, when moisture is lost during handling, when the base course is placed on a frozen surface, when air temperature is below 40 °F, or when freezing temperatures are forecast within the next 24 hours; susceptibility is lowered by keeping aggregates free of frost, storing them to prevent segregation and degradation, using clean hauling vehicles, covering the mixture to retain moisture, maintaining proper moisture content throughout construction, and observing the temperature restrictions [2].

Material interactions and compatibility

Coarse aggregate must be inert and essentially free of clay, silt, vegetation and deleterious minerals such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide because those substances can cause inconsistent performance in asphalt concrete or hardened Portland cement concrete. For all Type A use the maximum allowable absorption determined according to AASHTO T 85 is 3.0 %; for all Type B use it is 3.5 %. Do not exceed 3.0 % absorption for gravels and 2.0 % absorption for all other aggregates. Coarse aggregates with higher absorption may be allowed provided mix‑design specimens meet 300 freeze‑thaw cycles according to ASTM C666 with less than 0.04 % dilation and/or durability factor greater than 80. In cement concrete the coarse‑aggregate component is sized according to AASHTO limits and its bulk specific gravity (saturated surface‑dry basis) listed in Bulletin 14 is used in proportioning the mix; water absorption directly influences paste demand and the batch weight of aggregate must be adjusted to compensate for free water on the particles. Aggregates will be tested according to ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303 and listed in Bulletin 14, and when classified as reactive they must be used only with cements or cement‑SCM blends expressly permitted, with mitigation measures incorporated; if one or both of the aggregates (coarse or fine) used in a mix is reactive, mitigation is required. Any new source with an expansion that indicates the aggregate is non‑reactive (R0) will initially be listed with an expansion of 0.11 % (R1) requiring ASR mitigation until ASTM C1293 verification is completed. Supply the approximate absolute volume of coarse aggregate, the suggested coarse‑aggregate factor and percent absorption to the District Materials Unit before trial batching, and keep the material in a stable, uniform, saturated condition so that it is at moisture greater than saturated surface dry. Coarse aggregate must be compatible with emulsified‑asphalt binder for seal coats; the surface temperature must be above 60 °F and the aggregate should be dry or only damp. Immediately after applying the emulsified asphalt a single layer of coarse aggregate is spread at the design rate of 15–25 lb/yd² and within five minutes the material is rolled with pneumatic‑tire rollers at 40–50 psi to embed the stones before the binder sets, and if less than 50 % of the aggregate becomes embedded a fog seal may be required. In micro‑surfacing the coarse aggregate is combined with polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt binder and a filler (Type 1 Portland cement or hydrated lime) according to dry‑weight proportions; aggregates must satisfy the quality requirements of Table B, Section 703.2(a), achieve the required SRL designation, and a blend of two aggregates may be used in a 50 %‑by‑weight ratio if the blend meets or exceeds that SRL. During base‑course construction the coarse aggregate is first spread uniformly over a thin fine‑material bed and compacted to create a skeletal framework; after thorough compaction a uniform layer of fine material is spread, water is sprinkled and the surface rolled so the fines settle, form a grout, and bond the coarse particles into a monolithic surface. For permeable bases the coarse aggregate must be Type A and, when crushed gravel is used, at least 75 % of the particles shall be crushed with a minimum of three fracture faces; before asphalt cement is introduced hydrated lime is added at a rate of at least 1 % by weight and mixed so that the lime uniformly coats the aggregate, reducing stripping potential. An optional heat‑stable anti‑stripping additive may be blended with the asphalt cement in an amount not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt and must be compatible with both the aggregate and the asphalt. Coarse aggregate supplied from an approved source listed in Bulletin 14 and meeting Table B requirements is uniformly graded together with fine aggregate, filler and asphalt binder so that the combined fractions satisfy the Joint Mix Formula composition defined in Table C; its size distribution must meet the sieve‑passing limits of 75 µm (No. 200), 2.36 mm (No. 8) and 6.3 mm (1/4 inch). In pervious concrete, coarse aggregate specified as AASHTO No. 8 Type A is incorporated with cement, supplementary cementitious materials, water, admixtures and any required geotextile; its presence influences paste demand so that the design must achieve a plastic unit weight between 115 lb and 130 lb and a water‑cement ratio of 0.36 ± 0.04, and if the coarse aggregate is reactive it must be remediated for alkali‑silica reaction. For partial‑depth repair, coarse aggregate Type A No. 8 is required for both modified and accelerated Class AA cement concrete and its use is limited by a maximum water‑cement ratio of 0.42; when rapid‑set patching material is placed at depths greater than 2 in., any aggregate extender must be AASHTO No. 8 coarse aggregate with an expansion index less than 0.10. In asphalt concrete mixes the coarse‑aggregate portion is characterized by its bulk specific gravity and percent absorption, which are entered on the Joint Mix Formula and used to compute voids in mineral aggregate and the amount of binder needed to achieve the specified Voids Filled with Asphalt; angularity and flat or elongated pieces govern inter‑particle interlock and thus load‑transfer capacity, and any change in aggregate source must be approved to keep interaction with binder and fine aggregates within warranty performance criteria. The water absorption of coarse aggregate, determined according to AASHTO T‑85, directly controls the moisture balance of an asphalt mix; when absorption exceeds 2.0 % the mixture must be resampled and the resulting apparent moisture content is limited to no more than 0.5 %, and because the coarse aggregate also defines gradation, asphalt content and theoretical maximum specific gravity, these properties are checked daily in quality‑control testing to ensure binder demand and void structure stay within specification tolerances. Within a cement‑treated permeable base course, coarse aggregate serves as the primary skeleton and its saturated surface‑dry weight is used to size both cement and water quantities; the mix design calls for 200 lb of cement per cubic yard and a water‑cement ratio that must not exceed 0.40, ensuring that the paste can fully envelop the particles, while fine material passing the No. 200 sieve is restricted to a maximum of 2 % to maintain adequate aggregate‑paste contact, and the finished mix must achieve 100 % coverage of aggregate without runoff. In bonded concrete overlay the coarse aggregate must be a durable stone or gravel that is washed thoroughly before placement; when the aggregate exhibits potential reactivity defined as a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % it may only be used if paired with an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend that limits resulting expansion to less than 0.1 %, any aggregate showing more than 0.30 % expansion is rejected, and blast furnace slag aggregate is prohibited for use in cement concrete paving applications. For flowable backfill coarse aggregate may be Type A, B or C to AASHTO 10 with no more than 10 % material finer than the No. 200 sieve and must appear on Bulletin 14; its expansion potential does not trigger alkali‑silica remediation, but a revised mix design must be submitted if the source changes because interaction with other constituents can affect density and strength. During the second application of an asphalt surface treatment emulsified asphalt is applied at a rate of 0.25 gallon per square yard to 0.50 gallon per square yard and then No. 8 coarse aggregate is spread at 15–30 lb/yd²; when the binder remains tacky, backrolling with a pneumatic‑tire roller or steel‑wheel tandem roller forces the particles to adhere to the emulsified asphalt, producing a bonded surface layer. In the first application emulsified asphalt is applied at the same rate and then a mechanical spreader distributes 25–45 lb/yd² of No. 67 coarse aggregate onto the fresh binder; rolling compacts the aggregate into the emulsion, creating the necessary bond before curing. During base‑course construction the coarse aggregate is measured separately from the fine material and blended in the prescribed proportions before any water or additives are introduced, ensuring uniform coating with moisture; calcium chloride may be added at 7 lb per ton for Type 1 (or 5.7 lb per ton for Type 2) so that the mixture is homogeneous and the coarse particles can develop a positive bond with the surrounding paste, while uniform water distribution by spray and continuous mixing prevents segregation and allows the aggregate to absorb the required moisture, which must be maintained until the next layer is placed, and frequent moisture‑control tests together with keeping base layers moist until covered reinforce that the interaction among coarse aggregate, water and any binder or additive directly controls density, strength and durability. In thin bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements the selection of coarse aggregate is tied to overlay thickness; when the overlay depth is less than 3 inches the specification requires No. 67 coarse aggregate instead of No. 57, indicating a finer aggregate is preferred for shallow sections, and macrofibers are added at a rate to achieve the design residual strength value (f150) with their contribution evaluated by running ASTM C1609‑12 at 14 days, demonstrating the fiber‑matrix interaction that helps meet performance targets. In the construction of a geosynthetic‑stabilized bridge approach coarse aggregate is placed as backfill in two 6‑inch compacted layers against each stabilization geosynthetic, building up to the elevation of the next geosynthetic layer and creating a stepped slope profile; the top layer of stabilization geosynthetic is similarly backfilled with PennDOT No. 2A coarse aggregate so that the aggregate functions by mechanically supporting and protecting the geogrid/geotextile system. Influence of production and placement

The Pavement Policy Manual states that because the polishing resistance of the coarse aggregate is identified as the most significant material property for surface friction, a mix design that selects aggregates with high resistance, together with appropriate construction techniques, determines whether the pavement will achieve and retain adequate friction throughout its service life. The Pennsylvania specifications require that mixing of coarse aggregate follow the uniform grading and proportioning requirements of the Joint Mix Formula so that the weight‑percentage composition matches the design, and the operation must be carried out at the manufacturer‑specified drum speed of 70 to 100 revolutions per minute. When the water absorption of a coarse aggregate exceeds 2.0 % as determined by AASHTO T 85, the mixture shall be sampled in accordance with PTM No. 1 and the producer quality‑control plan, and the apparent moisture content of the produced mix may not exceed 0.5 %. During production the mix water may be adjusted if the mixture appears dull or dry so that a wet metallic sheen is obtained without causing paste to flow from the aggregate; any additional water added must keep the plastic unit weight within five pounds per cubic foot of the target design unit weight. In Section 312, coarse and fine material are measured separately by volume or weight, fed into the mixer in proper proportions, and measured water is distributed uniformly with a spray system until a homogeneous blend is obtained; this practice prevents segregation and frost inclusion and underpins the strength and durability of the base course. Placement of plant‑mixed asphalt must be at the specified laying temperature and without cold lumps so that the coarse aggregate remains uniformly distributed, and spreading is done in layers whose compacted depth is not less than three inches nor more than six inches; any course exceeding six inches shall be constructed in two or more approximately equal layers to avoid segregation. For seal‑coat applications dry or only damp aggregates are used, a single layer of coarse aggregate is spread at the design rate of 15 to 25 lb per square yard with a calibrated mechanical spreader, and rolling must begin within five minutes using pneumatic‑tire rollers traveling at no more than ten miles per hour with a contact pressure of 40 to 50 psi; at least two overlapping passes are required. Each placed layer in Section 312 shall achieve not less than 100 % of the maximum density defined by PTM No. 106 Method B, and if this density is not met the layer must be re‑loosened, remoistened, reshaped and recompacted. Compaction must meet the density acceptance requirements while adjusting roller speed, amplitude, frequency, pattern and size so as to eliminate displacement, shoving, cracking and aggregate breakage; pattern segregation is deemed unacceptable when the difference in average pavement texture depth between non‑segregated and segregated areas exceeds 0.024 inch. For pervious asphalt placement shall be in maximum four‑inch compacted lifts using a track paver or mechanical spreader, with screed adjustment to achieve the design grade; each lifted layer must be placed only when air or surface temperature is at least 50 °F and allowed to cool to 100 °F before subsequent placement. Compaction of pervious asphalt shall be performed with smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode using one to four passes, limiting the number of passes to not more than four so that surface porosity is not reduced and aggregate particles are not crushed. Pervious concrete must be placed within 60 minutes of mix completion and within 90 minutes after final introduction of any mix water; placement shall occur when air or surface temperature is at least 50 °F, and each layer must cool to 100 °F before the next. Compaction of pervious concrete uses smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode with one to four passes and a maximum two‑ton roller, achieving a hardened density within ±5 lb/ft³ and not exceeding 130 lb/ft³ while preserving open voids that allow infiltration rates above 200 in/hr. Finishing for Section 312 requires the use of templates and straightedges to detect surface irregularities exceeding one‑half inch, which are then corrected by blading or reshaping; after asphalt placement the exposed outer edges are worked to eliminate sharp, ragged and open edges, reducing edge breakdown. In pervious concrete a light sprinkling of water followed by rolling continues until voids are filled and a slight wave of grout forms in front of the roller wheels. Curing practices include using a polymer‑modified latex modifier that enables the micro‑surfacing mix to cure fast enough for traffic within one hour without damaging the surface, protecting pervious concrete from freezing for the full seven‑day curing period as required by ACI 306.1, and applying hydration‑stabilizing admixture increments of five degrees Fahrenheit in hot weather to provide time for placing and finishing before initial set. Curing should begin within twenty minutes of discharge and a polyethylene cover shall be maintained for at least seven days to prevent moisture loss, allowing the concrete to gain sufficient compressive strength while preserving aggregate voids. Thus, according to the Pavement Policy Manual, mixing, placement, compaction, finishing and curing practices collectively influence whether the coarse‑aggregate pavement will retain adequate friction over its service life. During construction the long‑term behavior of coarse aggregate is most strongly governed by any variation that causes the material to fall outside the limits set in the applicable specifications. In the Superpave 6.3 mm wearing‑course requirements (SECTION 412—SUPERPAVE MIXTURE DESIGN) the coarse aggregate must not exceed the maximum percent loss for abrasion loss specified in Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A, must not contain flat or elongated particles retained on the No. 4 sieve above 10 percent for a 1:5 gradation or above 20 percent for a 1:3 gradation, and must meet the minimum percent of crushed fragments with fractured faces required by AASHTO M 323 Table 6; failure to satisfy any of these limits degrades resistance to rutting, cracking, durability and structural integrity over time. The same limits on abrasion, flat‑piece content and crushed‑fragment percentage are reiterated in the standard and RPS construction specifications for plant‑mixed asphalt courses (SECTION 413—SUPERPAVE MIXTURE DESIGN) where exceeding those percentages is identified as a defect that can degrade strength, durability and overall pavement behavior. Under SECTION 703—AGGREGATE the presence during construction of deleterious fine materials such as clay, silt, vegetation, reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide in amounts sufficient to cause inconsistent performance is identified as a primary defect that can affect durability. For all asphalt wearing courses at least 85 percent of the material must be crushed particles with two fracture faces, and the maximum allowable absorption is 3.0 percent for Type A aggregates and 3.5 percent for Type B aggregates; any excess constitutes a defect (SECTION 703—AGGREGATE). In cement concrete (SECTION 704—CEMENT CONCRETE) excessive absorption of more than 3.0 percent for gravels or more than 2.0 percent for other aggregates can compromise long‑term durability unless the mix design is proven to survive 300 freeze‑thaw cycles with less than 0.04 percent dilation or a durability factor over 80, and failure to adjust the batch weight of the aggregate for the free water it carries introduces proportioning errors that affect strength and longevity; batching by volume instead of the required weight further increases variability in the finished concrete. In seal‑coat applications (SECTION 470—ASPHALT SEAL COAT) variations that prevent proper adhesion and embedment of the coarse aggregate most affect long‑term performance: using aggregates with excessive free moisture, failing to begin rolling the aggregate within five minutes after applying the emulsified asphalt, allowing traffic before the emulsion has set, or applying when air, surface or aggregate temperatures are below 60 °F can leave less than 50 percent of the aggregate embedded in the asphalt and render the surface unacceptable. Acceptance procedures (SECTION 703—AGGREGATE, ACCEPTANCE OF CONSTRUCTION AGGREGATES) require that a Percent Within Limits less than 90 percent for source verification, project verification or lot acceptance samples trigger additional sampling and may shift the aggregate from certification to lot‑by‑lot acceptance; any crushed‑fragment test result that does not meet its lower limit contributes to a Degree of Non‑Conformance calculation, and weight discrepancies greater than three percent between invoiced and verified net mass are deemed excessive and require immediate corrective action. Storage and handling (SECTION 106—CONTROL OF MATERIAL, STORAGE OF MATERIAL) must prevent segregation or contamination with earth or foreign material, avoid inadvertent mixing of different aggregate sizes when divided bins are used, and maintain uniform moisture; without a water‑spray system to maintain coarse‑aggregate moisture, these conditions may compromise durability, and allowing aggregates to drop below the required temperature of not less than 40 °F during cool or cold weather can introduce frost into the particles, also compromising long‑term performance. Production testing (SECTION 703—AGGREGATE, PRODUCTION TESTING) identifies additional defects: poor soundness evaluated by a five‑cycle sodium sulfate immersion/drying test, excessive abrasion loss measured by AASHTO T 96, high proportions of thin or elongated particles assessed with ASTM D4791 (ratio 5:1) limited to the acceptable percentages, an average coefficient of uniformity below four point zero (individual samples not below three point five), excess fines finer than the 75‑µm sieve, insufficient crushed fragments, deleterious shale determined by four wet‑dry cycles, friable particles identified by PTM 620 loss, glassy particles exceeding four percent for cement concrete or ten percent otherwise, and the presence of metallic iron and clay lumps identified visually; each of these constitutes a defect that can degrade durability, reduce strength and cause premature distress. Construction practices that introduce segregation of the placed material, inadvertent mixing of subgrade or subbase into the base course, surface irregularities exceeding one‑half inch, compacted depth deficiencies of one‑half inch or more, and damage to the finished surface must be corrected by removal and replacement with well‑graded material (SECTION 310—CRUSHED AGGREGATE BASE COURSE; SECTION 313—BASE COURSE CONSTRUCTION). In permeable base courses (SECTION 360—ASPHALT TREATED PERMEABLE BASE COURSE) at least a minimum of 75 percent of the crushed gravel must provide three fracture faces, and aggregate containing at least 3 percent free moisture is required to ensure effective lime treatment and limit stripping potential. For pervious asphalt pavement systems (SECTION 420—PERVIOUS ASPHALT PAVEMENT SYSTEM) compacted thickness may not be more than one‑quarter inch less than the design thickness, compaction is limited to no more than four passes, pneumatic tire rollers are prohibited, placement must be avoided when air or surface temperature is below 50 °F, and over‑compaction that eliminates free draining or crushes the aggregate is forbidden. In cement‑treated permeable base courses (SECTION 303—CEMENT TREATED PERMEABLE BASE COURSE) material passing the No. 200 sieve is restricted to a maximum of 2 percent, fine aggregate is expressly forbidden, and at least 75 percent of crushed particles must possess a minimum of two fracture faces. Bonded concrete overlays (SECTION 545—BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF CONCRETE‑SURFACED PAVEMENTS) require durable stone free from slate texture or cleavage planes, thorough washing during production, prohibition of blast‑furnace slag aggregate, and rejection of aggregates with 14‑day expansion greater than 0.30 percent; aggregates with expansion between 0.10 percent and 0.30 percent may be used only when paired with an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material combination that reduces the expansion to less than 0.1 percent. In crushed aggregate base courses (SECTION 310) stone having unsatisfactory cementing qualities may be used only if limestone fines in an amount of 20 percent are blended, granulated blast furnace slag is acceptable provided its density does not exceed 90 pounds per cubic foot, and slag material must be reasonably uniform, free from harmful amounts of clay, silt, vegetation or other objectionable substances, with a maximum size of 2 inches and not more than 20 percent passing the No. 100 sieve. Flowable backfill (SECTION 220) limits particles passing the No. 200 sieve to no more than 10 percent, requires coarse aggregate to be Type A, B or C as defined in AASHTO 10, treats use of material that does not meet this classification as a defect, and mandates submission of a revised mix design if the source of material is altered. In crushed‑aggregate base course type DG (SECTION 312) aggregates must be stockpiled for proportioning in a manner that prevents segregation and degradation, spreaders must place material without segregation, each layer must be compacted to not less than 100 percent of maximum density, surface irregularities exceeding one‑half inch must be corrected because they create weak zones, any area where the depth is one‑half inch or more deficient must be removed and replaced, and cutting into a cured layer or forming construction joints without scarifying and moistening the existing surface must be avoided to preserve a continuous bond. Finally, in ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses (SECTION 489) coarse aggregate must come from an approved source listed in Bulletin 14 and satisfy size requirements; if the percent passing the 75‑µm sieve falls outside the tolerances set out in Table E, or if the mixture does not stay within the JMF tolerances specified in Table E, the material is considered defective and corrective action is required. Across all of these specifications, any construction‑introduced variation that causes the coarse aggregate to exceed the prescribed limits for abrasion loss, flat or elongated particle content, crushed‑fragment percentage, absorption, moisture content, segregation, contamination, improper compaction, temperature constraints, or failure to meet acceptance testing thresholds directly influences its long‑term performance and durability. Selection, specification, and improvement

In rehabilitation projects where unsuitable material is removed by rubblization, the specification requires that AASHTO No. 1 coarse aggregate be used in place of a flexible‑base course replacement or concrete patches [1]. When designing an asphalt wearing course to provide surface friction, the primary selection criterion is the polishing resistance of the coarse aggregate because this property most strongly influences skid performance; the aggregate must also carry a Skid Resistance Level (SRL) designation that reflects its behavior in properly designed dense‑graded asphalt as verified by AASHTO T 242 friction tests, and the appropriate SRL is chosen according to the anticipated initial average daily traffic on new facilities or the current ADT for resurfacing, following the guidance in Table 5.4 while mix design and construction techniques are considered part of the overall criteria [1]. Coarse aggregate used in any asphalt wearing course or in a 6.3 mm thin overlay must be sourced from producers listed in Bulletin 14, satisfy the Superpave quality requirements of Bulletin 27 with any Section 412.2 modifications, carry at least the SRL designation specified in the contract, and may be blended 50 % by weight of each component provided the blended SRL equals or exceeds that level; it must also conform to Type A specifications of Section 703.2, meet the abrasion‑loss limit set in Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A, contain no more than 10 % flat or elongated particles for a 1:5 shape ratio (or no more than 20 % for a 1:3 ratio) as measured by ASTM D4791 on material retained on the No. 4 sieve, and satisfy the minimum crushed‑fragment percentage required in AASHTO M 323 Table 6 for the same sieve fraction [2]. For Superpave mixture designs, coarse aggregate shall be Type A material as defined in Section 703.2, the quality limits of Table B are applied, abrasion may not exceed the maximum percent shown in Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A, flat‑ and elongated‑particle percentages are limited to those given in AASHTO M 323 Table 6, crushed fragments must meet at least the minimum percentage prescribed in that table, and the final gradation shall fall within the limits of Table A [2]. In polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing, coarse aggregate must come from a Bulletin 14 source, satisfy the quality requirements of Table B Section 703.2(a), its gradation must meet the final limits of Table A, and for wearing‑course applications it must possess at least the SRL designation specified; a 50 %‑by‑weight blend of two approved aggregates may be used if the blended product attains or exceeds that SRL [2]. Seal‑coat mixes using reclaimed asphalt pavement require the coarse aggregate to be crushed, screened and graded to meet the gradation limits of Table A, be free of deleterious construction debris, originate from a source that employed only Bulletin 14‑approved aggregates, and a quality‑control plan must sample and test the finished aggregate’s gradation on the first day of production and at least once for every 1,000 tons produced; any clumps or oversized material retained on the 12.5 mm sieve shall be reprocessed, and aggregates stored uncovered longer than one month or covered longer than three months must be reprocessed prior to use [2]. For asphalt‑treated permeable base courses and pervious asphalt pavement systems, coarse aggregate shall be Type A; when crushed gravel is used the mix must contain a minimum of 75 % crushed particles each having at least three fracture faces, and the same abrasion, flat‑particle and crushed‑fragment limits described for Superpave apply [2]. Backfill placed behind extensible reinforcement of MSE walls shall meet the quality requirements of Type C or better as specified in Section 703.2(a) Table B, be free of clay lumps, friable particles, coal, coke, metallurgical slag or cinders, have a plasticity index not greater than 3, possess a pH between 5.0 and 9.0, and the portion finer than the No. 8 sieve must exhibit an angle of internal friction of at least 34 degrees; acceptable types include AASHTO No. 8, AASHTO No. 57 or PennDOT No. 2A [2]. Ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses require coarse aggregate to be obtained from a Bulletin 14 approved supplier and must satisfy all property and performance limits defined in Table B of the specification [2]. In pervious concrete pavement systems, selection follows the intended function, requiring AASHTO No. 8 Type A for concrete mixes, while detention‑basin installations may use either AASHTO No. 3 topped with AASHTO No. 57 as a choker and leveling course, or solely AASHTO No. 57 as designed [2]. Cement‑concrete applications specify coarse aggregate as Type A and limit it to one of the approved maximum sizes—AASHTO No. 467, No. 57, No. 67, or No. 8—and water absorption may not exceed 3.0 % for gravels or 2.0 % for all other aggregates; higher absorption is permissible only if mix‑design specimens demonstrate durability by meeting 300 freeze‑thaw cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation and a durability factor greater than 80, and the bulk specific gravity (saturated surface‑dry basis) listed in Bulletin 14 shall be used for proportioning; glassy particles may not exceed 4 % for cement concrete (10 % for other uses), and selection is governed primarily by potential alkali‑silica reactivity, with field performance history reviewed according to AASHTO R 80 Section 6.1, petrographic examination used to classify potentially reactive material, and expansion testing (ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303) determining the reactivity class that dictates the level of ASR mitigation required; reactive aggregates may be used only with cements or cement‑SCM combinations specified in Section 704.1(g)3, and reactivity classes are retested on a five‑year cycle to trigger mix‑design updates within ninety calendar days of any class change [2]. Production testing required by Section 703.2(b) mandates that aggregate demonstrate acceptable soundness after five cycles of immersion and drying, meet abrasion‑loss limits defined by AASHTO T 96, contain no more than the permitted proportion of flat or elongated particles as measured by ASTM D4791 with a length‑to‑thickness ratio of 5:1, achieve a coefficient of uniformity that averages at least 4.0 for the lot with no individual sample falling below 3.5, and satisfy additional limits on fines finer than the No. 200 sieve, deleterious shale, friable particles, coal or coke, and glassy particles (maximum 4 % for cement concrete and 10 % for other uses); acceptance is governed by Section 703.5 and the AASHTO No. 1 specification referenced in Section 850.2(a)1, daily shipment testing shall be performed as outlined in Section 106.03(b)3, a source‑verification sample of three increments must be taken according to AASHTO R 90 or the mini‑stockpile method, and material is accepted only when the Percent Within Limits (PWL) is at least 90 %; any result below this threshold triggers additional sampling and possible rejection [2]. For embankment and fill work, coarse aggregate must be a natural or synthetic mineral that fits within the maximum particle size that can be placed and compacted in six‑inch layers; when classified as Type 1 at least 70 % of the particles shall pass the 3/8‑inch sieve (or no more than 70 % for Type 2), less than 20 % may pass the No. 200 sieve, grades such as AASHTO No. 8, No. 57, PennDOT 2A or OGS are acceptable, and the material shall be reasonably free of organic matter, coal, carbonaceous materials or other objectionable substances and may be used wet, dry, or frozen provided it is appropriately conditioned [2]. Pipe underdrain and pavement‑base drain work requires coarse aggregate to meet at least a Type C classification as defined in Section 703.2; the required gradation is No. 57 for Type I backfill and No. 8 for Type II backfill, and the specification does not require a limit on the percentage of crushed fragments when gravel is selected [2]. Crushed‑aggregate base courses are governed by classification as Type A or Type B and size designation—No. 10 for fine material and No. 1 for coarse material—and must meet gradation requirements for the specified size, have a dry rodded density that does not exceed 90 pounds per cubic foot, be reasonably uniform and free of harmful clay, silt, vegetation or other objectionable substances, be limited to a maximum particle size of 2 inches with no more than 20 % passing the No. 100 sieve; stone having unsatisfactory cementing qualities may be used provided limestone fines in an amount of 20 % are blended with the material, and granulated blast‑furnace slag is acceptable providing it does not have a density greater than 90 pounds per cubic foot (dry rodded density, AASHTO T 19) [2]. Type DG crushed‑aggregate base courses require that aggregate be measured and proportioned separately from fine material, stockpiled in a manner that prevents segregation or degradation, contain no frost or frozen pieces before mixing, and that the mix design call for mass‑proportioning plants equipped with separate bins for each size; the placed layer must be capable of reaching at least 100 % of the maximum density specified, and if the aggregate is so coarse that standard density methods cannot be used, compaction is evaluated by its resistance to movement under the prescribed equipment [2]. Flowable backfill specifies that coarse aggregate shall be classified as Type A, B, or C and conform to the requirements of AASHTO 10 as referenced in Section 703.2, no more than 10 % of the material may pass the No. 200 sieve, and the aggregate must be obtained from a source listed in Bulletin 14 [2]. The first application of an asphalt surface treatment requires No. 67 coarse aggregate to be placed at a rate between 25 pounds per square yard and 45 pounds per square yard and uniformly spread with a mechanical spreader, while the second application calls for No. 8 coarse aggregate applied at a rate of 15–30 pounds per square yard and compacted with pneumatic‑tire rollers delivering a contact pressure of 40–50 psi [2]. For thin bonded concrete overlays where the overlay depth is less than three inches, the specification calls for No. 67 coarse aggregate rather than No. 57; the mix design must satisfy permeability requirements targeting low, very low or negligible chloride‑ion penetration after 56 days as measured by AASHTO T 277 or T 358, with limits of not more than 2,800 coulombs or resistance values of 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4‑by‑8‑in cylinders and 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6‑by‑12‑in cylinders [2]. Ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses also require coarse aggregate to be obtained from a Bulletin 14 approved supplier and must satisfy all property and performance limits defined in Table B of the specification [2].

Performance evaluation and implications

Laboratory evaluation of coarse aggregate for asphalt wearing courses relies on the AASHTO T 242 friction test, which provides quantitative results used by PennDOT to reassess Skid Resistance Level designations. The most significant material property affecting surface friction is the polishing resistance of the coarse aggregate and field evidence is obtained from properly designed and produced dense‑graded asphalt surfaces where this property governs observed friction values under traffic and environmental conditions. Long‑term performance in that source is monitored through service‑life studies that show an annual sine‑shaped cycle in friction, with low values in late summer and fall that vary according to the aggregate’s characteristics and traffic volume, confirming durability of the selected aggregate. For the 6.3 mm wearing course specified in Section 412, laboratory testing must demonstrate that abrasion loss does not exceed the maximum percent loss listed in Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A; flat and elongated particles on the No. 4 sieve may not exceed ten percent for a 1:5 aggregate‑to‑binder ratio and twenty percent for a 1:3 ratio; fractured‑face content must meet the minimum percent required by AASHTO M 323 Table 6; and the Skid Resistance Level rating prescribed in Bulletin 14 must be satisfied. Section 703.2 requires petrographic analysis according to PTM No. 518, and when durability is a concern additional tests such as an expansion test per PTM No. 130 for steel‑slag stockpiles must indicate total expansion less than 0.50 percent; failure triggers a long‑term curing period of at least six months, possibly extended by two months, before retesting. Lightweight aggregate is limited to an absorption factor not exceeding 2.5 percent as measured by PTM No. 526, and its freeze‑thaw resistance is judged by AASHTO T 161 Procedure B so that the dynamic modulus after three hundred cycles may not decrease more than sixty percent. In cement‑concrete mixes described in Section 704.1, coarse‑aggregate absorption shall not exceed three point zero percent for gravels or two point zero percent for other aggregates; higher absorption may be accepted if mix‑design specimens pass a three hundred‑cycle freeze‑thaw test per ASTM C666 showing less than 0.04 percent dilation or attaining a durability factor greater than eighty. Potential alkali‑silica reaction is first screened by field performance history documented according to AASHTO R 80 Section 6.1; petrographic examination may flag an aggregate as potentially reactive, but a laboratory expansion test using either ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303 is required to quantify the reactivity and assign the aggregate to a class in Bulletin 14, with long‑term performance monitored by retesting Type A sources on a five‑year cycle according to ASTM C1293. Section 106 requires that aggregates be stored in separate stockpiles by size and type so they can be inspected and field‑tested; verification samples (n = 3) are taken from each stockpile and tested against Tables A, B, C and D plus the Crushed Fragments test, with acceptance based on achieving a Percent Within Limits of at least ninety percent. For ongoing placement an acceptance sample (n = 3) is obtained at the point of placement for every seven‑thousand tons of material placed, and five consecutive lots must meet the same PWL threshold before certification may resume. The seal‑coat field evaluation described in Section 470 requires a Representative to inspect the completed surface one day after sealing and final sweeping; if less than fifty percent of the aggregate surface appears embedded, the Representative may require a fog seal as corrective treatment. Production testing mandated by Section 703.2(b) assigns a PennDOT Certified Aggregate Technician to conduct source testing that includes soundness loss after five sodium‑sulfate cycles per PTM No. 510, abrasion loss per AASHTO T 96, shape assessment of thin and elongated particles by ASTM D4791 Method B on material retained on the 4.75 mm sieve, gradation uniformity with a minimum average coefficient of uniformity of four point zero and individual samples not less than three point five per PTM No. 149, fines content determined by washing finer than the 75 µm sieve per PTM No. 100, specific gravity and absorption per AASHTO T 85, bulk density/voids per AASHTO T 19, and petrographic limits that restrict coal, coke and glassy particles to a maximum of four percent for cement concrete and ten percent otherwise; the LTS may still accept an aggregate that fails soundness if a written record demonstrates satisfactory long‑term performance. For pervious asphalt pavement systems in Section 420.2, laboratory evaluation uses the same abrasion, flat‑and‑elongated piece, and crushed‑fragment criteria as Superpave, while field performance is demonstrated by constructing two test sections of at least 225 ft² each with the approved joint mix formula, performing infiltration testing per ASTM C1701 to achieve an average rate exceeding two hundred inches per hour, and ensuring the compacted thickness does not fall more than one quarter inch below design. Long‑term performance of asphalt concrete covered by a sixty‑month warranty (Section 496) is tracked by a warranty bond that remains in effect for sixty months from the effective date or until remediation identified in final annual performance surveys is completed. The LTS testing for Superpave mixtures under Section 413 also requires water absorption measured by AASHTO T85; values exceeding two point zero percent trigger additional mixture sampling per PTM No. 1, and apparent moisture content must not exceed zero point five percent. For cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303) laboratory evaluation includes wash loss per PTM 100, sieve analysis limiting material passing the No. 200 sieve to a maximum of two percent, and a visual check that at least seventy‑five percent of crushed gravel particles display two fracture faces; this section does not cite field or long‑term performance evidence. For bonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545) an expansion test requires reactive aggregates to have fourteen‑day expansion between 0.10 percent and 0.30 percent, with the approved cement‑supplementary material blend reducing resulting concrete expansion to less than 0.1 percent; field verification includes water‑cement ratio checks using batch tickets and AASHTO T318‑02 fresh‑concrete moisture testing, while long‑term performance is limited by overall mortar expansion not exceeding 0.02 percent at fourteen days and 0.06 percent at eight weeks. The hardness of the coarse aggregate determines the amount of material that must be removed during diamond‑grinding or non‑grooved chip sealing, so harder aggregates increase construction and maintenance costs and require contractors to identify the aggregate type for realistic unit pricing (Section 4.7 DIAMOND GRINDING AND GROOVING | 5.8 PROVIDING FRICTION IN ASPHALT WEARING COURSES) [1]. When the aggregate is prone to polishing, longitudinal diamond grooving is recommended because it helps retain skid resistance and surface texture, thereby preserving friction performance (Section 4.7) [1]. Polishing resistance is identified as the most significant material property governing surface friction, so aggregates that resist polishing help maintain adequate tire‑pavement grip throughout service life and reduce the risk of water pressure buildup (Section 5.8) [1]. Designers assign a Skid Resistance Level (SRL) to each aggregate source based on its properties, must select an SRL appropriate to the anticipated initial average daily traffic for new construction, overlays or resurfacing, and record that designation on design approvals, typical sections and contract proposals; using an excessively high SRL on non‑wearing courses, leveling courses or shoulders is prohibited (Section 5.8) [1]. Matching the coarse‑aggregate SRL to traffic volume allows the pavement’s friction performance to be retained under traffic and environmental conditions, supporting durability and safe operation (Section 5.8) [1]. The hard, tough and durable nature of coarse aggregate, together with its freedom from clay, silt, vegetation and other deleterious substances, underpins the strength and long‑term durability of both asphalt and concrete pavements (Section 703.2) [2]. For Type A coarse aggregate the maximum allowable absorption determined according to AASHTO T 85 is 3.0 % and for all Type B use it may not exceed 3.5 %, while lightweight aggregate must meet an Absorption Factor max of 2.5 % (Section 703.2) [2]. Do not exceed 3.0 % absorption for gravels and 2.0 % absorption for all other aggregates, and when the water absorption of a coarse aggregate determined per AASHTO T 85 exceeds 2.0 %, the specification requires additional mixture sampling and mandates that the produced mix keep apparent moisture content at or below 0.5 % (Section 525.2) [2]. Higher absorption may be allowed provided mix‑design specimens meet 300 freeze‑thaw cycles according to ASTM C666 with less than 0.04 % dilation and/or a durability factor greater than 80 (Section 703.2) [2]. The coarse‑aggregate portion of a Superpave mix must satisfy that its abrasion loss not exceed the maximum percent specified in Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A, that thin and elongated pieces stay within the maximum percent allowed by AASHTO M 323 Table 6 for flat and elongated particles, and that fractured faces meet at least the minimum percent required by the same table; keeping abrasion low, limiting thin or elongated particles, and ensuring sufficient crushed fragments allows the mix to achieve the required strength and durability during design, construction and service (Section 413.2) [2]. The specified coarse‑aggregate types (AASHTO No. 467, 57, 67 or 8) set the maximum particle size used in pavement concrete and therefore control the gradation and load‑transfer capacity of the mix (Section 703.2) [2]. Coarse aggregate passing the No. 200 sieve is limited to a maximum of 2 % and no fine aggregate may be used in the mixture, so only coarse particles provide structural support (Section 420.3) [2]. When crushed gravel is selected, at least 75 % must be crushed particles with at least two faces resulting from fracture, supplying the interlock needed for strength and stable construction performance (Section 420.3) [2]. For wearing courses the aggregate must provide at least the specified SRL designation, and when existing material does not achieve the required SRL, blends are 50 % by weight of each aggregate to obtain an SRL equal to or better than the target (Section 413.2) [2]. The reactivity class of the aggregate—determined from field performance history, petrographic examination and especially expansion testing per ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303—is a key factor in pavement durability; if one or both of the aggregates used in a mix is reactive, mitigation is required as specified (Section 704.1) [2]. Any new source with an expansion that indicates the aggregate is non‑reactive (R0) will initially be listed with an expansion of 0.11 % (R1) requiring ASR mitigation until ASTM C1293 testing by LTS is completed, and existing Bulletin 14 aggregates are retested on a five‑year cycle; any change in reactivity class that increases the value forces the development and submission of new concrete mix designs within ninety calendar days (Section 704.1) [2]. When potentially reactive aggregates are used, the specification limits 14‑day expansion to between 0.10 % and 0.30 % only if an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend can reduce the expansion to less than 0.1 %, and any aggregate showing more than 0.30 % expansion must be rejected (Section 545.2) [2]. Total expansion determined according to PTM No. 130 is required to stay less than 0.50 % (Section 703.2) [2]. During base‑course construction the coarse aggregate must be spread uniformly over the fine‑material bed and kept well graded; any segregation is removed and replaced with well‑graded material before compaction, and the material is placed in layers whose compacted thickness is limited to between 4 inches and 8 inches, or when vibratory equipment is used, between 5 inches and 10 inches (Section 310.3) [2]. Uniform spreading, strict control of layer depth, and thorough compaction fill voids with fines and produce a monolithic surface, which directly supports the strength, durability and long‑term performance of the pavement while reducing the need for later maintenance (Section 310.3) [2]. Each layer must be compacted promptly—no more than the distance that can be churned and compacted in an average day’s work ahead of choking and compacting—to prevent loss of stability (Section 310.3) [2]. For base‑course spreading, adjustable self‑propelled mechanical spreaders must place and screed the mixture without segregation, and moisture must be kept uniform so that each layer can be compacted to not less than 100 % of maximum density as determined by PTM No. 106 Method B; if this density is not attained, additional compaction or replacement of the affected area is required (Section 312.3) [2]. Mass‑proportioning plants equipped with separate bins for each size aggregate must be used to measure coarse and fine material separately, by either volume or weight, and feed them into the mixer in proper proportions (Section 312.3) [2]. Immediately after applying the emulsified asphalt, a single layer of coarse aggregate is uniformly spread at the design rate using a mechanical spreader capable of spreading 15 pounds per square yard to 25 pounds per square yard, and within five minutes the aggregate is rolled with pneumatic‑tire rollers at a speed less than or equal to 10 miles per hour and a contact pressure from 40 pounds per square inch to 50 pounds per square inch (Section 470.3) [2]. Use dry aggregates or damp aggregates for seal coats; avoid aggregates with excessive free moisture (Section 470.3) [2]. In pervious asphalt systems the coarse aggregate must retain its void structure during placement; compacted thickness cannot be more than ¼‑inch less than the design thickness and compaction is limited to not more than four passes with a static steel‑wheeled roller, because additional passes can crush the aggregate and close the pores (Section 420.3) [2]. Provide separate stockpiles for each aggregate size and type at cement concrete plants; do not use aggregates that become segregated or mixed with earth or foreign material, store aggregates on a constructed base course of at least 4 inches depth, control uniformity of moisture and gradation, keep the aggregates dry and at uniform moisture through water sprays, and maintain them at a temperature of not less than 40 °F before and during batching operations (Section 106.05) [2]. Pattern segregation—continuous or repeated areas where coarse and fine particles are non‑uniformly distributed—directly alters the surface macrotexture and density of the mat; the pavement is deemed unacceptable when the average texture depth difference between segregated and well‑mixed zones exceeds 0.024 inch, and it may be classified as defective if sieve‑gradation deviations reach 20 % or more, core density falls outside limits, asphalt content is off‑spec, or the mixture fails the percent passing the 75 μm (No. 200) sieve, triggering immediate corrective actions that affect performance, durability, construction schedule and future maintenance needs (Section 413.3) [2].
