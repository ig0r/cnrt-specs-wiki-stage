Coarse Aggregate

Part of: Mix Design is-subtopic-of
Coarse Aggregate is a topic under Mix Design.
coarse-aggregate
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Coarse aggregate is the larger fraction of an aggregate mix composed of hard, tough, durable, uncoated inert particles that are reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation and deleterious minerals such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide [1]. In both asphalt wearing courses and hardened Portland‑cement concrete it functions as the bulk skeleton whose quality governs strength, durability and reliable service by providing interlock, resisting polishing, and influencing grinding or grooving costs through its hardness and polishing tendency [1][2]. It must satisfy defined size limits (e.g., AASHTO No. 467, 57, 67 or 8), absorption caps (≤3 % for Type A gravels, ≤2 % for other aggregates), and performance criteria such as maximum abrasion loss, limited flat/elongated particles, minimum crushed‑fragment content, and an appropriate Skid Resistance Level designation to ensure adequate friction and long‑term pavement performance [1][2].

Material purpose and applicability

Coarse aggregate is the larger fraction of an aggregate mix composed of hard, tough, durable, uncoated inert particles that are reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation and other deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide; in asphalt concrete and hardened Portland cement concrete these particles provide the bulk material whose quality must avoid substances that could cause inconsistent performance, thereby supporting strength, durability and reliable service of the pavement. In the context of Portland‑cement concrete pavements, coarse aggregate is the larger mineral particles that are mixed with cement and natural sand, and its hardness and tendency to polish directly affect how much material must be removed during diamond grinding, NGCS or grooving, which in turn controls the cost of those surface‑treatment operations and influences the resulting pavement friction and durability. For cement‑concrete pavements coarse aggregate refers to larger mineral particles—stone, gravel or slag—identified as Type A and limited to the AASHTO maximum sizes No. 467, No. 57, No. 67 or No. 8; it is one of the essential ingredients together with Portland cement, fine aggregate, water and air‑entraining admixture, and its absolute volume, weight per unit yard, dry‑loose weight, specific gravity and absorption are required inputs for concrete mix design to ensure the specified gradation and performance criteria. The reactivity of coarse aggregate must be evaluated because a reactive coarse aggregate can trigger alkali‑silica reaction; if one or both of the aggregates (coarse or fine) used in a mix is reactive, mitigation is required for all concrete used in paving or permanent structures on Department projects. In volumetric mixing plants the coarse aggregate is a measured ingredient that must be kept separate from fine aggregate to avoid contamination during loading and transport, and the feeding system coordinates its flow with cement and fine‑aggregate feeds so that the mix contains the required proportion of coarse aggregate, which is controlled to a weight tolerance of ±2 %. In Superpave asphalt mixtures the larger‑sized mineral particles meeting Section 703.2 Type A must satisfy quality criteria that include a maximum abrasion percent according to Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A, a maximum thin or elongated piece percent according to AASHTO M 323 Table 6, and a minimum crushed‑fragment percent according to the same table; by meeting these limits the coarse aggregate contributes to a mixture that conforms to Superpave design requirements and thus supports the intended strength and durability of the pavement. For polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing the larger‑sized fraction supplied for the mix must be obtained from approved sources and meet the quality requirements of Table B Section 703.2(a); combined with fine aggregate and filler it achieves the required overall gradation and provides at least the specified shape‑rating‑level for wearing courses, thereby contributing to structural integrity and surface texture. In asphalt wearing courses coarse aggregate is the component whose polishing resistance largely controls surface friction; selecting a source of coarse aggregate by its Skid Resistance Level (SRL) designation ensures that the pavement develops and retains adequate tire‑pavement friction throughout its service life under traffic and environmental conditions. In an asphalt seal‑coat the dry or damp larger stone particles are placed as the top layer; immediately after applying the emulsified asphalt a single layer of coarse aggregate is uniformly spread at a design rate using a mechanical spreader capable of delivering 15 pounds per square yard to 25 pounds per square yard, and within five minutes it is rolled with pneumatic‑tire rollers under a contact pressure of 40 psi to 50 psi at speeds not exceeding ten miles per hour to embed the stones, providing a protective wearing surface. In crushed‑aggregate base courses (Section 310) coarse aggregate, called “coarse material,” is the larger rock particles placed over a thin bed of fine material; it is spread uniformly on that initial layer to full width unless otherwise specified and then compacted with rollers or vibratory equipment to create a dense, load‑bearing base that supports the pavement structure and provides voids for later filling with fines. In pervious asphalt pavement systems (Section 420 material) coarse aggregate is the larger‑sized fraction of the aggregate blend identified as Type A coarse aggregate referenced to Section 703.2 and used in Asphalt JMF mixes, while a similar coarse fraction such as AASHTO No. 3 or No. 57 may be placed in detention basins; its role is to supply the required coarse component of the mix within the quality limits set for abrasion, thin pieces, and crushed fragments. In extensible reinforcement for mechanically stabilized earth walls (Section 221) AASHTO No. 8 coarse aggregate provides a free‑draining fill required in areas that need drainage or are at or below the 100‑year flood elevation, and it must meet the quality requirements of Type C coarse aggregate or better, being free of clay lumps, friable particles, coal, coke, metallurgical slag, or cinders. In ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses (Section 489) coarse aggregate supplied from an approved source listed in Bulletin 14 and required to meet the specifications set out in Table B is combined with fine aggregate, filler and asphalt binder to form a plant‑mixed asphalt concrete wearing course where size, uniform grading and proportion of the coarse fraction are controlled so that the total aggregate and binder satisfy Joint Mix Formula composition and performance criteria. In geosynthetic stabilized bridge approaches (Section 224) larger graded materials such as AASHTO No. 1 or No. 3 and PennDOT No. 2A placed as backfill provide a stable, load‑bearing base for the pavement system; a sufficient thickness of No. 2A aggregate is placed and compacted on the proof‑rolled surface to cap and fill any surface voids, followed by two 6‑inch compacted layers of the required coarse aggregate up to each successive geosynthetic layer, with the topmost layer also consisting of two 6‑inch compacted layers of PennDOT No. 2A coarse aggregate. In asphalt surface‑treatment specifications (Section 480) coarse aggregate identified as Type A material No. 8 and No. 67 is provided so that the mix achieves at least the SRL designation indicated in the contract item; a blend of two aggregates may be used provided each component contributes 50 % by weight and the blended material meets or exceeds that SRL, with the first application spreading No. 67 coarse aggregate over emulsified asphalt applied at 0.25–0.5 gallon per square yard at a rate of 25‑lb/yd² to 45‑lb/yd², then rolling and sweeping to form the exposed wearing surface, and the second application applying No. 8 coarse aggregate over tacky emulsified asphalt at a rate of 15‑lb/yd² to 30‑lb/yd², back‑rolling until it adheres, thereby creating the protective stone layer of the pavement surface. In pervious concrete pavement systems (Section 520 construction) coarse aggregate denotes the larger stone material used in the detention‑basin portion; it is placed over a fine‑aggregate base in lifts not exceeding eight inches and compacted as required, providing a stable graded layer that supports the pavement and facilitates drainage. Coarse aggregate should be used only in asphalt wearing courses, where its Skid Resistance Level (SRL) is selected according to the anticipated initial ADT for new facilities or the current ADT for resurfacing, and the chosen SRL must be shown on design documents. Any SRL that is too high is prohibited on non‑wearing courses, leveling courses, and shoulders, which therefore require a different material. In cement concrete work, Coarse Aggregate Type A is specified for the AA Slip Form Paving, AA Form Paving, AA Accelerated Paving and HES Paving layer types. Its use requires that absorption not exceed 3.0 % for gravels or 2.0 % for other aggregates. If higher absorption is unavoidable, the mix must satisfy a durability test of 300 freeze‑thaw cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80, otherwise an alternative material should be selected. In pervious asphalt pavement systems, coarse aggregate is specified for the detention‑basin layer and may also be required in the permeable base course when the design calls for buses or light trucks. Placement of any pervious pavement mixture containing coarse aggregate may not occur when the air or surface temperature is lower than 50 °F, implying that a different material or construction approach should be used under colder conditions. For those pervious layers, the subgrade beneath must be left as undisturbed, un‑compacted soil so that permeability is retained. [1][2]

Composition and characteristics

Coarse aggregate consists of hard, tough, durable, uncoated inert particles that are reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation and deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide. It may be produced from durable stone, durable gravel, air‑cooled blast‑furnace slag, steel slag, granulated slag, lightweight aggregate or recycled concrete, each meeting the applicable physical limits. For Type A material absorption shall not exceed 3.0 % as determined by AASHTO T 85 while Type B may allow up to 3.5 %, and blast‑furnace slag must have a unit weight of at least 70 lb/ft³ (Section 703.2). Gravel intended for asphalt wearing courses must contain at least 85 % crushed particles with a minimum of two fracture faces, whereas material used as open‑graded subbase requires at least 75 % crushed particles with three fracture faces (Section 703.2). Coarse aggregate in asphalt wearing courses is defined primarily by its polishing resistance, which is the most significant material property influencing surface friction, and aggregates are classified by a Skid Resistance Level (SRL) that reflects performance in properly designed dense‑graded asphalt surfaces and AASHTO T 242 friction tests (Section 5.8). For micro‑surfacing the coarse aggregate must possess the SRL designation specified for the wearing course, and if a single material cannot meet that SRL the contractor may blend two aggregates each contributing 50 % by weight provided the blended SRL equals or exceeds the required level (Section 483; Section 5.8). In seal‑coat applications using reclaimed asphalt pavement the aggregate must be crushed, screened and graded to meet Table A, be free of deleterious construction debris, contain no clumps or particles retained on a 12.5 mm sieve, and any oversized material must be removed before shipment; if stored uncovered for more than one month—or covered for more than three months—it must be reprocessed to eliminate such clumps and oversize particles (Section 473). Pervious asphalt pavement systems require Type A aggregate; the maximum abrasion percentage shall follow the values set out in Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A, thin or elongated pieces may not exceed the maxima listed in AASHTO M 323 Table 6, and a minimum percent of crushed fragments as prescribed in the same table must be present (Section 420). For flowable backfill coarse aggregate shall be classified as Type A, B, or C in accordance with AASHTO 10; up to 10 % may be finer than the No. 200 sieve and the material must be sourced from a supplier listed in Bulletin 14 (Section 220). In asphalt‑treated permeable base courses coarse aggregate is Type A, and when crushed gravel is used the mix must contain at least 75 % crushed particles with each particle displaying a minimum of three fracture faces (Section 360). Within embankment and fill placement specifications coarse aggregate is classified as Granular Material Type 2 and may consist of natural or synthetic mineral aggregates that have no more than 70 % passing the 3/8‑inch sieve (therefore at least 30 % retained) and less than 20 % passing the No. 200 sieve; this category includes AASHTO No. 8 or No. 57 as well as PennDOT No. 2A or OGS when they satisfy Section 703.2 (Section 206). Asphalt surface treatment specifies coarse aggregate as Type A material identified by numbers 8 and 67, which must be supplied so that its Stone Ratio meets at least the designation called for in the contract item; blends of two aggregates are permitted provided each constitutes 50 % by weight and the combined SRL equals or exceeds the specification (Section 480). Seal‑coat requirements state that coarse aggregate shall be Type A or No. 8, and on roadways with less than 1,000 average daily traffic it may be No. 89; the aggregate must conform to a gradation listed in Table A, possess the SRL specified in the contract item, be sourced from a supplier identified in Bulletin 14, and blends are allowed under the same 50 %‑by‑weight rule (Section 470). The cement‑treated permeable base course permits coarse aggregate of Type A or Type B for AASHTO No. 67 or No. 57, which may be blast‑furnace slag or crushed gravel; the portion passing a No. 200 sieve is limited to a maximum of 2 %, and if crushed gravel is selected at least 75 % of the particles must be fractured with a minimum of two faces (Section 303). In Class AA cement concrete overlay coarse aggregate is Type A sized No. 57, No. 67 or No. 8 and may be stone, gravel or slag; stone must be durable and free from slate texture or cleavage planes, gravel must consist of durable particles, both shall be washed thoroughly during production, and blast‑furnace‑slag aggregate is expressly prohibited for use in cement‑concrete paving applications (Section 545). Pipe underdrain and pavement base drain provisions require coarse aggregate to be Type C or better; no percentage of crushed fragments is required when the aggregate is gravel, No. 57 is used as Type I backfill while No. 8 serves as Type II backfill (Section 610). The crushed‑aggregate base course allows stone or granulated blast‑furnace slag classified as Type A or Type B and sized as No. 10 (fine) or No. 1 (coarse); stone with poor cementing qualities may be blended with limestone fines equal to 20 % of the mix, whereas acceptable slag must have a dry rodded density not greater than 90 lb/ft³, be reasonably uniform and free of harmful clay, silt or vegetation, be limited to a maximum particle size of 2 inches and contain no more than 20 % passing the No. 100 sieve (Section 310). Coarse aggregate shall be stored separate from other sizes or types to prevent segregation, kept in covered stockpiles or bins, and its moisture must be controlled by water sprays so that it remains free of excessive free moisture; during cool weather the temperature shall not fall below 40 °F before and during batching (Section 106.05). During the second‑application asphalt surface treatment coarse aggregate is identified as No. 8 sized stone particles that are spread at a rate of 15 to 30 lb per square yard using a mechanical spreader and then compacted with pneumatic‑tire rollers applying a contact pressure of 40 to 50 psi (Section 480). Quantitative descriptors used in mix design include the approximate absolute volume in cubic feet, the suggested coarse‑aggregate factor expressed in pounds per cubic yard, the oven‑dry loose weight in pounds per cubic foot, the specific‑gravity factor and percent absorption; when lightweight aggregates are used they must be stored and used in a stable, uniform, saturated condition so that moisture at batching exceeds the saturated‑surface‑dry state (Section 704.1). In volumetric mixed‑cement concrete systems the weight of coarse aggregate is measured within a tolerance of plus‑or‑minus 2 % of the design proportion, with separate covered bins and accurately controlled gates delivering the material to the mixer (Section 704.3). Full depth reclamation may use coarse aggregate supplied either by an approved producer listed in Bulletin 14 or as reclaimed material that meets a specified size such as AASHTO No. 57, and for design purposes it must conform to the classifications defined in Section 703.2—Type A, B, or C (Section 344). Ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses define coarse aggregate as the larger particles supplied by an approved source listed in Bulletin 14 and require compliance with Table B of that section (Section 489). Asphalt concrete pavement with a 60‑month warranty identifies coarse aggregate by material code/class and supplier code, characterizes it by measured bulk specific gravity and percent absorption, evaluates shape and texture through consensus properties including angularity and the presence of flat or elongated pieces, requires the specified SRL designation, and mandates sourcing from a supplier listed in Bulletin 14 (Section 496). Mechanically stabilized earth wall backfill uses natural mineral particles meeting AASHTO No. 8 (or No. 57 or PennDOT No. 2A) and Type C quality or better, free of organic matter, clay lumps, friable particles, coal, coke, metallurgical slag or cinders; the fines portion must have a maximum plasticity index of 3, a pH between 5.0 and 9.0, and an angle of internal friction not less than 34°, while the coarse aggregate itself is exempt from angle‑of‑friction testing (Section 221). Variations in the mineral composition and source rock of coarse aggregate change its hardness, and that hardness directly influences the cost of diamond grinding because the amount of material to be removed is a function of aggregate hardness; for aggregates that are polishing‑susceptible, longitudinal diamond grooving after grinding helps retain skid resistance [1]. The petrographic properties of the aggregate also determine its polishing resistance, which is identified as the most significant material property affecting surface friction, so aggregates that polish more readily lose friction faster over the annual cycle [1]. Because these petrographic differences are tied to each quarry source, PennDOT assigns every approved source a Skid Resistance Level (SRL) and designers must select an SRL appropriate for the expected traffic volume; selecting a source with a higher SRL or blending aggregates to meet the required SRL directly affects the pavement’s ability to retain adequate friction throughout its service life [1]. Coarse aggregate must be free from deleterious constituents such as clay, silt, vegetation, reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica, and hydrated iron oxide, since their presence directly influences durability [2]. For gravel intended for Type A use the maximum allowable absorption determined according to AASHTO T 85 is 3.0 %, whereas for all other aggregates the moisture condition is limited to 2.0 % absorption; higher absorption may be permitted provided mix‑design specimens satisfy 300 freeze‑thaw cycles according to ASTM C666 with less than 0.04 % dilation and/or a durability factor greater than 80 [2]. When steel‑slag stockpiles are used, testing of expansive characteristics must show a total expansion less than 0.50 % before acceptance, and non‑granulated slag may not exceed 20 % by mass while steel‑slag contamination may not exceed 5 % to keep bulk density, absorption, and expansion within the thresholds set in Section 703.2 [2]. For coarse aggregate used in cement concrete the maximum percentage of glassy particles allowed is 4 %, and for other uses the maximum is 10 % [2]. Mix‑design limits the maximum percent loss by abrasion as set in Bulletin 27, and flat and elongated particles are limited to a maximum of 10 % for a 1:5 ratio or 20 % for a 1:3 ratio according to ASTM D4791; a minimum percentage of crushed fragments must be achieved per AASHTO M 323, because exceeding these limits reduces resistance to wear and diminishes interlock and load‑transfer capacity [2]. If a single source cannot meet the specified SRL, the contractor may blend two aggregates in equal mass so that the blend attains an SRL designation equal to or better than the required value, and this proportioning directly influences gradation and thus pavement structural performance [1][2]. Changes in the source or composition of coarse aggregate trigger a recalibration of volumetric mixing‑plant weight measurements, which must be within ±2 %, because such variations can alter material characteristics and affect proportion accuracy; a revised mix design is required when the source changes [2]. Free water on the aggregate changes its effective weight, so the batch weight must be adjusted based on tests of representative stockpile samples using the bulk specific gravity listed in Bulletin 14 [2]. When the water absorption of a coarse aggregate, determined according to AASHTO T 85, exceeds 2.0 %, additional mixture sampling is required and the apparent moisture content of the produced mix must not exceed 0.5 %; likewise, if the theoretical maximum specific gravity varies by 0.030 or more from the previous test or from the Joint Mix Formula value, it must be reported immediately because such shifts affect calculated unfilled voids and VMA [2]. Variations in mineral composition or quarry source can change alkali‑silica reactivity, which is evaluated by field performance history, petrographic examination and expansion testing such as ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303; the resulting reactivity class determines whether the aggregate may be used only with specified cement or cement‑SCM blends and requires mitigation, and when aggregates of different reactivity levels are combined the highest level governs the preventive measures [2]. Under Superpave mixture design any variation in mineral composition or source that raises the abrasion percentage above the maximum in Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A, or increases thin/elongated pieces beyond the ASTM D4791 limits, will reduce resistance to wear; likewise a lower proportion of crushed fragments than the minimum required by AASHTO M 323 can diminish interlock and load‑transfer capacity [2]. For polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing, any change of source triggers a revised Joint Mix Formula before use, ensuring that the material’s inherent qualities remain acceptable; deviations in composition or grading therefore affect whether the mix satisfies the specified tables and consequently its performance [2]. In seal‑coat applications, if the source or gradation of aggregate changes a new seal‑coat design must be submitted; aggregates must be free of deleterious construction debris, meet the required gradation, and any clumps or oversized particles detected on the 12.5 mm sieve must be removed by reprocessing [2]. For flowable backfill coarse aggregate must be classified as Type A, B, or C according to AASHTO 10 with no more than a 10 % maximum of material finer than the No. 200 sieve, and any change in source triggers the requirement to submit a revised mix design before using the material [2]. When the overlay depth of a thin bonded concrete overlay is less than 3 inches, No. 67 coarse aggregate is used instead of No. 57 coarse aggregate, a proportioning adjustment intended to meet performance criteria for shallow overlays [2]. Pattern segregation in Superpave construction is unacceptable if the difference in average pavement texture depth between non‑segregated and segregated areas exceeds 0.024 inch, and problematic aggregates may require special testing such as PTM No. 757 or PTM No. 702 modified Method D to verify asphalt content and gradation [2].

Key material properties

Hardness of coarse aggregate is a key mechanical property because it directly controls the amount of material that must be removed during diamond grinding, grooving or NGCS and therefore influences both cost and the ability to achieve the desired surface texture. For pavements constructed with a polishing‑susceptible aggregate, low polishing susceptibility (i.e., high polishing resistance) is critical; aggregates that polish slowly retain surface texture and maintain adequate tire‑pavement grip throughout traffic and environmental exposure, and polishing resistance is identified as the most significant material property affecting surface friction, often dictating the use of NGCS when the aggregate tends to polish. Petrographic characteristics of the aggregate source are linked to friction performance because they determine how the material behaves under polishing and thus influence the assigned Skid Resistance Level. Coarse aggregate must consist of hard, tough, durable, inert particles that are reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation and deleterious minerals such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide. The maximum water absorption for Type A aggregates is limited to 3.0 % by mass as measured in accordance with AASHTO T 85; gravels may not exceed 3.0 % absorption and all other aggregates may not exceed 2.0 %, although higher absorption can be permitted when mix‑design specimens satisfy a freeze‑thaw durability requirement of 300 cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80. Natural aggregates whose absorption exceeds 2 % must also meet either a sodium‑sulfate soundness level below 16 % or a freeze‑thaw loss after 25 cycles not greater than 7.0 %. The unit weight of blast‑furnace slag used as coarse aggregate may not be less than 70 lb/ft³, and the total expansion of steel slag must be less than 0.50 % as determined by PTM No. 130; glassy particles are limited to a maximum of 4 % in cement concrete and 10 % in other applications. Freeze‑thaw resilience for coarse aggregate is limited to a maximum loss of 25 % according to PTM No. 525, providing a thermal durability criterion. Size limits require that coarse aggregate conform to the maximum size provisions of AASHTO No. 467, 57, 67 or 8, and for lightweight cement concrete the equilibrium density shall fall between 110 lb/ft³ and 117 lb/ft³ with a target of 115 lb/ft³; bulk specific gravity is measured on a saturated surface‑dry basis as prescribed in Bulletin 14. In pervious asphalt pavement systems material passing a No. 200 sieve may not exceed 2 %, fine aggregate is prohibited, and at least 75 % of crushed particles must display two fracture faces, establishing both physical gradation and fragment shape requirements. Crushed‑aggregate base courses are limited to a dry rodded density not greater than 90 lb/ft³, a maximum particle size of 2 inches, and no more than 20 % passing the No. 100 sieve; when stone with unsatisfactory cementing qualities is used, limestone fines may be blended up to 20 %. Mechanical quality is controlled by abrasion loss, which shall not exceed the maximum percent specified in Bulletin 27, Chapter 2A, Table 6A; flat‑and‑elongated particle content retained on the No. 4 sieve may not exceed 10 % for a 1:5 shape ratio or 20 % for a 1:3 ratio as required by ASTM D4791; and a minimum percentage of crushed fragments with fractured faces must be present in accordance with AASHTO M 323, Table 6. Acceptance testing requires that sieve‑size compliance and the Crushed Fragments Test meet the specified limits for each size and that the overall Percent Within Limits be at least 90 %; if it falls below this value additional verification sampling is required. Chemically, coarse aggregate must be evaluated for alkali‑silica reactivity in accordance with ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303, and mitigation measures are required when either the coarse or fine aggregate is reactive. For Class AA paving concrete aggregates must be durable stone or gravel free from slate texture or cleavage planes; reactive aggregates may be used only if their 14‑day expansion lies between 0.10 % and 0.30 % together with an approved cement‑supplementary material system, while any aggregate showing a 14‑day expansion greater than 0.30 % must be rejected. Backfill aggregates for mechanically stabilized earth walls must have a pH between 5.0 and 9.0, a maximum plasticity index of 3 as measured by AASHTO T 89/T 90, and the portion finer than the No. 8 sieve must exhibit an angle of internal friction of at least 34°. Thermal and handling requirements specify that aggregates for precoated seal coats shall contain a residual asphalt binder between 0.6 % and 1.2 % by weight of the mix (or 0.4 % to 1.0 % when other binders are used), that mixing temperature may not exceed 350 °F, that aggregate temperature may not exceed 150 °F when cut‑back asphalts are employed, and that after dry sieving for ten minutes the fraction passing a No. 200 sieve must not exceed 0.5 %. For pervious asphalt pavement the mixture should not be placed when air or surface temperature is lower than 50 °F and compaction shall be limited to no more than four passes to avoid crushing of the aggregate and loss of surface porosity. Storage requirements call for maintaining uniform moisture and gradation, preventing segregation or contamination with earth or foreign material, and keeping aggregates at a temperature not less than 40 °F before and during batching operations. Behavior and mechanisms

Coarse aggregate consists of hard, tough, durable and inert particles that are reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation and deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide, which ensures consistent performance under loading. For all Type A use the maximum allowable absorption determined according to AASHTO T 85 is 3.0%, and for all Type B use it is 3.5%. Natural aggregates with an absorption less than 2% are considered freeze‑thaw resistant and not subject to testing according to AASHTO T 103, Procedure A, while those exceeding 2% may still be accepted if the sodium sulfate soundness loss is less than 16% or the AASHTO T 103 loss after 25 cycles does not exceed 7.0%. Temperature cycling performance is evaluated by freeze‑thaw resistance tests such as a decrease of dynamic modulus at 300 cycles (AASHTO T 161, Procedure B) and a total expansion less than 0.50% for steel slag according to PTM No. 130. Aggregates containing reactive minerals are prohibited and chemical testing is required for blast‑furnace or steel slag produced after 1 January 2007 per DEP General Permit WMGR144, ensuring that deleterious chemistry does not compromise durability. Coarse aggregate used in cement concrete shall not exceed 3.0% absorption for gravels and 2.0% absorption for all other aggregates. If the absorption is higher, the mix design must demonstrate that specimens survive 300 freeze‑thaw cycles with less than 0.04% dilation and a durability factor greater than 80, indicating acceptable performance under moisture variation and temperature cycling. Coarse aggregate placed in a seal coat must be handled while the air, surface and aggregate temperatures exceed 60 °F to permit proper penetration of emulsified asphalt. The specification calls for dry or only damp aggregate and explicitly avoids material with excessive free moisture, indicating that excess moisture can impair adhesion. Rolling is required within five minutes using pneumatic‑tire rollers traveling no faster than 10 mph and applying a contact pressure of 40–50 psi to keep the stones from turning over. Vehicular traffic or loads are prohibited until the emulsified asphalt has obtained adequate stability and adhesion, otherwise distortion, flushing of asphalt, or loss of aggregate may occur. In pervious asphalt construction the coarse aggregate is placed in lifts no thicker than eight inches and must be compacted with a static steel‑wheeled roller, but compaction is limited to four passes so that surface porosity is retained. Excessive compaction would crush the aggregate and impede drainage. Placement of the pervious mixture shall not be attempted when the air or surface temperature is lower than 50 °F, indicating sensitivity to low temperatures during construction. [2]

Durability and deterioration

In SECTION 703—AGGREGATE, coarse aggregate must be free of deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide, and it should contain only minimal clay, silt or vegetation because these materials cause inconsistent performance in asphalt concrete or hardened Portland cement concrete. Durability concerns are addressed by soundness loss measured with sodium‑sulfate cycles; the aggregate is acceptable if the soundness level is less than 16 % or, when absorption exceeds 2 %, the AASHTO T 103 freeze‑thaw loss after 25 cycles does not exceed 7.0 % [2]. The Freeze‑Thaw Resistance of Aggregate limit is a maximum loss of 25 % and for steel‑slag stockpiles the total expansion determined according to PTM No. 130 must be less than 0.50 % [2]. In SECTION 412—SUPERPAVE MIXTURE DESIGN, CONSTRUCTION OF PLANT‑MIXED 6.3 mm THIN ASPHALT OVERLAY COURSES, the aggregate’s abrasion loss may not exceed the maximum percent loss specified in Bulletin 27, Chapter 2A, Table 6A, and flat or elongated particles are limited to a maximum of 10 % for a 1:5 ratio or 20 % for a 1:3 ratio as measured on material retained on the No. 4 sieve according to ASTM D4791; a minimum percentage of crushed fragments with fractured faces must be met as defined in AASHTO M 323 Table 6 [2]. In SECTION 704—CEMENT CONCRETE, water absorption shall not exceed 3.0 % for gravels and 2.0 % for all other aggregates; if an aggregate exceeds these limits it may be used only when mix‑design test specimens satisfy at least 300 freeze‑thaw cycles per ASTM C666 with less than 0.04 % dilation and/or achieve a durability factor greater than 80, and alkali‑silica reaction is identified as a durability threat because aggregates that exhibit an expansion of 0.11 % (R1) are classified as reactive and require mitigation measures, with the highest reactivity class among mix constituents governing the approach [2]. SECTION 106—CONTROL OF MATERIAL requires that coarse aggregate must not become segregated or contaminated with earth or other foreign material; separate stockpiles for each size and type shall be provided, moisture must be kept uniform by water sprays when needed, and aggregates must be stored at a temperature of not less than 40 °F before and during batching to eliminate frost in or around the particles [2]. In SECTION 310—CRUSHED AGGREGATE BASE COURSE, performance can deteriorate when the base course becomes mixed with subgrade or subbase material, when segregation occurs, or when it is placed on wet, frozen, or otherwise unsuitable subgrade; such conditions require removal of the mixture, reshaping and recompaction of the subgrade, and reconstruction of the affected area, while insufficient compaction that allows the material to creep or wave ahead of the roller wheels creates voids that must be filled with fine material and water‑sprinkled rolling until a monolithic surface is achieved [2]. SECTION 473—ASPHALT SEAL COAT USING AGGREGATE FROM RAP specifies that seal‑coat aggregate must be free of clumps and particles that retain on the 12.5 mm (½‑inch) sieve; when such oversized material is present it is identified as a damage process that requires reprocessing, and if processed seal‑coat aggregate remains in an uncovered stockpile for more than one month, or in a covered stockpile for more than three months, it must be reprocessed to eliminate any clumps or oversized particles [2]. For pervious asphalt systems the durability of coarse aggregate is governed by three quality limits: abrasion loss must not exceed the maximum percent defined in Bulletin 27, Chapter 2A, Table 6A; the proportion of thin or elongated pieces must stay within the maximum percent set out in AASHTO M 323, Table 6 for flat and elongated particles; and a minimum percent of crushed fragments with fractured faces must be met as specified in AASHTO M 323, Table 6. Exceeding the abrasion or shape limits can lead to premature wear and loss of structural integrity, while insufficient crushed fragments may reduce interlock and increase susceptibility to breakage under traffic loads [2]. In pervious asphalt construction, compaction is limited to not more than four passes using only smooth steel‑wheeled rollers and pneumatic tire rollers are prohibited because excessive compaction eliminates free drainage and damages the particles; storage of equipment, materials, or soils on pervious pavement beds during construction is also prohibited, as such storage can contaminate or physically damage the aggregate [2]. In concrete pavements (section 4.7 DIAMOND GRINDING AND GROOVING), when the coarse aggregate is prone to polishing its surface texture can deteriorate, reducing skid resistance and thereby requiring remedial treatments such as longitudinal diamond grooving or next‑generation concrete surfacing to retain friction; the hardness of the coarse aggregate also influences how much material must be removed during grinding, which in turn affects maintenance cost [1]. In asphalt wearing courses (section 5.8 PROVIDING FRICTION IN ASPHALT WEARING COURSES), because surface friction depends heavily on the polishing resistance of the coarse aggregate, loss of texture through polishing is a primary durability concern; as traffic smooths the aggregate surface, skid resistance declines, with especially low values observed in late summer and fall and the magnitude of that decline varying with the aggregate’s petrographic characteristics and traffic volume, and initial friction measurements may appear adequate but do not guarantee sustained performance over the pavement’s service life [1]. Under a 60‑month warranty for asphalt concrete pavements, coarse aggregate must be sourced from an SRL‑designated supplier and its durability is evaluated through moisture‑sensitivity testing (tensile strength ratio or Hamburg wheel tracking) together with documentation of shape characteristics such as coarse‑aggregate angularity and the presence of flat or elongated pieces; these factors are recognized as mechanisms that can cause stripping, loss of bond, or premature structural damage [2]. Pattern segregation—continuous or repeated areas of non‑uniform distribution of coarse and fine particles in the finished mat—can compromise coarse‑aggregate performance, and when the difference in average pavement texture depth between segregated and non‑segregated zones exceeds 0.024 inch the pavement is considered defective; aggressive compaction that does not keep the mixture in proper condition may produce displacement, shoving, cracking, or aggregate breakage, so roller speed, amplitude, frequency, pattern and size must be adjusted to eliminate those effects [2].

Material interactions and compatibility

According to Section 703, coarse aggregate must be hard, tough, durable and essentially inert, being free from clay, silt, vegetation and deleterious minerals such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide because those substances can cause inconsistent performance in asphalt concrete or hardened Portland cement concrete. Section 703 also limits water absorption to a maximum of 3.0 % for all Type A use, while natural aggregates that absorb less than 2 % are considered freeze‑thaw resistant and therefore exempt from additional testing. In cement concrete the coarse aggregate (Type A, maximum size AASHTO No. 467, No. 57, No. 67 or No. 8) is blended with Portland cement, fine aggregate, water and admixtures, and Section 704.1 requires adjustment of the batch weight to compensate for the free water on the aggregate based on representative stockpile tests. Section 704.1 further limits absorption to not exceed 3.0 % for gravels and 2.0 % for all other aggregates, although higher‑absorption material may be permitted if mix‑design specimens satisfy the freeze‑thaw durability criteria of 300 cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80. Section 704.1(c) mandates that lightweight aggregates be stored in a stable, uniform, saturated condition so that their moisture content is greater than saturated‑surface‑dry at batching. During volumetric mixing, Section 704.3 requires the coarse aggregate to be stored in its own sealed compartment to prevent contamination, and the batching system must deliver coarse‑aggregate weight within a ±2 % tolerance while coordinating feed rates with the cement feed to achieve the required proportions. Section 106.05 calls for separate stockpiles for each aggregate size and type, active moisture control by water sprays, and maintenance of aggregates at a temperature not less than 40 °F before and during batching so that frost does not develop on the particles. Section 704.1(e) states that coarse aggregate can participate in alkali‑silica reaction when combined with cement or cement‑SCM binders; its potential to cause deleterious expansion is evaluated through field performance history, petrographic examination and ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303 testing, the resulting reactivity class governs whether mitigation measures must be applied, and new Type A sources lacking listed LTS expansion are initially tested by AASHTO T 303 and treated as reactive (R1) until ASTM C1293 results are available, at which point mix designs must be updated within ninety calendar days if the reactivity class changes. In the pre‑coat process for asphalt seal coat, Section 471.2 requires the coarse aggregate to be deliberately bonded to a thin film of asphalt binder so that at least ninety percent of its visible surface is covered, with residual binder content required to lie between 0.6 % and 1.2 % by weight of the mix; if visual inspection shows less than ninety percent coverage, acceptance is limited to a passing percentage on the No. 200 sieve that does not exceed 0.5 %. For conventional asphalt seal coats, Section 470.3 specifies that dry or only slightly damp aggregates are used while excessive free moisture is avoided, the aggregate is spread uniformly immediately after the binder is laid, and rolling must begin within five minutes of application using pneumatic‑tire rollers at a contact pressure of 40 psi to 50 psi and a speed not exceeding ten miles per hour, which promotes sufficient embedment of the stones in the asphalt film; if less than fifty percent of the aggregate becomes embedded a fog seal may be required. In micro‑surfacing systems, Section 483.2 mandates that coarse aggregates obtained from sources listed in Bulletin 14 satisfy the quality limits of Table B, Section 703.2(a) and are often blended at 50 % by weight of each aggregate to achieve the required SRL designation; they are combined with a filler—either Type 1 Portland cement or hydrated lime—whenever the mix needs enough material passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve, both proportioned by dry‑weight of the aggregate in the certified mix design, and polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt supplied as a latex‑based modifier is added in the percentages specified on the JMF so that it coats the graded aggregate skeleton, providing adhesion and rapid cure while water free of harmful soluble salts supplies the moisture needed for the emulsion to set. When constructing a crushed‑aggregate base course, Section 310.3 directs that the coarse aggregate be first spread uniformly over an initial fine layer placed to a depth of 2 inches on subgrade or 1 inch on subbase and then compacted; after this thorough compaction the fine material is again spread uniformly over the surface to fill the voids within the coarse mass, and water is sprinkled while rolling so that the water and fines migrate into those voids, producing a slight wave of grout ahead of the roller wheels that bonds the aggregate particles into a monolithic layer. In pervious concrete, Section 520.2 specifies that coarse aggregate designated as AASHTO No. 8 Type A is combined with cement, supplementary cementitious materials, fine aggregate, water and admixtures to achieve the required mix proportions, the design calling for a plastic unit weight between 115 pounds and 130 pounds and a water‑cement ratio of 0.36 ± 0.04; any extra water may be added only if the resulting plastic unit weight remains within 5 pounds per cubic foot of the target design unit weight, thereby controlling paste demand, and when reactive coarse aggregates are present the mixture must be remedied for Alkali‑Silica Reaction as required in Section 704.1(g). For asphalt concrete covered by a 60‑month warranty, Section 413.2 records the coarse aggregate’s bulk specific gravity and percent absorption in the mix design so that the calculated Voids in Mineral Aggregate (VMA) and Voids Filled with Asphalt (VFA) reflect the amount of binder needed to meet the warranty performance criteria; by specifying the aggregate class, its angularity, flatness, and absorption together with the asphalt cement’s bulk specific gravity, the volumetric design ensures that the coarse particles provide sufficient skeletal support while allowing the binder to fill the remaining voids, thereby controlling moisture sensitivity and overall mixture durability, and when a blend is used it may consist of 50 % by weight of each aggregate source, which must still meet or exceed the designated SRL designation for wearing courses. Section 413.2(d) requires that if the water absorption of a coarse aggregate, determined according to AASHTO T 85, exceeds 2.0 %, the mixture must be resampled and the apparent moisture content of the produced mix is limited to not exceed 0.5 %; the high‑absorption aggregate also triggers daily verification of asphalt content, gradation and theoretical maximum specific gravity from the same sample for mixes greater than fifty tons per day, which together control volumetric properties such as air voids and VMA within the production limits. In cement‑treated permeable base courses, Section 303.2 states that the coarse aggregate serves as the skeleton and its saturated surface‑dry rodded unit weight is used to size both the cement (200 lb of Type I or II cement per cubic yard) and the water; the mix design must not exceed a water‑cement ratio of 0.40, which together provide full 100 % coverage of aggregate without runoff, the mixture may substitute fly ash for up to 10 % of the required cement, fine aggregate is prohibited, no more than 2 % of material may pass the No. 200 sieve, and when crushed gravel is used at least 75 % of the particles must have two fracture faces, all of which control paste demand and binder‑aggregate interaction for durability. For bonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements, Section 545.2 requires the coarse aggregate to be a durable stone or gravel that is washed thoroughly before being placed in cement concrete, while blast‑furnace slag aggregate is prohibited; if the aggregate shows any reactivity it may only be used when its 14‑day expansion lies between 0.10 % and 0.30 % and an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend is provided that reduces the measured expansion to less than 0.1 %, otherwise aggregates with a 14‑day expansion greater than 0.30 % are rejected. In flowable backfill, Section 220.2 permits coarse aggregate of Type A, B, or C meeting AASHTO 10 to contain no more than 10 % material finer than the No. 200 sieve, and alkali‑silica remediation is not required regardless of the expansion value of the aggregates used in the mix design, indicating that any potential reactivity with the cement binder does not require additional treatment. During the second application of an asphalt surface treatment, Section 480.3 directs that emulsified asphalt be laid at a rate of 0.25–0.50 gallon per square yard and then No. 8 coarse aggregate be spread at 15–30 lb per square yard; pneumatic‑tire rollers apply a contact pressure of 40–50 psi, and when the emulsified asphalt has a tacky surface backrolling forces the coarse particles to adhere to the binder, establishing the required aggregate‑binder bond. In base‑course construction, Section 310.2 requires that the coarse and fine material be measured separately from the fine material and blended in the prescribed proportions before water is introduced; calcium chloride is added at 7 lb per ton of mixture for Type 1 or 5.7 lb per ton for Type 2 prior to the water, a chemical interaction that helps control absorption and ensures adequate paste demand, promoting uniform bonding between adjacent lanes and successive layers provided the mixture remains moist until the next layer is placed. For thin bonded concrete overlays with an overlay depth of less than three inches, Section 541.2 specifies No. 67 coarse aggregate instead of No. 57 to match the limited cement paste volume in a shallow layer, influencing workability and paste demand; macrofibers are added at a rate that achieves the specified residual strength value (f150), allowing the fibers to interact with the cementitious matrix through fiber‑matrix bonding. Finally, Section 413.4 states that the pavement will be considered defective when the payment factor for asphalt content, percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve, and percent passing the No. 8 sieve all reach 85 %; additionally, when either the coarse aggregate or fine aggregate in the mixture has a water absorption of ≥1.5 % a supplemental procedure for porous aggregate is required to adjust binder demand. Influence of production and placement

Because the polishing resistance of the coarse aggregate is identified as the most significant material property for surface friction, a mix design that selects aggregates with high resistance is essential; this mix design, together with proper placement, compaction, finishing and curing—collectively described as construction techniques—determines whether the pavement will achieve and retain adequate friction throughout its service life under traffic and environmental conditions. In SECTION 471—ASPHALT SEAL COAT USING PRECOATED AGGREGATE, the mix is required to be produced at a temperature not exceeding 350 °F so that the asphalt cement can form a thin brownish film covering at least 90 % of the aggregate surface; when cut‑back asphalts are used the aggregate temperature must not exceed 150 °F to protect film integrity, and the mixed material shall be stocked on a suitable base no higher than four feet to control placement uniformity and preserve the coating. SECTION 106—CONTROL OF MATERIAL specifies that fine and coarse aggregates be batched separately while maintaining uniform moisture content and gradation; a water‑spray system may be employed as required to maintain coarse‑aggregate moisture control, and during cool or cold weather concrete production the aggregates must be kept at a temperature of not less than 40 °F before and during batching so that frost is eliminated. SECTION 483—POLYMER‑MODIFIED EMULSIFIED ASPHALT PAVING SYSTEM requires aggregate fractions to be sized, uniformly graded, and combined in the proportions given in Table A; blending shall be performed by an approved method using mixing equipment documented on a certified calibration sheet dated within 60 days of use, and the latex‑based polymer modifier must cure fast enough to permit traffic placement on the pavement within 1 hour without damaging the surface. SECTION 413—SUPERPAVE MIXTURE DESIGN states that when the coarse‑aggregate water absorption exceeds 2.0 % (AASHTO T 85) the apparent moisture content of the mix shall not exceed 0.5 %, and the mixture is produced at the SGC compaction temperature that yields the target air voids defined in the joint mix formula; compaction quality is verified by preparing a minimum of two specimens from each sample in accordance with AASHTO T 312 and confirming that air voids and VMA match the target values set in Table B. SECTION 520—PERVIOUS CONCRETE PAVEMENT SYSTEM allows the mix water to be adjusted during production if the mixture appears dull or dry so as to achieve a wet metallic sheen without causing paste to flow from the coarse aggregate, provided that the plastic unit weight remains within 5 lb/ft³ of the target design unit weight (115–130 lb); the pervious concrete must be protected from freezing throughout the full 7‑day curing period, and in hot weather a hydration‑stabilizing admixture is added in 5 °F increments to provide sufficient time for placing and finishing before initial set; curing begins within 20 minutes of discharge and the pavement is covered with polyethylene for at least 7 days, followed by a subsequent 60‑day cure required to achieve a resistivity greater than 2000 ohm·cm and to maintain pH between 5.0 and 9.5 as well as chloride, sulfate and sulfide concentrations within specified limits. SECTION 470—ASPHALT SEAL COAT requires that immediately after applying emulsified asphalt a single layer of dry or slightly damp coarse aggregate be uniformly spread at a design rate of 15 to 25 lb/yd² using a mechanical spreader, and rolling shall begin within five minutes with a minimum of two passes at a speed not exceeding 10 miles per hour and a contact pressure from 40 pounds per square inch to 50 pounds per square inch; vehicular traffic is prohibited on the newly completed surface until the emulsified asphalt has attained adequate stability and adhesion, and shaded areas may require longer set times. SECTION 310—CRUSHED AGGREGATE BASE COURSE mandates that coarse aggregate be spread uniformly over the fine‑material bed to full width and placed in layers whose thicknesses are limited to no less than 4 inches nor more than 8 inches when compacted with rollers alone, or no less than 5 inches nor more than 10 inches when vibratory equipment is used; compaction begins at the shoulders and proceeds toward the center using rollers or vibratory rollers as required, continuing until the material no longer creeps or waves ahead of the roller wheels, after which fine material is spread and water sprinkled while rolling so that a thin grout wave forms and fills remaining voids, producing a smooth hard monolithic surface. SECTION 224—GEOSYNTHETIC STABILIZED BRIDGE APPROACH requires that coarse‑aggregate backfill be placed and compacted in two 6‑inch lifts, the material being placed gently by hand, light rain from an excavator bucket, or gradual blading rather than dumped directly onto the exposed geosynthetic; when the subgrade consists of AASHTO No. 1 or No. 3 coarse aggregate, R‑rock, or shot rock, a sufficient thickness of No. 2A aggregate is placed and compacted on the proof‑rolled surface to cap voids and irregularities. SECTION 206—PLACEMENT AND COMPACTION OF EMBANKMENT AND FILL specifies that placement and compaction of coarse aggregate must respect the size limit that allows the material to fit within compacted 6‑inch layers, because oversized particles would prevent achieving the required density and thus degrade pavement performance; the coarse aggregate used in Type 2 granular material, such as AASHTO No. 8 or 57, is acceptable only when it meets this size criterion and is reasonably free of organic matter, coal, carbonaceous materials, or other objectionable substances. SECTION 420—PERVIOUS ASPHALT PAVEMENT SYSTEM requires placement of the pervious mix using a track paver or mechanical spreader in maximum 4‑inch compacted lifts, and the mixture should be allowed to cool to 100 °F before any subsequent layer is placed; compaction shall be performed with pneumatic‑tire rollers providing a contact pressure of 40 to 50 psi, but no more than four passes are permitted so that the aggregate remains free‑draining and is not crushed. SECTION 480—ASPHALT SURFACE TREATMENT (SECOND APPLICATION) directs that emulsified asphalt be applied at a rate of 0.25 to 0.50 gallon per square yard, followed by spreading No. 8 coarse aggregate at 15 to 30 lb per square yard with a mechanical spreader; when the emulsified asphalt has a tacky surface, back‑rolling shall be performed until the aggregate adheres; final surface finish is achieved with a maximum 2‑ton roller to eliminate seams, and any surface irregularities exceeding ½ inch shall be corrected immediately by blading, loosening, reshaping, and recompacting; edges must be finished immediately after spreading to prevent ragged or open edges. SECTION 312—CRUSHED AGGREGATE BASE COURSE, TYPE DG requires that if the base course is more than 6 inches in compacted depth it be constructed in two or more layers of approximately equal depth, with no layer less than 3 inches nor more than 6 inches; each layer shall be compacted to not less than 100 % of maximum density. SECTION 220—FLOWABLE BACKFILL specifies that coarse aggregate of Type A, B, or C per AASHTO 10 may be used provided no more than ten percent passes the No. 200 sieve; before adding water the required quantities of materials shall be proportioned and uniformly blended, and mixing shall occur at a manufacturer‑specified speed of 70 to 100 drum revolutions. Selection, specification, and improvement

Coarse aggregate used in pavement projects must be hard, tough, durable and essentially free of clay, silt, vegetation and deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide, and it must be obtained from a source listed in Bulletin 14 or otherwise approved by the Laboratory Testing Services. [2] When unsuitable material is replaced after a rubblization process prior to overlay, the specification calls for AASHTO No. 1 coarse aggregate rather than flexible‑base course replacement or concrete patches. Performance evaluation and implications

In the laboratory, coarse aggregate intended for asphalt wearing courses is evaluated with the AASHTO T 242 friction test; the quantitative results are used by PennDOT to reassess Skid Resistance Level designations. Field evidence of the same aggregate is obtained from properly designed and produced dense‑graded asphalt surfaces, where the polishing resistance of the coarse aggregate governs the observed friction values under traffic and environmental conditions. Long‑term performance is monitored through service‑life studies that reveal an annual sine‑shaped cycle in friction, with low values in late summer and fall that vary according to the aggregate’s characteristics and traffic volume, thereby confirming the durability of the selected aggregate. Laboratory evaluation of coarse aggregate across the specifications begins with petrographic analysis performed in accordance with PTM 518 and is supplemented by durability‑related tests such as soundness (PTM 510), abrasion (AASHTO T 96), freeze‑thaw resistance, specific gravity and absorption (AASHTO T 85); when steel slag is used, expansion testing per PTM 130 is required and the total expansion must be less than 0.50 %. For the 6.3 mm wearing course the abrasion loss must not exceed the maximum percent defined in Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A, flat and elongated particles are limited to a maximum of 10 percent for a 1:5 ratio and 20 percent for a 1:3 ratio as determined on the No. 4 sieve according to ASTM D4791, and crushed fragments must meet the minimum percent specified in AASHTO M 323 Table 6 for fractured faces on the same sieve size; the aggregate also must satisfy the prescribed SRL rating. In cement concrete the water absorption shall not exceed 3.0 % for gravels or 2.0 % for all other aggregates, and if higher absorption is proposed the mix‑design specimens must pass 300 freeze‑thaw cycles according to ASTM C666 with less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80; bulk specific gravity is determined on a saturated‑surface‑dry basis using values listed in Bulletin 14. For pre‑coated seal coat the visual inspection must confirm that at least 90 % of the visible surface area is covered by asphalt film before production, and if coverage falls below that level a dry sieving on a 75 µm (No. 200) sieve for ten minutes is performed with acceptance limited to a percent passing not exceeding 0.5 %. Field verification of construction aggregates relies on three‑sample verification taken from each source stockpile and at placement before trimming and compaction, with an additional lot‑acceptance sample of three increments collected for every 7,500 tons placed; all increments are tested against the prescribed Tables A–D (including the Crushed Fragments Test where applicable) and acceptance requires a Percent Within Limits of 90 or greater, with any batch yielding a PWL below 90 prompting further sampling until the threshold is met. Superpave mixture design laboratory measurements include water absorption per AASHTO T 85 (values exceeding 2.0 % trigger additional sampling), sieve gradation obtained with PTM 757, PTM 702 or PTM 739 checked against Table A tolerances, and field evidence consists of daily production quality‑control sampling that verifies asphalt content, apparent moisture content via PTM 749, theoretical maximum specific gravity per Bulletin 27, and volumetric properties of compacted specimens prepared according to AASHTO T 312 compared with the multiple‑sample tolerances in Tables A and B. Macrotexture field evidence is obtained using PTM 751; pattern segregation is deemed unacceptable if the texture depth difference between segregated and non‑segregated zones exceeds 0.024 inch and flushing is flagged when average texture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 inch. For pervious asphalt pavement systems two test sections each at least 225 sq ft are constructed, infiltration is measured in accordance with ASTM C1701 and the average must exceed 200 inches per hour, and compacted thickness may not be more than ¼‑inch less than design thickness. Long‑term performance evidence may be drawn from field performance histories documented according to AASHTO R‑80 Section 6.1 for assessing alkali‑silica reaction potential; aggregates classified as Type A are retested by the LTS on a five‑year cycle using ASTM C1293, and asphalt concrete pavement with a 60‑month warranty is monitored under that warranty with annual performance surveys confirming compliance. The hardness of the coarse aggregate governs how much material must be removed during diamond‑grinding or NGCS, so harder aggregates raise construction and maintenance costs and specifications often require the contractor to identify the aggregate type to develop realistic unit prices [1]. When the aggregate is prone to polishing, longitudinal diamond grooving (or NGCS) is recommended because it helps retain skid resistance and surface texture, thereby preserving friction performance [1]. The most significant material property affecting surface friction is the polishing resistance of the coarse aggregate, so aggregates that resist polishing help maintain adequate tire‑pavement grip throughout service life and reduce the risk of water pressure buildup [1]. Coarse‑aggregate SRL designations are assigned based on these properties; designers must select an SRL appropriate to the anticipated initial ADT for new construction, overlays or resurfacing, and record that designation on design approvals, typical sections and contract proposals, while using an excessively high SRL on non‑wearing courses, leveling courses or shoulders is prohibited [1]. By matching the coarse‑aggregate SRL to traffic volume, the pavement’s friction performance can be retained under traffic and environmental conditions, supporting durability and safe operation [1]. Coarse aggregate shall consist of hard, tough, durable particles that are free from clay, silt, vegetation and deleterious minerals such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide [2]. For Type A applications the maximum absorption determined in accordance with AASHTO T 85 may not exceed 3.0 % and for Type B it may not exceed 3.5 %; aggregates absorbing less than 2 % are automatically considered freeze‑thaw resistant, while higher absorption is permissible only when either the sodium sulfate soundness level is less than 16 % or the loss after 25 freeze‑thaw cycles does not exceed 7.0 % [2]. In cement‑concrete pavements coarse aggregate shall not exceed 3.0 % absorption for gravels and 2.0 % for all other aggregates, and higher absorption may be allowed provided mix‑design specimens meet 300 freeze‑thaw cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation and a durability factor greater than 80 [2]. When the water‑absorption characteristic of coarse aggregate exceeds 2.0 %, additional sampling shall be performed and the mixture must be produced with an apparent moisture content that does not exceed 0.5 % [2]. At least 85 % of asphalt wearing‑course gravel shall be crushed particles having two fracture faces (three for open‑graded subbase) to provide interlock, and flat or elongated particles shall not exceed 10 % for a 1:5 aggregate‑to‑asphalt ratio or 20 % for a 1:3 ratio [2]. The coarse aggregate must satisfy the abrasion loss limits specified in Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A, and it must contain the minimum percentage of crushed fragments with fractured faces required by AASHTO M 323 Table 6 [2]. For Superpave mixture design the aggregate shall also meet the SRL requirement; if a single source cannot achieve the designated SRL the contractor may blend two aggregates 50 % by weight each provided the blend attains an equal or better SRL [2][1]. The weight of coarse aggregate dispensed by a mass‑proportioning plant shall be within ±2 % and separate bins and covered storage shall be provided to keep fine and coarse aggregates from contaminating each other during loading and transport, with any change in the source triggering a mandatory recalibration to maintain those tolerances [2]. Coarse aggregate shall be stored in dedicated stockpiles free from earth or foreign material; a water‑spray system must maintain uniform moisture, and during cool or cold weather aggregates shall be at a temperature of not less than 40 °F before and during batching so that frost is eliminated, preserving gradation, preventing segregation and helping achieve consistent mix quality [2]. Reactivity of coarse aggregate shall be evaluated by expansion testing (ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303) and each source shall be assigned a reactivity class that determines the level of alkali‑silica reaction mitigation required; if either coarse or fine aggregate is reactive, mitigation per Section 704.1(g)3 shall be mandatory for all paving concrete, with periodic retesting and mix‑design updates when the reactivity class increases [2]. For asphalt seal coats using pre‑coated aggregate the material must be free‑flowing for stocking, handling and uniform spreading by a self‑propelled spreader, receive a thin brownish translucent film that covers at least 90 % of its visible surface, and contain residual binder limited to 0.6 %–1.2 % (or 0.4 %–1.0 % with other asphalt materials); if coverage falls below the 90 % threshold the fine fraction passing a No. 200 sieve after ten minutes of dry sieving shall not exceed 0.5 % [2]. The aggregate is spread at a design rate of 15 – 25 lb/yd² and must be rolled within five minutes using pneumatic‑tire rollers traveling at no more than 10 mph with a contact pressure of 40 – 50 psi so that the stones remain properly embedded and distortion or loss of material under traffic is avoided, thereby supporting pavement durability and performance [2]. In crushed‑aggregate base courses the coarse and fine material shall be measured separately by volume or weight and fed into a mass‑proportioning plant with separate bins for each size; each layer shall be compacted to not less than 100 % of maximum density as determined by PTM No. 106 Method B, and pattern segregation is unacceptable when the difference in average pavement texture depth between segregated and non‑segregated areas exceeds 0.024 inch [2]. For pervious asphalt pavement detention‑basin coarse aggregate shall be installed in lifts not exceeding eight inches, the final compacted thickness may not be more than one‑quarter inch less than the design thickness, compaction shall be limited to no more than four passes with a ten‑ton steel‑wheeled static roller, and over‑compaction that crushes the aggregate or eliminates its free‑draining nature shall be avoided to preserve porosity and service life [2]. In pervious concrete systems detention‑basin coarse aggregate is also placed in lifts of eight inches maximum, a fine‑aggregate base at least two inches thick is provided to keep soils from entering the storm‑water storage area, the plastic unit weight shall be between 115 lb/ft³ and 130 lb/ft³, and any additional water shall be limited so that the final unit weight stays within five pounds per cubic foot of the target, thereby ensuring proper workability without excess paste flow [2]. For bonded concrete overlays the coarse aggregate must be durable stone or gravel free of slate texture or cleavage planes; aggregates that are potentially reactive may be used only if the 14‑day expansion lies between 0.10 % and 0.30 % with an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend, whereas any aggregate showing more than 0.30 % expansion shall be rejected and blast furnace slag aggregate is prohibited, preventing weak zones or excessive swelling that could reduce strength and durability [2]. In thin bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements when the overlay depth is less than three inches the design calls for No. 67 coarse aggregate rather than No. 57, a change in gradation required to achieve acceptable performance and durability in thinner sections and directly influencing mix‑design choices and structural capacity [2]. For polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing the coarse aggregate shall meet the quality requirements of Table B Section 703.2(a) and conform to the final gradation in Table A, ensuring structural stability needed for durable micro‑surfacing; wearing courses additionally require aggregate with at least the SRL designation specified, and when a single source cannot achieve that SRL the contractor may blend two aggregates 50 % by weight each provided the blend attains an equal or better SRL, allowing the design to maintain intended surface skid resistance and wear performance [2][1].
