Rehabilitation Strategy

Part of: Rehabilitation is-subtopic-of
Rehabilitation Strategy is a topic under Rehabilitation.
rehabilitation-strategiesrehabilitation-strategyrehabilitation-techniquesmajor-rehabilitationminor-rehabilitation
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

The Rehabilitation Strategy is a systematic, repeatable decision‑making framework that guides the selection of a rehabilitation treatment—such as bonded or unbonded overlays, full‑depth reclamation, rubblizing, or slab‑fracturing—by matching the pavement’s existing structural condition and surface distress to the technique most likely to restore or enhance load‑carrying capacity while minimizing total cost over the analysis period[1]. It operates on regularly collected highway‑condition data, traffic‑level estimates, in‑situ strength tests (e.g., Falling Weight Deflectometer), and representative material sampling, which together generate initial construction‑cost estimates for life‑cycle cost analysis and provide performance verification before treatment implementation[1][2]. Within the pavement‑management planning process the strategy is used to decide whether a section requires routine maintenance, major rehabilitation, or full reconstruction, ensuring that the chosen approach addresses drainage or subgrade deficiencies, complies with design specifications, and satisfies safety constraints such as clearances from utilities and required modulus limits[1][2].

Purpose and decision context

The Rehabilitation Strategy guides the decision of which rehabilitation treatment to apply to an existing pavement by matching the selected technique to the pavement’s current condition. For pavements that are in good to fair structural condition, the strategy points to a bonded overlay to add capacity and eliminate surface distress; for pavements that are moderately to significantly deteriorated, it recommends an unbonded overlay to restore capacity. In the context of Full Depth Reclamation, the Rehabilitation Strategy is intended to support the decision of whether FDR is an appropriate treatment for a given roadway, following a systematic evaluation that includes traffic level estimates, pavement condition surveys, in‑situ testing, sampling, determination of layer thicknesses and drainage conditions, and finally an applicability assessment based on observed surface distresses. Within project‑level pavement management, the Rehabilitation Strategy is used to support the decision of selecting the best treatment for a particular pavement section, ensuring that the chosen approach will meet desired service levels while minimizing total cost over the analysis period. Additionally, the Rehabilitation Strategy is intended to support the decision of which project type to select—routine maintenance, major rehabilitation, or full reconstruction—by evaluating both the maintenance and reconstruction cycles defined for the network; by comparing a pavement’s condition against those cycles, planners can determine whether a treatment will extend service life or if a more extensive rehabilitation is required. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 11 (pp. 190–193)

The Rehabilitation Strategy addresses the problem of deteriorating pavements by providing a systematic, repeatable method for developing initial construction costs that enables consistent life‑cycle cost analysis and a performance‑based approach to select the most cost‑effective treatment to achieve desired service levels within budget constraints. It is applied when an existing pavement has lost structural capacity because of age‑related or environmental cracking, cannot satisfy current or projected traffic loads, or exhibits structural deficiencies, surface deterioration, or inadequate rideability—ranging from good to fair condition for bonded treatments and moderate to severe condition for unbonded treatments—while full reconstruction is not yet required. The goal is to restore or enhance load‑carrying capacity, add structural capacity, eliminate surface distress such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, and drainage problems, and extend service life, thereby preventing further deterioration, reducing future maintenance cycles, and postponing costly major rehabilitation or reconstruction. For severely distressed pavements with weak base layers that could cause reflective cracking or pavement failure, the strategy targets restoration through full‑depth reclamation, which pulverizes existing asphalt, mixes it with underlying aggregate, and builds structure within the cross‑section to improve strength, flexibility, sustainability, and drainage while minimizing disposal and virgin material use. The approach requires reliable estimates of future traffic loading, detailed condition surveys, in‑situ subgrade testing, and representative sampling to evaluate existing structure and water‑related damage; these inputs guide selection of appropriate layer thicknesses and a stabilization mechanism (pulverization, mechanical, or chemical) that provides the required structural capacity and ensures a well‑drained pavement system. In the specific case of concrete pavements, the rehabilitation strategy is needed to restore deteriorated slabs by applying controlled full‑depth fracturing that produces continuous cracks across the full width while maintaining aggregate interlock between pieces, followed by seating the fractured pavement until no vertical movement of the substrate is observed. The performance goal for this slab‑fracture method is verified by falling weight deflectometer testing to confirm that the average elastic modulus of the fractured layer falls within the range specified in Table A before an asphalt overlay can be placed, and final seating is considered satisfactory when vertical deflection under a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller indicates non‑movement of the substrate. Scope and applicability

In accordance with Section 4.9 Rehabilitation, concrete or asphalt overlays become appropriate once a project reaches the design stage and a Falling Weight Deflectometer test has been performed on the existing Portland cement concrete pavement. For pavements that are in fair to excellent condition and where the subgrade is stable and drainage functional, bonded rigid concrete overlays may be used to add structural capacity and eliminate surface distress; for pavements that are moderately to significantly deteriorated, unbonded concrete or asphalt overlays can be employed as a new pavement placed on a stable platform capable of correcting deficiencies from minor structural loss to complete failure. Unbonded overlays are acceptable for all existing pavement conditions, whereas bonded overlays are generally suitable only for fair‑to‑excellent pavements. Section 5.15 Full Depth Reclamation Techniques states that full‑depth reclamation is appropriate when a project seeks to rehabilitate a distressed pavement, especially where the existing asphalt and base layers are severely deteriorated, and it can be used to upgrade existing bases and recycle in‑place materials, making it suitable for assets that require structural improvement without extensive new material import. The technique also serves as an initial step in staged construction, allowing additional structure to be added later as traffic demands increase. Rehabilitation using full‑depth reclamation is appropriate after the initial evaluation and assessment phase for roadways whose support layers need improvement rather than merely surface distress correction; the project must have a recent pavement condition survey, traffic level estimates, in‑situ subgrade testing such as FWD, DCP or LWD, adequate sampling to define material properties, and the design must consider existing layer thicknesses and drainage conditions before reclamation can proceed. Table 5.8 provides an indication of when full‑depth reclamation is a suitable rehabilitation strategy based on pavement surface distresses present. According to Section 12.7 100 % State Funded Pavement Preservation Guidelines, a rehabilitation strategy is appropriate for projects that apply structural enhancements to an existing pavement whose capacity has been reduced by age‑related or environmental cracking, or when additional thickness is needed to meet current or projected traffic loads. Both minor rehabilitation—non‑structural treatments that eliminate surface cracking—and major rehabilitation—structural upgrades that increase load‑carrying capability—are covered under this approach and are used after the pavement has been placed but before full reconstruction becomes necessary. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213)

A rehabilitation strategy that relies on bonded concrete overlays is not appropriate when the existing asphalt or composite pavement exhibits significant structural deterioration, unstable support, stripping of the asphalt, or drainage problems that have not been addressed; in those cases an unbonded overlay or another method should be considered. When a concrete pavement is so deteriorated that patching is no longer economical, cracking/breaking and seating the slabs to create a stable uniform support may be used instead of an overlay. If the subgrade conditions are poor or moisture levels are high, rubblizing the existing concrete is often preferred because it provides a more reliable base for any subsequent overlay. Full‑depth reclamation (FDR) is generally indicated only when improvement of the support layers is required; it becomes inappropriate when the dominant problems are surface‑related distresses, when the existing pavement lacks sufficient thickness or suitable subgrade material to form a reclaimed layer, or when drainage deficiencies such as wet subgrade cannot be corrected before work. Reconstruction, involving full removal and replacement of the structure, is required when a pavement has either failed or become functionally obsolete, making any rehabilitation strategy inappropriate. When the intended purpose of a treatment is merely to restore function and extend service life without increasing capacity or strength, a rehabilitation strategy is not appropriate; instead a pavement preservation approach such as preventive maintenance or minor non‑structural rehabilitation should be used. Pavement preservation activities are preferred for pavements that remain in good condition and have significant remaining service life, because they avoid costly rehabilitation or reconstruction. When distress consists only of age‑related surface cracking that does not affect structural capacity, the appropriate response is classified as minor rehabilitation, which belongs to the preservation category rather than a major structural rehab. Slab‑fracturing rehabilitation must be abandoned if any culvert, cross pipe or utility sleeve lies within ten feet horizontally and ten feet vertically of the slab. It also cannot be performed when the pavement is less than forty feet from a bridge, tunnel or other structure approach. Additionally, fracture work must not be undertaken if a fixed object such as a manhole, inlet or similar appurtenance is closer than three feet to the slab. Sonic vibratory fracturing equipment may only be used when its operation will not cause soil liquefaction of the underlying subgrade; otherwise an alternative treatment such as full‑depth sawcutting and segment replacement should be selected. The required FWD verification testing for the fracture‑and‑seat method cannot be performed on frozen surfaces or when ambient temperature is below 45 °F or above 90 °F, so under those thermal conditions a different rehabilitation approach should be chosen. [1][2]

Information needs

A Rehabilitation Strategy relies on regular collection of highway condition data that are entered into a computer database together with construction (site) details, traffic volumes, inventory (cost) records and maintenance histories, the stored dataset then feeding an analysis program that evaluates alternatives and selects the most cost‑effective treatment for the pavement section. For Portland cement concrete (PCC) pavements, detailed condition and site information must be obtained before selecting an overlay type; Falling Weight Deflectometer testing of the existing concrete is required to assess structural capacity, and observations of slab movement, faulting, rocking or pumping dictate whether slab stabilization or jacking is needed. Information on subdrainage problems and overall drainage functionality is also required to decide if adequate subdrainage must be provided. The existing pavement’s structural condition—rated fair‑to‑excellent for bonded overlays and moderately to significantly deteriorated for unbonded overlays—determines the appropriate overlay type, while high average daily traffic may favor thin‑bonded JPCP overlays on local streets. When selecting a full‑depth reclamation strategy, planners must gather traffic level data to obtain a reliable estimate of future loading, conduct a recent pavement condition survey that documents distress types and severity, perform in‑situ testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer to evaluate subgrade stiffness, develop a sampling plan covering number, locations, depth and handling of material samples, determine appropriate layer thicknesses based on existing pavement composition and the structural needs dictated by anticipated traffic and environmental conditions, and identify any drainage problems or excess moisture so that effective drainage can be installed before reclamation. The strategy must also be informed by traffic characteristics that define the treatment network, specifically average daily traffic and truck‑percentage thresholds used to identify recycling‑eligible routes, and it requires site information captured in the RMS such as pavement surface type code and functional class code, which together determine whether a segment belongs to the High Level Asphalt, Low Level Asphalt, Concrete or Unpaved networks. Districts may manually edit network indicators when facility usage or other factors that affect how routes are maintained suggest a different treatment, reflecting stakeholder input into the strategy. For slab‑fracturing rehabilitation, the plan must incorporate detailed condition and site data including the presence of overlying asphalt courses, joint seal conditions, slab lengths, exposed reinforcing steel, and subgrade suitability when sonic vibratory equipment is considered. Precise utility location information is required because fracturing may not be performed where culverts, cross pipes, or utility sleeves are within ten feet horizontally and ten feet from the bottom of the slab, within forty feet of bridge or tunnel approaches, or within three feet of any fixed object such as a manhole or inlet. Environmental constraints must be included; sonic equipment may not be used if soil liquefaction could occur, roller seating is prohibited in wet conditions, and Falling Weight Deflectometer testing is restricted to surface temperatures between forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit and ninety degrees Fahrenheit when the pavement surface is not frozen. Stakeholder input is required from the Representative for approval of the fracturing plan, test sections and any alternate action plans, and from utility owners to confirm that cracking limits are acceptable; traffic control measures also require prohibiting vehicular traffic after fracturing until the asphalt overlay is placed. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 11 (pp. 190–193); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

When evaluating a rehabilitation strategy such as full‑depth reclamation, the required information must be gathered with sufficient accuracy and completeness: a reliable estimate of future traffic loading is needed before design; a recent pavement condition survey—performed according to the specified manuals—must document current distress levels and their causes; in‑situ testing (e.g., Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer, or lightweight deflectometer) is required to assess subgrade strength; and proper sampling of existing materials must follow the detailed procedures for number, size, technique, depth, and handling. Together these elements ensure that the data are both current and comprehensive enough to support a sound rehabilitation decision. [1]

Alternatives

Under a Rehabilitation Strategy the set of feasible alternatives that should be compared includes a range of overlay and recycling treatments as described in the Pavement Policy Manual. A bonded concrete overlay is generally suitable when the existing Portland cement concrete pavement is in good to fair condition, the subgrade is stable and drainage is functional, and it may be placed as a thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) of 2.5 to 4 inches or as a bonded JPCP of 4 to 8 inches [1]. An unbonded concrete overlay provides structural capacity restoration for moderately to significantly deteriorated pavements and is classified as either minor or major rehabilitation depending on the extent of the enhancement [1]. Asphalt overlays sized by the AASHTO design method are also listed as a feasible alternative for structural improvement [1]. The crack‑and‑seat treatment, in which existing concrete slabs are fractured and then seated, may be combined with a structural overlay as one option to evaluate [1]. Rubblization of the existing pavement, followed by a structural overlay, is another alternative; the Type 1 rubblize process reduces the pavement to pieces no larger than 12 inches maximum, while the Type 2 process limits piece size to 8 inches maximum [1]. Full Depth Reclamation (FDR) is identified as an effective method that can be compared with Cold‑In‑Place Recycling and Hot‑In‑Place Recycling, and within FDR the feasible stabilization mechanisms include pulverization stabilization, mechanical stabilization, chemical stabilization, calcium chloride stabilization, and emulsified asphalt stabilization, each differing in the use of additives or supplemental material [1]. Additional 3R treatments that may be considered are partial recycling of the existing pavement and adding surface material to return the pavement to adequacy, as well as structural asphalt overlays and concrete overlays applied directly [1]. The Pavement Policy Manual also distinguishes minor rehabilitation—non‑structural enhancements that eliminate age‑related surface cracking—from major rehabilitation—structural enhancements that increase thickness and load‑carrying capacity [1]. In the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania specifications for slab fracturing, the feasible alternatives to compare are the Crack‑and‑Seat method and the Break‑and‑Seat method; both require establishing a minimum 1,000‑foot test section to determine the optimal fracturing pattern [2]. The seating step differs in that the roller must make at least three passes for the Crack‑and‑Seat method and at least five passes for the Break‑and‑Seat method [2]. Selection between these two alternatives is guided by whether the average elastic modulus of the fractured concrete layer, measured after falling‑weight deflectometer testing, falls within the range specified in Table A [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

The Break‑and‑Seat and Crack‑and‑Seat slab‑fracturing options both require a pre‑fracture phase that removes any overlying asphalt, saw‑cuts slabs longer than twenty feet (to about one‑inch off two‑thirds of the depth for Break‑and‑Seat) and excludes work within ten feet of utilities or forty feet of structures; they then demand impact equipment capable of producing continuous full‑depth cracks without spalling, followed by seating with a minimum fifty‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller that must make at least five passes for Break‑and‑Seat and at least three passes for Crack‑and‑Seat, using Subbase No. 2A beneath the fractured slab and a Flexible Base Replacement on top, while FWD testing of a one‑thousand‑foot test section (and subsequent lots every thousand feet) must confirm that the elastic modulus falls within Table A before production can continue, and traffic is prohibited after fracturing and seating until an asphalt leveling course is placed within one week, with any damage to utilities, bridge decks or approaches repaired at no extra cost. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Decision criteria

For full‑depth reclamation the primary factors that should drive a rehabilitation‑strategy choice are the ability to lower overall rehabilitation costs and to enhance the project’s sustainability; the technique is promoted because it reduces expenses while minimizing material disposal and virgin‑material use, thereby supporting sustainable road management. More generally, in the pavement‑management introduction rehabilitation‑strategy decisions are guided chiefly by performance metrics and life‑cycle cost considerations; the analysis seeks the most cost‑effective treatment that satisfies objective measures of system condition and performance while allowing users to impose budget or resource constraints as needed. In slab‑fracturing of existing concrete pavements the decision to employ a rehabilitation strategy must be governed primarily by safety constraints such as maintaining required clearances from culverts, utilities, bridges and other fixed objects. The safety requirement specifies that concrete pavement shall not be fractured where culverts, cross pipes, and utility sleeves are within 10 feet horizontally and 10 feet from the bottom of the slab, and shall not be fractured within 40 feet of approaches to structures, bridge decks, or tunnels. In addition, the strategy must satisfy a performance criterion demonstrated through falling weight deflectometer testing that verifies the fractured layer’s elastic modulus stays within the range set in Table A; a minimum 1,000‑foot test section is required and at least three such lots per lane mile must be tested to ensure continuous compliance. For slab‑fracturing projects safety and measured structural performance are the controlling criteria, while for full‑depth reclamation projects cost effectiveness and sustainability predominate, with performance also central. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (p. 124), Ch. 11 (pp. 190–191); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

The primary weighting criterion for a rehabilitation strategy is the structural condition of the existing pavement, which must be confirmed by FWD testing; if the pavement is in good to fair condition and the subgrade is stable with adequate drainage, a bonded overlay is preferred because it adds capacity while allowing the original structure to share load. When the pavement is moderately to significantly deteriorated, or when support stability or drainage are deficient, unbonded overlays, rubblizing, or other major rehabilitation methods become the higher‑priority alternatives. Consequently, condition of the pavement outweighs subgrade and drainage considerations, which in turn outweigh the choice between bonded and unbonded treatments. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Analysis methods and tools

The Rehabilitation Strategy evaluates alternatives primarily through life‑cycle cost analysis (LCCA) as described in the Pavement Policy Manual [1]. To support that LCCA, the manual prescribes a “Cost Estimating for LCCA” Excel spreadsheet that displays historical costs with a trendline based on price and quantity and is to be used only for the LCCA and shall not be employed for general project estimating [1]. Initial construction costs for each alternative are derived by applying a statistical method that generates a comparable price range from historic bid data based on quantity, with outliers excluded as an estimating best practice, thereby providing consistent documented cost inputs for the LCCA [1]. Systematic collection of highway condition data underpins the pavement‑management framework, and recent pavement condition surveys—conducted according to Publication 336, Publication 343, or procedures such as MicroPaver™—supply condition ratings and distress insight that are required before rehabilitation [1]. In‑situ testing, including Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing together with dynamic cone penetrometer (DCP) and light weight deflectometer (LWD) tests, yields quantitative stiffness information for the existing pavement and subgrade, while sampling follows criteria that must be considered to obtain representative material samples [1]. For slab‑fracturing projects the strategy relies on FWD testing combined with a back‑calculation analysis; the operator must perform FWD testing and use an approved software package such as MODULUS or EVERCALC to compute the elastic pavement modulus of the fractured concrete layer [2]. The average elastic modulus for each 1,000‑foot test section must fall within the range specified in Table A, and if it exceeds the upper limit the breaking pattern must be adjusted; verification testing is required on at least three one‑thousand‑foot lots per lane mile with data collected at intervals of 50 feet or less [2]. The initial decision‑making step references Table 5.8 to determine when full‑depth reclamation is a suitable rehabilitation strategy based on observed surface distresses, and an analysis program is used to identify the most cost‑effective treatments [1]. Risk‑based asset management planning required by federal guidance, together with performance measures and statistical analysis, enables cost/benefit assessments and objective documentation of treatment effectiveness over the analysis period [1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (p. 67), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 11 (pp. 190–191); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Under section 3.3 Guidelines for Developing Initial Costs of the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), the results for a rehabilitation strategy assume that the initial construction cost for each alternate is obtained by applying the statistical method to historic ECMS item prices, generating a price‑quantity trendline in the Cost Estimating for LCCA spreadsheet and excluding outliers as an estimating best practice; it further assumes that past bid data are representative of future costs. The same source notes uncertainty because the tool does not consider current market values, recent or sufficient historical entries may be lacking—requiring expansion to surrounding districts or similar items—and location adjustments can affect unit prices, while the spreadsheet is intended only for LCCA and shall not be used for project estimating. In section 4.9 Rehabilitation, the manual assumes that a falling weight deflectometer test should be performed on the existing PCC pavement before an overlay design is prepared; the choice between bonded and unbonded overlays assumes that the structural condition of the existing pavement, subgrade stability, and drainage functionality are known. Still within section 4.9 Rehabilitation, bonded overlays presuppose relatively good pavement condition with a stable subgrade and functional drainage, whereas unbonded overlays assume only a stable platform regardless of pavement quality. The manual further indicates that the presence of slab movement or faulting introduces uncertainty that must be addressed by slab stabilization or jacking before overlay placement, so uncertainties in these underlying assessments directly affect which rehabilitation method is selected and its expected performance. Under section 5.15 Full Depth Reclamation Techniques > B. Selection of Projects, the reliability of the strategy hinges on the assumption that a reliable estimate of future traffic loading can be obtained because the design must follow standard pavement design traffic analysis procedures. The same section assumes that a recent pavement condition survey and a distress survey provide accurate information about existing problems, but uncertainty is introduced by the accuracy of those surveys. Also in section 5.15 Full Depth Reclamation Techniques > B. Selection of Projects, in‑situ testing and the representativeness of collected samples are assumed to define subgrade stiffness and material properties that drive layer‑thickness decisions, while uncertainties about existing drainage conditions must be resolved before reclamation can proceed. The selection and proportion of stabilization agents rely on laboratory testing, so any variation in test results creates further uncertainty in achieving the desired strength without brittleness (section 5.15 Full Depth Reclamation Techniques). The results are further predicated on the assumption that the treatment cycles defined by the MECE initiative—derived from the PennDOT 1988‑92 Cycle Task Force recommendations, the current manual, and FHWA‑SA‑96‑027—accurately reflect pavement deterioration behavior and that the RMS‑generated surface type and functional class codes correctly place each segment in its appropriate network; it is also assumed that applying maintenance according to these cycles will extend pavement life and postpone major rehabilitation (section 11.2 Pavement Treatment Cycles). Uncertainty arises because districts may manually change a segment’s network indicator between Low and High Level Asphalt based on perceived usage or other factors, because the Recycling Network is not yet defined in the RMS, and because any lag in updating indicators when the RMS surface type or functional class changes introduces variability into the predicted outcomes (section 11.2 Pavement Treatment Cycles). In Section 522—Slab Fracturing of Existing Concrete Pavements > 522.3 Construction, the rehabilitation strategy assumes that the fracturing equipment can deliver a controllable variable force without causing surface spalling. The same section assumes that any sonic vibratory equipment will not induce soil liquefaction of the subgrade. Field testing is assumed to be carried out only when temperatures are between 45 °F and 90 °F and the pavement is not frozen (Section 522—Slab Fracturing of Existing Concrete Pavements > 522.3 Construction). Results are uncertain because the actual average elastic modulus of the fractured concrete must fall within the Table A range; if it deviates, the breaking pattern must be altered and retested, and repeated failures may require establishing a new test section to determine an optimum pattern (Section 522—Slab Fracturing of Existing Concrete Pavements > 522.3 Construction). Stakeholders and constraints

The MECE Pavement Management Subgroup provides the primary input by defining pavement‑cycle charts that serve as the basis for selecting appropriate treatments [1]. Condition data, such as falling weight deflectometer (FWD) testing of existing Portland cement concrete pavements, must be collected before any overlay design is prepared [1]. The certified Project Management Engineer or Project Manager then prepares the design package using this information and recommends its approval [1]. For PennDOT‑oversight projects the District Engineer makes the final decision, while the District Pavement Engineer exercises discretion to select the specific treatment—bonded or unbonded concrete overlay, asphalt overlay, rubblizing type, or full‑depth patching—and must approve any preparatory actions [1]. The Bureau of Design and Development reviews any waivers and performs a quality‑control review before the recommendation reaches the District Engineer [1]. District engineers may manually edit treatment‑network indicators from “L” to “H” or vice versa when facility usage or other factors justify a change, although they cannot alter the concrete (“C”) or unpaved (“U”) classifications [1]. In Federal‑oversight projects the design package first receives BODD’s review and approval; after all BODD comments are addressed it is forwarded to FHWA, which provides the ultimate approval [1]. The party that prepares and submits the pavement‑fracturing plan together with an equipment list supplies technical input for a rehabilitation strategy [2]. The Department’s Representative reviews those submissions, approves the initial plan, any alternate action plans, and all FWD test results before work may proceed or resume [2].

The selection of any rehabilitation strategy must first satisfy the design requirements set out in the PCCP Guidelines for concrete pavements (section 4.9 Rehabilitation) and conform to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242) before a project can proceed [1]. Before an overlay is designed, a Falling Weight Deflectometer test must be performed on the existing concrete pavement, and surface preparation must include full‑depth patching when approved, slab stabilization or jacking if movement or faulting exists, and adequate subdrainage where subsurface drainage problems are present [1]. Bonded concrete overlays may only be used when the underlying pavement is in good to fair condition, the subgrade is stable and existing drainage is functional, whereas unbonded overlays are required for moderately to severely deteriorated pavements and must incorporate at least a 1‑inch fine‑graded asphalt separation layer or an equivalent geotextile interlayer as specified in Publication 408 Section 548 [1]. The allowable thickness of thin‑bonded JPCP overlays is limited to 2.5–4 inches (Publication 408 Section 541) and that of thicker bonded overlays to 4–8 inches (Section 540); overlay design depth for asphalt must follow the AASHTO Design Method in Chapter 10 [1]. When a full‑depth reclamation approach is considered, the Department’s standard specification in Publication 408 Section 344 governs the required procedures and material limits, requiring equipment that fully penetrates the existing asphalt layer into the underlying base or subgrade and mandating that any stabilizing additives such as cement, fly ash, or lime kiln dust be applied in accordance with that standard [1]. Future traffic loading must be reliably estimated and the overall pavement design must be consistent with standard pavement design traffic analysis procedures, and a pavement condition survey must follow PennDOT Publication 336 or Publication 343 (or an equivalent distress evaluation) before rehabilitation can be authorized [1]. All rehabilitation treatments must receive Board of Directors (BODD) approval through the PennDOT Oversight process; Federal‑aid projects require FHWA approval unless a waiver is granted, and any departure from the manual’s specifications must be submitted as a waiver to BODD for review and approval [1]. Designers must be certified PME/PMs who have completed the mandated DARWin design portfolio, and even projects involving less than 30,000 square yards of mainline pavement surface work must provide documented justification for the chosen pavement type [1]. The policy framework of Section 119(e) of Title 23 USC obligates the State to develop a risk‑based asset management plan for the National Highway System, and the Maintenance Efficiency and Cost Effectiveness (MECE) initiative requires that the Pavement Management plan use the cycle charts derived from the 1988‑92 Cycle Task Force recommendations, with treatment alternatives fitting within the RMS‑defined Treatment Type Networks assigned by surface‑type code and Functional Class Code; districts may only switch a route between High Level Asphalt (H) and Low Level Asphalt (L), never to or from Concrete (C) or Unpaved (U) [1]. Under the 100 % State Funded Pavement Preservation Guidelines, rehabilitation strategies must be financed entirely by the state; routine maintenance activities are not normally eligible for Federal‑aid funding, which limits the use of lower‑level treatments that rely on such aid and distinguishes minor rehabilitation (non‑structural preservation) from major rehabilitation (structural enhancements) [1]. Any rehabilitation that employs slab fracturing must first have a pavement fracturing plan and equipment list approved, and the selected equipment must be capable of variable force impact without causing extensive surface spalling, punching holes, or shattering the concrete; sonic vibratory devices are only permitted when they will not liquefy the subgrade [2]. Fracturing operations are prohibited within 10 feet horizontally and vertically of culverts, cross pipes, or utility sleeves, within 40 feet of bridge approaches or tunnels, and within 3 feet of fixed objects, requiring saw‑cut segments in those zones and repair of any damage at no additional cost [2]. Seating the fractured slabs requires a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller meeting specific wheel count and inflation pressure criteria, may not use vibratory rollers, and must follow prescribed pass patterns; bridge crossings are limited to approved instances [2]. Falling Weight Deflectometer testing performed in accordance with AASHTO T 256 and ASTM D4694/D4695/D5858 standards, calibrated at a SHRP facility within the current year, and avoided when temperatures are below 45 °F or above 90 °F, must verify that the elastic modulus of the fractured layer falls within the range set in Table A before production can continue [2].

Timing and implementation

For full‑depth reclamation projects, the decision to adopt a rehabilitation strategy should be made only after the initial evaluation and assessment of the existing roadway has been completed, which must gather traffic level estimates, a recent pavement condition/distress survey, in‑situ strength testing such as FWD, DCP or LWD, and appropriate material sampling, and then determine suitable layer thicknesses and drainage conditions before evaluating applicability. More generally, the decision on which rehabilitation strategy to employ should be made during the pavement‑management planning process, when the system’s analysis identifies the optimum time for treatment; at that point, after regular condition data have been collected and entered into the Pavement Asset Management System, project‑level analysis determines the best treatment for a specific section so that it meets desired service levels at the least total cost over the analysis period. The decision should also be made during the project‑type selection phase, after both the maintenance cycles and the reconstruction cycles have been evaluated, using the defined cycle charts to assess whether a pavement is “out of cycle” in order to determine if a minor treatment or a major rehabilitation is appropriate. For slab fracturing of existing concrete pavements, the rehabilitation strategy must be decided and captured in a pavement fracturing plan that lists the proposed equipment, and that plan must be submitted for approval before any construction begins. Production fracturing may commence only after the Representative approves both the submitted plan and the test section. [1][2]

In the Pavement Policy Manual a rehabilitation strategy is triggered when a pavement shows structural deficiencies, surface deterioration, or reduced rideability, and before any overlay design the existing PCCP must be evaluated with FWD testing. If slab movement or faulting is observed, slab stabilization or jacking is required prior to rehabilitation, and where subsurface drainage problems exist adequate subdrainage must be provided. A Full Depth Reclamation rehabilitation strategy is activated when severe distress cannot be remedied by lighter treatments, when the existing base layer needs to be upgraded to provide additional structure, or when drainage and cross‑slope conditions must be restored. The initial evaluation using a recent pavement condition survey and distress assessment must identify surface distresses that match the indications in Table 5.8—such as severe rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes or drainage problems—and subgrade moisture or drainage deficiencies must be corrected before reclamation. A rehabilitation strategy is also triggered when a pavement segment falls out of its prescribed treatment cycle—that is, it has exceeded the defined timeframes and is classified as “out of cycle”—or when the RMS records a change in surface type (concrete, unpaved, or asphalt) or functional class code, prompting an update of the network indicator. Eligibility for recycling treatments depends on network classification; routes that have an ADT < 1000 and a Truck Percentage < 10% are placed in the Recycling Network and are treated according to its cycle. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require that action for a rehabilitation strategy be triggered when the required pavement fracturing plan and equipment list have not been approved before construction begins. If satisfactory fracturing results are not being obtained during operations, all work must be ceased and an alternate action plan and/or list of capable equipment must be submitted to the Representative. When FWD testing shows that the average elastic modulus of the fractured concrete layer does not fall within the range specified in Table A—i.e., exceeds the upper limit—a pattern adjustment, re‑fracture and retesting of the lot is required, or a new 1,000‑foot test section must be established after two consecutive lots fail. Results must be provided to the Representative within seven calendar days, and no further fracturing may resume until approval is received. Finally, the fractured pavement must be overlaid with an asphalt leveling course within one week of seating. [1][2]

Outcomes and follow-up

Success for a rehabilitation strategy is realized when the required pre‑overlay investigations and preparations are completed, the pavement condition is verified as good to fair by joint cores, and the selected overlay type fulfills its intended performance. For bonded overlays the goal is to add structural capacity, extend service life and reduce maintenance cycles, whereas unbonded overlays must restore structural capacity on moderately to severely deteriorated pavements through full‑depth patching, slab stabilization, adequate subdrainage, and when required a minimum 1‑inch asphalt separation layer or geotextile interlayer. Success is measured by confirming that falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD) testing is performed on the existing Portland cement concrete pavement prior to design, that any necessary slab stabilization, drainage improvements and full‑depth patching have been executed, and that overlay thickness conforms to specifications—thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavements of 2.5 to 4 inches for surface correction or 4 to 8 inches for structural addition. When rubblizing is employed the resulting concrete pieces must not exceed the maximum size stipulated—12 inches for Type 1 and 8 inches for Type 2—before overlay placement. For a full‑depth reclamation rehabilitation success is achieved when the design and construction address the identified distress, provide an appropriate reclaimed layer thickness, and ensure proper drainage so that the rebuilt pavement can support anticipated traffic loads, with documentation through a post‑survey summary of distress severity and verification by pre‑and post‑rehabilitation FWD tests (and other subgrade tests as needed) that confirm subgrade stiffness before work and required new‑pavement stiffness after construction. From a pavement management system perspective success is realized when the selected treatment provides the intended service level at the least total cost over the analysis period, meets predefined performance measures, and its life‑cycle performance—tracked by comparing forecasted versus observed pavement condition—is documented through continuous collection of condition data, a database linking traffic and maintenance information, and statistical analysis that quantifies cost‑effectiveness. Maintaining roadways according to the prescribed treatment cycles is another indicator of success because it extends pavement life and lengthens the interval before major rehabilitation or reconstruction; effectiveness is measured by monitoring compliance with each network’s cycle schedule and by quantifying the backlog of roadways that are out of cycle, where a reduction in that backlog signals that the strategy is effective. Under the 100 % State‑Funded Pavement Preservation guidelines success is achieved when the preservation treatment restores the function of the existing pavement and extends its service life while delivering broader program goals such as improved safety and mobility, reduced congestion, smoother longer‑lasting pavements, and demonstrable cost‑effectiveness, with evidence including documented preservation of investment on NHS and other Federal‑aid roadways, enhanced pavement performance, reduced user delays, and measurable improvements in overall safety and mobility. Finally, when the rehabilitation treatment provides structural enhancements that either restore existing capacity by eliminating age‑related cracking or add thickness so the section can meet current and projected traffic loads, success is measured by confirming an increase in load‑carrying capability or a documented life extension compared with pre‑treatment conditions and by demonstrating that the discounted cost of the rehabilitation series remains less than the discounted cost of full reconstruction. For a rehabilitation strategy that employs slab fracturing and seating, success is achieved when the fractured concrete meets both structural and visual criteria, beginning with a minimum 1,000‑foot test section whose average elastic modulus from FWD testing falls within the range specified in Table A and each production lot likewise satisfies that modulus limit. After fracturing, visual inspection must confirm no vertical movement of the substrate when a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic tire roller is used with a rolling pattern that provides at least three passes for the Crack and Seat method or at least five passes for the Break and Seat method. All FWD measurements must be taken on a dry surface with ambient temperature not below 45 °F nor above 90 °F, using a 9,000‑pound drop weight at intervals of 50 feet or less, and the final seated surface must be swept clean before asphalt overlay within one week. For full‑depth reclamation rehabilitation strategies, results should be monitored through both pre‑ and post‑construction investigations that include a pavement condition/distress survey, in‑situ strength tests such as Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) and optionally DCP or LWD, and sampling of reclaimed materials. Falling Weight Deflectometer testing before rehabilitation provides valuable information about the stiffness of the existing pavement materials, while testing after construction is useful for determining the stiffness of the new pavement. Results should be regularly collected and entered into a pavement management database that stores condition, construction, traffic, inventory and maintenance records, creating an objective documentation of what was done and how the pavement responded. An analysis program then uses these performance measures to conduct statistical analyses, cost‑benefit forecasts and life‑cycle evaluations, enabling agencies to refine criteria, compare alternatives and improve future rehabilitation decisions. Monitoring also includes comparing actual treatment actions to the pavement cycle charts defined in the MECE initiative, using RMS data to identify any “out‑of‑cycle” sections as indicated by the methodology that assesses backlog. Treatment type networks are recorded on the RMS Network Identification Screen based on surface type and functional class code, and districts may manually edit network indicators from “L” to “H” or vice versa according to facility usage or other factors; these documented cycle statuses and edits are fed back into decision making when selecting future rehabilitation projects. For a rehabilitation strategy involving slab fracturing of existing concrete pavements, monitoring is performed by establishing at least one 1,000‑foot‑long test section and conducting FWD tests on the fractured and seated pavement at intervals of 50 feet or less, using a drop weight of 9,000 pounds. The average elastic modulus for the test section must fall within the range specified in Table A; if it does, the test section is deemed satisfactory.
