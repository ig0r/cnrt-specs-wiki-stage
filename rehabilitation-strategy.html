Rehabilitation Strategy

Part of: Rehabilitation is-subtopic-of
Rehabilitation Strategy is a topic under Rehabilitation.
rehabilitation-strategiesrehabilitation-strategyrehabilitation-techniquesmajor-rehabilitationminor-rehabilitation
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

The Rehabilitation Strategy is a systematic, repeatable decision‑making framework that matches an existing pavement’s current structural condition and supporting factors to the most appropriate treatment—such as bonded or unbonded overlays, full‑depth reclamation, rubblizing, or slab fracturing—to restore or enhance load‑carrying capacity, eliminate surface distress, and extend service life while minimizing total cost over the analysis period [1]. It operates within pavement‑management planning by collecting regular condition data, traffic estimates, in‑situ testing (e.g., falling‑weight deflectometer), and material sampling, then evaluating alternatives through life‑cycle cost analysis and performance criteria to select a treatment that meets desired service levels and budget constraints [1][2]. Defining characteristics include its reliance on documented evaluation of pavement condition, subgrade stability, drainage functionality, and the structural weighting of condition over other factors, as well as its integration with maintenance cycles and network indicators to keep assets “in‑cycle” and prioritize interventions [1].

Purpose and decision context

The Rehabilitation Strategy guides the decision of which rehabilitation treatment to apply to an existing pavement by matching the selected technique to the pavement’s current condition. For pavements that are in good to fair structural condition, the strategy points to a bonded overlay to add capacity and eliminate surface distress; for pavements that are moderately to significantly deteriorated, it recommends an unbonded overlay to restore capacity. In the context of Full Depth Reclamation, the Rehabilitation Strategy is intended to support the decision of whether FDR is an appropriate treatment for a given roadway, following a systematic evaluation that includes traffic level estimates, pavement condition surveys, in‑situ testing, sampling, determination of layer thicknesses and drainage conditions, and finally an applicability assessment based on observed surface distresses. Within project‑level pavement management, the Rehabilitation Strategy is used to support the decision of selecting the best treatment for a particular pavement section, ensuring that the chosen approach will meet desired service levels while minimizing total cost over the analysis period. Additionally, the Rehabilitation Strategy is intended to support the decision of which project type to select—routine maintenance, major rehabilitation, or full reconstruction—by evaluating both the maintenance and reconstruction cycles defined for the network; by comparing a pavement’s condition against those cycles, planners can determine whether a treatment will extend service life or if a more extensive rehabilitation is required. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 11 (pp. 190–193)

The Rehabilitation Strategy is intended to address the problem of deteriorating pavements by providing a systematic, repeatable method for developing initial construction costs that enables consistent life‑cycle cost analysis and a performance‑based selection of the most cost‑effective treatment to achieve desired service levels within budget constraints. [1] It is applied when an existing pavement has lost structural capacity because of age‑related or environmental cracking, or when it cannot satisfy current or projected traffic loads, as well as when the pavement exhibits structural deficiencies, surface deterioration, or inadequate rideability—ranging from good to fair condition for bonded treatments and moderate to severe condition for unbonded treatments—while full reconstruction is not yet required. [1] The goal of the strategy is to restore or enhance load‑carrying capacity, add structural capacity, eliminate surface distress such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, and drainage problems, and extend service life, thereby preventing further deterioration, reducing future maintenance cycles, and postponing costly major rehabilitation or reconstruction. [1] For severely distressed pavements with weak base layers that could cause reflective cracking or pavement failure, the strategy targets restoration through full‑depth reclamation, which pulverizes existing asphalt, mixes it with underlying aggregate, and builds structure within the cross‑section to improve strength, flexibility, sustainability, and drainage while minimizing disposal and virgin material use. [1] Achieving these outcomes requires reliable estimates of future traffic loading, detailed condition surveys, in‑situ subgrade testing, and representative sampling to evaluate existing structure and water‑related damage; these inputs guide selection of appropriate layer thicknesses and a stabilization mechanism (pulverization, mechanical, or chemical) that provides the required structural capacity and ensures a well‑drained pavement system. [1] By applying treatments according to prescribed maintenance cycles defined through the MECE initiative and identified via RMS surface‑type and functional class codes, the strategy keeps pavements “in cycle,” quantifies the backlog of out‑of‑cycle roadways, and prioritizes interventions such as overlays, recycling, or structural upgrades to extend pavement life. [1] Ultimately, the Rehabilitation Strategy seeks to proactively restore existing function, improve safety and mobility for the traveling public, reduce congestion, and deliver smoother, longer‑lasting roadways while avoiding expensive, time‑consuming rehabilitation or reconstruction projects and associated traffic disruptions. [1] The rehabilitation strategy for existing concrete pavements addresses the problem of deteriorated slab performance by requiring a controlled full‑depth fracturing and reseating operation that restores structural continuity while avoiding surface spalling, shattering or damage to adjacent utilities. [2] To meet this need the contractor must submit an approved fracture plan, use equipment capable of variable impact force, create at least one 1,000‑foot test section, and verify through falling weight deflectometer testing that the elastic modulus of the fractured layer falls within the range set in Table A, with a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic tire roller providing three passes for Crack‑and‑Seat or five passes for Break‑and‑Seat methods. [2] The treatment is considered successful when continuous visible cracks are achieved, aggregate interlock is maintained, and no prohibited damage occurs to utilities, bridges or subgrade. [2]

Scope and applicability

In accordance with Section 4.9 Rehabilitation, concrete or asphalt overlays become appropriate once a project reaches the design stage and a Falling Weight Deflectometer test has been performed on the existing Portland cement concrete pavement. For pavements that are in fair to excellent condition and where the subgrade is stable and drainage functional, bonded rigid concrete overlays may be used to add structural capacity and eliminate surface distress; for pavements that are moderately to significantly deteriorated, unbonded concrete or asphalt overlays can be employed as a new pavement placed on a stable platform capable of correcting deficiencies from minor structural loss to complete failure. Unbonded overlays are acceptable for all existing pavement conditions, whereas bonded overlays are generally suitable only for fair‑to‑excellent pavements. Section 5.15 Full Depth Reclamation Techniques states that full‑depth reclamation is appropriate when a project seeks to rehabilitate a distressed pavement, especially where the existing asphalt and base layers are severely deteriorated, and it can be used to upgrade existing bases and recycle in‑place materials, making it suitable for assets that require structural improvement without extensive new material import. The technique also serves as an initial step in staged construction, allowing additional structure to be added later as traffic demands increase. Rehabilitation using full‑depth reclamation is appropriate after the initial evaluation and assessment phase for roadways whose support layers need improvement rather than merely surface distress correction; the project must have a recent pavement condition survey, traffic level estimates, in‑situ subgrade testing such as FWD, DCP or LWD, adequate sampling to define material properties, and the design must consider existing layer thicknesses and drainage conditions before reclamation can proceed. Table 5.8 provides an indication of when full‑depth reclamation is a suitable rehabilitation strategy based on pavement surface distresses present. According to Section 12.7 100 % State Funded Pavement Preservation Guidelines, a rehabilitation strategy is appropriate for projects that apply structural enhancements to an existing pavement whose capacity has been reduced by age‑related or environmental cracking, or when additional thickness is needed to meet current or projected traffic loads. Both minor rehabilitation—non‑structural treatments that eliminate surface cracking—and major rehabilitation—structural upgrades that increase load‑carrying capability—are covered under this approach and are used after the pavement has been placed but before full reconstruction becomes necessary. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213)

A rehabilitation strategy that relies on bonded concrete overlays is not appropriate when the existing asphalt or composite pavement exhibits significant structural deterioration, unstable support, stripping of the asphalt, or drainage problems that have not been addressed; in those cases an unbonded overlay or another method should be considered. When the pavement is badly distressed and the subgrade has poor conditions or high moisture, rubblizing the existing concrete is often preferred because it provides a more reliable base for any subsequent overlay. If the concrete pavement is so deteriorated that patching is no longer economical, cracking/breaking and seating the slabs to create a stable uniform support may be used instead of an overlay. When the dominant problems are surface‑related distresses rather than deficient support layers, a full‑depth reclamation rehabilitation strategy may not be appropriate and an overlay or other surface treatment is generally preferred; likewise, if the existing pavement lacks sufficient thickness or suitable subgrade material to form a reclaimed layer, or if drainage deficiencies such as wet subgrade cannot be corrected before work, FDR’s practicality diminishes. Rehabilitation is intended to resurface or structurally enhance an existing pavement to extend its service life, but when a pavement has either failed or become functionally obsolete reconstruction—full removal and replacement of the structure—is required instead, making rehabilitation inappropriate in those conditions; in such cases a different approach, namely full‑depth reconstruction using new or recycled materials, is preferred. When the intended purpose of a treatment is only to restore function and extend service life without increasing capacity or strength, a Rehabilitation Strategy is not appropriate; instead a pavement preservation approach—such as preventive maintenance or minor non‑structural rehabilitation—is preferred. Rehabilitation is also not the preferred treatment when a pavement remains in good condition and still has significant remaining service life; in those cases preventive‑maintenance or other preservation strategy should be used instead. Likewise, when distress consists only of age‑related surface cracking that does not affect structural capacity, the appropriate response is classified as minor rehabilitation, which is placed within the preservation category rather than as a major structural rehab. Rehabilitation through slab fracturing and seating is not appropriate when concrete slabs are within proximity limits that could jeopardize nearby infrastructure—specifically, where culverts, cross pipes or utility sleeves lie within 10 feet horizontally and 10 feet from the bottom of the slab, where the pavement lies within 40 feet of bridge approaches, decks or tunnels, or where it is less than 3 feet from a fixed object such as a manhole or inlet; it also must be avoided if sonic vibratory equipment would cause soil liquefaction of the subgrade. In those locations the specification requires sawcutting the uncracked panels to full depth and segmenting them (5 feet longitudinally by 6 feet transversely) rather than fracturing, thereby using a different rehabilitation approach. Information needs

A Rehabilitation Strategy relies on a systematic flow of information that begins with regular collection of highway condition data entered into a computer database together with construction (site) details, traffic volumes, inventory (cost) records and maintenance histories, which feed an analysis program to evaluate alternatives and select the most cost‑effective treatment for the pavement section. For Portland cement concrete (PCC) pavements, detailed condition and site information must be obtained before selecting an overlay type; Falling Weight Deflectometer testing of the existing concrete is required to assess structural capacity, and observations of slab movement, faulting, rocking or pumping dictate whether slab stabilization or jacking is needed. Information on subdrainage problems and overall drainage functionality is required to decide if adequate subdrainage must be provided, while the existing pavement’s structural condition (fair‑to‑excellent for bonded overlays, moderately to significantly deteriorated for unbonded overlays) determines the appropriate overlay type; high average daily traffic may favor thin‑bonded JPCP overlays on local streets. When selecting a full‑depth reclamation strategy, planners must gather traffic level data to obtain a reliable estimate of future loading, conduct a recent pavement condition survey that documents distress types and severity, perform in‑situ testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer to evaluate subgrade stiffness, develop a sampling plan covering number, locations, depth and handling of material samples, determine appropriate layer thicknesses based on existing pavement composition and the structural needs dictated by anticipated traffic and environmental conditions, and identify any drainage problems or excess moisture so that effective drainage can be installed before reclamation. A Rehabilitation Strategy must also be informed by the traffic characteristics that define the treatment network, specifically average daily traffic and truck‑percentage thresholds used to identify recycling‑eligible routes, and it requires site information captured in the RMS such as pavement surface type code and functional class code, which together determine whether a segment belongs to the High Level Asphalt, Low Level Asphalt, Concrete or Unpaved networks. Districts are allowed to adjust network indicators manually when facility usage or other factors that affect how routes are maintained suggest a different treatment, reflecting stakeholder input into the strategy. For concrete slab fracture rehabilitation, detailed site and condition data must include the location of culverts, cross‑pipes, and utility sleeves, which must be at least 10 ft horizontally and 10 ft from the slab bottom; proximity to bridges, tunnels or other structures requires that no work occur within 40 ft of approaches, and fixed objects such as manholes require a minimum clearance of 3 ft. The plan must also document subgrade suitability because sonic vibratory equipment may only be used when soil liquefaction will not occur, and any existing asphalt overlay, joint seals, or damaged drainage that need removal or repair must be identified. Cost‑related constraints are reflected in the requirement to replace unsuitable subgrade and repair utility damage at no additional cost to the Department. Traffic considerations prohibit vehicular or construction traffic on the fractured lane until seating is complete. Environmental limits include temperature restrictions that forbid fracturing when pavement is frozen or air temperature is below 45 °F or above 90 °F, and require protection of underground utilities. Stakeholder input is required from utility owners and the Representative for approvals of equipment lists, test‑section results (minimum 1,000‑ft section with average elastic modulus within Table A), and any alternate action plans. [1][2]

When evaluating a rehabilitation strategy such as full‑depth reclamation, the required information must be gathered with sufficient accuracy and completeness: a reliable estimate of future traffic loading is needed before design; a recent pavement condition survey—performed according to the specified manuals—must document current distress levels and their causes; in‑situ testing (e.g., Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer, or lightweight deflectometer) is required to assess subgrade strength; and proper sampling of existing materials must follow the detailed procedures for number, size, technique, depth, and handling. Together these elements ensure that the data are both current and comprehensive enough to support a sound rehabilitation decision. [1]

Alternatives

Under a Rehabilitation Strategy the set of feasible alternatives that should be compared spans several treatment categories. In Section 4.9 REHABILITATION the process begins with FWD testing on the existing PCCP before any overlay design, and then the alternatives to compare are a bonded concrete overlay—generally suitable when the pavement is in good to fair condition, the subgrade is stable and drainage functional—and an unbonded concrete overlay, which restores structural capacity for moderately to significantly deteriorated pavements; both may be applied as rigid overlays of varying thickness (thin‑bonded JPCP 2.5 to 4 inches or bonded JPCP 4 to 8 inches) or as asphalt overlays sized by the AASHTO design method, while additional options include cracking/breaking and seating the existing concrete to provide a uniform support base, and rubblizing the pavement (Type 1 reduced to 12‑inch pieces maximum or Type 2 reduced to 8‑inch pieces maximum) when subgrade conditions are poor, with the choice depending on structural condition, drainage status, grade constraints and required overlay thickness. Section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES lists feasible alternatives that include Full Depth Reclamation (FDR) together with the other common techniques of Cold In‑Place Recycling and Hot In‑Place Recycling; when FDR is selected its performance can be compared across different stabilizing agents such as chemical binders (e.g., cement, fly ash, lime kiln dust), asphalt binder or calcium chloride. Within Section 5.15 > B. Selection of Projects the feasible alternatives for full‑depth reclamation are the various stabilization mechanisms—pulverization stabilization, mechanical stabilization, chemical stabilization, calcium chloride stabilization and emulsified asphalt stabilization—each differing in whether additives are used or what supplemental material is incorporated, influencing strength development and construction procedures, and requiring assessment of traffic levels, pavement condition, subgrade stiffness and drainage before deciding which mechanism best meets structural and durability requirements. Section 6.1 Pavement Design Analysis/Submittal – Pavement Type Definitions identifies the feasible alternatives as the various 3R treatments that enhance structural or functional capacity, such as applying a crack‑and‑seat treatment with a structural overlay, performing rubblization followed by a structural overlay, placing a structural asphalt overlay, installing a concrete overlay, partially recycling the existing pavement, or adding additional surface material to return the pavement to adequacy. Finally, Section 12.7 100% STATE FUNDED PAVEMENT PRESERVATION GUIDELINES – Definitions for Pavement Maintenance Terminology defines the feasible alternatives to evaluate as minor rehabilitation—non‑structural enhancements that eliminate age‑related surface cracking—and major rehabilitation—structural enhancements such as restoration treatments or structural overlays that increase pavement thickness and load‑carrying capacity. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213)

Both Crack‑and‑Seat and Break‑and‑Seat rehabilitation options require full‑depth fracturing followed by seating with a minimum‑weight pneumatic roller; the Crack‑and‑Seat method mandates at least three roller passes while Break‑and‑Seat requires at least five, and both must backfill to the slab bottom with Subbase No. 2A and patch the top with Flexible Base Replacement before applying a tack coat and placing an asphalt leveling course within one week of seating; pre‑fracturing work includes sawcutting slabs longer than 20 ft to about ±1 in for Break‑and‑Seat (no joint sawcuts unless directed) and traffic is prohibited after fracturing and seating until the overlay is placed, with additional constraints that equipment must not cause spalling, shattering or soil liquefaction, operations must cease if results are unsatisfactory, and utilities, bridge decks and other structures must be protected, while future maintenance requirements are not detailed in the source. [2]

Decision criteria

The primary weighting criterion for a rehabilitation strategy is the structural condition of the existing pavement, which must be confirmed by FWD testing; if the pavement is in good to fair condition and the subgrade is stable with adequate drainage, a bonded overlay is preferred because it adds capacity while allowing the original structure to share load. When the pavement is moderately to significantly deteriorated, or when support stability or drainage are deficient, unbonded overlays, rubblizing, or other major rehabilitation methods become the higher‑priority alternatives. Consequently, condition of the pavement outweighs subgrade and drainage considerations, which in turn outweigh the choice between bonded and unbonded treatments. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Analysis methods and tools

The Rehabilitation Strategy evaluates alternatives primarily through life‑cycle cost analysis (LCCA), for which the “Cost Estimating for LCCA” Excel spreadsheet is prescribed to display historical costs with a trendline based on price and quantity and shall be used only for the LCCA and not for general project estimating [1]. Initial construction costs for each alternative are derived by applying a statistical method that generates a comparable price range from historic bid data based on quantity, with outliers excluded as an estimating best practice, thereby providing consistent documented cost inputs for the LCCA [1]. Systematic collection of highway condition data underpins the pavement‑management framework, and recent pavement condition surveys—conducted according to Publication 336, Publication 343, or using procedures such as MicroPaver™—supply condition ratings and distress insight that are required before rehabilitation [1]. In‑situ testing, including falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD) testing together with dynamic cone penetrometer (DCP) and light weight deflectometer (LWD) tests, yields quantitative stiffness information for the existing pavement and subgrade, while sampling follows criteria that must be considered to obtain representative material samples [1]. For slab‑fracturing rehabilitation, quantitative evaluation is performed through FWD testing on a minimum 1,000‑foot test section, with additional measurements taken at intervals of 50 feet or less along the lane centerline, and the resulting data are back‑calculated using an approved software package such as MODULUS or EVERCALC to obtain the elastic modulus of the fractured concrete layer [2]. The 1,000‑foot test section is deemed satisfactory only when the average elastic modulus lies within the range defined in Table A, providing a basis for acceptance of the fracturing pattern and any necessary adjustments before production work proceeds [2]. The initial decision‑making step references Table 5.8 to determine when full‑depth reclamation is a suitable rehabilitation strategy based on observed surface distresses, and an analysis program is used to identify the most cost‑effective treatments [1]. Risk‑based asset management planning required by federal guidance, together with performance measures and statistical analysis, enables cost/benefit assessments and objective documentation of treatment effectiveness over the analysis period [1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (p. 67), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 11 (pp. 190–191); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–415)

The rehabilitation strategy presumes that the initial construction cost for each alternative can be derived by applying a statistical method to historic ECMS item prices, generating a price‑quantity trendline in the Cost Estimating for LCCA spreadsheet and excluding outliers as an estimating best practice; it further assumes that past bid data are representative of future costs. The tool is not intended to incorporate current market values, so uncertainty arises when recent or sufficient historical entries are lacking, requiring expansion to surrounding districts or similar items, and location adjustments may affect unit prices. For overlay designs the approach assumes that a falling weight deflectometer (FWD) test will be performed on the existing PCC pavement before design; selection between bonded and unbonded overlays rests on the assumption that the structural condition of the existing pavement, subgrade stability, and drainage functionality are known. Bonded overlays presuppose relatively good pavement condition with a stable subgrade and functional drainage, whereas unbonded overlays assume only a stable platform regardless of pavement quality. The presence of slab movement or faulting introduces uncertainty that must be addressed by slab stabilization or jacking before overlay placement. For full‑depth reclamation the strategy assumes that a reliable estimate of future traffic loading can be obtained because design must follow standard pavement design traffic analysis procedures, and it also assumes that recent pavement condition and distress surveys provide accurate information about existing problems. The accuracy of those surveys is an uncertainty source, as is the representativeness of in‑situ testing and collected samples used to define subgrade stiffness and material properties that drive layer‑thickness decisions; uncertainties about existing drainage conditions must be resolved before reclamation can proceed. Selection and proportion of stabilization agents rely on laboratory testing, so any variation in test results creates further uncertainty in achieving the desired strength without brittleness. The treatment cycles defined by the MECE initiative—derived from the PennDOT 1988‑92 Cycle Task Force recommendations, the current manual, and FHWA‑SA‑96‑027—and the RMS‑generated surface type and functional class codes are assumed to accurately reflect pavement deterioration behavior and correctly place each segment in its appropriate network; it is also assumed that applying maintenance according to these cycles will extend pavement life and postpone major rehabilitation. Uncertainty arises because districts may manually change a segment’s network indicator between Low and High Level Asphalt based on perceived usage, the Recycling Network is not yet defined in the RMS, and any lag in updating indicators when the RMS surface type or functional class changes introduces variability into predicted outcomes. The slab‑fracturing rehabilitation assumes that the selected equipment can deliver a controllable variable force without causing surface spalling, shattering or excessive hole punching, and that sonic vibratory equipment will not induce soil liquefaction; if subgrade instability is suspected it must be replaced at no extra cost. Effectiveness of the fracture pattern remains uncertain until a minimum 1,000‑foot test section is evaluated under FWD testing, which itself assumes ambient temperatures between 45 °F and 90 °F, calibrated equipment, and that the average elastic modulus falls within the range specified in Table A; deviations require adjustment of breaking energy and retesting. Seating performance assumes a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic tire roller with tires inflated to at least 100 psi and applied for at least three passes (Crack and Seat) or five passes (Break and Seat), and uncertainty remains if wet conditions prevent proper seating or if vertical deflection is not visually observed. Stakeholders and constraints

The party responsible for the rehabilitation must submit a pavement fracturing plan and equipment list as input, and any alternate action plans or test‑section results must be sent to the Representative; the Representative reviews those submissions and provides the decision or approval required before construction can proceed or resume. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–415)

The selection of a rehabilitation strategy must first satisfy the design requirements set out in the PCCP Guidelines for concrete pavements (section 4.9 Rehabilitation) and conform to Publication 242, Pavement Policy Manual, with Board of Directors approval through the PennDOT Oversight process; Federal‑aid projects require FHWA approval unless waived, and any deviation must be submitted as a waiver to BODD for review and approval. Designers must be certified PME/PMs who have completed the mandated DARWin design portfolio, and even projects involving less than 30,000 square yards of mainline pavement surface work must provide documented justification for the chosen pavement type. Before any overlay can be designed, a Falling Weight Deflectometer test must be performed on the existing concrete pavement; surface preparation must include full‑depth patching when approved, slab stabilization or jacking if movement or faulting exists, and adequate subdrainage where subsurface drainage problems are present. Bonded concrete overlays may only be used when the underlying pavement is in good to fair condition, the subgrade is stable and existing drainage is functional, whereas unbonded overlays are required for moderately to severely deteriorated pavements and must incorporate at least a 1‑inch fine‑graded asphalt separation layer or an equivalent geotextile interlayer as specified in Publication 408 Section 548. The allowable thickness of thin‑bonded JPCP overlays is limited to 2.5–4 inches (Publication 408 Section 541) and that of thicker bonded overlays to 4–8 inches (Section 540); overlay design depth for asphalt must follow the AASHTO Design Method in Chapter 10, and the choice between Type 1 or Type 2 rubblizing is left to the District Pavement Engineer and governed by Publication 408 Section 526. When a full‑depth reclamation approach is considered, the Department’s standard specification in Publication 408 Section 344 governs the required procedures and material limits; the reclamation equipment must fully penetrate the existing asphalt layer into the underlying base or subgrade to eliminate weak‑base conditions, and any stabilizing additives such as cement, fly ash, or lime kiln dust must be applied in accordance with that standard. Future traffic loading must be reliably estimated and the overall pavement design must be consistent with standard pavement design traffic analysis procedures; a pavement condition survey must follow PennDOT Publication 336 or Publication 343 (or an equivalent distress evaluation), and in‑situ testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer, or lightweight deflectometer is required before rehabilitation. All rehabilitation treatments must conform to the Maintenance Efficiency and Cost Effectiveness initiative, use the cycle charts derived from the PennDOT 1988‑92 Cycle Task Force recommendations, FHWA‑SA‑96‑027, and fit within the RMS‑defined treatment type networks assigned by surface‑type code and Functional Class Code—Concrete (C), Unpaved (U), High Level Asphalt (H) or Low Level Asphalt (L); districts may only switch a route between H and L, never to or from C or U. Under the 100 % State Funded Pavement Preservation Guidelines, rehabilitation strategies must be financed entirely by the state; routine maintenance activities are not normally eligible for Federal‑aid funding, which limits the use of lower‑level treatments that rely on such aid, and the definitions distinguish minor rehabilitation (non‑structural preservation) from major rehabilitation (structural enhancements that extend service life or improve load‑carrying capability). The slab‑fracturing rehabilitation strategy must first obtain permit approval by submitting a pavement fracturing plan and list of proposed equipment for review before construction begins, and the selected equipment must be capable of variable‑force impact without causing extensive surface spalling or shattering. Seating of the fractured concrete must be performed with a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic tire roller whose tires must be spaced to carry equal loads and must be capable of a minimum inflation pressure of 100 psi, and such rollers may only cross bridges as approved by the District Bridge Engineer. Design requirements prohibit fracturing within 10 feet horizontally and 10 feet vertically of culverts, cross pipes, or utility sleeves and within 40 feet of structures, mandate sawcutting uncracked panels around those zones to specified dimensions, and require removal of overlying asphalt and joint seals before fracturing. Post‑fracture verification must follow AASHTO T256 and ASTM D4694, D4695, and D5858 using calibrated falling weight deflectometer equipment on a test section at least 1,000 feet long, and Falling Weight Deflectometer testing should not be performed when the pavement surface is frozen or when air temperature falls below 45°F or exceeds 90°F. Timing and implementation

The decision to adopt a rehabilitation strategy for full‑depth reclamation projects should be made only after the initial evaluation and assessment of the existing roadway has been completed, which must gather traffic level estimates, a recent pavement condition/distress survey, in‑situ strength testing (e.g., FWD, DCP, LWD) and appropriate material sampling, and then determine suitable layer thicknesses and drainage conditions before evaluating applicability. More generally, the decision on which rehabilitation strategy to employ should be made during the pavement‑management planning process, when the system’s analysis identifies the optimum time for treatment; at that point, after regular condition data have been collected and entered into the Pavement Asset Management System, project‑level analysis determines the best treatment for a specific section so that it meets desired service levels at the least total cost over the analysis period. The decision should also be made during the project‑type selection phase, after both the maintenance cycles and the reconstruction cycles have been evaluated; using the defined cycle charts to assess whether a pavement is “out of cycle” enables planners to determine if a minor treatment or a major rehabilitation is appropriate. For slab fracturing of existing concrete pavements, the decision on the rehabilitation strategy must be made by submitting a pavement fracturing plan and the associated equipment list for approval before any construction begins; only after this pre‑construction approval can production fracturing start. Outcomes and follow-up

Success for a rehabilitation strategy is achieved when the rehabilitated pavement meets the required structural and functional capacity criteria, thereby extending its service life and returning it to a condition of structural or functional adequacy; this outcome is documented by confirming that the applied resurfacing, restoration or other structural enhancements satisfy those criteria [1]. For overlay‑based treatments success is realized when all pre‑overlay investigations and preparations are completed, joint cores verify a good‑to‑fair pavement condition, and the selected overlay type fulfills its intended performance. Bonded overlays must be placed to add structural capacity, while unbonded overlays must restore structural capacity on moderately to severely deteriorated pavements by providing full‑depth patching, slab stabilization, adequate subdrainage and, when needed, a minimum 1‑inch asphalt separation layer or geotextile interlayer; success is measured by confirming that falling weight deflectometer (FWD) testing is required and performed on the existing PCCP prior to design, that any necessary slab stabilization, drainage improvements and full‑depth patching have been executed, and that overlay thickness conforms to specifications—thin‑bonded JPCP overlays of 2.5 to 4 inches for surface correction or 4 to 8 inches for structural addition [1]. When rubblizing is employed, success requires that the resulting concrete pieces do not exceed the maximum size stipulated—12 inches for Type 1 and 8 inches for Type 2—before overlay placement [1]. For full‑depth reclamation rehabilitation success is achieved when the project’s design and construction address the identified distress, provide an appropriate reclaimed layer thickness, and ensure proper drainage so that the rebuilt pavement can support anticipated traffic loads; this outcome is documented through a post‑survey summary of distress severity and verified by pre‑and post‑rehabilitation FWD tests (and other subgrade tests as needed) that confirm subgrade stiffness before work and required new‑pavement stiffness after construction, together with confirmation that the selected layer thickness and drainage corrections meet structural requirements [1]. From a Pavement Management System perspective success is realized when the selected treatment provides the intended service level at the least total cost over the analysis period and meets predefined performance measures; it is measured by tracking life‑cycle performance of the treatment, comparing forecasted versus observed pavement condition, documenting the impact of funding decisions through statistical analysis and regular reporting, and maintaining a continuous collection of condition data linked to traffic and maintenance information in an analysis program that quantifies cost‑effectiveness and verifies compliance with established thresholds [1]. Maintaining roadways according to prescribed treatment cycles also constitutes success because it extends pavement life and lengthens the interval before major rehabilitation or reconstruction; effectiveness is measured by monitoring compliance with each network’s cycle schedule and by quantifying the backlog of roadways that are out of cycle, where a reduction in that backlog signals that the strategy is effective [1]. Under the 100 % State‑Funded Pavement Preservation guidelines success is achieved when the preservation treatment restores the function of the existing pavement and extends its service life while delivering broader program goals such as improved safety and mobility, reduced congestion, smoother longer‑lasting pavements, and demonstrable cost‑effectiveness; evidence of success includes documented preservation of investment on NHS and other Federal‑aid roadways, enhanced pavement performance, reduced user delays, and measurable improvements in overall safety and mobility [1]. Economic success is further indicated when structural enhancements either restore existing capacity by eliminating age‑related cracking or add thickness so the section can meet current and projected traffic loads, as measured by confirming an increase in load‑carrying capability or a documented life extension compared with pre‑treatment conditions and by demonstrating that the discounted cost of the rehabilitation series remains less than the discounted cost of full reconstruction, which is generally more economical [1]. For slab fracturing and seating treatments success is defined by meeting both visual and quantitative performance criteria after the operation; the fractured concrete must display continuous full‑depth cracks that are visible on the surface, and the subsequent seating operation must leave no observable vertical deflection of the substrate, indicating that the pieces are fully seated. Mechanical verification is provided by falling weight deflectometer testing on a 1,000‑foot test section and on selected lots, where the average elastic modulus of the fractured layer must fall within the range specified in Table A for the treatment to be accepted; compliance is documented through visual inspection reports, roller pass records (minimum three passes for Crack‑and‑Seat or at least five passes for Break‑and‑Seat), and FWD data collected at intervals of 50 feet or less using a drop weight of 9,000 pounds [2].

Monitoring of rehabilitation strategies begins with a pre‑construction investigation that includes a pavement condition and distress survey together with in‑situ strength testing such as falling weight deflectometer (FWD) measurements, and may also incorporate DCP or LWD tests and sampling of reclaimed materials; the post‑construction phase repeats the same suite of investigations to capture changes in stiffness and surface condition. The distress survey must be followed by a summary report that documents the level of distress and observations, recording the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems. FWD testing before rehabilitation provides information on the existing pavement’s stiffness, while testing after construction determines the stiffness of the newly placed or treated layer. For slab‑fracturing rehabilitations a minimum 1,000‑foot‑long test section is established and FWD measurements are taken at intervals of 50 feet or less with a 9,000‑pound drop weight; the elastic modulus is back‑calculated according to AASHTO T256, ASTM D4694, D4695, and D5858 and compared with the range in Table A, and if the average value falls outside that range the breaking pattern is adjusted, a new test section created, and testing repeated. During production at least three 1,000‑foot lots per lane mile are selected for FWD verification, and all test data, equipment lists, and fracture plans are submitted to the Representative with final results delivered within seven calendar days before any further work proceeds. Highway condition data collected regularly are entered into a pavement management database that stores condition, construction, traffic, inventory and maintenance records, creating an objective documentation of actions taken and pavement response. The stored information is processed by an analysis program that uses performance measures to conduct statistical analyses, cost‑benefit forecasts and life‑cycle evaluations, allowing agencies to refine criteria, compare alternatives and improve future rehabilitation decisions. Actual treatment actions are compared with the pavement cycle charts defined in the MECE initiative using RMS data to identify any out‑of‑cycle sections; the treatment type networks displayed on the RMS Network Identification Screen are recorded and districts may manually edit network indicators (for example from “L” to “H”) based on facility usage or other factors. The documented cycle status and any manual edits are fed back into decision making because both maintenance cycles and reconstruction cycles must be evaluated when selecting the project type, ensuring that observed performance influences later rehabilitation choices. The documented outcomes of the slab‑fracturing tests are used to modify fracturing patterns, equipment selection, or to re‑fracture sections, thereby informing subsequent rehabilitation decisions.
