Gradation

Part of: Mix Design is-subtopic-of
Gradation is a topic under Mix Design.
gradationgradations
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-29

Gradation is the particle‑size distribution of the aggregate or soil component in pavement layers, quantified by sieve analysis that records the percent passing (or retained) for a series of standard sieves [1][2]. By shaping the proportion of coarse, medium and fine fractions it governs key engineering attributes such as permeability, void structure, compaction density, California Bearing Ratio and ultimately the strength, durability and overall performance of both flexible and rigid pavements [1][2]. The distribution is expressed through cumulative percent‑retained or passing charts (e.g., PTM 529 Combined Gradation Percent Retained Chart) and characterized by parameters such as D10, D30, D60, the coefficient of uniformity (Cu) and curvature (Cc), which must satisfy Unified Soil Classification System limits for a well‑graded material [1][2].

Material purpose and applicability

Gradation is the particle‑size distribution of the aggregates or soils that constitute the various layers of a pavement structure and is obtained through gradation tests that quantify the percent passing for each size fraction [1][2]. In base and subbase layers the distribution serves as an indicator of how permeable the material will be, allowing engineers to assess drainage potential, although particle shape also influences flow paths so gradation alone does not guarantee the desired drainage [1]. For the subgrade, the shape of the gradation curve reveals how the soil will behave during compaction and under traffic loading; a well‑graded mix containing an even range of large, medium and small particles achieves higher dry unit weights and correspondingly higher California Bearing Ratio values, whereas poorly or gap‑graded soils retain more voids and provide lower strength [1]. The same gradation information is required for proper soil classification and for determining the engineering properties that feed into CBR testing [1]. In rigid concrete slabs the aggregate gradation is expressed as a cumulative percent‑retained chart obtained from sieve analysis of plant‑stockpiled coarse and fine aggregates, and the chart must fall inside the limits set out in PTM No. 529 with any deviation limited to less than 5 % of the required percentages [2]. In asphalt mixtures the aggregate fractions are sized, uniformly graded, and combined according to Table A or Superpave material‑gradation‑volumetric requirements, producing a blend that passes 100 % of a 50 mm sieve, retains between 15 % and 60 % on a 4.75 mm sieve, and includes up to 30 % passing a 150 µm sieve [2]. By maintaining these gradation limits the aggregate skeleton is tightly packed, creates the intended void structure, allows uniform coating by binder without excess runoff, and thereby provides the strength, durability, and overall performance demanded of the pavement system [1][2]. In dense‑graded asphalt mixtures the wide range of aggregate sizes yields a tightly packed skeleton with minimal void space, enabling the crumb‑rubber modified binder—having smaller particle size and lower viscosity—to fill the limited voids and impart high strength and durability to the surface [1].

Composition and characteristics

Gradation refers to the particle‑size distribution of the aggregate component in pavement materials, whether the mix is an asphalt concrete, a cement concrete, or a granular base layer. [1][2] The constituents that contribute to the gradation include coarse aggregates, medium‑sized particles, and fine fractions that may pass a #200 (75 µm) sieve, together with any mineral filler, reclaimed asphalt pavement, crumb‑rubber, cement dust, fly ash, stone fines or other approved fillers incorporated into the mix. [1][2] In modified binders the crumb‑rubber portion is typically limited to particles that are #30 mesh or smaller. [1] The composition of a gradation is expressed as the cumulative percent retained or percent passing for each sieve in a standard series, most commonly 50 mm (2‑inch), 4.75 mm (No. 4), 2.36 mm (No. 8), 150 µm (No. 100) and 75 µm (No. 200). [2] From the sieve results the characteristic diameters D10, D30 and D60 are derived, allowing calculation of the coefficient of uniformity (Cu) and the coefficient of curvature (Cc), which are used to assess whether a material meets the Unified Soil Classification System criteria for a well‑graded soil. [1] For gravels a Cu of at least 4 and for sands a Cu of at least 6, together with a Cc between 1 and 3, satisfy those USCS requirements. [1] In ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses three gradation types—A, B and C—are prescribed, distinguished by nominal maximum aggregate sizes of 6.3 mm, 9.5 mm and 12.5 mm respectively, thereby controlling the internal structure of the hot‑mix layer. [1] The PTM specifications require that 100 % of the coarse material pass the 50 mm sieve, that roughly 15 % to 60 % pass the 4.75 mm sieve, and that no more than about 30 % pass the 150 µm sieve, with additional limits defined for the 2.36 mm and 75 µm sieves. [2] Deviations greater than five percent on any single sieve are generally prohibited unless a written approval is obtained; occasional allowances of up to five percent may be granted under special circumstances. [2] The fineness modulus of the fine‑aggregate portion must remain within the limits set by the applicable table, and the variation among at least nine of ten consecutive samples from a single source may not exceed ±0.20 around their average value. [2] A dense‑graded mixture contains a broad spectrum of particle sizes that pack tightly, producing minimal void space, higher dry densities, greater CBR values and improved resistance to fatigue. [1] Conversely, gap‑graded or poorly graded blends lack one or more size fractions, creating larger inter‑particle voids that increase permeability, reduce capillary rise and generally diminish strength. [1] The physical characteristics of gradation—permeability, void content, compaction behavior and overall performance—are therefore governed by the proportionate blend of particle‑size fractions that yields a uniformly graded skeleton capable of being fully coated with binder without observable runoff. [1][2] During production, samples are taken from the finished hot‑mix or from the combined aggregate stream and tested to verify that the measured distribution remains within the prescribed limits; daily monitoring of plant‑stockpiled aggregates before production and whenever bins are restocked ensures compliance with the cumulative percent retained chart defined by PTM 529 or the Combined Gradation Percent Retained Chart. [2] When these constituent, compositional and structural requirements are satisfied, the resulting pavement mix exhibits the desired volumetric properties, proper coating of aggregates by binder, and the strength and durability needed for long‑term performance. [1][2]

Gradation is the particle‑size distribution of the aggregate blend and directly controls strength, durability, and overall pavement performance [2]. Changing composition, source, processing, or proportioning reshapes the gradation curve, which in turn influences layer thickness, sealing ability, resistance to environmental degradation, structural capacity, compaction density and strength indicators such as CBR [1][2]. In ultra‑thin wearing courses three predefined gradations—Type A, B, and C—are distinguished by nominal maximum aggregate sizes of 6.3 mm, 9.5 mm and 12.5 mm; selecting a larger size leads to a thicker surface because design thickness is set at roughly one to one‑and‑a‑half times the coarsest particle diameter [1]. The increased thickness together with a finer overall distribution improves the membrane’s sealing of the pavement, reducing oxidation and weathering and thereby enhancing resistance to fatigue cracking, low‑temperature cracking, raveling and moderate rutting [1]. When reclaimed pavement is reused, gradation can be altered either by simple pulverization that uses only the in‑place material or by mechanical stabilization that adds imported granular aggregate or RAP; the former mirrors the original particle distribution and limits attainable strength, whereas the latter adjusts the coarse‑to‑fine ratio to achieve a more desirable gradation, increasing structural integrity and layer volume without reducing thickness [1]. Processing equipment such as reclamation machines must pulverize material so that no particle exceeds two inches; operators can vary cutting‑drum speed independently of forward travel to limit oversized particles, achieving 95 % passing the two‑inch sieve and fine‑tuning the size spread [2]. In dense‑graded asphalt mixes, incorporating crumb rubber with lower content and finer particles (passing #30 mesh) produces a binder of reduced viscosity that can infiltrate limited voids among a wide range of aggregate sizes, allowing tighter packing while preserving strength comparable to polymer‑modified binders [1]. For granular soils and unbound aggregates, well‑graded materials—those meeting Cu ≥ 4 for gravels or Cu ≥ 6 for sands with Cc between 1 and 3—compact to higher dry unit weights and yield higher California Bearing Ratio values than poorly graded or gap‑graded mixes that over‑represent or lack certain size fractions [1]. Variation in the source of stockpiled aggregate can shift the fineness modulus; nine out of ten consecutive samples must remain within ±0.20 of the average, and any blend of fine aggregates requires written approval before use [2]. Daily sieve analysis of plant stockpiles is required before production and whenever bins are restocked; percent‑retained plots must lie within limits defined by PTM No. 529, generally allowing less than five percent deviation, and if the curve drifts beyond this allowance aggregate proportions must be adjusted during mixing [2]. Proportioning rules constrain gradation control: blends of different aggregates are limited to equal‑mass (50 % by weight) portions unless an approved blending plan exists; water addition may not vary more than two percentage points from the optimum moisture, and at least ninety percent of test samples must meet Table A tolerances; failure triggers review of the job‑mix formula and possibly a new mix design [2]. Adding mineral filler, RAP, reclaimed asphalt shingles, cement dust, fly ash or other fines changes the cumulative percent‑retained curve; compliance is demonstrated by at least three independent test results, and if three or more samples exceed single‑sample tolerances production is halted until corrective action restores gradation [2]. Specific compositional controls such as blending twenty percent limestone fines with coarse stone of poor cementing quality, limiting coarse aggregate to no more than ten percent finer than the No. 200 sieve, and restricting granulated blast‑furnace slag to no more than twenty percent passing the No. 100 sieve keep the mix uniformly sized and prevent excess fines that would reduce strength [2]. Reclaimed asphalt pavement must be crushed, screened and graded free of deleterious debris; its gradation is sampled on the first day of production and at least once per 1,000 tons, with clumps or oversized particles retained on a 12.5 mm sieve requiring reprocessing before incorporation [2]. Overall, by monitoring composition, source variability, processing settings and proportioning ratios through frequent sampling, numerical tolerances for fineness modulus, percent‑retained deviation limits, water‑addition bounds, blend proportions and equipment controls, engineers can keep the gradation within design limits and thereby ensure that pavement retains its intended strength, durability and performance [1][2].

Key material properties

The performance of a pavement gradation is governed by a tightly defined set of physical attributes that describe the particle‑size distribution, void structure, and moisture condition of the aggregate skeleton [1][2]. Across the guides the gradation curve is quantified by the proportion of material passing specified sieves, with at least ninety‑five percent required to pass a two‑inch sieve, no more than ten percent finer than a No. 200 (75 µm) sieve, and the percentages passing the 4.75 mm (No. 4), 2.36 mm (No. 8) and 75 µm sieves each constrained to prescribed limits [2]. The fineness modulus of the fine‑aggregate fraction must remain within ±0.20 of the batch average, while the maximum individual particle size may not exceed two inches and no more than twenty percent of the material may pass a No. 100 sieve [2]. These sizing requirements together with an uncompacted void content of at least forty‑five percent and a bulk specific gravity that, when combined with calculated air voids and VMA, defines the mixture’s density envelope, ensure that the aggregate skeleton can achieve the targeted dry rodded density, which is capped at ninety pounds per cubic foot [2]. High permeability is emphasized because large inter‑particle voids allow gravity‑drainable water to be removed rapidly and suppress capillary rise, although a modest reduction in drainability may be tolerated to provide a more stable work platform during construction [1][2]. Moisture control is also critical; the apparent moisture content of the gradation shall not exceed 0.5 % and uniformity of moisture must be maintained throughout batching to preserve the intended density and strength characteristics [2][1]. Mechanically, a well‑graded aggregate blend that includes an even mix of large, medium and small particles attains a higher maximum dry density and a lower optimum moisture content, which in turn enhances the resilient modulus and California bearing ratio of the layer [1][2]. The same gradation quality contributes to the ability of thin bonded wearing courses to resist fatigue cracking and low‑temperature distress by providing a dense, interlocked skeleton that limits deformation under repeated loading [1]. Durability requirements are expressed through tests such as the AASHTO T‑71 strength ratio for aggregates whose organic impurity level exceeds the specified color plate, and abrasion loss limits prescribed in Bulletin 27, both of which must be satisfied to ensure that the aggregate particles retain their shape under traffic loading [2]. The proportion of thin or elongated pieces is limited to maximum percentages, while an adequate fraction of crushed fragments is required to promote interlock and maintain tensile strength ratios in moisture‑sensitivity testing after compaction [2]. Proper compaction to meet the density acceptance limits preserves the intended gradation after placement and secures a satisfactory tensile strength ratio in moisture‑sensitivity tests, further supporting the structural performance of the pavement [2][1]. Chemically, the selected gradation can act as a seal that limits oxidation and weathering of the surface, thereby preserving resistance to chemical degradation over the service life [1]. The Atterberg limits—liquid limit, plastic limit and plasticity index—characterize the susceptibility of fine‑aggregate soils to strength loss and compressibility when moisture conditions vary, influencing the overall stability of the gradation [1]. A sand equivalent value of at least forty‑five percent is required to screen out deleterious clay‑like material, while a sodium‑sulfate soundness loss not exceeding fifteen percent after five immersion–drying cycles (twenty percent may be permitted for fine aggregates) guards against disintegration under freeze‑thaw conditions [2]. Washing loss is evaluated according to PTM 100, and the aggregate must be free of vegetable or organic matter, excessive clay, and no more than ten percent deleterious shale by mass [2]. When cementing ability is insufficient, up to twenty percent limestone fines may be blended, but the plasticity index of any mineral filler used is limited to four, and if supplementary materials such as fly ash are incorporated their loss on ignition shall not exceed sixteen percent [2]. For backfill applications that contact metal, the pH of the material is required to lie between five and nine‑point‑five, electrical resistivity must exceed two thousand ohm‑centimetres after sixty days, and chloride, sulfate and sulfide concentrations are kept low to avoid corrosion [2]. Thermal considerations focus on maintaining the asphalt production temperature within the range prescribed for normal paving so that binder coating is achieved without compromising gradation stability [2][1]. Warm‑mix additives must pass additional moisture‑susceptibility testing to ensure they do not impair water resistance of the mixture [2]. Aggregates are stored at temperatures not below forty degrees Fahrenheit before and during batching to prevent frost damage, and the mixture is delivered and placed within the specified laying temperature range for its class to avoid thermal cracking [2][1]. Although explicit thermal parameters are not measured directly for gradation, the sealing effect of a properly graded aggregate reduces water ingress and freeze‑thaw cycling, thereby mitigating low‑temperature cracking in service [1]. The nominal maximum aggregate size selected for the gradation—six point three millimetres, nine point five millimetres or twelve point five millimetres for Types A, B and C respectively—and a layer thickness set at roughly one to one‑and‑a‑half times that size are prescribed to ensure adequate structural capacity while preserving the desired physical and mechanical behavior [1].

Gradation is measured by obtaining representative samples in accordance with standard quality‑control plans, typically on the first day of production and thereafter at least once for every 1,000 tons produced or as otherwise required, with samples taken from the finished hot‑mix asphalt, the combined aggregate stream in the plant bins or belt, or directly from stockpiled aggregate before each concrete shift. [2] The collected material is subjected to a dry‑sieve analysis using prescribed test methods, and percent passing is recorded for a series of standard sieves that commonly include No. 4 (4.75 mm), No. 8 (2.36 mm), No. 200 (75 µm) and the No. 100 sieve, while additional finer sieves such as #10, #30 and #60 may be employed to capture the fine‑aggregate fraction. [1][2] From the cumulative percent‑passing data the particle‑size distribution curve is plotted either as a cumulative percent‑retained chart compared with the Combined Gradation Percent Retained Chart or against the FHWA 0.45 power‑type curve incorporated into the job‑mix formula. [2] The analysis also yields characteristic sieve sizes D10, D30 and D60, which are used to compute the coefficient of uniformity (Cu = D60/D10) and the coefficient of curvature (Cc = D30²/(D10·D60)). [1] These Cu and Cc values are then compared with limits defined by the Unified Soil Classification System, whereby a gravel aggregate is considered well‑graded when Cu ≥ 4 and 1 ≤ Cc ≤ 3, and a sand aggregate when Cu ≥ 6 with the same curvature range; values outside these ranges denote a poorly graded material. [1] In pavement specifications the resulting gradation curve also determines the mix type, with dense‑graded mixes described by a wide size distribution that leaves very little void space, and gap‑graded mixes characterized by coarser particles that create larger inter‑particle voids. [1] Specialized mixtures such as crumb‑rubber modified binders are required to use a finer gradation in which the rubber material passes a #30 mesh sieve or smaller, whereas asphalt‑rubber gap‑graded blends retain coarser aggregates. [1] Ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses are classified into three predefined gradations—Type A, Type B and Type C—based solely on the nominal maximum aggregate size, with limits of 6.3 mm for Type A, 9.5 mm for Type B, and 12.5 mm for Type C. [1] Agencies such as PennDOT treat the particle‑size distribution obtained from the sieve test as an indirect indicator of permeability because direct field measurement is difficult, although they acknowledge that mixes with identical gradations may exhibit different permeabilities due to particle shape influencing void structure. [1] Characterization of the measured gradation follows tolerance limits set out in specification tables; fine‑aggregate quality is judged by a fineness modulus that must vary less than ±0.20 around its average for at least nine of ten consecutive samples, while coarse‑aggregate quality is evaluated against separate tables, and the overall mix must meet a target curve defined in the job‑mix formula. [2] Compliance is first verified after a minimum of three test results, and when five or more results are available the running average of the last five consecutive tests must remain within the multiple‑sample tolerances; daily production is considered acceptable when at least ninety percent of the samples fall inside these limits, with isolated sieve deviations of less than five percent sometimes permitted on a case‑by‑case basis. [2] Classification uses predefined aggregate type designations; coarse aggregates are assigned to Type A, B or C per AASHTO 10 provided that no more than ten percent pass the No. 200 sieve, while fine aggregates share the same type categories but must also satisfy a maximum twenty‑percent loss in the soundness test. [2] Additional grading criteria specified by PTM No. 616 require that an acceptable granular material have 100 % passing the 50‑mm (2‑inch) sieve, between 15 % and 60 % passing the 4.75‑mm sieve, and up to 30 % passing the 150‑µm (No. 100) sieve; mineral filler must conform to AASHTO M‑17 limits and be documented with a hydrometer analysis per AASHTO T‑88. [2] Beyond size distribution, mix designs record combined aggregate consensus properties such as sand equivalency, fine‑aggregate angularity, coarse‑aggregate angularity, and the proportion of flat or elongated particles, which further refine the gradation classification, and pattern segregation is identified when the summed absolute deviation between any two sieves reaches twenty percent or more relative to the job‑mix formula. [2]

Behavior and mechanisms

Gradation influences how the subgrade responds to loading by affecting compaction density; well‑graded soils with a smooth particle‑size distribution compact more densely and achieve higher dry unit weights and CBR values, whereas poorly or gap‑graded soils compact less efficiently. Moisture sensitivity is tied to the fines content revealed by the #200 sieve: higher fines make the soil more moisture sensitive, harder to compact, lower its CBR, and increase susceptibility to frost heave. The source does not provide specific information on how gradation itself changes with temperature fluctuations, chemical exposure, or other service conditions. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143)

Gradation behavior is governed by the combined action of physical packing, chemical interactions, and mechanical processes that together shape the aggregate skeleton and its performance [1][2]. Physically, the particle‑size distribution is first quantified through routine sieve analysis of stockpiled material before each production shift and whenever bins are restocked, producing a cumulative percent retained chart that must fall within the limits defined by the Combined Gradation Percent Retained Chart (PTM No. 529) [2]. Crushing, screening, and grading operations—especially for reclaimed asphalt pavement—physically separate particles, with clumped or oversized pieces removed on the 12.5 mm (½‑inch) sieve and any material exceeding a maximum dimension of two inches reduced by adjusting the cutting drum speed of a rotary reclaimer independent of forward travel [2]. A wide range of sizes in a dense‑graded mix creates a compact skeleton with minimal inter‑particle voids, while well‑graded soils that display a smooth gradation curve achieve higher dry unit weights and CBR values than poorly or gap‑graded materials [1]. The proportion of fine particles passing the #200 sieve directly influences moisture sensitivity and compressibility; high fines content, particularly silt and clay sized fractions, makes the material more difficult to compact and more susceptible to strength loss under moisture variations [1][2]. Chemically, the presence of fines introduces plasticity that is governed by Atterberg limits, where increasing liquid limit and plasticity index raise compressibility and reduce strength, especially when moisture changes occur [1]. The addition of anti‑strip additives or hydrated lime modifies the binder–aggregate interface, providing at least 90 % coating as verified by ASTM D3625 testing and thereby reducing moisture susceptibility while preserving the intended gradation [2]. Other chemical stabilizers such as asphalt binders, Portland cement, calcium chloride, or crumb‑rubber modifiers introduced after sizing enhance particle bonding; a crumb‑rubber modifier used at a fine gradation of 30 mesh or smaller lowers binder viscosity and permits the binder to flow into the tightly packed aggregate framework, improving coating and load transfer [1][2]. Mechanically, gradation can be altered by pulverizing existing pavement, reshaping, and recompacting reclaimed material while blending supplemental aggregate or RAP, a process that directly changes the particle‑size distribution of the base layer [1]. During production, the mix crew may adjust aggregate proportions to bring any deviation back within specification limits, and mechanical actions such as varying drum rpm, re‑processing seal‑coat aggregates, and adding cellulose fibers according to prescribed tolerances provide structural support that influences stiffness and void stability [2][1]. Compaction practices—including selection of roller speed, amplitude, frequency, pattern, and size—are tuned to prevent displacement, shoving, cracking, or aggregate breakage that would otherwise modify the gradation, thereby preserving the designed void structure [2]. Maintaining uniform moisture through water sprays and keeping aggregates above a minimum temperature prevents frost‑induced changes that could alter particle arrangement, while separate stockpiles for each size and type avoid inadvertent mixing or segregation that would disturb the intended distribution [2]. Pattern segregation, a physical phenomenon of non‑uniform arrangement of coarse and fine particles in the finished mat, is monitored by testing the percent passing the 75 mm (No. 200) sieve during acceptance sampling to ensure that the aggregate skeleton remains uniform [2].

Durability and deterioration

Gradation durability is compromised when soils contain excessive fines because the higher moisture sensitivity makes compaction more difficult, reduces CBR values and increases susceptibility to frost heave [1]. Similarly, soils with elevated plasticity indices and liquid limits exhibit greater compressibility and elasticity, which can lead to loss of strength under increased loading or added water [1]. Gap‑graded aggregates also diminish durability since the lack of intermediate particle sizes reduces dense packing, lowers dry unit weight and CBR, and accelerates traffic‑induced deterioration [1]. During production, vibration of the mixing plant can cause aggregate particles to segregate, altering the intended particle‑size distribution; therefore segregation must be avoided while vibrating and any deviation in combined percent retained should be corrected to stay within PTM No. 529 limits with a permissible excursion of no more than five percent [2]. Continuous monitoring through sieve analysis is required to verify compliance with those gradation tolerances [2]. The physical condition of the aggregates further influences durability: excessive abrasion, limited by the maximum percentages in Bulletin 27 Chapter 2A Table 6A, reduces interlock among particles [2]. Thin or elongated pieces must not exceed the maxima set out in AASHTO M 323 Table 6 for flat and elongated particles, while a minimum proportion of crushed fragments, defined in the same table, is essential to maintain stability [2]. All processed material must be kept free of foreign substances and segregation minimized to preserve the designed gradation [2]. When seal‑coat aggregates derived from reclaimed asphalt pavement are stored, clumps or oversized particles that remain on the 12.5 mm sieve can develop; such material must be reprocessed if it has been in an uncovered stockpile for more than one month or in a covered pile for over three months [2]. Excess free moisture in seal‑coat aggregates promotes particle loss and uneven distribution, while draindown of binder—checked by a one‑hour AASHTO T 305 test—and inadequate coating of aggregate particles (the mix must achieve at least 95 % coated particles as verified by AASHTO T 195) can lead to stripping under traffic [2]. Any warm‑mix additive must be demonstrated not to impair moisture resistance, and daily draindown testing is mandated to ensure this condition [2]. Uniformity of moisture during storage and batching is critical; aggregates should be kept at temperatures no lower than 40 °F to prevent frost formation that alters particle shape and size relationships [2]. Pattern segregation—continuous zones where coarse and fine aggregates are unevenly distributed—is unacceptable when the difference in average pavement texture depth between segregated and non‑segregated areas exceeds 0.024 inches, while flushing, defined as excess asphalt coating the surface, is defective if the average texture depth falls to 0.006 inches or less [2]. Improper compaction that causes displacement, shoving, cracking, or aggregate breakage also degrades the intended gradation and reduces pavement performance [2]. Finally, any sieve‑passing percentages outside the prescribed tolerances for the 4.75 mm (No. 4), 2.36 mm (No. 8), and 75 µm (No. 200) sieves—or asphalt content beyond Table D limits—render the mixture non‑conforming and require corrective action [2].

Conversely, soils or aggregates that are poorly graded or gap‑graded, contain an excessive proportion of #200‑sieve fines, or display elevated liquid limits and plasticity indices become more moisture‑Sensitive, compact to lower densities, and lose strength when wet or frozen, thereby increasing the potential for gradation‑related deterioration such as reduced bearing capacity or frost heave. Low fines content and low Atterberg limits further mitigate susceptibility, while excessive fines, high plasticity, and irregular gradation amplify it. The presence of objectionable constituents—including vegetable or organic matter, an excess of clay, or more than ten percent deleterious shale by mass—weakens the particle framework and accelerates breakdown under traffic and environmental loading. Materials with poor cementing qualities increase susceptibility, although blending approximately twenty percent limestone fines can offset this weakness. Granulated blast‑furnace slag used in base courses must have a dry rodded density below ninety pounds per cubic foot; densities above this threshold raise the risk of gradation deterioration. Particle‑size limits also influence durability, with no individual particle exceeding two inches and not more than twenty percent passing the No. 100 sieve. Exposure conditions that promote segregation or contamination of stored aggregates with earth or foreign material disrupt the gradation and heighten deterioration risk. Uncontrolled moisture variations encourage binding or separation of particles, whereas maintaining uniform moisture through water‑spray systems helps preserve the intended distribution. Keeping aggregates at temperatures no lower than 40 °F before batching prevents frost damage, and avoiding air temperatures below 40 °F during the required cure period further reduces susceptibility. Placement or reclamation in rain, or when rain is anticipated within two hours of completion, accelerates breakdown of the gradation. Selecting durable sources such as bank‑crushed gravel, stone or slag that are free from vegetable matter and limited to ten percent deleterious shale, promptly removing clumps or oversized particles, limiting storage time of RAP aggregate, adhering to the specified size limits, and using equipment capable of controlling oversize material all serve to reduce susceptibility. Maintaining each size fraction in dedicated stockpiles, controlling moisture uniformly, and avoiding low temperatures and rain during placement and curing help maintain a stable gradation over the pavement’s service life. Material interactions and compatibility

Gradation determines how the particle‑size distribution of aggregates accommodates other constituents such as cementitious binders, mineral fillers, reclaimed materials and additives. When mineral filler, RAP or RAS are added, the finished blend must be tested for its combined gradation because any shift can alter the void structure needed for proper binder coating and filler placement. In full‑depth reclamation, additional granular material or reclaimed asphalt pavement is introduced specifically to modify the gradation so that voids are more completely filled and the structural capacity of the rebuilt base is increased. The dosage of chemical stabilizers such as Portland cement, lime or fly ash interacts directly with this adjusted particle blend; increasing the stabilizer proportion generally raises strength but excessive amounts can make the mix brittle, requiring a balance between chemistry and gradation to retain durability. For concrete mixes, changes in aggregate proportions that affect gradation also change the amount of cement paste or fly ash required for full coating, so any such adjustment mandates a new mix design to preserve strength and durability. Coarse‑aggregate water absorption is another critical interaction—if AASHTO T 85 shows absorption above 2 %, the mixture must be sampled according to quality‑control procedures and its apparent moisture content limited to no more than 0.5 % to keep the gradation within tolerance while providing enough water for optimum compaction. Daily monitoring of aggregate gradations by sieve analysis of stockpiled material and at each bin replenishment ensures that the combined percent‑retained limits are met; any drift triggers an immediate rebalance of size fractions to maintain the target void space. In dense‑graded asphalt mixtures, the wide range of particle sizes creates a tightly packed skeleton with limited inter‑aggregate voids, which dictates how much binder is needed for coating and influences moisture resistance. Crumb‑rubber modified binders are produced with a fine gradation (30 mesh or smaller) and lower viscosity; their small particles can migrate into the restricted gaps of a dense aggregate skeleton, achieving full coating despite limited void space and delivering performance comparable to polymer‑modified binders. The binder demand is directly linked to the void volume left by the chosen gradation; the asphalt content must be adjusted within specified ranges so that at least 95 % of particles are coated and no observable runoff occurs. When fine‑aggregate fineness modulus deviates beyond ±0.20 for nine out of ten consecutive samples, filler such as cement dust, fly ash or stone‑crushing fines is added to raise the percent passing the No. 200 sieve, thereby restoring the intended gradation without introducing excessive clay that would increase water demand. Stone lacking inherent cementing ability can be rendered compatible with paste by blending it with about twenty percent limestone fines, which improves aggregate‑paste adhesion while respecting the overall gradation limits. Granulated blast‑furnace slag is acceptable only when it conforms to a No. 10 gradation, stays below a dry rodded density of ninety pounds per cubic foot, has a maximum size of two inches and no more than twenty percent passing the No. 100 sieve, ensuring a uniform particle pack that does not impair aggregate‑paste bonding. In warm‑mix technologies, the uniformly graded blend of aggregates, asphalt and any approved additive must satisfy both material and volumetric requirements without compromising moisture resistance; the gradation therefore governs how much additive can be incorporated while maintaining coating integrity. For seal‑coat applications, any change in aggregate source or size distribution necessitates a new design because it alters the penetration and adhesion of emulsified asphalt; aggregates must be dry or only slightly damp to allow effective binder infiltration. Fine aggregate must be screened for organic impurities, with testing prescribed by AASHTO T 21 and a color‑value comparison to Organic Plate No. 3; if the plate is exceeded, a minimum strength ratio determined per AASHTO T 71 is required. Mineral fillers such as cement dust, fly ash, or crushed stone fines are required to be reasonably free of clay and entirely free of organic contaminants.

Sulfate exposure is addressed through two complementary limits. The sodium‑sulfate soundness test (PTM No. 510) mandates that loss after five immersion‑drying cycles not exceed 15 %, while the flowable backfill specification caps residual sulfates in cured material at less than 1,000 ppm. Contaminant control extends to recycled materials; crumb‑rubber incorporated into the gradation must contain no more than 2.5 % total contaminants, with individual caps of 1.0 % for iron, 1.0 % for fiber, and 0.5 % for other foreign matter. This restriction guards against impurity‑driven instability in blended aggregates.

However, no explicit provisions are made for interactions with dissimilar materials, shrinkage restraint effects, or broader chemical attack beyond sulfate considerations.

When the gradation mix contacts exposed metal or thin concrete‑coated steel (less than a half‑inch thick), corrosion resistance must be verified through testing of resistivity, pH, chloride and sulfate levels. This requirement acknowledges that metallic interfaces can introduce durability concerns that indirectly affect gradation performance.

Finally, for aggregates with poor cementing qualities—such as certain stone types—the specification permits their use provided they are blended with limestone fines at a rate of 20 %. Influence of production and placement

During production the contractor must continuously verify that the aggregate gradation conforms to the design limits. This is achieved by performing daily sieve analyses of plant‑stockpiled material and whenever bins are restocked, then adjusting the proportions of aggregates on‑site so that the combined percent retained remains within the bounds set out in PTM No. 529. If any change to the aggregate blend also requires a modification of cement or water content, a new mix design with trial batches must be prepared. Uniform combination of all ingredients with a predetermined amount of water—maintaining the optimum moisture content and not varying it by more than two percentage points—ensures that the compacted mixture meets the job‑mix formula gradation limits. At least ninety percent of laboratory test results must fall within Table A tolerances, allowing any deviation to be corrected by revising the mix design or substituting non‑conforming material.

Mixing equipment capable of varying cutting‑drum speed and precisely metering water, stabilizing additives, emulsion, or foamed asphalt creates a homogeneous blend in which oversized particles are broken down, directly controlling the final particle‑size distribution. For reclaimed‑asphalt pavement, the reclaimed material, aggregates, and binder are combined according to the approved mix design at the recommended optimum moisture and binder content; when RAP gradations differ significantly, field adjustments prescribed in the quality‑control plan are made to achieve satisfactory coating and the specified compaction.

Placement must preserve the intended distribution by using dry or only slightly damp aggregates and avoiding excess free moisture. After applying emulsified or foamed asphalt, a single layer of coarse aggregate is spread uniformly at a rate of fifteen to twenty‑five pounds per square yard with a calibrated spreader; no segregated material may be added thereafter, and the mix must be placed at approved temperatures (concrete above 80 °F is acceptable but not linked to gradation quality). The mat is then compacted promptly—within five minutes of emulsion application—using pneumatic‑tire rollers that make a minimum of two passes at speeds no greater than ten miles per hour and contact pressures between forty and fifty pounds per square inch. Roller selection follows depth criteria: a 25‑ton pneumatic tire roller for shallow mixes, a 25‑ton vibratory padfoot roller for depths of eight inches or more, and steel‑drum finish rollers of twelve to fourteen tons for final smoothing. Compaction parameters—roller speed, pattern, amplitude, frequency, and pressure—are adjusted to avoid aggregate breakage, displacement, shoving, or cracking, thereby locking the particle‑size distribution in place.

Finishing practices focus on preventing segregation and temperature‑related stiffening; after spreading, any material that is segregated, below minimum temperature, or has an asphalt content deficiency or excess must not be added to the pavement mat. Macrotexture testing monitors pattern segregation, with differences exceeding 0.024 in considered unacceptable. Field acceptance samples are tested according to PTM No. 757, PTM No. 702 (Modified Method D), or PTM No. 739 when problematic aggregates are present, confirming asphalt content and the percent passing the 75 mm (No. 200) sieve.

Curing requirements protect moisture loss from joints in concrete pavements and ensure that stabilization agents in hot‑mix or cold‑mix applications fully react. The curing plan must keep temperatures above forty degrees Fahrenheit for at least seven days and avoid rain or imminent precipitation, thereby preserving the gradation achieved during mixing, placement, and compaction and contributing to the pavement’s intended strength and durability. Construction variations that disturb the intended particle‑size distribution are the primary drivers of long‑term gradation performance [1][2]. Using poorly graded or gap‑graded aggregate, where one size dominates or a range is missing, prevents dense compaction and lowers dry unit weight, which reduces CBR values and compromises durability [1]. An excess of fine material, indicated by a high percentage passing the #200 sieve, makes the mix more moisture sensitive, further lowering CBR and increasing susceptibility to frost heave [1]. Deviations that move the aggregate size distribution beyond the specification envelope—whether measured at the No 8 (2.36 mm), No 4 (4.75 mm), or No 200 (75 µm) sieves, or in the cumulative‑percent‑retained chart—create a non‑uniform particle mix that erodes strength and durability [2]. Segregation of coarse and fine particles in stockpiles, during cold‑feed operations, or within the finished mat produces pattern segregation; it becomes unacceptable when the difference in average pavement texture depth between segregated and well‑graded zones exceeds 0.024 inch [2]. Improper blending, such as failing to maintain a 50 %/50 % weight blend for surface‑recycling layers or neglecting the required 20 % limestone fines when coarse aggregate has poor cementing quality, also disturbs gradation and must be corrected [2]. Inadequate mixing that leaves visible lumps, balls, or unmixed material in the surface similarly undermines the intended distribution of particle sizes [2]. Moisture content must be kept within two percentage points of the optimum value; any excess runoff or deficient binder content creates zones with improper coating and can alter gradation over time [2]. Oversized particles, such as clumps retained on the 12.5 mm (½‑inch) sieve, must be reprocessed so that at least 95 % of aggregate passes the two‑inch sieve; failure to meet this threshold introduces voids and weak zones that affect long‑term behavior [2]. The combined percent retained may not exceed a five‑percent tolerance from the limits defined in PTM No. 529, and any single sample outside these tolerances requires immediate corrective action such as adjusting aggregate proportions or halting production [2]. Contaminants and foreign substances must be limited to no more than 2.5 % of the mix, with iron, fiber, and other impurities each capped at 1.0 %, and deleterious shale restricted to 10 % by mass; exceeding these limits introduces material that can interfere with gradation stability [2]. Shape‑related defects are also controlled: abrasion must stay within the maximum percent defined in Bulletin 27, thin or elongated pieces must not exceed the AASHTO M 323 limits, and a minimum percentage of crushed fragments is required to ensure interlock [2]. Finally, equipment calibration, haul‑vehicle loading procedures, and proper maintenance of mixing drums are essential because uncalibrated or poorly maintained equipment can produce uneven gradation and oversized material [2].

Selection, specification, and improvement

Selection of gradation begins with the functional requirements of the pavement layer, such as the need for high permeability in base and sub‑base to allow rapid gravity drainage while also providing sufficient stability for construction equipment [1]. Consequently, specifications often require that the aggregate pass a 50 mm (2‑inch) sieve completely and that the percentage passing the No. 4 (4.75 mm) sieve fall between fifteen and sixty percent, with no more than thirty percent passing the No. 100 (150 µm) sieve, supplemented by tight tolerances on intermediate sieves such as No. 8 and No. 200 [2]. For thin unbound granular wearing courses the nominal maximum aggregate size determines the available gradation type—Type A with 6.3 mm, Type B with 9.5 mm, or Type C with 12.5 mm—and also limits the lift thickness to roughly one‑to‑one‑and‑a‑half times the coarsest particle, resulting in a half‑inch layer for Type A and three‑quarters inch for Types B and C [1]. In dense‑graded crumb‑rubber modified asphalt mixtures the gradation must be fine enough—typically #30 mesh or smaller—to fit within the limited void space of the aggregate skeleton, and the crumb‑rubber content is kept low to maintain a lower binder viscosity as required by the AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) specification [1]. Across both aggregates and soils the shape of the particle‑size distribution is quantified by the coefficient of uniformity (Cu) and the coefficient of curvature (Cc), with well‑graded gravels requiring Cu ≥ 4, sands Cu ≥ 6, and all material groups needing Cc between 1 and 3 to achieve dense compaction and higher dry unit weights [1]. Quality criteria further restrict deleterious constituents; organic matter, excessive clay, lumps, construction debris and shale are limited to ten percent by weight or less, while abrasion limits, a sand‑equivalent value of at least forty‑five percent, and an uncompacted void content of no less than forty‑five percent must also be satisfied [2]. Fine aggregates must meet at least a Type B classification, and coarse aggregates are required to conform to the type designations (e.g., Type A, No. 8 or No. 89) appropriate for the traffic level of the section, with additional specifications such as a maximum dry rodded density of ninety pounds per cubic foot for certain materials like granulated blast‑furnace slag [2]. When crumb‑rubber additives are used, loss on a 200 mm screen may not exceed seven point six five percent, and special recycled materials such as RAP are limited to twenty percent by weight with at least ninety‑five percent passing the 2‑inch sieve after oversize reduction [2]. Compliance is demonstrated through daily or per‑batch sieve analyses of plant‑stockpiled material, plotting cumulative percent‑retained charts that must remain within tolerance bands; a deviation of less than five percent on a single sieve may be permitted before a new mix design is required, while multiple‑sample averages must satisfy the prescribed tolerances for at least ninety percent of field samples [2]. For asphalt mixtures the verification emphasizes the percent passing the No. 200 sieve and conformity to the Joint Mix Formula curve, with the sum of absolute deviations for any two sieves not allowed to exceed twenty percent of the corresponding JMF values; failure to meet this threshold mandates corrective action before paving can continue [2]. All aggregate sources must appear on an approved supplier list, and where a single source cannot achieve the required Sand‑Rafting Level (SRL) designation, a documented blend of two or more approved sources may be used provided the combined SRL equals or exceeds the contract value [2]. Finally, gradation data are essential for soil classification and for determining engineering properties such as the California Bearing Ratio, so the selected distribution must satisfy both the classification criteria and provide the information needed to conduct CBR testing [1].

Improving the performance of gradation begins with strict control of aggregate sources, requiring that material be obtained from suppliers listed in Bulletin 14 or from a qualified Selected Borrow Excavation and that common borrow be stock‑piled, sampled and tested to verify at least 95 percent passes the 2‑inch sieve while deleterious shale does not exceed ten percent by weight; aggregates must also be free of vegetation, lumps, excessive clay or other foreign substances [2]. When native material fails to meet the required sand‑replacement level or particle‑size distribution, blends may be created on a one‑to‑one mass basis up to fifty percent of the total mix weight, provided the resulting blend achieves an SRL equal to or better than specified and satisfies all Table A tolerances; limestone fines up to about twenty percent can be blended with poor‑cementing stone, and granulated blast‑furnace slag that meets density, size and sieve limits is accepted as a substitute for natural coarse aggregate [2]. For reclaimed pavement layers, mechanical stabilization during pulverization—adding clean aggregate or recycled‑asphalt pavement material to fill voids in the particle‑size distribution—produces a more uniform gradation, a process that is supported by systematic sampling and pre‑ and post‑construction in‑situ testing such as falling weight deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer and light weight deflectometer measurements; the required equipment includes a self‑propelled rotary reclaimer with an independently variable cutting drum speed capable of fracturing oversized particles to the two‑inch maximum while uniformly introducing water, dry or liquid stabilizers and emulsion [1][2]. Chemical stabilization can further raise strength by incorporating wet or dry additives such as Portland cement, lime or fly ash, provided laboratory mix designs confirm that brittleness does not become excessive; mineral fillers including cement dust, fly ash or stone‑crushing fines essentially free of clay may also be added in accordance with AASHTO M‑17 to fine‑tune the gradation and address moisture susceptibility [1][2]. Binder modifications are another avenue for improving gradation: ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses employ a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane together with one of three prescribed gradations (Type A, B or C) and are placed in a single lift whose thickness is set at 1 to 1.5 times the diameter of the coarsest aggregate, thereby sealing the surface and limiting oxidation, raveling and moderate rutting; dense‑graded mixtures may incorporate crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder (CRMAB) that contains a lower rubber content and fine aggregate #30 mesh or finer to provide low viscosity for limited void space, while Stone Matrix Asphalt wearing courses are permitted because their stone‑on‑stone skeleton yields a more resilient gradation, and crumb‑rubber inclusion is limited to a sieve‑analysis loss not exceeding 7.65 percent [1][2]. Across all mix designs the aggregates should meet well‑graded criteria—specifically a coefficient of uniformity Cu of at least four for gravels (or six for sands) and a curvature Cc between one and three—and the percentage passing the #200 sieve should be limited to control moisture sensitivity and frost‑heave potential; these parameters are verified through laboratory gradation, moisture‑density relationships and resilient modulus tests before construction [1][2]. Subgrade modification offers additional means of achieving uniform particle distribution: selective grading, blending and mixing of native soils together with boulder removal can create a more even gradation, while stabilizing agents such as cement or lime raise the CBR and resilient modulus of weak layers; alternative stiffening measures include rock capping using high‑CBR rock modeled at CBR = 20 and the placement of geogrids at the subgrade‑aggregate interface, with class 4 geotextiles acting as separators to preserve gradation during placement [1]. Rigorous quality control underpins every intervention: daily sieve analysis of plant‑stockpiled aggregate before production begins and each time a bin is restocked generates a cumulative percent‑retained chart that must fall within five percent of the required percentages, with any larger deviation on a single sieve requiring case‑by‑case approval and immediate corrective action; at least three gradation and binder‑content tests are performed on day one to verify compliance with the Joint Mix Formula, and ongoing testing follows an approved QC plan overseen by a PennDOT Certified Aggregate Technician, who ensures fineness modulus variation, sand equivalent, sodium‑sulfate soundness loss and uncompacted void content meet specified limits [2]. Moisture control is maintained by keeping optimum moisture content within plus or minus two percentage points of the target, using water sprays or controlled dampening to avoid excess free moisture; when moisture stripping is a concern, heat‑stable amine‑based liquid anti‑strip agents are blended at the manufacturer‑specified rate (minimum 0.25 percent for pervious mixes), and Warm‑Mix Asphalt technologies are incorporated into the Job Mix Formula with calibration against asphalt content and the 75‑µm sieve, followed by a moisture‑susceptibility analysis and verification that the finished mix achieves at least ninety‑five percent coated particles as measured by AASHTO T 195 [2]. Pattern segregation is monitored through macro‑texture measurements, and any difference exceeding 0.024 inch between segregated and non‑segregated zones triggers immediate suspension of paving and corrective actions such as re‑blending or core testing; lot‑by‑lot sampling of the uncompacted mix requires that at least ninety percent of test results fall within Table A tolerances, with a running average of the last five consecutive samples used to detect drift [2]. By integrating qualified source selection, calibrated blending ratios, appropriate fillers and anti‑strip additives, controlled use of recycled materials, moisture management, daily gradation monitoring, stringent laboratory verification, in‑situ performance measurements and equipment that enforces size limits, the gradation can be consistently maintained within design tolerances, thereby enhancing pavement strength, durability and overall performance.

Performance evaluation and implications

Evaluation of gradation begins with a laboratory sieve analysis that quantifies the percent passing each standard sieve and generates a particle‑size distribution curve [1][2]. For soils or subbase material, D10, D30, and D60 values are extracted and used to compute the coefficient of uniformity (Cu) and curvature (Cc) in accordance with ASTM D2487, providing a classification of well‑graded versus poorly‑graded material [1]. For aggregates intended for hot‑mix asphalt, sizing is performed using AASHTO T‑27 or the PTM suite (e.g., PTM 757 for mixtures, PTM 702/739 for aggregate‑only samples), and bulk specific gravity is measured per AASHTO T‑209 [2]. Acceptance limits are defined by a series of percentage‑passing criteria such as 100 % passing the 50 mm (2‑inch) sieve, 15–60 % passing the No. 4 (4.75 mm) sieve, and no more than ten percent passing the No. 200 sieve, together with sand‑equivalent values of at least 45 %, loss after sieving not exceeding 7.65 %, and a fineness modulus for Type A fine aggregate that may vary no more than ±0.20 over nine of ten consecutive samples; reclaimed material must achieve at least ninety‑five percent passing the 2‑inch sieve before further processing [2]. The laboratory results also support determination of engineering properties such as uncompacted void content (AASHTO T 304) and California Bearing Ratio performance, linking gradation to compaction density and bearing capacity [1][2]. Because direct field measurement of permeability is impractical, agencies treat the laboratory gradation data themselves as an indirect indicator of drainage capability for base materials [1]. In practice, field verification is carried out at the asphalt plant where stockpiled aggregate is sieved before each production shift or whenever bins are restocked; a cumulative percent‑retained chart is prepared and compared against the limits defined in PTM 529 (Combined Gradation Percent Retained Chart) [2]. Samples of the hot‑mix, either taken at the plant laboratory according to PTM No. 1 or collected immediately after placement on the roadway, are tested using the same PTM methods as for the raw aggregate; at least three satisfactory test results (or a running average of five) must be obtained before the mix is accepted, and daily production exceeding 50 tons requires at least one determination [2]. Compacted mixture samples are taken at least twice daily, and ninety percent of those samples must satisfy the tolerance limits set out in Table A; visual inspection of stockpiles for clumps or oversized particles retained on a 12.5 mm sieve is also required, with re‑processing until 95 % passes the 2‑inch sieve [2]. During the first day of paving a test strip is constructed and evaluated according to ISSA TB 102, after which cores are extracted (minimum three six‑inch specimens) for density, asphalt‑content, and gradation analysis; pattern segregation is deemed unacceptable when texture‑depth differences exceed 0.024 inches [2]. Long‑term evaluation of gradation in some guidance relies primarily on the original laboratory characterization, with no separate field monitoring program cited [1]. Other guidance records continued compliance through production documentation; historical logs showing that each mix met all Joint Mix Formula tolerances, annual verification of the JMF, and material certifications issued by the producer constitute the primary long‑term record of gradation performance [2]. Additional post‑construction data such as moisture‑sensitivity measurements (tensile strength ratio), Hamburg wheel‑tracking results, warranty bonds, and periodic performance surveys are retained to demonstrate that the specified gradation continues to satisfy durability criteria throughout service life [2].

Gradation, defined as the particle‑size distribution of aggregates or soils, is a primary factor governing density, strength, durability and overall performance of both flexible and rigid pavements. In base and subbase layers a gradation that creates relatively large inter‑particle voids promotes high permeability, allowing gravity‑drainable water to escape rapidly and preventing capillary rise that could otherwise retain moisture in fine soils; this reduces the risk of frost heave, rutting and loss of ride quality, thereby extending service life. However, achieving the target gradation alone does not guarantee drainage, so designers must verify permeability through testing. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses (UTWC) the selected gradation fixes the nominal maximum aggregate size—6.3 mm for Type A, 9.5 mm for Type B and 12.5 mm for Type C—and consequently dictates the required layer thickness of about one‑half inch for Type A and three‑quarters inch for Types B and C, roughly 1 to 1.5 times the largest aggregate diameter. A properly graded UTWC forms a continuous seal that limits oxidation and weathering, preserving fatigue resistance, reducing low‑temperature cracking, and mitigating surface raveling and moderate rutting. In dense‑graded asphalt mixtures a wide spectrum of particle sizes produces a tightly packed skeleton with minimal void space; this improves load distribution and resistance to deformation. When the binder is a crumb‑rubber modified asphalt formulated with finer particles (#30 mesh or smaller) and lower viscosity, it can be incorporated into the limited‑void dense matrix without compromising density, delivering performance comparable to polymer‑modified binders while reducing maintenance needs. For concrete pavements the target gradation is first established by PTM 528 and refined under PTM 529; coarse‑ and fine‑aggregate volume fractions must remain within prescribed limits, with a single‑sieve deviation of up to five percent permissible only when expressly authorized. Daily sieve analyses of plant‑stockpiled material are performed before production begins and each time bins are restocked, and the cumulative percent retained is compared against the Combined Gradation Percent Retained Chart; if measurements fall outside the envelope, the contractor must rebalance the aggregate blend on the fly. Any adjustment that also changes cement or water content triggers a new concrete mix design to preserve intended strength, permeability and shrinkage characteristics. For asphalt mixtures uniform grading of coarse and fine fractions is required by Table A (or Table D for specific tolerances) so that particles can be evenly coated with binder; the asphalt content must stay within 0.8 % of the Joint Mix Formula and the fineness modulus of Type A fine aggregate from at least nine of ten consecutive samples may not vary more than ± 0.20 from the average. Superpave quality criteria—including void content, sand‑equivalent, abrasion resistance, shape limits and a minimum SRL designation—must be satisfied to achieve required volumetric targets (air voids, VMA) and moisture‑susceptibility performance; when gradation deviates from these tolerances corrective actions such as adjusting aggregate proportions or suspending production are mandated because an improper size distribution prevents adequate particle coating, reduces load‑bearing capacity and accelerates stripping or cracking. Construction practices reinforce gradation control by storing each aggregate size in a dedicated stockpile, batching fine and coarse fractions separately, prohibiting mixing with earth or foreign material, and maintaining uniform moisture to avoid segregation; these measures help the mix attain consistent density and resistance to premature deterioration. When reclaimed asphalt pavement is incorporated, any variation in its gradation or composition triggers field adjustments prescribed in the quality‑control plan, and the cutting drum on recycling equipment must be capable of varying rotational speed independently of forward travel so that oversized particles can be trimmed and overall gradation fine‑tuned. Acceptance criteria are strict: at least 95 % of aggregate must pass the 2‑inch sieve, aggregates must meet Table A limits with an overall rating of at least 90 %, and the percent passing the No. 200 (75 µm) sieve is monitored continuously; if the absolute deviation from the job‑mix formula exceeds 20 % on any two sieves, pattern segregation may develop, producing macrotexture differences greater than 0.024 inch—conditions that are unacceptable and require removal and replacement of the defective pavement. Well‑graded subgrade soils containing an even spread of large, medium and small particles compact to higher dry unit weights, yielding higher California Bearing Ratio values; because resilient modulus used in pavement design is derived from CBR, better‑graded subgrades provide greater elastic support, improve load distribution and enhance durability, allowing designers to adopt less aggressive improvement measures and thereby lowering construction effort and future maintenance. Gradation testing during reconstruction projects establishes the particle‑size distribution of existing subgrade or base materials, enabling proper soil classification and accurate determination of engineering properties such as CBR; precise gradation data therefore influence the calculated resilient modulus, structural coefficients, layer thicknesses and material selections, while inaccurate or unknown gradation can lead to under‑ or over‑engineered designs and increased risk of premature deterioration and higher maintenance costs. In full‑depth reclamation, mechanical stabilization that includes pulverizing existing pavement, reshaping, recompacting and adding aggregate or RAP to improve gradation enhances structural integrity; the improved gradation permits additional volume for raising elevation or widening without sacrificing required thickness, which in turn raises load‑bearing capacity and long‑term durability. Across both rigid and flexible pavements, rigorous control of gradation from design through material selection, plant production, field placement and quality‑control testing ensures that the finished pavement achieves its intended strength, crack resistance, moisture durability and serviceability while minimizing future maintenance demands.
