Gradation

Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-28

Gradation is the particle‑size distribution of the aggregates or soils that form the various layers of a pavement structure, quantified through standardized sieve analyses that report percent passing or retained for each size fraction and yield characteristic values such as D10, D30, D60, the coefficient of uniformity (C u) and curvature (C c) [1][2]. This distribution governs key engineering behaviors—permeability, compaction density, California Bearing Ratio, and ultimately the ability of the pavement to resist traffic loads and environmental stresses—by controlling how large, medium and fine particles interlock to create a dense or open skeleton [1][2]. Maintaining the prescribed gradation within tight tolerance limits (e.g., specific percent‑passing ranges on standard sieves, minimum C u of 4.0 and C c between 1 and 3) ensures that the pavement achieves its intended strength, durability, uniformity and long‑term performance [1][2].

Material purpose and applicability

Gradation is the particle‑size distribution of the aggregates or soils that constitute the various layers of a pavement structure and is obtained through gradation tests that quantify the percent passing or retained for each size fraction [1][2]. In base and subbase layers the shape of the gradation curve indicates potential permeability and thus drainage capacity, although particle shape also influences flow paths so gradation alone does not guarantee the desired drainage [1]. For subgrade soils a well‑graded mix containing an even range of large, medium and small particles compacts to higher dry unit weights and yields higher California Bearing Ratio values, whereas poorly or gap‑graded soils retain more voids and provide lower strength [1]. The same gradation information is required for proper soil classification and for determining the engineering properties that feed into CBR testing [1]. In dense‑graded asphalt mixtures the wide range of aggregate sizes creates a tightly packed skeleton with minimal void space, allowing a crumb‑rubber modified binder—characterized by smaller particle size and lower viscosity—to fill the limited voids and impart high strength and durability to the pavement surface [1]. For concrete pavements or base‑course mixes, gradation is monitored by sieve analysis of stockpiled aggregate before production and each time bins are restocked, producing a cumulative percent‑retained chart that is compared against limits prescribed in PTM No. 529 (or the Combined Gradation Percent Retained Chart) [2]. The mix must fall within these specified limits—generally no more than five percent outside the required retention values—to achieve the intended consistency, strength, durability, uniformity, density and overall performance of the pavement [2]. When measured gradation deviates from the target, aggregate proportions are adjusted during production, often by uniformly blending materials with a predetermined water content, so that the compacted mixture meets the job‑mix formula requirements [2]. Maintaining the prescribed gradation therefore ensures that the pavement system can reliably resist traffic loads and environmental stresses throughout its service life [1][2].

Composition and characteristics

Gradation is defined by the particle‑size distribution of aggregates or other granular components used in pavement materials such as base courses, concrete, hot‑mix asphalt, seal‑coat and soil fills. [1][2] The constituents that make up a gradable mix include coarse aggregates, medium‑sized particles, fine fractions that may pass a #200 sieve, mineral filler or crumb rubber, and optionally reclaimed materials such as RAP, granulated blast‑furnace slag, or other approved additives. [1][2] In modified binders the crumb rubber is limited to mesh sizes of #30 or smaller, while mineral filler must be a finely divided rock or limestone dust free of organic impurities and with a plasticity index not exceeding four. [1][2] The composition of gradation is expressed as the cumulative percent passing each standard sieve, producing characteristic values such as D10, D30, D60 and enabling calculation of the coefficient of uniformity (Cu) and curvature (Cc). [1][2] Specification limits commonly require that 100 % of material pass a 50 mm (2‑inch) sieve, between 15 % and 60 % may pass the 4.75 mm (No. 4) sieve, and no more than 30 % may pass the 150 µm (No. 100) sieve, with additional limits for intermediate sieves such as 6.3 mm, 2.36 mm, 300 µm and 75 µm depending on the application. [2] For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses three gradation types (A, B, C) are prescribed with nominal maximum aggregate sizes of 6.3 mm, 9.5 mm and 12.5 mm respectively, controlling the internal structure of the hot‑mix layer. [1][2] Dense‑graded mixtures contain a broad spectrum of particle sizes that pack tightly, yielding limited void space, higher dry densities, greater CBR values and improved fatigue resistance, whereas gap‑graded or poorly graded blends lack certain size fractions, creating larger inter‑particle voids that increase permeability but may reduce strength. [1][2] The fineness modulus of fine aggregates must vary no more than ±0.20 across consecutive samples, providing a measure of overall coarseness. [2] Uncompacted void content is determined by standard test methods and must meet the limits set in relevant tables, influencing the mixture’s ability to achieve target densities after compaction. [1][2] Shape criteria require that particles retained on the 4.75 mm sieve have a length‑to‑thickness ratio not greater than five to one and limit the proportion of thin or elongated pieces, contributing to stability of the gradated skeleton. [2] The overall structure achieved by uniformly combining the aggregate fractions—often in equal weight proportions or as prescribed by a job‑mix formula—must remain within extreme limits defined in specification tables, with clumps and oversized material on sieves such as 12.5 mm prohibited. [2][1] The resulting gradation governs permeability, capillary rise, compaction behavior, and long‑term performance of pavement layers, with well‑graded soils compacting more densely than poorly graded or gap‑graded soils. [1][2]

Gradation is highly responsive to any alteration in the composition, source, processing method or proportioning of the constituent materials, because such changes reshape the particle‑size distribution curve that governs pavement behavior [1][2]. In ultra‑thin wearing courses three predefined gradations—Type A, B and C—with nominal maximum aggregate sizes of 6.3 mm, 9.5 mm and 12.5 mm respectively are used; selecting a larger nominal size increases the design thickness to roughly one to one‑and‑a‑half times the particle diameter, which together with a finer overall distribution improves surface sealing, reduces oxidation and weathering, and consequently enhances resistance to fatigue cracking, low‑temperature cracking, raveling and moderate rutting [1]. When reclaimed pavement is incorporated, simple pulverization reproduces the original in‑situ particle‑size spectrum and therefore limits attainable strength, whereas mechanical stabilization that adds imported granular aggregate or recycled asphalt pavement modifies the coarse‑to‑fine ratio, yields a more desirable gradation, raises structural integrity, increases layer volume without sacrificing thickness, and helps satisfy load‑bearing and durability requirements [1]. In dense‑graded mixes the amount and fineness of crumb rubber in the binder also affect gradation; a terminal‑blended CRMAB produced with lower crumb‑rubber content and particles passing a #30 mesh creates a low‑viscosity binder that can infiltrate limited voids among a wide range of aggregate sizes, achieving tighter packing while preserving strength and durability comparable to polymer‑modified binders despite variations in source material or processing [1]. For granular soils and unbound aggregates the shape of the gradation curve directly controls compaction density and related strength indices: well‑graded materials that contain a balanced mix of large, medium and fine particles compact to higher dry unit weights and exhibit higher California Bearing Ratio values than poorly graded or gap‑graded mixes, with quantitative limits for gravels of coefficient of uniformity Cu ≥ 4 and curvature Cc between 1 and 3, and for sands of Cu ≥ 6 with the same Cc range [1]. Because permissible particle‑size distributions are expressed on cumulative percent‑retained charts, substituting material from a different source or altering relative size amounts can shift the curve beyond the limits defined in PTM No. 529; therefore specifications require that plant‑stockpiled aggregate be sieved before each production shift and whenever bins are replenished, with any out‑of‑tolerance result prompting on‑site adjustments to aggregate ratios and, if those adjustments also affect cementitious material or water content, the development of a new mix design to preserve strength and workability [2]. Only aggregates listed in Bulletin 14 may be used, each meeting a designated Stone Ratio Level; when a single source cannot meet the target SRL, contractors may blend two aggregates in an equal‑weight proportion so that the combined SRL meets or exceeds the requirement, and the same 50 % by weight rule applies to blending reclaimed asphalt shingle with fine aggregate or to incorporating RAP that contains at least 5 % cold‑milled material, which must be shredded and screened so that 100 % passes a 9.5 mm sieve and any clumps or particles larger than 12.5 mm are removed through re‑processing [2]. The fine‑aggregate portion must achieve a minimum sand‑equivalent value of 45 % and an uncompacted void content within prescribed limits; mineral composition or processing that reduces these indices causes gradation failure, while the acceptable range for material passing the No. 4 (4.75 mm) sieve is 15 % to 60 % and deleterious shale may not exceed ten percent of the total mass, with all materials required to be free from organic matter, lumps or excess clay that would alter size distribution [2]. Water content also influences gradation compliance: the mix must be uniformly combined with a predetermined water quantity so that at least 90 % of test samples fall within tolerance limits and water may not vary by more than two percentage points from optimum moisture content; any compositional, source, processing or proportioning change that causes measured gradation to deviate therefore necessitates revision of the job‑mix formula or replacement of the material [2]. When aggregate cementing quality is poor, blending twenty percent limestone fines restores the particle‑size distribution to meet required limits, and granulated blast‑furnace slag may be used only if produced by rapid water quenching, with a dry rodded density not greater than 90 lb/ft³, a maximum size of two inches and no more than twenty percent passing the No. 100 sieve, controls that prevent excess fines from upsetting gradation [2]. Any change in material makeup—whether substitution of aggregate fractions, alteration of binder type or warm‑mix additive content, or switching source—requires development of a new Job Mix Formula to verify that percent passing each sieve remains within the tolerances specified and that moisture resistance is not compromised; for reclaimed materials, differences between RAP and RAM or variations in asphalt grade modify particle‑size distribution, prompting field adjustments to moisture, emulsion or foamed‑asphalt content, reduction of oversized particles until ninety‑five percent pass a two‑inch sieve, or preparation of an entirely new mix design [2]. Gradation quality is further measured by the coefficient of uniformity, which must average at least 4.0 and may not fall below 3.5 for any individual sample, with failure resulting in lot rejection; non‑uniform blends can produce pattern segregation—continuous areas where coarse and fine particles are unevenly distributed—and if the summation of absolute deviations between any two sieves reaches or exceeds twenty percent of the Joint Mix Formula the pavement is deemed defective, requiring cessation of production until compliance is restored [2]. Processing equipment also influences gradation: adjustable cutting‑drum speeds control oversized material in full‑depth reclamation, and integrated liquid‑proportioning systems regulate water and additive rates based on cut depth, width and travel speed to produce a homogeneous blend; over‑compaction of fine‑graded aggregate can crush particles and alter the original distribution, so handling practices that avoid crushing, limit moisture content and use dry or appropriately damp aggregates are mandated for seal‑coat applications [2]. Consequently, variations in composition, source, processing technique or proportioning reshape the gradation curve, which in turn governs layer thickness, sealing capability, resistance to environmental degradation, structural capacity, compaction density and strength indicators such as CBR, ensuring that deliberate adjustment of these variables enables engineers to tailor pavement performance to specific loading and climatic demands.

Key material properties

Gradation performance depends on a tightly controlled set of physical, mechanical, chemical and thermal attributes of the aggregate blend. Physically, the particle‑size distribution must follow the prescribed gradation curve and be characterized by parameters such as the coefficient of uniformity (Cu) – required to be at least 4.0 – and curvature (Cc), which together describe how well large, medium and fine particles fill voids [1][2]. The design also limits the nominal maximum aggregate size to two inches in general, while specific pavement types use nominal sizes of 6.3 mm, 9.5 mm or 12.5 mm for Types A, B and C respectively, with layer thicknesses set at roughly one to one‑and‑a‑half times the maximum size to achieve adequate structural capacity [1]. A high proportion of material passing the No. 200 sieve (75 µm) and a sand‑equivalent value of at least 45 % are required to ensure sufficient permeability, allowing gravity‑drainable water to be removed rapidly and preventing capillary rise through larger voids; uncompacted void content must meet or exceed the AASHTO T304 minimum of about 45 % [1][2]. Bulk unit weight is constrained between 70 and 110 lb/ft³, with dry rodded density not exceeding 90 lb/ft³, providing a target range for compaction density that supports bearing capacity as reflected in CBR values [2]. Mechanically, well‑graded aggregates achieve higher maximum dry densities and lower optimum moisture contents, which translate into improved resilient modulus and CBR performance and enhance the ability of thin bonded wearing courses to resist fatigue and low‑temperature cracking [1]. Consistency of the fine‑aggregate fineness modulus is controlled to within ±0.20 of the average for at least nine of ten consecutive samples, ensuring a stable size distribution during production [2]. Specific gravity and absorption are measured per AASHTO T84 to assess density and porosity, while Los Angeles abrasion loss is limited to 55 % (or the maximum specified in Bulletin 27) to guarantee durability [2]. Shape criteria restrict flat or elongated particles to no more than 10 % for a 1:5 shape ratio or 20 % for a 1:3 ratio, and a minimum proportion of crushed fragments is required to provide interlock; soundness testing after five sodium‑sulfate wet–dry cycles must show loss not exceeding 15 %, confirming resistance to freeze–thaw damage [2]. At least 95 % of aggregate particles must be coated with binder, draindown testing verifies that the binder remains attached during production, and compaction pressures between 625 and 1,025 lb/ft² (or a static weight of 110–220 lb) are applied without crushing the gradation [2]. Chemically, the Atterberg limits—liquid limit, plastic limit and plasticity index—describe the soil’s susceptibility to strength loss and compressibility when moisture conditions vary; the plasticity index is limited to a maximum of 4 % for mineral filler [1][2]. Organic impurity is evaluated by color value, and if it exceeds Organic Plate No. 3 a minimum strength ratio per AASHTO T71 must be met, restricting use to fine aggregate that satisfies that ratio [2]. Metallic iron content on the 4.75 mm sieve may not exceed 1.0 % of the dry mass, deleterious shale is limited to 10 % by mass, and sand‑equivalent values must meet the 45 % minimum; up to 20 % limestone fines may be blended when stone lacks adequate cementing quality, providing additional flexibility while maintaining performance [2]. The blend must also be free of excessive vegetable or organic matter, lumps, or objectionable substances that could impair strength or durability [1][2]. Thermally, temperature control is essential to preserve the intended gradation and mixture properties. A concrete temperature‑control plan mandates actions if mix temperature exceeds 80 °F at placement, while aggregate temperatures must be maintained at or above 40 °F during cold‑weather batching and at least 60 °F for seal‑coat applications; ambient conditions must stay above 40 °F throughout the seven‑day cure period, and accelerating admixtures may not be used when temperatures are expected to fall below 35 °F within the first 24 hours after placement [2]. Although explicit thermal parameters are not measured for gradation, the sealing effect of a properly graded aggregate reduces oxidation and limits water ingress, thereby mitigating low‑temperature distress and freeze–thaw cycling that could otherwise lead to cracking [1]. Together, these physical, mechanical, chemical and thermal attributes ensure that gradation contributes effectively to pavement strength, durability and long‑term performance.

Gradation is quantified by extracting representative samples of the aggregate or mix at intervals defined in a quality‑control plan and subjecting them to a standardized sieve analysis, typically following AASHTO T 27, which employs a series of sieves such as the 4.75 mm (No. 4), 2.36 mm (No. 8) and 75 µm (No. 200) sizes[2]. The test determines the percent passing each sieve; these percentages are plotted as a cumulative percent‑retained curve from which characteristic particle diameters D10, D30 and D60 are derived[1]. Using the D values, the coefficient of uniformity (Cu = D60/D10) and the coefficient of curvature (Cc = D30²/(D10·D60)) are calculated, and the resulting numbers are compared with classification limits—gravel requires Cu ≥ 4 and 1 ≤ Cc ≤ 3, sand requires Cu ≥ 6 with the same curvature range—to distinguish well‑graded from poorly graded material[1]. The coefficient of uniformity is also required to average at least 4.0 for each batch, with no individual sample falling below 3.5, providing an additional check on gradation quality[2]. Classification of the aggregate blend proceeds by evaluating the particle‑size distribution against extreme limits presented in tables (Table A, Table B or Table D) and by plotting the results on a FHWA 0.45 power‑type gradation chart with the Joint Mix Formula superimposed; consensus properties such as sand equivalency, fine‑aggregate angularity, coarse‑aggregate angularity and flat/elongated piece content are then used to finalize the aggregate classification[2]. Dense‑graded mixes are identified by a wide size distribution that leaves minimal void space, whereas gap‑graded mixes contain coarser particles with larger inter‑particle voids; these distinctions are inferred directly from the shape of the gradation curve[1]. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, the gradation is further categorized into three predefined types—A, B and C—based solely on the nominal maximum aggregate size, with limits of 6.3 mm for Type A, 9.5 mm for Type B and 12.5 mm for Type C[1]. When crumb‑rubber is incorporated, a dry sieve analysis performed to ASTM D5461 (or FM 5‑559) using 200 mm sieves is required, and the material must not lose more than 7.65 % after sieving; finer gradations for rubber‑modified binders are defined by passing a #30 mesh sieve or smaller, contrasting with coarser gap‑graded asphalt‑rubber mixtures[1][2]. Mineral filler gradation is verified by a hydrometer analysis in accordance with AASHTO T 88 and must satisfy the grading requirements of AASHTO M 17, including a maximum plasticity index of four[2]. Specific percent‑passing criteria are also imposed—for example, 100 % passing a 50 mm sieve, 15–60 % passing a 4.75 mm sieve and 0–30 % passing a 150 µm sieve—to ensure compliance with contract specifications[2]. Flat and elongated particles are identified on material retained on the No. 4 sieve using ASTM D4791 Method B, where a length‑to‑thickness ratio of five to one defines such pieces and their mass percentage is reported as part of the aggregate’s consensus properties[2]. Compliance with gradation limits is first established after three test results; when five or more results are available, the running average of the last five consecutive tests is used, and a percent‑within‑limits (PWL) of at least 90 % is required for stockpile approval[2]. In concrete production, daily checks of plant‑stockpiled aggregate are performed before each shift and whenever bins are restocked, with deviations greater than five percent from the combined gradation percent retained chart generally prompting adjustment of aggregate proportions[2]. Agencies such as PennDOT also employ the particle‑size distribution obtained from these tests as an indirect indicator of permeability because direct field measurement is difficult, although they acknowledge that mixes with identical gradations may exhibit different permeabilities due to particle shape influencing void structure[1]

Behavior and mechanisms

Gradation governs the way subgrade and fine‑grained aggregate respond to mechanical loading because it controls how densely the material can be compacted; well‑graded soils with a continuous distribution of particle sizes achieve higher dry unit weights and CBR values, while excessive compaction pressure on fine‑grained aggregate can crush particles and modify the intended gradation, so placement specifications limit compaction intensity [1][2]. The presence of fines, identified by the proportion passing the #200 sieve, makes the material more moisture sensitive: higher fines content reduces achievable dry density, lowers CBR, and raises susceptibility to frost heave, prompting designers to monitor and control moisture levels during construction [1]. For fine‑grained aggregate, moisture is also managed by ensuring that standing water deeper than six inches is removed before placement, a practice intended to prevent adverse effects on compaction and gradation even though the moisture itself does not directly alter particle size distribution [2]. The guides do not provide specific guidance on how temperature fluctuations affect gradation, nor do they describe changes caused by chemical exposure or other long‑term service conditions; consequently, the behavior of gradation under those service environments remains unspecified in the referenced material [1][2].

Gradation behavior in pavement materials is governed by an interrelated set of physical packing characteristics, chemical interactions of fines, and mechanical processes that modify the particle‑size distribution. [1][2] The primary physical control is the size spectrum of aggregates; crushing and screening are used to limit the maximum dimension to two inches and to eliminate material retained on a 12.5 mm (½‑inch) sieve, thereby establishing the coarse‑size envelope required for the target gradation. [2] Within that envelope, the coefficient of uniformity quantifies the spread of sizes; an average value of at least 4.0 across a batch and no less than 3.5 for any individual sample are specified to ensure sufficient diversity of large, medium, and small particles that can fill inter‑particle voids and produce a dense skeleton. [2] A smooth gradation curve, characteristic of well‑graded soils or aggregates, enables higher dry unit weights and CBR values because the particles nestle together with minimal void space, whereas gap‑graded or poorly graded mixes leave excess voids and reduce strength. [1][2] The proportion of fine material passing the #200 sieve directly influences moisture sensitivity; higher fines content increases the likelihood of silt and clay fractions that raise compressibility and diminish stability under wet conditions. Durability and deterioration

Gradation quality directly influences pavement durability because the particle‑size distribution controls moisture sensitivity, compaction characteristics and load‑bearing capacity [1][2]. The presence of excessive fine particles makes a soil more prone to water absorption, reduces its California Bearing Ratio (CBR) and increases susceptibility to frost heave [1]. Similarly, soils with high liquid limit and plasticity index exhibit greater compressibility and may lose strength when subjected to additional loading or moisture [1]. When aggregates are poorly graded or gap‑graded, the lack of intermediate sizes prevents dense packing, leading to lower dry unit weight, reduced CBR values and accelerated deterioration under traffic loads [1]. During construction, fine particles finer than the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve can be washed out, especially if organic impurities exceed prescribed limits, thereby altering the intended gradation [2]. A soundness test that shows a loss of more than fifteen percent mass after five immersion‑drying cycles in a sodium‑sulfate solution signals degradation of fine aggregate and requires rejection to preserve gradation integrity [2]. Segregation caused by concrete vibration or inadequate mixing disrupts the uniform blend, so proportion adjustments must keep the combined percent retained within the five‑percent tolerance defined by PTM No. 529 [2]. The inclusion of crumb‑rubber material is limited to a maximum sieve‑analysis loss of 7.65 % and total contaminants such as iron, fiber or cord not exceeding 2.5 %, because excess foreign matter can shift the particle‑size distribution [2]. Abrasion, excessive thin or elongated particles, and insufficient crushed fragments are each capped by limits in Bulletin 27 and AASHTO M‑323; surpassing these thresholds generates finer fragments that erode gradation uniformity [2]. Foreign material contamination and frost‑induced clumping must be avoided through separate stockpiles, strict moisture control, and maintaining aggregate temperatures of at least 40 °F during cold weather to prevent particle swelling [2]. Seal‑coat aggregate derived from reclaimed asphalt pavement can develop clumps or retain oversized particles larger than the 12.5 mm sieve; if such material remains in uncovered stockpiles for more than one month—or in covered stockpiles for over three months—it must be reprocessed to restore proper gradation [2]. The overall gradation quality also depends on achieving a minimum average coefficient of uniformity of 4.0 (individual samples not below 3.5) and on keeping aggregates free of excessive free moisture [2]. Premature traffic loading before the emulsified asphalt cures can cause binder flushing, surface distortion and loss of coarse particles; when less than fifty percent of seal‑coat aggregate becomes properly embedded, additional fog sealing is required to protect gradation [2]. Mechanical actions such as aggressive compaction, heavy traffic loads or repeated handling may crush particles, altering the size distribution; specifications therefore call for minimizing handling, limiting trafficking on exposed compacted surfaces and prohibiting vibratory rolling unless testing confirms no aggregate breakage or binder flushing [2]. Core inspections of each lot are required to verify that no aggregate breakage has occurred during manufacture, shipment or placement [2]. The presence of excess moisture or unburned fuel oil in aggregates or the mixed material degrades the mixture and changes particle distribution, necessitating elimination according to specified limits [2]. Pattern segregation—continuous zones where coarse and fine particles are unevenly distributed—is deemed unacceptable when the difference in macro‑texture depth exceeds 0.024 inches or when the summed absolute deviation from any two sieves surpasses twenty percent of the Joint Mix Formula, because such non‑uniformity compromises pavement performance [2]. Improper compaction that produces displacement, shoving, cracking or further aggregate breakage can exacerbate segregation; therefore roller speed, amplitude, frequency, pattern and size must be adjusted to preserve the intended gradation [2].

A well‑graded particle distribution that includes balanced large, medium and fine fractions yields higher dry densities and CBR values, which in turn lowers the likelihood of gradation‑related loss of bearing capacity or frost heave [1]. Conversely, soils or aggregates that are poorly graded, gap graded, contain a high proportion of #200‑sieve fines, or exhibit elevated liquid limits and plasticity indices become more moisture‑sensitive, compact less densely, and lose strength when wet or frozen, thereby increasing susceptibility to deterioration [1]. Excessive fines content and high Atterberg limits therefore amplify gradation vulnerability, whereas low fines content and low plasticity mitigate it [1]. The stability of the gradation also depends on controlling moisture; adding water within two percentage points of the optimum value and achieving at least ninety percent of test samples within the specified tolerance limits markedly reduces the risk of degradation [2]. Allowing the moisture content to deviate beyond this narrow range or producing mixes in which fewer than ninety percent of samples meet the tolerances increases the potential for gradation loss [2]. Oversized particles that pass the 12.5 mm sieve and clumped material disrupt the intended particle distribution, so such material must be removed promptly to prevent accelerated deterioration [2]. Stockpiles of seal‑coat aggregate stored uncovered for more than one month or covered for more than three months must be reprocessed because prolonged storage promotes the development of oversized or clumped particles that compromise gradation [2]. Segregation, whether caused by mixing aggregates with earth or foreign material or by pattern segregation in which the combined deviation between any two sieve sizes exceeds roughly twenty percent of the joint mix formula, also accelerates loss of the designed gradation [2]. Temperature and moisture conditions during storage, batching and curing are critical; aggregates should be kept at or above 40 °F to avoid frost, and placement must not occur when rain is expected within two hours or when standing water deeper than six inches remains on the surface, as these exposures degrade gradation [2]. Flood‑prone storage areas likewise increase susceptibility, so keeping material away from such locations reduces risk [2]. Mechanical actions that crush particles—excessive compaction effort, traffic on the exposed layer, or direct roller contact—alter the size distribution and raise vulnerability to deterioration [2]. Protective practices that lower susceptibility include maintaining separate stockpiles for each aggregate size and type, uniformly controlling moisture, using equipment such as a variable‑speed cutting drum to limit oversize material, minimizing handling, removing excess water or ice before placement, limiting ground pressures, employing geotextile separators, and promptly covering the layer with at least six inches of capping material [2].

Material interactions and compatibility

Gradation defines the proportion of coarse and fine particles and therefore dictates the amount of cementitious binder, water, or asphalt paste needed to fill the voids created by the aggregate skeleton. In full‑depth reclamation projects the particle‑size distribution is intentionally modified by adding granular material or reclaimed asphalt pavement so that the adjusted gradation more effectively fills voids and raises the structural capacity of the rebuilt base. When chemical stabilizers such as Portland cement, lime, or fly ash are introduced, their dosage must be balanced against this altered blend because a higher proportion generally increases strength but an excess can make the mix brittle, requiring careful coordination between chemistry and gradation to preserve durability. In concrete production the aggregate blend is continuously monitored by sieve analysis before daily batching and whenever bins are restocked; any shift beyond the five‑percent tolerance specified in PTM 529 triggers a report and a rebalancing of the mix because changes in the fine‑coarse ratio alter cement and water quantities needed for target strength and workability, often necessitating a new trial batch. Lightweight aggregates are required to be stored at or above saturated surface dry so that their absorption does not upset the paste demand dictated by the prescribed gradation. Dense‑graded asphalt mixtures employ a wide range of aggregate sizes that pack tightly, leaving limited inter‑aggregate void space; consequently the binder content is adjusted within 0.8 % of the target asphalt content to achieve uniform coating and prevent observable runoff, while crumb‑rubber modified binders are produced with a finer gradation—30 mesh or smaller—and exhibit lower viscosity, allowing their small particles to migrate into the restricted gaps of the dense aggregate skeleton and deliver strength and durability comparable to polymer‑modified binders. The water added for compaction in asphalt mixes is kept within two percentage points of the optimum moisture level so that the final compacted mixture conforms to the job‑mix formula limits derived from the prescribed gradation. If the particle‑size distribution does not provide sufficient material passing the No. 200 sieve, filler such as Portland cement or hydrated lime is supplied to meet that target, directly linking gradation to the cementitious component and influencing paste demand. Achieving a specified stone‑on‑stone rating often requires blending two aggregates in a 50/50 weight ratio so that the combined gradation meets or exceeds the required SRL, thereby ensuring proper aggregate‑binder bonding and surface durability; similarly, when a coarse stone lacks adequate cementing ability, blending twenty percent limestone fines restores compatibility with the cement paste and improves bond strength. Granulated blast‑furnace slag must satisfy No. 10 gradation limits and remain below a dry rodded density of 90 lb/ft³ to prevent excessive water absorption that would disturb the intended paste volume. The aggregate skeleton must also meet a minimum average coefficient of uniformity of four and have at least ninety‑five percent passing a two‑inch sieve; oversize material is reduced until these criteria are satisfied, ensuring that the gradation supports adequate coating and volumetric consistency. Specific gravity and absorption tests further affect the volume of paste required to reach the desired mix consistency, reinforcing the role of gradation in determining binder demand across concrete and asphalt applications. When reclaimed pavement material, source variation, or excessive free moisture alters the gradation, field adjustments or a new design submission are required to maintain adequate coating, prevent pattern segregation, and achieve target density and texture tolerances, highlighting the need for ongoing coordination between gradation and other mix constituents. Warm‑mix additives are introduced only after aggregates have been uniformly graded, and specifications require verification that the additive does not impair moisture resistance, demonstrating another interaction where gradation precedes supplementary technology application. Across concrete, asphalt, micro‑surfacing, and specialty mixes, gradation remains the controlling factor that links aggregate sizing to binder quantity, water content, filler addition, and supplementary technologies, ultimately shaping strength, durability, and overall pavement performance. Influence of production and placement

Effective control of particle‑size distribution throughout pavement construction hinges on disciplined mixing, placement, compaction, finishing and curing operations. At the mixing stage the aggregate stockpile is screened daily; a sieve analysis performed before production starts and each time bins are restocked ensures that the cumulative percent‑retained curve remains within the limits prescribed in PTM No. 529, with an allowable deviation of up to five percent. When the measured gradation falls outside these bounds, the proportions of the various size fractions are adjusted on‑site so that the combined blend conforms to the job‑mix formula. Uniform blending is achieved by adding a predetermined quantity of water and maintaining optimum moisture within two percentage points of the target set by PTM No. 106, Method B. The mixing equipment may include a cutting drum capable of variable speeds to reduce oversized particles to a maximum size of two inches, while an integral liquid‑proportioning system regulates water and additive delivery relative to cut depth, width and speed. For reclaimed asphalt pavement the material is first trimmed until at least ninety‑five percent passes a two‑inch sieve; it is then combined with emulsified or foamed asphalt at the mix‑design moisture and binder contents, and any field deviations in RAP gradation are corrected according to the quality‑control plan. Stabilizers are introduced in the weigh hopper and both dry‑ and wet‑mixing times are extended as needed; clumps of fibers or pellets trigger additional mixing intensity, and three loose samples are taken at the plant for verification of asphalt content, gradation and draindown.

Placement must preserve the freshly blended gradation. Materials are shipped promptly—typically within two hours—and spread on dry surfaces under temperature conditions that prevent cooling below the minimum required; any material that is already segregated, below temperature, or has an improper asphalt content is excluded from the mat. Motor graders or other approved spreading equipment lay the mix uniformly, and placement is avoided when ambient air temperature is expected to drop below forty degrees Fahrenheit during the subsequent seven‑day cure period (or below thirty‑five degrees for cement‑slurry mixes with accelerators). Temperature control is also observed for concrete placements exceeding eighty degrees Fahrenheit, although this step does not directly affect gradation.

Compaction locks the intended particle packing in place. The selection of rollers follows depth criteria: a pneumatic‑tire roller of twenty‑five tons or greater provides breakdown and intermediate rolling for mixes less than eight inches deep; deeper layers receive an initial pass with a vibratory padfoot roller of comparable weight, followed by finish rolling with single or tandem steel‑drum static rollers weighing twelve to fourteen tons. Compaction is performed at speeds, amplitudes and patterns that avoid displacement, shoving, cracking or aggregate breakage; excessive traffic, overly aggressive rollers, or direct roller contact on the fine‑graded aggregate layer are prohibited because they can crush particles and alter sieve proportions, reducing strength and durability. Minimal handling of the fill material further protects the gradation from physical damage.

Finishing operations focus on alignment and moisture protection rather than altering gradation, while curing practices emphasize temperature stability to allow the stabilized gradation to develop its full strength. Materials must not be exposed to temperatures below forty degrees Fahrenheit during the seven‑day cure period, ensuring that the achieved density and particle distribution remain intact.

When any deviation in gradation is detected—such as a pattern segregation that produces texture depth differences greater than 0.024 inch or absolute sieve‑size deviations totaling twenty percent from the job‑mix formula—the affected lane must be removed and replaced. By rigorously controlling mixing proportions, moisture content, placement temperature, compaction methodology and curing conditions, the intended gradation is preserved, delivering the strength, durability and performance required of a high‑quality pavement. Using aggregates that are poorly graded or gap‑graded—where one size dominates or a range of sizes is missing—reduces dense compaction, lowers dry unit weight and CBR values, and therefore degrades the designed gradation over time [1]. An excess of fine material, reflected by a high percent passing the No. 200 sieve, makes the mix more moisture‑sensitive, reduces CBR, increases susceptibility to frost heave, and erodes the intended gradation performance [1]. Selection, specification, and improvement

The selection of an aggregate gradation begins with the functional demands of the pavement layer; base and sub‑base materials are required to be highly permeable so that gravity‑drainable water can be removed rapidly, yet they must also retain sufficient stability for construction equipment, which may necessitate a compromise between ideal permeability and workability [1]. For thin surface treatments such as an unbound granular wearing course, the nominal maximum aggregate size determines the available gradation type—Type A (6.3 mm), Type B (9.5 mm) or Type C (12.5 mm)—and also limits the lift thickness to roughly one‑to‑one‑and‑a‑half times the diameter of the coarsest aggregate, resulting in a half‑inch layer for Type A and three‑quarters inch for Types B and C [1]. Dense‑graded crumb‑rubber modified asphalt mixtures must employ a fine gradation (typically #30 mesh or smaller) to fit within the limited void space of the aggregate skeleton while using a low crumb‑rubber content to maintain lower binder viscosity, thereby meeting the physical requirements of the AASHTO M 320 specification [1]. All aggregates used in pavement mixes must be sourced from suppliers listed in Bulletin 14 and satisfy the Superpave quality provisions of Bulletin 27; acceptable materials include durable bank, crushed gravel, stone, slag or suitable blends with clean mineral filler, and they must be free of organic matter, lumps, excessive clay, foreign substances and contain no more than ten percent deleterious shale by weight [2]. The coarse‑aggregate type is selected to match the project’s nominal maximum aggregate size (NMAS) and serviceability rating level (SRL), with typical selections being Type A or AASHTO No. 8 for most highways and No. 89 permitted on low‑traffic roads, each required to meet the gradation limits shown in Table A [2]. Sieve‑size limits are imposed across specifications: 100 % of material must pass a 50 mm (2‑inch) sieve; between fifteen and sixty percent must pass the 4.75 mm (No. 4) sieve; no more than thirty percent may pass a 150 µm sieve; for mixes limited to a No. 10 aggregate size, the maximum particle dimension may not exceed two inches, no more than twenty percent may pass the No. 100 sieve, and the dry rodded density must be ninety pounds per cubic foot or less [2]. The shape of the particle‑size distribution is quantified by the coefficient of uniformity (C_u) and the coefficient of curvature (C_c); for gravels a C_u of at least four and for sands a C_u of at least six are required, with both material groups needing a C_c between one and three to be classified as well graded, while performance‑related acceptance criteria call for an average C_u of at least four point zero across the lot and no individual sample falling below three point five [1][2]. Fine‑aggregate quality is further controlled through fineness‑modulus consistency—at least nine of ten consecutive samples from a source must vary no more than ±0.20 from their average—and through an uncompacted void content of at least forty‑five percent; sand‑equivalent values, Los Angeles abrasion loss (capped at fifty‑five percent), flat or elongated particle limits (maximum ten percent for a 1:5 shape ratio or twenty percent for a 1:3 ratio), bulk density (70–110 lb/ft³ for anti‑skid materials), iron content (≤1 % by weight) and mineral filler grading per AASHTO M‑17 are also mandated [2]. The final blended gradation must satisfy cumulative percent‑retained charts in PTM 529 for concrete and the extreme limits of Table A for asphalt, with a single‑sieve deviation of up to five percent permissible only when authorized by the district material engineer or manager; otherwise the percent‑within‑limits (PWL) for particle‑size distribution must be ninety percent or greater before placement, and absolute deviations between any two sieves may not exceed twenty percent of the job‑mix formula values [2]. When crumb‑rubber is incorporated, an ASTM D5461 sieve analysis on 200‑mm sieves must show a maximum loss of 7.65 percent; mineral filler must conform to AASHTO M‑17 grading requirements, and any use of reclaimed asphalt pavement or shingles requires gradation determination from the finished mix or stockpile in accordance with Bulletin 27 [2]. Acceptance is confirmed only after material certifications demonstrate compliance with asphalt content, gradation, air‑voids and other volumetric requirements, written approval from the district material engineer/manager has been obtained, blended aggregates are limited to an equal‑weight mixture of two approved sources mixed by an approved method, and observable runoff of excess asphalt from coated aggregate is absent [2]. Finally, gradation data are essential for proper soil classification and for developing engineering properties such as the California Bearing Ratio (CBR) test, which requires a documented particle‑size distribution; therefore the selected gradation must satisfy classification criteria and provide the information needed to conduct CBR evaluation [1].

Improving the gradation of pavement materials is achieved through a combination of mechanical, chemical, and binder‑related modifications together with rigorous quality‑control programs. Mechanical stabilization of reclaimed pavement layers is accomplished by pulverizing the existing surface and adding clean aggregate or recycled‑asphalt pavement (RAP) to fill voids in the particle‑size distribution, thereby producing a more uniform gradation; RAP must constitute at least five percent of the mixture, be screened so that ninety‑five percent passes a two‑inch sieve, and any variation is corrected on site according to the approved quality‑control plan, while shredded reclaimed shingles are processed until all particles pass a 9.5 mm sieve and blended with fine aggregate on a fifty‑fifty weight basis to prevent agglomeration [1][2]. Chemical stabilization employs wet or dry additives such as Portland cement, lime, or fly ash, which raise strength provided that laboratory mix designs confirm brittleness does not become excessive; mineral fillers including non‑air‑entrained Type 1 Portland cement, cement dust, and stone‑crush fines free of clay are also permitted, and stabilizing fibers—mineral or cellulose—are added at 0.2 to 0.4 percent of the total mix to improve cohesion [1][2]. Crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder (CRMAB) with a lower rubber content and fine aggregate #30 mesh or finer supplies the low‑viscosity, small‑particle binder needed for dense‑graded mixes, and must satisfy AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) specifications [1][2]. Warm‑mix technologies, anti‑strip agents, and hydrated lime (minimum 0.25 percent of the asphalt weight for pervious mixes) are further approved additives that reduce draindown, enhance moisture resistance, and mitigate segregation [2]. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane is overlaid with hot‑mix concrete using one of three prescribed gradations—Type A (maximum 6.3 mm), Type B (9.5 mm) or Type C (12.5 mm)—and the hot‑mix layer is placed in a single lift whose final thickness equals one to one‑point‑five times the diameter of the coarsest aggregate, which seals the surface, limits oxidation and reduces raveling or moderate rutting [1]. On the granular side the guides require well‑graded aggregates or subgrade soils with a coefficient of uniformity (Cu) of at least four for gravels (six for sands) and a curvature (Cc) between one and three; limiting the percentage passing the #200 sieve controls fines content, thereby reducing moisture sensitivity and frost‑heave potential, while laboratory gradation, CBR, and resilient‑modulus tests verify compliance before construction [1][2]. Alternative aggregate selections must meet bulk‑density limits of 70 to 110 lb/ft³, contain at least thirty‑five percent crushed fragments on the No. 8 (2.36 mm) sieve, limit metallic iron to one percent by weight, and exhibit Los Angeles abrasion loss not exceeding fifty‑five percent; granulated blast‑furnace slag is acceptable provided its dry rodded density does not exceed ninety pounds per cubic foot, its maximum particle size is two inches, and no more than twenty percent passes the No. 100 sieve [2]. Fine aggregates used to achieve a required SRL designation may be blended in equal weight if the resulting SRL meets or exceeds the specification, and mineral filler must have a plasticity index of four or less and conform to AASHTO M 17 grading [2]. Subgrade modification further enhances gradation stability: selective grading, blending, mixing of native soils, and removal of boulders create a more uniform particle distribution; cement or lime stabilization raises the CBR and resilient modulus of weak layers, while rock capping with high‑CBR rock (modeled at CBR = 20) and placement of geogrids at the subgrade‑aggregate interface provide additional stiffness, and class‑4 geotextiles act as separators to preserve gradation during placement [1][2]. Plant‑stockpiled aggregate must be subjected to daily sieve analysis before production begins and each time bins are restocked; the resulting combined percent‑retained chart is compared with limits in PTM 529, and on‑site adjustments to size fractions are made when drift occurs, with deviations of up to five percent on a single sieve permissible only after written approval and documentation in the QC plan [2]. A Penn‑DOT Certified Aggregate Technician oversees testing, ensuring that at least nine of ten consecutive fine‑aggregate samples remain within ±0.20 of the average fineness modulus, and any aggregate blends require written district approval [2]. Moisture content is monitored at least twice daily and water is added only to achieve optimum moisture content at compaction; lightweight aggregates are stored in a stable saturated‑surface‑dry condition, all sizes are kept on separate cement‑concrete bases protected from earth or foreign material, and covered RAP stockpiles must be used within three months (uncovered within one month), with clumps or oversized particles retained on the 12.5 mm sieve triggering reprocessing [2]. Additional laboratory checks include organic‑impurity screening, sodium‑sulfate soundness, specific gravity, absorption, sand equivalent (minimum 45 %), uncompacted void content per AASHTO T304, and a coefficient of uniformity with an average minimum of four and no individual sample below three‑point‑five; lots failing these criteria are rejected and fines removed by washing [2]. Production mixtures are certified daily only when all test results satisfy the Job Mix Formula tolerances in Table A, including percent passing the 2.36 mm and 75 µm sieves, and IA samples taken at the plant together with on‑site QA samples trigger immediate corrective actions, possible suspension of certification, and repeat verification before production resumes if non‑conformance is detected [2]. Documentation of these controls requires a project‑specific QC plan submitted at the start of work and reviewed annually; the Job Mix Formula must be signed by a certified Level 2 plant technician, list material sources, calibration factors for asphalt content and the 75 µm sieve, any warm‑mix additive dosage, and be filed with the District Maintenance Engineer/Manager at least three weeks before production begins, while any change in source or blend demands written approval and an updated JMF prior to use [2]. Plant‑level modifications support the incorporation of additives: proportioning devices interlocked with the control system maintain fiber weight within ±10 % of the required amount, and warm‑mix technologies are introduced only after documented plant modifications approved by a technical representative; the mixing plant should be fully automated, keep aggregates and the finished mix free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture, and ship material to the job site within two hours of mixing to avoid segregation, while a certified asphalt field technician oversees placement temperature, mixing, and compaction adjustments that prevent flushing or segregation, thereby preserving the intended particle distribution throughout the pavement [2][1].

Performance evaluation and implications

Evaluation of gradation is based primarily on laboratory sieve analysis that determines the particle‑size distribution curve for subgrade, subbase or aggregate material [1][2]. The standard test follows ASTM D2487 for soils and a suite of state‑specific PTM methods (e.g., PTM 757, PTM 702, PTM 739 for hot‑mix asphalt; PTM 743 for combined aggregate; PTM 616 and PTM 529 for concrete aggregate; PTM 501 for fine‑aggregate fineness modulus; ASTM D5461 or FM 5‑559 for crumb rubber; AASHTO T 88 for mineral filler) for aggregates, as described in the guides [1][2]. From the sieve results the D10, D30 and D60 values are extracted to compute the coefficient of uniformity (C_u) and curvature (C_c), which classify material as well‑graded or poorly‑graded and relate to compaction density and CBR performance [1]. In addition, each gradation test yields a coefficient of uniformity that must meet minimum average values of at least 4.0 for open‑graded stone (OGS) material and no individual sample below 3.5, as required by the specifications [2]. For Type A fine aggregate the fineness modulus of at least nine out of ten consecutive samples must remain within ±0.20 of the average of those samples, providing a tighter control on fine‑aggregate grading [2]. Crumb‑rubber aggregates are limited to a maximum sieve loss of 7.65 % as determined by ASTM D5461 or FM 5‑559 testing [2]. Concrete aggregate must pass 100 % of the material through a 50 mm (2‑inch) sieve, retain between 15 % and 60 % on a No. 4 (4.75 mm) sieve, and have no more than 30 % passing a No. 100 (150 µm) sieve; these limits are verified by the PTM 616/529 procedures [2]. Additional laboratory indices such as uncompacted void content (AASHTO T 304), sand equivalent (AASHTO T 176), maximum abrasion (Bulletin 27 Table 6A) and thin or elongated pieces (AASHTO M 323) are also recorded to support the gradation evaluation [2]. Field verification mirrors the laboratory program by sampling the finished hot‑mix, drum‑plant belt or combined aggregate from stockpiles in accordance with PTM 1 and the producer’s quality‑control plan; when daily production exceeds fifty tons a gradation measurement is required at least once per day, and asphalt plants must conduct at least two daily analyses [2]. For concrete production the plant sieves the aggregate before each shift and whenever bins are restocked, generating a cumulative percent‑retained chart that is compared with PTM 529 limits; a design‑mix exception permits up to five percent deviation from the required retained percentages [2]. Visual inspections of stockpiles and truck loads are performed prior to shipment to identify clumps or oversized particles, and material retained on a 12.5 mm sieve is reprocessed until at least ninety‑five percent passes a two‑inch screen [2]. In‑plant (IA) samples are taken in the presence of agency personnel and tested for asphalt content and gradation according to the QC plan, while quality‑assurance (QA) samples may be collected from the plant or directly from uncompacted pavement and analyzed per PTM 757 or PTM 702 (Modified Method D) when problematic aggregates are present [2]. Cores extracted from placed pavement are returned to the plant for density, asphalt‑content and gradation analysis, providing an additional field check on the aggregate grading after placement [2]. The guides do not prescribe any separate long‑term performance monitoring program for gradation; instead, agencies treat the laboratory particle‑size distribution as an indirect field indicator of drainage capability and rely on the compiled laboratory and field data to infer long‑term behavior [1][2]. Consequently, evidence of long‑term performance is limited to the initial laboratory characterization and ongoing compliance checks rather than observed service‑life monitoring or aging studies [1][2].

Gradation, expressed as the particle‑size distribution of aggregates or soils, governs a range of behaviors that are central to pavement performance and longevity across both flexible and rigid systems [1][2]. In base and subbase layers a gradation that creates larger inter‑particle voids yields high permeability, allowing gravity‑drainable water to escape quickly and preventing capillary rise; the resulting reduction in moisture content mitigates loss of subgrade support, frost heave, rutting and ride‑quality degradation, thereby extending service life even though drainage is not guaranteed by gradation alone [1][2]. Well‑graded subgrade soils compact to higher dry unit weights and achieve higher California Bearing Ratio values, which raise the resilient modulus used in structural design, improve elastic support and load distribution, and permit less aggressive improvement measures that lower construction effort and future maintenance needs [1]. In rigid‑pavement concrete production the guides require daily sieve analysis of stockpiled aggregate before each shift and whenever bins are restocked, with the cumulative percent‑retained curve constrained by PTM 529; deviations greater than five per cent trigger on‑site proportion adjustments and a new mix design, while minor departures may be permitted case‑by‑case, ensuring that the aggregate skeleton can attain design densities, uniform cementitious coating and long‑term durability [2]. For asphalt mixtures the prescribed gradation is verified at each batch by percent‑passing tests on specified sieves (e.g., 2.36 mm and No. 200), and any failure to meet tolerance limits results in non‑certification, immediate corrective actions and possible suspension of plant approval; maintaining this gradation prevents pattern segregation—defined as a cumulative deviation of twenty per cent or more between any two sieves—which would otherwise reduce macro‑texture, core density and overall strength, rendering the pavement defective and requiring removal [2]. When reclaimed asphalt pavement is incorporated, field adjustments outlined in the quality‑control plan are required to achieve the target gradation and ensure satisfactory coating and compaction, while oversized concrete aggregate must be reduced until at least ninety‑five per cent passes a 2‑inch sieve [2]. Handling practices that crush fine‑grained aggregate alter the size distribution and compromise engineered strength and durability, so specifications call for minimal handling and compaction methods that avoid crushing, preserving the intended performance throughout service life [2]. Ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses rely on gradation to define nominal maximum aggregate sizes—6.3 mm for Type A, 9.5 mm for Type B and 12.5 mm for Type C—and consequently dictate layer thicknesses of approximately one‑half inch for Type A and three‑quarters inch for Types B and C (generally 1 to 1.5 times the largest aggregate diameter); a properly graded UTWC forms a continuous seal that limits oxidation, weathering, fatigue cracking, surface raveling and moderate rutting [1]. Dense‑graded asphalt mixtures employ a wide spectrum of particle sizes to create a tightly packed skeleton with minimal void space, improving load distribution and resistance to deformation; when the binder is crumb‑rubber modified asphalt formulated with finer particles (#30 mesh or smaller) and lower viscosity, it can be incorporated into this limited‑void matrix without compromising density, delivering performance comparable to polymer‑modified binders while reducing maintenance requirements [1]. Full‑depth reclamation uses mechanical stabilization—pulverizing, reshaping, recompacting and adding aggregate or RAP to improve gradation—to enhance structural integrity; the improved gradation permits additional volume for raising elevation or widening without sacrificing layer thickness, thereby increasing load‑bearing capacity and long‑term durability [1]. Gradation testing during reconstruction projects establishes the particle‑size distribution of existing subgrade or base materials, enabling accurate soil classification and determination of engineering properties such as CBR; because CBR feeds directly into resilient modulus and structural coefficients, precise gradation data influence pavement thickness and composition, whereas inaccurate or unknown gradation can lead to mis‑estimated strength, under‑ or over‑engineered designs, premature deterioration and higher maintenance costs [1]. Consequently, maintaining specified gradations through continuous monitoring, rigorous testing and careful handling is essential for achieving the intended structural performance, extending service life, optimizing construction effort and minimizing future maintenance across both concrete and asphalt pavements [1][2].
