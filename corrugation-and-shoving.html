Corrugation and Shoving

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Corrugation and Shoving is a topic under Distress.
corrugationscorrugationshoving
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Corrugation and shoving are surface‑level distresses that appear as minor ridges, grooves or slippage on pavement surfaces, with an average depth less than 1 ½ inches and must remain confined to the surface to be considered acceptable [1]. They can occur on full‑depth asphalt pavements and on the asphalt layer of composite pavements provided the underlying concrete joints are stable, and they are reported only on asphalt pavement surfaces in the Pennsylvania specifications [1][2]. Shoving develops when the SMA mixture is displaced during compaction because roller speed, pattern or size are not appropriate, while the resulting ridges from either distress degrade smoothness and vehicle comfort, leading profile‑milling requirements to mandate removal of bumps, corrugations and wheel ruts and a tolerance of 1/8 inch per 12 ft (Section 492.3(b); Section 492.3(d)) [2].

Description and identification

Corrugation and shoving are surface‑level distresses that may be present on pavements considered for hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) treatment; they appear as minor corrugation, shoving or slippage with an average depth less than 1 ½ inches. Such distresses are permissible for HIR candidate pavements provided the average depth stays under that threshold. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133)

Corrugation and shoving are identified as minor surface ridges or grooves whose average depth is less than 1 ½ inches, and they are considered acceptable only when the distress remains on the pavement surface. They are distinguished from longitudinal rutting, which is limited to a severity of less than 1 ½ inches but appears as depressions rather than ridges, and from transverse or miscellaneous cracking that must be shallower than 1.0 inch with crack widths not exceeding ½ inch; the requirement that distresses be surface‑only together with these depth limits provides the criteria used to separate corrugation/shoving from other pavement failures. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133)

Where it occurs

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, corrugation and shoving may appear on the surface of pavements that are candidates for Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) treatment; this includes full‑depth asphalt surfaces as well as the asphalt layer of a composite pavement, provided the average depth is <1 1/2 inches. The same source adds that when placed on a composite pavement, the underlying concrete must be stable with non‑movement at the joints. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that corrugation is reported only on asphalt pavement surfaces and that profile milling operations must remove bumps, corrugations and wheel ruts from the milled area, with milling cutters required to produce a surface free of grooves, ridges or other irregularities. Regarding shoving, the Pennsylvania specifications identify it on several asphalt‑based layers: for an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base Course rolling must be performed as soon as the mat has cooled sufficiently in order to avoid shoving or lateral movement; plant‑mixed 6.3 mm thin asphalt overlay courses must eliminate shoving by using the proper rolling pattern, roller speed, pattern and size as required; Stone Matrix Asphalt mixtures also require elimination of shoving during compaction, which applies to both the asphalt pavement surface and any paved shoulders constructed with the same material; and flexible (asphalt) pavements are subject to shoving during compaction and must be protected by adjusting roller speed, amplitude, frequency, pattern and size in accordance with the specifications. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 234–236), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 250–350)

Corrugation is treated as a surface‑level irregularity; the profile‑milling specifications require removal of bumps, corrugations and wheel ruts from the pavement surface to achieve the required smoothness. The source does not mention any other locations such as joints, lifts, layer interfaces, or subgrade. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 349–350)

Causes and mechanisms

The excerpt only mentions shoving, noting that it can arise when the SMA mixture is not compacted under proper conditions; it advises eliminating shoving by adjusting roller speed, pattern, size, and (if approved) amplitude or frequency as required in Section 419.3(f). Corrugation is not discussed, and no table classifying distresses by cause appears in the source. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 297–301)

Shoving originates from displacement of the SMA mixture during rolling, so it appears when compaction causes the material to be pushed rather than uniformly densified; proper compaction is required to eliminate this distress. The condition is aggravated by using roller speed, pattern, or roller size that are not appropriate, or by compacting the mix when it is not in the proper condition, which together accelerate shoving development. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 297–301)

Severity levels and rating

For HIR asphalt candidate pavements the source permits only minor corrugation and shoving, defined by an average depth <1 1/2 inches; no higher severity levels or tabular classifications for these distresses are provided. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133)

In the Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) guidelines of Chapter 5, the severity rating for corrugation and shoving is taken from the Pub. 336 criteria used to evaluate candidate pavements; under that system a condition described as “minor corrugation, shoving, and slippage” is acceptable only when its average depth is less than 1 ½ inches. This rating applies during project selection for HIR asphalt where distresses must be limited to the surface and meet the Pub. 336 severity limits. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133)

Measurement, survey and extent

Corrugation and shoving are quantified by the surface profile smoothness, expressed as deviation in inches over a 12‑foot length; the required tolerance is a smoothness of 1/8 inch in 12 feet. The measurement is performed with a self‑propelled milling machine that includes a built‑in automatic grade control system and mounted profiler cutters, which monitor and adjust the longitudinal profile and transverse cross‑slope while the machine operates in automated mode to remove bumps, corrugations, and wheel ruts. After each pass the milled surface is checked against the 1/8 inch per 12 feet tolerance using the equipment’s grade control feedback. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 349–350)

Effects on performance

Corrugation creates ridges that degrade the smoothness of the riding surface, making vehicle travel less comfortable and classified as an irregularity detrimental to the safe operation of traffic; consequently profile milling must remove bumps, corrugations and wheel ruts (Section 492.3(b)) and produce a surface meeting the 1/8‑inch in 12‑ft smoothness tolerance (Section 492.3(d)) to restore ride quality and safety. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 349–350)

Repairs and treatments

Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) asphalt is identified as a treatment for corrugation and shoving when the pavement shows only minor corrugation, shoving, or slippage with an average depth less than 1 ½ inches, provided the distress is confined to the surface and all other HIR candidate criteria are met; this requirement appears in Chapter 5, Subsection 5.19 of the Pavement Policy Manual [1]. For asphalt‑treated permeable base courses (ATPBC), shoving is mitigated by rolling the mat as soon as it has cooled sufficiently, a step required in Section 360.3(i) of the Pennsylvania DOT specifications [2]. Corrugation‑type surface irregularities on ATPBC are corrected when the deviation exceeds ½ inch; the surface mix must be loosened and the ATPBC either removed or added using a method that does not generate contaminating fines or damage the base, and grinding or milling is expressly prohibited, as set out in Section 360.3(l) [2]. For conventional asphalt pavements, profile milling is prescribed as the repair for both corrugation and shoving; the specification mandates removal of bumps, corrugations, wheel ruts and other irregularities before establishing the required elevation or cross‑slope, with milling cutters designed, maintained and operated to produce a surface free of grooves, ridges, gouges or other detrimental features, and this treatment is required whenever such irregularities are present under Section 492.3(b) [2]. The milling operation must be performed in automated mode, traffic may not be allowed on the milled surface for more than six calendar days, and a new overlay must be placed within seven calendar days of the start of milling, as required by Sections 492.3(b) and related timing provisions [2]. In thin 6.3‑mm Superpave overlays, shoving is eliminated by adjusting roller speed, pattern, size and, when vibratory rolling is approved, amplitude and frequency in accordance with Section 412.3(f), and any loose, broken or improperly mixed areas must be removed and replaced with fresh hot paving mixture before compacting to match the surrounding surface as required by the Compaction subsection (i) of Section 412.3 [2]. For Stone Matrix Asphalt layers, shoving is addressed during compaction by adjusting roller speed, pattern, size or amplitude/frequency so that displacement, shoving, cracking and aggregate breakage are eliminated while meeting density‑acceptance requirements, as stipulated in Section 419.3(f) [2]. More generally, the Superpave construction specifications require that the mixture be compacted to meet density acceptance requirements and that all roller marks be eliminated by adjusting roller speed, amplitude, frequency, pattern and size during the compaction operation described in Section 413(i), thereby providing a means to prevent shoving across applicable pavement types [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 234–236), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 250–350)

According to the Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) asphalt guidelines in Chapter 5, HIR can be used when corrugation or shoving is only minor and the average depth of those features is less than 1 ½ inches; deeper or more severe corrugation/shoving would disqualify the pavement from an HIR treatment. This condition is stated as part of the selection criteria for standalone HIR projects. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133)

Prevention in design and construction

The specification for asphalt‑treated permeable base courses (Section 360.3) requires that rolling be performed as soon as the mat has cooled sufficiently so that shoving or lateral movement is avoided; this is achieved by seating the material with an 8‑ton to 10‑ton steel‑wheeled roller or a vibratory roller operated in static mode and applying four roller passes, and it applies only after the mat has reached the required cooling condition. The compaction requirements for thin asphalt overlay courses (Section 412.3) limit shoving by mandating adjustment of roller speed, pattern and size—and, when vibratory rolling is approved by the Representative, amplitude and frequency—to eliminate displacement and shoving while compacting the 6.33‑mm paving mixture under the compaction clause of Section 412.3(i); steel‑wheeled rollers of at least 10 tons must be used and pneumatic tire rollers are prohibited. The stone matrix asphalt construction provisions (Section 419.3) similarly call for adjustment of roller speed, pattern and size—and, if vibratory rolling is approved by the Representative, amplitude and frequency—to eliminate displacement, shoving, cracking and aggregate breakage while the mixture is in proper condition, applying during SMA placement compaction. The standard and RPS construction provisions for Superpave mixtures (Section 413.3(h)) require that, during compacting, the contractor adjust roller speed, amplitude, frequency, pattern and size to eliminate shoving (along with displacement, cracking and aggregate breakage) while meeting density acceptance requirements and removing all roller marks; this applies under the compaction clause of Section 413.3(h)(i). The profile‑milling provisions (Section 492.3) designate profile milling—using a self‑propelled milling machine equipped with an automatic longitudinal grade control system and appropriate transverse cross‑slope controls—as a design measure to prevent or reduce corrugation by removing existing ridges, grooves, bumps and wheel ruts and producing a surface free from such irregularities; this measure applies under the profiling operation requirements of Section 492.3. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 234–236), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 250–350)

The construction guidelines specify that shoving can be avoided by performing compaction under particular equipment and timing conditions. For asphalt‑treated permeable base courses, the mat must be rolled as soon as it has cooled sufficiently, using an eight‑ to ten‑ton steel‑wheeled roller or a vibratory roller operated only in static mode, and no more than four passes are permitted (Section 360.3(i)). In superpave thin asphalt overlay courses, rollers of at least ten tons must be steel‑wheeled, pneumatic rollers are prohibited, and vibratory rolling is allowed only when it can be demonstrated that aggregate breakage or binder flushing will not occur; the roller speed, pattern, size and, where approved, amplitude and frequency must be adjusted to eliminate displacement and shoving during compaction of the 6.33 mm wearing course (Section 412.3(f,i)). Similar requirements apply to stone matrix asphalt mixtures, where the SMA must be compacted while in proper condition and roller speed, pattern, size and, if approved, amplitude/frequency must be set to eliminate displacement and shoving (Section 419.3(f)). For standard superpave plant‑mixed courses, compaction must achieve density acceptance, remove all roller marks, and the paver must adjust roller speed, amplitude, frequency, pattern and size specifically to prevent displacement and shoving while the mixture is being compacted in proper condition (Section 413.3(h) subsection (i)). Corrugation is addressed through profile milling specifications that require milling cutters to be designed, maintained and operated so that the milled surface contains no grooves, ridges or other irregularities; the profiling operation must remove existing bumps, corrugations and wheel ruts by running in automated mode for one or more passes, and the finished surface must meet a smoothness tolerance of 1/8 inch per 12 feet (Section 492.3(a,b,d)). [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 234–236), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 250–350)

Specification limits and acceptance

Within the Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) asphalt guidelines, only minor corrugation and shoving are permitted provided that the average depth does not exceed 1 ½ inches; this depth is determined by averaging measurements taken across the pavement surface and the requirement appears in Chapter 5, Section 5.19 of the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) for candidate‑project HIR mixtures [1]. In the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation profile‑milling specifications, corrugations must be eliminated during the milling operation and the finished surface must meet a smoothness tolerance of 1/8 inch over any 12‑foot longitudinal segment; compliance is judged by measuring the longitudinal profile across the milled lane and any deviation greater than that limit constitutes defective work, as set out in Section 492.3(d) of Specification 408/2026 (Section 492—Profile Milling of Asphalt Pavement Surfaces) [2]. The milling specification does not provide a separate acceptance limit for shoving [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 349–350)

When corrugation reaches the specified limit, the specification calls for profile milling to remove the ridges and grooves; the contractor must use a self‑propelled milling machine equipped with automatic grade control to perform the work. The milled surface may not be opened to traffic for more than 6 calendar days, and the first asphalt overlay must be placed within 7 calendar days from the start of the milling operation. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 349–350)
