Pavement Design

Part of: PennDOT Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242) is-subtopic-of

Pavement design is the engineering process that creates a structural roadway surface capable of safely supporting projected traffic loads while resisting environmental and service‑related degradation throughout its intended service life. It does so by quantifying total equivalent single axle loads, selecting appropriate layer or slab thicknesses and material properties—such as concrete compressive strength, asphalt mix density, joint geometry, and drainage provisions—to achieve a required design structural number that exceeds the calculated demand, thereby providing uniform support, reliable load transfer, and durability against moisture, frost‑heave, and wear [1][2]. The resulting design also targets performance criteria including surface friction, resistance to rutting, cracking, raveling and potholes, as well as a terminal serviceability index around 3.0, while integrating economic optimization to deliver a safe, durable, and cost‑effective roadway [1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Pavement Design” also include the following subtopics: 18-kip ESAL, AASHTO 1993 Design Guide, Cross-Slope, DARWin / AASHTOWare, Design Exception, Design Life, Flexible Pavement, Flexible Pavement Design, Frost Heave, Functional Classification, Mix Design, Pavement Base Drain, Pavement Design Submission, Pavement Performance, Pavement Structure, Pavement Thickness, Pavement Type Selection, Pavement Widening, Rigid Pavement, Rigid Pavement Design, Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M), Service Life, Serviceability, Subsurface Drainage, Traffic Loading, Truck Factor, and Typical Section.

Pavement Design utilizes the 18-kip ESAL and Truck Factor as primary traffic loading inputs to determine structural capacity for both Flexible Pavement and Rigid Pavement systems. [1][2] The AASHTO 1993 Design Guide and DARWin / AASHTOWare software execute these calculations, incorporating Functional Classification and Serviceability targets alongside Mix Design specifications to define the final Pavement Structure and Pavement Thickness. [1][2] This process is constrained by Drainage requirements including Cross-Slope, Subsurface Drainage, and Pavement Base Drain, while managing environmental risks like Frost Heave through specific Design Life parameters. [1][2] The resulting configuration undergoes Pavement Type Selection and Pavement Performance evaluation before formal submission as a Pavement Design Submission, with Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) governing the implementation of Typical Section details and any necessary Pavement Widening or Design Exception adjustments. [1][2] Flexible Pavement Design and Rigid Pavement Design apply mechanistic-empirical methods using DARWin / AASHTOWare software to determine layer thicknesses that meet the required Service Life. [1][2]

Purpose and design objectives

Pavement design is fundamentally intended to produce a structural surface capable of safely sustaining the anticipated traffic loads while resisting environmental and service‑related degradation throughout its intended service life. [1][2] The first step is quantifying the total 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) expected over the performance period, using average daily traffic, truck percentages, growth rates, and applying a rigorous ESAL calculation method when the design‑year ADT exceeds 8 000, to establish the required Design Structural Number. [1] Layer thicknesses are then selected—typically ranging from four to eighteen inches but constrained by minimum and maximum depth limits—so that the calculated structural number exceeds the required value, providing uniform support for overlays and ensuring adequate load‑transfer at transverse joints in concrete sections. [1] For rigid concrete pavements the slab thickness must be placed within a quarter inch of the design depth, vertical differentials across any joint are limited to one‑quarter inch, and transverse joint widths are kept between one‑quarter and one‑half inch, with dowel bars at transverse joints and tie bars along longitudinal joints establishing reliable load transfer. [2] The concrete mix must achieve high strength, with a minimum compressive strength of 4 925 psi (early‑age requirements of at least 3 500 psi at 28 days and 3 000 psi by three to seven days) and a flexural strength of 650 psi, while being placed to obtain an average density of no less than 98 % of the maximum wet density and no individual test below 96 %. [2] Durability criteria include an air content of about 6 % in the plastic state, shrinkage limited to 500 µε (550 µε with approval), and low or negligible chloride ion penetration within 56 days as defined by AASHTO test methods. [2] Sub‑base and cement‑treated bedding layers must develop early strength of at least 500 psi before traffic opening, with grout placed in dowel slots required to reach a minimum compressive strength of 2 500 psi prior to slab service; bedding thickness is limited to one‑half inch for fine material and two inches overall. [2] When thin overlays are needed, macro‑fibers are added to meet prescribed residual strength values and coarse aggregate No. 67 replaces No. 57 for overlay depths under three inches. [2] Asphalt concrete designs address the same performance need by ensuring that the constructed pavement meets warranty criteria regardless of mix submission, using Superpave base courses or pervious mixes that provide sufficient structural capacity and durability for expected loads and climate exposure; pervious mixes incorporate anti‑strip additives or hydrated lime, require at least 90 % aggregate coating verified by ASTM D3625 testing, and control air voids through precise gradation and bulk specific gravity methods to protect against moisture‑induced stripping. [2] Adequate drainage is essential; surface and subsurface water must be removed and any failed base material replaced with structurally adequate fill to prevent loss of material integrity and weakening of capacity. [1] Seasonal variations in subgrade support are accounted for by adjusting the effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k) using values of 15 000 psi for spring‑thaw, 30 000 psi for normal conditions, and 50 000 psi for frozen conditions, with loss‑of‑support factors correcting k for potential erosion or pumping. [1] Concrete joint performance is further safeguarded by specifying fusion‑bonded epoxy‑coated dowel bars with a minimum non‑abraded thickness of 8 mil and limiting absolute differential deflection to no more than 7.5 mil after one million traffic cycles, with any increase beyond that limited to 2.5 mil at ten million cycles. [2] The design also targets performance criteria such as surface friction, resistance to rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, and overall ride quality, aiming for a terminal serviceability index of approximately 3.0 and incorporating an overall standard deviation to capture variability in traffic forecasts, material properties, and environmental conditions. [1] By integrating these considerations—traffic loading, structural adequacy, drainage, frost‑heave effects, joint integrity, material strength, durability, and economic optimization—the pavement design fulfills the engineering problem of delivering a durable, safe, and cost‑effective roadway that meets reliability targets over its service life. [1][2]

Pavement designs are required to provide effective removal of surface and subsurface water while delivering a level, stable platform capable of supporting the forecast traffic loads. [1] For low‑volume roads with average daily traffic of 2 000 vehicles or less and speed limits not exceeding 55 mph, the selected pavement type and thickness must correspond to those conditions, and highway ramps must employ the same pavement type and identical thickness as the adjoining high‑traffic roadway to ensure smooth merging. [1] In addition, rigid systems must achieve reliable load transfer between lanes and maintain uniform support beneath each precast slab through lane‑to‑lane ties, dowel bars at transverse joints, and longitudinal tie bars placed within tight tolerances. [2] Joint geometry is limited to a transverse width of one‑quarter to one‑half inch along its entire length and a longitudinal width not greater than three‑quarters inch, and surface textures such as brooming, turf‑dragging or tining are prescribed to meet the intended riding‑surface serviceability. [2] Structurally, the design must supply sufficient layer or slab thickness—determined by non‑destructive testing or other accepted procedures—to achieve the required load‑bearing capacity, with rigid concrete pavements typically ranging from four to eighteen inches in depth and a compressive strength between 4 000 and 10 000 psi. [1] Joint spacing of thirty feet for jointed reinforced concrete pavement or fifteen feet for jointed plain concrete pavement must be provided together with coated dowel bars, tie bars when patches exceed sixty‑five feet, and proper seals to ensure effective load transfer at transverse joints, while shoulders furnish lateral support that reduces edge stresses. [1] For precast rigid slabs the concrete mix shall attain a minimum compressive strength of 4 925 psi and a flexural strength of 650 psi, with over‑design strengths of at least 1 000 psi (500 psi for specified low‑strength types) and early‑age targets of 3 000 psi by three days and 3 500 psi by twenty‑eight days. [2] The cement‑treated bedding layer must reach 500 psi before traffic opening, dowel grout and transverse joint grouts must achieve 2 500 psi prior to traffic, and cementitious bedding grout is required to develop from an initial 1 000 psi to 2 500 psi within twelve hours. [2] Density control mandates an average of at least ninety‑eight percent of the maximum wet density for each lot with no individual test falling below ninety‑six percent, and thickness tolerances are limited to a quarter‑inch deviation from the design depth; thin overlays under three inches must employ No. 67 coarse aggregate together with macrofibers sized to meet the specified residual strength (f150). [2] Durability objectives are met by constructing a well‑drained structure that prevents excess moisture from degrading material integrity, selecting high‑strength low‑shrinkage mixes while avoiding high early‑strength concrete, and incorporating base repair, slab stabilization, crack sealing and appropriate overlays to target an approximate twenty‑year service life for roller‑compacted concrete pavements. [1] Concrete mixtures must contain six percent air in the plastic state, limit twenty‑eight‑day shrinkage to five hundred microstrain (or five hundred fifty microstrain with approval), and achieve low, very low or negligible chloride‑ion penetration within fifty‑six days as measured by AASHTO T 277 or T 358, with quantitative caps of 2 800 coulombs on the rapid permeability test or resistivity not exceeding fifteen point six kilohm·cm for four‑by‑eight‑inch cylinders and twelve point three kilohm·cm for six‑by‑twelve‑inch cylinders. [2] All reinforcement steel, dowels and tie bars are to be epoxy‑coated in accordance with ASTM A775 using a minimum non‑abraded fusion‑bonded thickness of eight mils and allowing no more than two holidays per linear foot as detected by a 67.5‑V detector, while joint sealing and slab undersealing further protect joint durability. [2] Safety requirements include achieving the specified skid‑resistance level and improving surface friction, with diamond grinding applied when wheel ruts are shallow or when the exposed surface serves speeds of thirty‑five miles per hour or greater, and maintaining shoulder integrity and a uniform riding surface to reduce hydroplaning risk. [1] Load‑transfer units must restrict joint differential deflection to no more than seven point five mil after one million load cycles and limit any increase to two point five mil after ten million cycles, ensuring structural integrity under repeated traffic loading; dowel bar ends must be smooth and burr‑free, and the overall pavement surface must remain free of hazardous defects. [2] Serviceability is satisfied by meeting both initial and terminal serviceability criteria, with flexible pavements using a terminal serviceability index that incorporates loss due to frost‑heave (for example a value of three point seven five when a loss of zero point seven five is calculated) and employing a recommended frost‑heave probability range of twenty‑five to seventy‑five percent; the prescribed joint geometry, dimensional tolerances, density requirements and surface textures together guarantee a smooth, safe riding experience throughout the pavement’s design life. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

Pavement design is employed for any highway project—whether flexible or rigid—whenever a structural layer must be engineered to satisfy explicit strength, durability and performance requirements, encompassing all functional classifications of highways and including new construction, reconstruction, rehabilitation, overlays, ramps and bridge approaches that exceed the length triggers set by the guides [1][2]. For rigid pavements the methodology covers conventional Portland‑cement concrete systems such as jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP), jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP), AAAP, slip‑form or fixed‑form paving, ASC, lightweight cement concrete and sulfate‑resistant mixes designed in accordance with ACI 211 (or ACI 211.2 for the lightweight variants); these mixes must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi except that AAAP, AAAP LW, AA Paving and ASC are limited to 500 psi and all require an air‑entraining admixture providing about 6 % plastic‑state air (7 % for the slip‑form/fixed‑form pavements) [2][1]. Design is triggered when a continuous pavement length of more than 500 ft on divided highways, more than 1,000 ft on undivided roads, or bridge approaches totaling over 1,000 ft are to be placed, and the same trigger applies to full‑width reconstruction of all lanes or replacement of a single lane extending beyond those distances for both Portland‑cement concrete roadways and shoulders [1][2]. When a lane addition equals ten feet or more on rigid pavements built since 1945, the added lane must be designed to the original slab thickness unless traffic loading justifies a thicker section; partial widenings under ten feet must duplicate the existing pavement type and depth, with JRCP able to be widened using either reinforced or plain cement concrete while JPCP widening is limited to plain cement concrete [1]. Rigid designs on Interstates and expressways are further constrained to an asphalt‑treated or cement‑treated permeable base over a 2A subbase, prohibiting open‑graded subbases, and effective subgrade reaction moduli are taken as 15,000 psi in spring thaw, 30,000 psi under normal conditions and 50,000 psi when frozen, with adjustments for loss‑of‑support factors, subbase thickness and bedrock within ten feet [1]. Precast concrete pavement slabs (PCPS) may be used as equivalents on high‑speed, high‑ADT routes for intermittent patch repairs or continuous installations that incorporate cast‑in load‑transfer features such as dowel bars and lane‑to‑lane ties, provided the existing pavement thickness is verified with cores and suitable subbase and bedding are prepared [1][2]. Roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) is appropriate for low‑volume roads with an average daily traffic of 2,000 vehicles or less and a speed limit of 55 mph or lower; it can serve as a permanent surface, as a shoulder adjacent to reinforced or plain cement concrete, or as a base course beneath an asphalt wearing layer, with design thicknesses ranging from four to eighteen inches and control joints spaced according to slab thickness [1].

For flexible pavements the design methodology governs full‑depth asphalt‑concrete sections constructed with hot‑mix or warm‑mix asphalt that meet Superpave specifications, as well as overlays placed on thin concrete slabs (less than eight inches) or pre‑1945 concrete pavements, even those showing severe alkali‑silica reaction; rehabilitations may incorporate reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP), recycled concrete aggregate and a range of stabilizers such as calcium chloride, cement, lime, fly ash or emulsified asphalt, while the design evaluates drainage, base repair, binder‑course condition, surface friction and shoulder adequacy, selects layer thicknesses according to minimum specification values and requires density targets between roughly 92 % and 98.5 % with at least fifteen percent retainage of milled material [1][2]. The wearing‑course layers are defined by Superpave mix designs using the Gyratory Compactor; binder classes such as PG 64H‑22 or PG 64E‑22 are selected for pervious courses and PG 64S‑22 for underlying binder courses, aggregates must be sourced from approved producers and meet SRL ratings listed in Bulletin 14, Stone Matrix Asphalt mixes are permissible but blended aggregate may not exceed fifty percent by weight, RAP is limited to a maximum of ten percent in pervious mixes and prohibited in the NMAS 6.3 mm wearing course, fly ash cannot be used when projected traffic reaches or exceeds three million ESALs, hydrated lime may be added up to two percent of dry aggregate weight, and anti‑strip additives or hydrated lime must be incorporated at no less than 0.25 % of the asphalt in pervious mixes where moisture susceptibility testing indicates a need; cellulose fiber stabilizers are dosed between 0.2 % and 0.4 % of total mix weight [2]. Micro‑surfacing design is applied when a wearing or surface course is placed with an emulsified asphalt material classified as Class CQS‑1hPM together with a latex‑based polymer modifier that permits traffic reopening within one hour without damage; aggregates must satisfy the gradation and skid‑resistance specifications of the applicable tables, filler may be non‑air‑entrained Type 1 Portland cement or hydrated lime, water must be free of harmful soluble salts, and a certified mix design using the Micro‑Surfacing Mix Design and Materials Analysis Form must be submitted at least two weeks before use along with a quality‑control plan and daily run‑sheet documentation [2].

Full‑depth reclamation (FDR) is appropriate when existing support layers require upgrading rather than merely surface repair; it may be applied across a range of traffic levels provided reliable traffic forecasts are available and standard pavement‑design traffic analysis procedures are followed, with layer thicknesses determined in accordance with design criteria and stabilization achieved by mechanical means or chemical agents such as cement, lime, fly ash, calcium chloride or emulsified asphalt [1]. The design methodology also permits a single DARWin analysis to serve multiple adjacent projects on the same corridor when key parameters—total 18‑kip ESALs, effective subgrade reaction or resilient modulus, initial and terminal serviceability levels, reliability, unit material costs and existing pavement structure—remain essentially unchanged; for Interstates and expressways laboratory‑measured resilient modulus is preferred, while a California Bearing Ratio test may be used as an alternative, with any CBR greater than ten requiring geotechnical engineer recommendation and values below five triggering mandatory subgrade improvement [1]. Frost‑heave effects are incorporated for full‑depth flexible pavements on Interstates by increasing the terminal serviceability index using loss calculations based on frost‑heave rates taken from soil‑type tables with a probability range of 25 % to 75 %; the resulting index guides selection of layer thicknesses and materials, and slab thicknesses computed by DARWin are rounded to the nearest half‑inch (or to the nearest whole inch for slabs thicker than seven inches covering less than 30,000 square yards) [1].

Alternative design approaches become preferable whenever the premises of a conventional pavement design are not met. If providing a detour is prohibitively expensive or impractical, traffic must be kept in the work zone and the resulting loading on intermediate layers and cold longitudinal joints degrades performance, prompting the use of accelerated concrete that reaches opening strength in four to seven hours or precast concrete paving systems, especially where no reasonable detour exists. The Superpave Restricted Performance Specification may only be applied when the existing surface is stable, properly leveled, sealed and patched and satisfies the minimum thicknesses; otherwise a conventional overlay, total reconstruction, or another rehabilitation method must be selected. When an asphalt or concrete slab shows extensive deterioration—such as a deteriorated concrete section of roughly five hundred feet or intermittent patches that would leave ten feet or less of original slab between them—a full‑width reconstruction with a formal pavement design analysis to verify slab thickness is required. In high‑traffic corridors where minimizing work‑zone impacts is critical, accelerated concrete or precast systems are favored despite their higher cost, while roller‑compacted concrete is limited to roadways with an average daily traffic of two thousand vehicles or less and a speed limit of fifty‑five miles per hour; it also should be avoided where the International Roughness Index will be measured for ride‑quality incentives. For lane additions on rigid pavements, a full addition of ten feet or more that will carry heavier loads must be designed for the actual traffic rather than merely matching existing thickness, whereas partial widenings under ten feet must duplicate the existing pavement type and depth and adjust joint spacing to between ten and seventeen feet when original transverse joints exceed twenty feet. When distress is primarily surface‑related, full‑depth reclamation loses its advantage; similarly, if the remaining pavement thickness is too thin to form an effective reclaimed layer or the subgrade is excessively wet and cannot be adequately drained, other rehabilitation strategies are preferred. Under PennDOT oversight a DARWin pavement design analysis becomes mandatory when continuous length exceeds five hundred feet on divided roadways or one thousand feet on undivided roadways; for shorter sections the existing structure may be matched only if it meets minimum thickness criteria, but composite pavements that far exceed those minima or contain elements that cannot reasonably be replicated require a DARWin analysis directed by the District Pavement Management Engineer. Adjacent projects on a common route that exhibit significant differences in key inputs—such as total 18‑kip ESALs, subgrade or soil resilient modulus, initial or terminal serviceability, reliability level, or unit costs for pavement materials—must be treated with separate design approaches because the original assumptions no longer hold. Subgrade CBR values outside the normal range—greater than ten or less than five—or unreliable CBR data render the standard pavement design process insufficient; values above ten require chief geotechnical approval and values below five demand subgrade improvement, while values exceeding twenty are prohibited in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation and also trigger more conservative designs. When isolated low‑CBR zones or large stretches with markedly different soil types occur, designers should improve those weak sections locally or divide the project into subsections each using its own pavement design rather than applying a uniform structure. If back‑calculated resilient modulus from Falling Weight Deflectometer data cannot be applied because the required pavement situations are absent, alternative testing methods must be employed. The simple (short) design method is permissible only on collectors and local roads and only when the resulting layer thicknesses remain within the minimum values for those road types; if those limits are exceeded for new construction or a rehabilitation requires structural capacity beyond what the existing pavement can provide, the rigorous ESAL calculation must replace it, and for cross‑routes that connect directly to limited‑access freeways the rigorous method becomes mandatory once projected design‑year average daily traffic exceeds eight thousand vehicles per day. When significant differential frost heave is anticipated, the standard rigid‑pavement thickness calculation is inadequate; increasing slab thickness offers limited success, so a dedicated frost‑design procedure or an alternative structural system should be employed. Subgrades composed of OL, MH, or OH soils that fail to satisfy minimum specifications, or marginal ML‑OL material with uncertain properties, make a full‑depth flexible pavement design unsuitable, prompting the use of a different structural system or subgrade improvement. Finally, when design must obey prescribed minimum and maximum lift depths from the tables or when minimizing total pavement cost drives layer‑thickness selection, designers are instructed to avoid the layered analysis thickness method and instead rely on either the specified‑thickness design or optimized‑thickness design approaches; if those constraints cannot be satisfied, an alternative design strategy becomes preferable. Design inputs and requirements

The design process begins with a precise definition of the project site, which is documented on a location map that marks the start and end points, any intersecting roadways, and includes cover‑letter data such as MPMS number, county, state route and segment offsets together with the environmental approval date. The intended function—whether a shoulder, temporary surface or a permanent roadway—is identified, and for roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) applications the traffic is limited to an average daily traffic of 2 000 vehicles or less and a design speed not exceeding 55 mph. Traffic inputs must also contain a full ADT and truck‑percentage summary, a forecast of total 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs), and a breakdown of future loading that supports the selection of initial and terminal serviceability levels and an overall reliability target. Material information comprises representative samples collected at appropriate depths to characterize existing pavement layers and subgrade material. For RCC the mix design calls for cement, a dense aggregate gradation with a maximum size under one inch, and a water‑to‑cement ratio as low as 0.30 but typically around 0.40; optional air‑entraining admixtures may be used. The target compressive strength ranges from about 4 000 to 10 000 psi, and unit cost estimates for each material are recorded in the design package. Environmental considerations include an anticipated service life of roughly twenty years, climate‑driven surface treatments such as diamond grinding when operating speeds exceed 35 mph, and a frost‑heave probability assessment. Drainage evaluation is required to locate excess moisture or wet subgrade areas so that remedial measures can be incorporated before construction. Geotechnical data are gathered through in‑situ testing—such as falling weight deflectometer (FWD), dynamic cone penetrometer (DCP) and light weight deflectometer (LWD)—to determine the effective modulus of subgrade reaction or resilient modulus, CBR values, and overall subgrade stiffness. The subgrade and any existing subbase must be verified during design as capable of supporting RCC compaction, and all geotechnical results are signed by the district geotechnical engineer. Existing‑condition information is supplied through a recent pavement‑condition survey that documents current distress, RMS history of the pavement structure within project limits, and detailed typical section drawings that show existing layer thicknesses and any temporary pavements needed for construction sequencing. This comprehensive set of site, traffic, material, environmental, geotechnical, and existing‑condition inputs forms the foundation for a technically sound pavement design. [1]

Concrete pavement design follows both the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures as amended by PennDOT and the more specific concrete standards of ACI 211 together with the PennDOT PTM series; mix designs must achieve a compressive strength of at least 4,925 psi and a flexural strength of 650 psi, incorporate six percent air content in the plastic state (seven percent for slip‑form paving), and meet average density targets of not less than ninety‑eight percent of maximum wet density with no individual test below ninety‑six percent [1][2]. Joint geometry is limited to transverse widths between one‑quarter and one‑half inch, longitudinal widths not exceeding three‑quarters inch, and vertical differentials across joints no greater than one‑quarter inch, while dowel bar and tie‑slot locations must be placed within plus or minus one‑eighth inch of the shop drawing; all reinforcement steel, dowel bars, and joint ties are required to be epoxy‑coated in accordance with ASTM A775, and grout placed in joints must reach a compressive strength of at least 2,500 psi before traffic is permitted [2]. Minimum concrete compressive strength and timing for achieving that strength are prescribed in Section 501 of Publication 408, and early‑strength testing must show at least three thousand psi at three days and three thousand five hundred psi at twenty‑eight days on cylinders prepared per ASTM C1435 and PTM 604; slab thicknesses are cast a quarter‑inch thicker than nominal to provide a sacrificial layer and must be within one‑quarter inch of the design depth after construction [1][2]. Flexible asphalt pavements are governed by aggregate selection from sources listed in Bulletin 14 and must satisfy Superpave design criteria detailed in Bulletin 27, with aggregate grading following Table A, fine‑aggregate void content and sand equivalent complying with AASHTO M 323 Table 6, and coarse‑aggregate abrasion limits and flat‑and‑elongated particle restrictions as specified in the same bulletins; reclaimed material must pass a two‑inch sieve at ninety‑five percent by weight, RAP may constitute no more than ten percent of the total mixture, and when moisture susceptibility is anticipated an anti‑strip additive or hydrated lime not less than 0.25 percent of the asphalt cement must be added, while pervious mixes requiring additional stabilization are dosed with cellulose fibers between 0.2 and 0.4 percent by weight in accordance with AASHTO T305 [2]. All design documentation, including DARWin output forms, must be submitted first to the District Executive for approval and then forwarded to the Bureau of Design and Delivery and, when appropriate, to FHWA; for asphalt wearing courses the required Skid Resistance Level designation is recorded on Form D‑4332, completing the integrated set of federal guidance, state specifications, performance thresholds, analytical tools and documentation requirements that govern every aspect of pavement design within Pennsylvania [1].

Design methods and criteria

Pavement design begins with a comprehensive traffic analysis that quantifies future equivalent single axle loads (ESALs), using the rigorous ESAL calculation method in AASHTOWare® DARWin™ when the design‑year average daily traffic on cross routes exceeds eight thousand vehicles and applying directional factors of fifty percent together with truck distribution percentages of one hundred, ninety, or eighty for two‑lane, four‑lane, and six‑or‑more‑lane highways respectively; a simpler method may be used for lower‑traffic collectors provided that layer thickness minima are met and the annual truck‑factor growth rate is set to zero [1]. A condition survey follows, conducted according to PennDOT Publication 336 or 343 and supplemented by distress evaluation procedures such as MicroPaver, while in‑situ stiffness and strength of existing pavement are measured with non‑destructive testing methods including Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer, and lightweight deflectometer, with systematic sampling per Appendix J and back‑calculated moduli reduced by a factor of 0.25 and then halved to obtain static stiffness, any modulus above fifteen thousand psi being verified by an additional test [1]. Subgrade characterization proceeds through laboratory or field CBR testing, from which the resilient modulus is estimated using the Heukelom‑Klomp empirical relationship M r = 1 500 × CBR, limited to a maximum CBR of twenty and requiring approval for values greater than ten; when in‑situ density falls markedly below maximum dry density the multiplier is reduced to one thousand, establishing an upper bound of thirty thousand psi for M r , with dynamic cone results also convertible via a CBR chart and seasonal moisture adjustments applied as needed, while frost‑susceptible subgrades are either removed or treated using the AASHTO frost‑heave procedure and a structural coefficient of 0.12 for lime or cement stabilization [1]. For flexible pavements the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures are executed in DARWin 3.01, where the required design structural number is calculated as SN = Σ a i d i m i with structural coefficients taken from Table 9.3, layer thicknesses denoted by d i and drainage coefficients set to one; two design routes—Specified Thickness or Optimized Thickness—produce layer‑thickness outputs that satisfy lift limits and support life‑cycle cost analysis, while an overall standard deviation input of 0.45 for flexible pavements (0.40 for flexible over rigid, 0.35 for rigid) captures variability in inputs [1]. Concrete slab design for rigid pavements longer than about five hundred feet follows PennDOT Chapter 8 procedures, determining slab thickness from traffic loading, subgrade strength, water‑cement ratio typically between 0.30 and 0.40, and concrete compressive strengths ranging from four to ten thousand psi, with allowable thicknesses from roughly four to eighteen inches; control joint spacing follows an empirical rule of fifteen to twenty feet for slabs thinner than eight inches and a spacing equal to slab thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet for thicker slabs, while jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) requires thirty‑foot joint spacing, coated dowel bars, and appropriate seals per RC‑21M and jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) follows RC‑27M with fifteen‑foot spacing [1]. Reinforced concrete designs are similarly limited to traffic volumes of two thousand vehicles daily or less and speed limits of fifty‑five miles per hour or lower, with diamond grinding mandated for exposed surfaces supporting speeds of thirty‑five miles per hour or higher [1]. Full‑depth reclamation designs incorporate structure‑layer coefficient values from Table 9.3 to reflect the support contribution of stabilization materials such as pulverized aggregate, reclaimed asphalt pavement, cement, lime, or fly ash, with laboratory mix‑design testing defining the appropriate mechanism, and non‑destructive testing continues to verify structural capacity while compaction quality is assessed using the Percent Within Limits metric that captures both mean and standard deviation in a single measure [1]. Ramp pavements are required to match the pavement type and thickness of the adjoining higher‑traffic roadway, with design focusing on the ramp segment experiencing the greatest loading and applying minimum layer depths appropriate to that higher‑volume route [1]. Across both rigid and flexible systems, design tools include AASHTOWare® DARWin™ for structural analysis, an online Frost Heave Calculator for frost‑heave adjustments, PTM specifications for material handling and testing, standard test methods such as ACI 211 for concrete mix calculations, AASHTO T152/T196 for air‑entraining admixture verification, AASHTO T209/T312 for specific gravity determinations, Superpave Gyratory Compactor procedures for volumetric asphalt mix design, and the mechanistic‑empirical models presented in the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide, all of which together provide the empirical relationships, equations, and procedural framework that define modern pavement design [2].

Pavement design must achieve structural adequacy, durability and safety while providing effective drainage of surface water and subsurface moisture, and any visibly failed areas are required to be removed and replaced with materials that restore the necessary capacity [1]. Designs for projects governed by the Restricted Performance Specification must provide a uniform nominal depth of at least 5,600 square yards and the surface‑course thicknesses must follow the minimum values given in the applicable design tables, while asphalt layers are compacted to an in‑place density between 92 % and 98.5 % to satisfy the Percent Within Limits criterion [1]. All designs are performed in accordance with the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures (as amended) using the DARWin software package, and a design analysis is mandatory whenever a new or rehabilitated segment exceeds five hundred feet on divided highways, one thousand feet on undivided roads, or one thousand feet of bridge‑approach paving; if the approved design year differs by more than three years from the current project year a refreshed analysis must be prepared [1]. Structural section and dimensions

The structural thickness of rigid concrete pavements is permitted to range from four inches up to eighteen inches, although repair applications commonly adopt a minimum eight‑inch thickness [1]. Where the pavement thickness falls below eight inches, control joints are typically spaced between fifteen and twenty feet, while sections eight inches thick or greater employ a joint spacing calculated as 2.5 times the slab thickness expressed in feet [1]. For jointed reinforced concrete pavements the recommended transverse joint spacing is thirty feet, whereas jointed plain concrete pavements use fifteen‑foot spacing [1]. In lane‑widening situations, if the existing transverse joint spacing does not exceed twenty feet the new slabs are laid to the same length; when it exceeds twenty feet the added lane is divided with joints spaced between ten and seventeen feet [1]. Precast concrete slabs are limited in width to no more than a single travel lane and must be fabricated with lengths between six and fifteen feet, extending to twenty feet when prestressed units are employed [1][2]. In addition, each slab is required to be a quarter‑inch thicker than its nominal design thickness [2]. The transverse joints separating adjacent slabs must have a minimum opening of one‑quarter inch and may not exceed one‑half inch, while longitudinal joint openings are limited to three‑quarters inch [2]. Vertical edge differentials between neighboring slabs are constrained to a quarter‑inch, with a half‑inch allowance before grinding becomes necessary, and the subbase surface must be finished within plus or minus one‑half inch of the target grade [2]. The installed concrete depth must agree with the design depth to within a quarter‑inch; if core measurements fall outside this tolerance additional cores are taken and the average must still lie within the same quarter‑inch band [2]. Base layers for interstate and expressway rigid pavements are commonly constructed from an asphalt or cement‑treated permeable base of four inches placed over a two‑A subbase also four inches thick, while other specifications allow a compacted base course whose thickness may vary between two and five inches [1][2]. The guides do not prescribe explicit dimensions for treated‑soil layers, shoulders, or edge‑support elements [1][2]. Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are called for only when concrete patches exceed approximately sixty‑five feet in length; no other reinforcement dimensions are detailed [1].

The design thickness of a rigid pavement is first bounded by the standard range of four to eighteen inches, which is chosen according to the anticipated concrete compressive strength—typically between four‑thousand and ten‑thousand psi—and may be increased after a full‑width analysis when more than five hundred feet of all‑lane replacement is required. The required total depth can be placed in a single lift or built up with multiple lifts to achieve the design thickness, and the subbase and bedding layers are prepared within plus‑or‑minus half an inch of the intended grade before slab placement. When a lane addition is ten feet wide or more, the new pavement normally adopts the same thickness as the existing pavement unless traffic loading calculations indicate a need for greater strength; narrow widenings under ten feet must match both the concrete type and depth of the adjacent section. Jointed reinforced concrete pavements employ perpendicular transverse joints spaced at thirty feet, whereas jointed plain concrete pavements use fifteen‑foot spacing, and for roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) slabs less than eight inches thick control joints are placed every fifteen to twenty feet while thicker RCC sections use a spacing equal to 2.5 times the slab thickness in feet. Longitudinal continuity is provided by tie bars or tie bolts placed along longitudinal joints for continuous sections longer than sixty‑five feet; these fasteners are required only when the concrete patch exceeds that length. Cross‑slope continuity across a replacement zone is achieved by setting precast slabs approximately one‑quarter inch higher than the surrounding surface and then applying a final diamond grind to restore the designed slope, while laser‑controlled screeding devices are used to set the required cross‑slope during placement and to limit surface variation to no more than one‑eighth inch over each slab length. Materials and mixture design

Pavement design must identify the material system for every structural layer and prescribe the essential properties and mixture proportions required for performance [1][2]. For flexible pavements the designer selects an asphalt mix—hot‑mix, warm‑mix or reclaimed‑asphalt‑pavement‑based—and applies the minimum surface‑course thicknesses required for Superpave wearing and binder courses [1]. The mix must achieve target values for asphalt content, percent passing the No. 200 sieve, percent passing the primary control sieve, and an in‑place density typically between 92 % and 98.5 % [1]. When reclaimed asphalt pavement is used its constituents must pass at least ninety‑five percent of a 2‑inch sieve and may not exceed ten percent of the total mixture [1][2]. If an anti‑strip additive is incorporated its dosage shall not be less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt, and any pervious stabilizer must be added at a rate between 0.2 % and 0.4 % of the whole mixture [2]. The binder shall be a PG‑64 grade asphalt cement appropriate to the projected traffic level and climate, such as PG 64H‑22, PG 64E‑22 or PG 64S‑22 [2]. All aggregates must be sourced from producers listed in Bulletin 14, satisfy Superpave quality requirements, meet the specified SRL rating, and conform to the volumetric limits for fine‑aggregate void content, sand equivalency, coarse‑aggregate abrasion loss, flatness/elongation percentages and minimum crushed‑fragment content [2]. Recycled concrete aggregate, when used, must be governed by the applicable reclaimed‑material specifications [1]. Filler may consist of non‑air‑entrained Type 1 Portland cement or hydrated lime, with lime permitted up to two percent of the dry aggregate weight, and fly ash is prohibited when design traffic equals or exceeds three million ESALs [2]. The volumetric mix design for each asphalt layer shall be documented in a Joint Mix Formula that lists for every component its supplier code, material class, percentage of the total mix, bulk specific gravity and absorption together with aggregate bulk specific gravity (Gsb), theoretical maximum specific gravity (Gmm) and laboratory specific gravity at design density (Gmb), and must include target VMA, VTM and VFA percentages as well as production tolerances such as SGC gyration values, moisture‑sensitivity results and aggregate consensus properties [2]. For micro‑surfacing mixes the specification requires a Class CQS‑1hPM emulsified asphalt combined with a latex‑based polymer modifier that cures within one hour; fine aggregate must be Type B or better and filler must maintain the prescribed percent passing the No. 200 sieve [2]. For rigid pavements the designer prepares concrete mix designs in accordance with Publication 408 Section 704 or ACI 211, using an optimized aggregate gradation and, when required, a higher‑strength Class AA cement concrete [1][2]. The mix shall achieve a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a flexural strength of 650 psi; over‑design strengths shall not fall below 1,000 psi except for AAAP‑type mixes where the limit is 500 psi [2]. Air content in the plastic state must be six percent, rising to seven percent for AAAP and slip‑form/fixed‑form mixes, achieved with an appropriate air‑entraining admixture [2]. Supplementary cementitious materials may replace a portion of the cement up to 25 % Grade 100 slag, at least 15 % fly ash, or 5–10 % silica fume [2]. Aggregate gradations must satisfy PTM 528 (or PTM 529 for slip‑form/fixed‑form) and lightweight concrete shall target an equilibrium density of 115 lb/ft³ within the range 110–117 lb/ft³ [2]. Chloride‑ion penetration must be classified as Low to Very Low after no more than fifty‑six days of curing, and shrinkage shall be limited to five hundred microstrain (0.05 % for slip‑form/fixed‑form) [2]. Roller‑compacted concrete mixes consist of cement, aggregate and water with a low water‑to‑cement ratio as low as 0.30 and typically around 0.40, employ a dense aggregate gradation whose maximum size is less than one inch, may include air entrainment or limited admixtures at the designer’s discretion, and are expected to achieve compressive strengths between four thousand and ten thousand psi [1][2]. RCC placements must reach an average density of at least 98 % of the maximum wet density with no individual test below 96 %, and early‑strength requirements include a minimum of three thousand psi at three to seven days and three‑five hundred psi at twenty‑eight days [2]. Slab thickness shall be within one‑quarter inch of the plan depth, with an additional quarter‑inch sacrificial layer for precast slabs [2]. Dowels and joint reinforcement must be supplied by approved manufacturers and may be epoxy‑coated, polyester, vinylester, mechanically galvanized, stainless steel, corrosion‑resistant or GFRP; diameters shall be 1.5 in (or 1.25 in) with tolerances of +0.25/–0.001 in, length 18 in ± ½ in, coating thickness at least eight mils non‑abraded and no more than two holidays per linear foot; grout placed in dowel slots must attain a compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic loading, while cementitious bedding grout shall reach 1,000 psi at opening and 2,500 psi within twelve hours [1][2]. Foam gasket material for joints shall be open‑cell two‑pound polyester meeting ASTM D3574, and rebar coupling devices must develop at least 125 % of the specified yield strength of the reinforcement steel [2]. The base layer for flexible pavements may be provided as either an aggregate‑cement mix or an aggregate‑lime‑pozzolan mix, both of which shall be presented as bid alternatives [1]. For rigid pavement designs the base and subbase are defined as an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base (ATPB) or Cement Treated Permeable Base (CTPB) over a 2A subbase, each with assigned thicknesses, and the loss‑of‑support factor is applied to every layer for effective k‑value calculations [1]. Seasonal elastic moduli for these bases are fifteen thousand psi for spring thaw, thirty thousand psi for normal conditions and fifty thousand psi for frozen conditions, and the combined stiffness of the subbase relative to the underlying soil resilient modulus is limited to a ratio of four; open‑graded subbases are prohibited and drainage layers are confined to ATPB or CTPB [1]. Structural coefficients for each layer are obtained from Table 9.3, while all layer drainage coefficients are fixed at one, and the overall structural number must meet or exceed the required value within the allowable lift depths shown in the minimum‑and‑maximum depth tables [1]. The subgrade material is defined by its resilient modulus—determined in consultation with a geotechnical engineer per Publication 293—or, alternatively, by a CBR value that must exceed ten; values below five require additional justification and are converted to resilient modulus using the Heukelom‑Klomp equation [1]. Detailed subgrade properties shall include gradation coefficients Cu and Cc, percent passing the No. 200 sieve, Atterberg limits (liquid limit, plastic limit, plasticity index), AASHTO group index, maximum dry density, optimum moisture content, and seasonal adjustments of ten to twenty percent reduction for spring values and a doubling of modulus for frozen conditions [1]. All borrow material and any stabilized subgrade must meet a minimum resilient modulus, rock capping shall consist of one to three feet of R‑3 or AASHTO No. 1 rock modeled with a CBR of 20, and a structural coefficient of 0.12 is used when lime or cement stabilization is modeled [1]. Geosynthetic reinforcement shall be provided where indicated, using Class 4 geotextile Type A for full‑lane layer separation and Type C for stabilization on low‑CBR soils, together with any geogrid that satisfies agency specifications [1]. Ramp sections must employ the same pavement material—concrete or asphalt—as the higher‑traffic roadway they serve, with acceleration and deceleration portions matching the mainline thickness and meeting at least the minimum depth appropriate for that traffic volume [1].

Pavement designers first quantify the expected traffic loads, evaluate existing pavement condition, and measure subgrade strength so that a required Design Structural Number (SN) can be established and subsequently allocated to individual layers using the standard equation SN = Σ(ai·di·mi) with structural coefficients taken from reference tables and all drainage coefficients set to 1.0, while ensuring that computed layer thicknesses satisfy prescribed minimum and maximum lift depths [1]. For rigid pavements the concrete mix is selected to meet both structural strength and durability objectives, employing an optimized aggregate gradation as defined in Publication 408 Section 704 which typically provides opening‑to‑traffic strength within 24 to 48 hours; high‑early‑strength concrete is avoided because of its tendency toward excessive shrinkage cracking, whereas accelerated concrete may be specified when early traffic service is required [1]. When a longer service life or higher durability is anticipated—such as for precast paving systems expected to remain in service beyond twenty years—a high‑strength Class AA mixture is chosen, often supplemented with high‑performance dowel bars to sustain structural performance over the extended period [1]. In cases where roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) is preferred, the mix is made very dry with a water‑cement ratio typically around 0.40 and as low as 0.30, producing a damp‑gravel consistency that yields compressive strengths between 4 000 and 10 000 psi; a dense aggregate gradation limited to one‑inch maximum size, optionally combined with air entrainment or minimal admixtures, permits placement with an asphalt paver and compaction by rollers without the need for forms, reinforcement, or dowel bars, and slab thicknesses are selected between four and eighteen inches in accordance with projected traffic loads [1]. When reclaimed materials are incorporated, designers select a stabilization method—pulverization alone, mechanical stabilization that adds aggregate to improve gradation, or chemical stabilization using cement, lime, fly ash, or similar additives—and proportion those agents based on laboratory‑tested mix designs so as to achieve the target strength while avoiding excessive brittleness and preserving constructability [1]. For the base and sub‑base layers of rigid pavements, the type of sub‑base (e.g., asphalt treated base or cement treated base) is chosen to provide a resilient modulus compatible with the measured subgrade modulus, and thicknesses are assigned so that, after applying loss‑of‑support factors, the weighted average k‑value meets traffic and environmental requirements; the ratio of sub‑base to roadbed soil resilient modulus is kept below four, open‑graded sub‑base materials are prohibited, and drainage layers are incorporated only where positive outlet drainage can be assured [1]. In widening or lane‑addition projects, full‑width additions of ten feet or more retain the original slab thickness, while partial widenings under ten feet match the existing pavement type and depth; jointed plain concrete pavements are widened with plain cement concrete, whereas jointed reinforced sections may be widened with either plain or reinforced concrete, ensuring transverse joints align and slab lengths respect prescribed spacing limits [1]. All material sources must be approved producers and conform to specified quality criteria before they can be used in a pavement design, and for concrete mixes the design package—including trial‑batch data—must be submitted at least twenty‑eight days prior to any production to demonstrate compliance with minimum compressive strength of 4 925 psi and flexural strength of 650 psi; overdesign strengths are required to be at least 1 000 psi (500 psi for AAAP‑type mixes), the minimum cement factor from Table A is applied, supplemental cementitious materials may be used within table limits, and a target plastic‑state air content of six to seven percent is achieved with an air‑entraining admixture [2]. Durability requirements are addressed by limiting chloride ion penetration to low, very‑low, or negligible levels after no more than fifty‑six days of curing and by keeping 28‑day shrinkage within the prescribed microstrain limits; when overlay thickness is less than three inches, No. 67 coarse aggregate replaces No. 57 to aid constructability, and macrofibers are added at rates that satisfy the residual strength (f150) values stipulated in Table B [2]. Bedding layers may not exceed one‑half inch, cement‑treated bedding must develop at least 500 psi before traffic is permitted, and dowel or joint grouts must reach a minimum of 2 500 psi prior to opening the pavement to service [2]. For flexible pavements the selection begins with an asphalt binder class appropriate to climate and service conditions, followed by aggregates that satisfy the Superpave shape‑rating (SRL) requirement from producers listed in Bulletin 14; if a single source cannot meet the SRL, a fifty‑percent‑by‑weight blend of two approved aggregates is used, fine aggregate must be Type B or better, pass at least ninety‑five percent of a two‑inch sieve and meet uncompacted void content and sand‑equivalent limits, while coarse aggregate must satisfy abrasion loss criteria and contain flat‑and‑elongated particles no greater than ten percent for a 1:5 gradation or twenty percent for a 1:3 gradation, also providing the minimum crushed‑fragment content [2]. Reclaimed asphalt pavement is limited to ten percent of the total mix, hydrated lime may be used as filler up to two percent of the dry aggregate weight, fly ash is excluded when design traffic exceeds three million equivalent single axle loads (ESALs), an anti‑strip additive is required at a minimum dosage of 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt, and a stabilizer is dosed between 0.2 % and 0.4 % of the total mix according to manufacturer recommendations [2]. Mixture proportioning for both concrete and asphalt follows a volumetric design procedure; asphalt mixes are developed with the Superpave Gyratory Compactor to produce a Joint Mix Formula that defines target values for voids in mineral aggregate, voids in total mixture, air voids, specific gravities and tolerances, aggregates are uniformly graded and often combined as a fifty‑percent blend of two sources to meet the weight percentages set out in Table A, and all component materials shown in the formula must be identical to those placed on the project with any source changes documented in writing [2]. Proportioning is controlled by metering equipment that limits liquid variation to plus or minus two percent by weight, ensures sufficient total liquids for complete blending of reclaimed material with emulsified binder, and additional water, when required, is added at the mill head using a calibrated meter [2]. The resulting base‑course mix is compacted to at least ninety‑six percent of the PTM‑402 target density, providing the structural integrity and durability demanded by the design; stabilized cold‑recycled aggregate bases are cured until average moisture content falls below two percent, emulsified‑asphalt‑stabilized mixes require a minimum two‑week cure before placement of the wearing or binder course, and foamed‑asphalt‑stabilized mixes follow the same moisture threshold before subsequent layers are placed [2]. Both concrete and asphalt designs mandate trial batches prepared under expected temperature conditions; these batches are tested for strength, air content, permeability, shrinkage or moisture susceptibility as appropriate, and successful laboratory verification authorizes final mix approval, after which full‑size production batches are used to fine‑tune proportions before field placement [1][2]. By adhering to these material‑selection and proportioning practices—grounded in traffic loading analysis, subgrade evaluation, prescribed structural coefficients, quality‑controlled mix designs, laboratory‑validated stabilization methods, and rigorous construction tolerances—the pavement system satisfies structural adequacy, durability, constructability, and overall performance requirements throughout its service life [1][2].

Structural details and interfaces

Pavement design for rigid pavements mandates a fully defined joint system that includes transverse joints whose spacing is dictated by the pavement classification; jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) requires approximately thirty‑foot spacing with perpendicular joints while jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) uses about fifteen‑foot spacing, and any reconstruction or widening must align new joints with existing ones, preserving original slab lengths when the original transverse spacing is twenty feet or less and limiting slab lengths to ten‑to‑seventeen feet with possible intermediate joints when the original spacing exceeds twenty feet [1]. The width of each transverse joint is limited to a range of one‑quarter to one‑half inch, whereas longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters of an inch in opening [2]. Load‑transfer across every transverse joint is provided by coated dowel bars that serve as the primary transfer units; these dowels are specified with diameters of 1.25 or 1.50 inches (allowable variation +0.25 in, –0.001 in), lengths of eighteen inches (tolerance ±½ in), and must be fully bonded along their embedded length using epoxy‑type binders, although polyester or vinylester binders are also acceptable, with the dowel slots filled with grout that must achieve at least 2,500 psi compressive strength before traffic is permitted [1][2]. The exposed portions of dowels receive a bond‑breaker coating prior to concrete placement, and when a Type B epoxy coating is used it must be a minimum of eight mils thick and non‑abraded; Type A coatings may be applied without a bond breaker [2]. Longitudinal reinforcement is achieved with epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars placed at an angle to cross‑stitch longitudinal cracks or joints in plain cement concrete pavements, and such tie bars (or tie bolts) are required only when a concrete patch exceeds sixty‑five feet; the tie bars are also installed along longitudinal joints to preserve lane continuity and must have smooth, burr‑free circular ends [1][2]. Reinforcement steel embedded in the slab is epoxy‑coated, and any splices must employ listed rebar coupling devices that develop at least 125 percent of the steel’s yield strength [2]. Additional structural details include joint sealing with either hot‑applied or cold‑applied sealers, routine joint sawing and resealing to protect against ingress, and mandated spall repair and joint rehabilitation activities such as cleaning and resealing of cracks [1][2]. Where vertical differentials exceed two inches, a temporary asphalt wedge transition is installed using a Superpave PG 64S‑22 mix that satisfies the prescribed ESAL range and thickness criteria; granular transition material is also used to create a gradual change in support conditions when a new pavement abuts an existing section of differing total depth [1][2]. For roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements, forms, steel reinforcement, and dowel bars are omitted; control joints are provided based on thickness—sections thinner than eight inches receive joints every fifteen to twenty feet, while thicker sections use a spacing equal to 2.5 times the pavement thickness in feet—and shoulder joints are aligned with the mainline [1]. Geogrids may be placed at subgrade‑aggregate interfaces to stabilize weak subgrades, and class 4 geotextiles (Type A for layer separation or Type C for combined separation and lateral restraint) are specified for separating dissimilar layers [1]. Frost‑susceptible soils are addressed either by removal and replacement with non‑frost‑susceptible fill or by providing a stronger base course that functions as a load‑transfer measure against differential heave, while tied concrete shoulders contribute to load transfer through the coefficient J incorporated in slab‑thickness calculations [1]. Ancillary connection details comprise recessed lifting devices, slab‑setting bolts or strongback beams, foam gaskets beneath slabs to allow full compression, and an undersealing coat applied to the slab surface; for concrete overlays thinner than three inches, synthetic or steel macrofibers are added at a dosage sufficient to meet the required residual strength (f150), and the coarse aggregate must be No. 67 rather than No. 57, with the overlay mix also meeting chloride‑ion penetration limits [2]. After thickness calculations, slab thicknesses are rounded to the nearest half‑inch, or to the nearest inch for slabs thicker than seven inches when the area is under thirty thousand square yards [1].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Effective pavement design starts with a systematic survey of existing drainage conditions and the correction of any deficiencies before reclaimed layers are placed, while soft, wet or otherwise unsuitable subbase and subgrade material is removed to provide a well‑compacted granular base for slab placement [1][2]. The design package must explicitly document these provisions on the typical section drawing, showing all edge drains and, where required, longitudinal edge drains for dense bases or positive‑outlet drainage for permeable base layers such as Asphalt Treated Permeable Base (ATPB) or Cement Treated Permeable Base (CTPB); open‑graded subbase is prohibited [1]. Blockouts are incorporated into precast slabs to accommodate existing utility castings and any required drainage structures, ensuring that the drainage system remains functional after slab installation [2]. Subgrade characterization incorporates seasonal moisture and temperature effects; laboratory or field resilient‑modulus tests on representative soil samples under wet and dry conditions reveal spring modulus values that are 10 %–20 % lower than summer values and frozen‑ground moduli roughly twice the normal values, which together with CBR, fines content, Atterberg limits and group index feed the AASHTO calculations for flexible pavements and the effective modulus of subgrade reaction for rigid pavements [1]. The effective modulus of subgrade reaction is taken as 15 000 psi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30 000 psi for normal summer/fall, and 50 000 psi for frozen winter, providing appropriate stiffness inputs for design analyses [1]. Frost‑heave probability assessments, typically ranging from 25 % to 75 % of the project area, are performed; for flexible pavements the designer selects a frost‑heave rate from tables based on soil type, determines depth of frost penetration using the Design Freezing Index for the nearest weather station, reads this depth against a drainage‑quality curve such as FAIR to obtain the maximum potential serviceability loss, and then applies the probability factor via the AASHTO chart to adjust the terminal serviceability index [1]. Where distinct soil zones exist, the project may be divided into sections so that each receives a drainage system sized for its specific pavement thickness and subgrade condition, allowing tailored moisture control across the alignment [1]. In frost‑susceptible soils the design calls for removal or replacement with a sufficiently thick layer of non‑frost‑susceptible material to insulate the roadbed, complemented by base drains or other suitable drainage features that limit water accumulation and differential heave [1]. Rigid pavements rely on saturated‑subgrade strength data to provide reserve capacity during spring thaws; no separate frost‑thickness adjustments are prescribed, but when differential frost heave is anticipated designers must consult dedicated frost‑design guidance, recognizing that increasing slab thickness alone offers limited mitigation [1]. A loss‑of‑support factor is applied to the weighted average subbase k‑value to account for potential erosion or pumping caused by moisture, ensuring that subbase performance is not overestimated [1]. The concrete mix design for continuous prestressed precast systems explicitly accounts for the site’s ambient average daily temperature and exposure conditions; standard AASHTO mechanistic‑empirical expansion coefficients are used to select appropriate panel dimensions and locate expansion joints, preventing thermal movements from compromising structural integrity [2]. For pervious asphalt mixes, moisture susceptibility is evaluated by testing the finished mix in accordance with ASTM D3625, requiring at least a 90 % coating of aggregate; when potential stripping is indicated, an anti‑strip additive or hydrated lime is added to the binder, and the same aggregates approved for dense‑graded mixtures are used with the corresponding PG grade binder, while additional moisture resistance may be achieved by incorporating a stabilizer after confirming its necessity through AASHTO T 305 testing and dosing cellulose fibers within the tolerances specified in Table B [2]. Moisture content of reclaimed base courses is tightly controlled; emulsified‑asphalt‑stabilized CRABC must be cured for at least two weeks or until its average moisture falls below 2 % before any wearing or binder layer is placed, and adequate total liquids are maintained during mixing with water added at the mill head using a calibrated meter as needed [2]. Construction timing reflects climatic constraints: CRABC placement is prohibited from September 1 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0, 2‑0, 3‑0, 4‑0, 9‑0, and 10‑0, and from October 1 to April 30 in all other districts; furthermore, placement may only proceed after a minimum of 24 hours without freezing temperatures when the ambient air temperature is at least 45 °F and rising [2]. Together, these drainage provisions, moisture management practices, material selections, testing protocols, and seasonal restrictions enable the pavement structure to withstand environmental loads while preserving long‑term performance.

Effective pavement design must first assure accessibility and safety by providing drainage that removes both surface and subsurface water and by delivering adequate skid resistance through appropriate surface friction. Lateral support is supplied by shoulders that reduce edge stresses, allowing the pavement to resist edge loads without compromising stability. Geometric control of joints is essential; reinforced concrete pavements require transverse joint spacing of thirty feet, plain concrete fifteen feet, all joints must be perpendicular, equipped with coated dowel bars and proper seals, and when widening existing rigid pavement full‑lane additions ten feet or wider retain the original thickness unless heavier traffic dictates otherwise while partial widenings under ten feet must match the existing pavement type and depth. When the original transverse joint spacing is twenty feet or less, slab lengths are duplicated in the new work; if the original spacing exceeds twenty feet, slabs between ten and seventeen feet with intermediate joints are used to keep lengths roughly equal. In addition, the geometry of each joint is tightly controlled: transverse joints must be no narrower than one‑quarter inch and no wider than one‑half inch along their entire length, longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters inch, and the finished vertical differential across any joint is limited to a quarter inch. Slab dimensions are limited to a maximum width of one lane, with lengths ranging from six to fifteen feet, extending to twenty feet for prestressed units, and surface corrections and cross‑slope adjustments are achieved through leveling courses while maintaining constant cutting depth, width, cross‑slope and uniform grade throughout the structure. Surface smoothness is verified with a twelve‑foot straight edge and any average deviation over a three‑thousand‑square‑yard area exceeding one‑half inch must be corrected. Roller‑compacted concrete may be employed only on roadways with an average daily traffic of two thousand vehicles or fewer and a speed limit not exceeding fifty‑five miles per hour; if the surface is exposed and the posted speed is thirty‑five miles per hour or higher, diamond grinding must texture the pavement to provide sufficient skid resistance, and control joint spacing for RCC follows thickness criteria—sections thinner than eight inches receive joints every fifteen to twenty feet, while thicker sections use a spacing equal to two point five times the pavement thickness in feet. Ramp pavements on limited‑access highways must use the same material and thickness as the higher‑traffic roadway they connect to, with classification consistent with that road, and design is driven by the ramp experiencing the greatest traffic loading, incorporating minimum depths appropriate for the higher‑volume road while anticipating future development and potential heavy‑truck traffic. Safety provisions include protecting the base course from equipment damage, controlling traffic when raveling could occur by employing a pilot car to regulate speed, restricting base‑course construction to daylight hours to ensure adequate visibility, and not allowing traffic on the slab until grout has reached a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi. User considerations require planning for early opening of concrete repairs using accelerated or precast systems so that lanes can be reopened within four to seven hours, and specifying liquidated damages if lane closures exceed prescribed time frames, thereby ensuring the pavement supports expected loads without compromising public safety. Verification and expected performance

The completed pavement design is compiled into a full package containing a cover letter, Form D‑4332, typical section drawings, DARWin printouts, structural calculations, traffic and geotechnical data, and life‑cycle cost analysis results. This package is submitted to Central Office where it is directly compared with an AASHTOWare Pavement ME design performed by the office to confirm adherence to 1993 AASHTO procedures, minimum thickness and material criteria, traffic loads, environmental conditions, cost‑effectiveness targets, and schedule constraints, providing final approval. For rigid pavements the calculated layer depths are rounded according to prescribed rules—nearest half‑inch for typical sections and, when a pavement exceeds seven inches in depth and its area is under thirty thousand square yards, nearest inch—and then checked against the minimum and maximum limits listed in Table 8.3 to ensure acceptability. The designer also evaluates combinations of design variables that yield the most economical solution while preserving required durability and rideability, thereby confirming performance expectations. For flexible pavements verification begins by ensuring that the structural number derived from the thickness design exceeds the required design structural number, establishing structural adequacy. Layer thicknesses are generated using either the Specified Thickness Design or Optimized Thickness Design method in DARWin, with the Layered Analysis approach expressly excluded, and each layer depth must respect the minimum and maximum lift limits set out in Tables 9.4 and 9.5 to address constructability concerns. Drainage coefficients are fixed at 1.0 and structural coefficients are taken from Table 9.3; per‑inch cost factors for surface, base, and subbase materials and any mandated base material alternatives are examined to ensure consistency with project specifications and budgetary goals. Throughout this verification process any constructability issues or alignment questions are resolved through coordination with the District Pavement Management Engineer or Pavement Manager, guaranteeing that the design remains compliant, constructible, performs as intended, and aligns with overall project objectives. In parallel, the designer must submit shop and working drawings, a manufacturer’s quality‑control plan, and detailed mix designs—including a Job Mix Formula signed by a certified plant technician—well before production; concrete submissions are required at least twenty‑one calendar days prior to the pre‑production meeting and asphalt mix designs no later than three weeks in advance. These documents specify each component material, aggregate size, filler, polymer or fiber content, and any grout strengths, and must meet minimum concrete compressive strength of 4,925 psi and flexural strength of 650 psi for overlays as well as volumetric tolerances for asphalt content, gradation, and air voids. Supporting test data such as slump, air‑content, and compressive‑strength results per ACI 211, trial mixtures, and a full‑size batch produced with the planned mixing equipment are reviewed by the Representative before any material placement, and a project‑specific Quality‑Control Plan prepared in accordance with Section 106 must be approved prior to construction. During construction field surveys verify that surface elevations, joint locations, and slab dimensions fall within the tolerances shown in the design tables; density testing of each lot must achieve an average of at least 98 % of maximum wet density with no individual measurement below 96 %. Early‑age compressive strength tests must reach a minimum of 3,000 psi at three days and 28‑day cylinders must attain at least 3,500 psi; concrete cores are accepted only when the measured depth is within one‑quarter inch of the designed thickness. Placement tolerances demand line and grade accuracy within a quarter‑inch vertical differential and joint widths between one‑quarter and one‑half inch, while bedding thicknesses are limited to half an inch for fine‑grained material and two inches for cement‑treated layers; fiber‑reinforced overlays use No. 67 coarse aggregate when the overlay depth is under three inches and macro‑fiber dosages that achieve the design residual strength (f150) as demonstrated by a 14‑day ASTM C1609‑12 test. Permeability requirements are satisfied by targeting low or negligible chloride ion penetration within 56 days. Performance of load‑transfer units is verified by measuring differential deflection with Linear Variable Differential Transformers; the deflection must not exceed 7.5 mils after one million loading cycles and may increase no more than an additional 2.5 mils at ten million cycles. All dowel bars receive a Type B fusion‑bonded epoxy coating of at least eight mils non‑abraded thickness, and holiday detection using a 67.5‑volt detector must reveal no more than two holidays per linear foot; any surface repairs are limited to the allowable percentage of bar area and dowel dimensions must meet prescribed tolerances. If any material source, mix adjustment, or test result falls outside the approved parameters, a written notification and revised Job Mix Formula are required before work may proceed, and non‑conforming pavement that fails density, strength, thickness, joint, deflection, coating, or holiday criteria must be removed or repaired in accordance with district directives. This integrated program of documentation, testing, inspection, and coordination ensures that the completed pavement design is verified for compliance with specifications, constructability, performance under anticipated loads, and consistency with overall project requirements. The guides anticipate a durable service life of roughly twenty years, based on selected materials, anticipated traffic volumes and prevailing environmental conditions. Structural durability is achieved by meeting specified compressive‑strength requirements, with roller‑compacted concrete required to attain a high compressive strength ranging from four thousand to ten thousand psi, while conventional concrete pavements must reach at least three thousand pounds per square inch by three to seven days and three‑thousand‑five hundred psi by twenty‑eight days. These strengths are verified through laboratory cylinder tests conducted at three, seven and twenty‑eight days in accordance with ASTM C1435, and the maturity method correlates degree‑hours of cure to strength at defined intervals per PTM No. 640. Density requirements ensure structural adequacy; roller‑compacted concrete must be placed to achieve at least ninety‑eight percent of the maximum wet density recorded during rolling, while the supporting base course for all pavements must be compacted to a minimum of ninety‑six percent of the PTM target density and confirmed by in‑place density measurements per AASHTO T310. Thickness control is enforced by requiring the finished pavement thickness to be within one quarter inch of the design depth, verified through coring and measurement procedures such as AASHTO T‑24. Shrinkage restraint is measured with ASTM C157, limiting shrinkage to five hundred microstrain (or up to five hundred fifty with approval). Air‑void content and durability against chloride ingress are evaluated using the limits of AASHTO T152/T196 and ASTM C457 for voids, and a penetration rating of low, very low or negligible measured by AASHTO T277 or T358, not exceeding 2 800 coulombs after fifty‑six days of curing. Serviceability is addressed through defined initial and terminal serviceability levels, and when an asphalt wearing course is present the pavement must satisfy a specified Skid Resistance Level; both are assessed using the DARWin mechanistic‑empirical analysis recorded on Form D‑4332 together with the stipulated reliability level and projected total ESALs. Ride quality for roller‑compacted concrete is not evaluated by the International Roughness Index, reflecting its inherently rougher surface texture; consequently payment or performance incentives based on IRI are not applied. For conventional concrete pavements ride quality is controlled by testing smoothness with a twelve‑foot straight edge across three locations per segment and correcting any area where the average gap exceeds one half inch. Joint movement under traffic is limited by structural adequacy testing that records LVDT differential deflection, which may not exceed 7.5 mils after one million load cycles and may increase no more than 2.5 mils after ten million cycles. Dowel‑bar bond performance is verified with pull‑out load tests to the requirements of AASHTO M 254, and coating integrity is inspected with a holiday detector and visual criteria. For thin bonded overlays incorporating macrofibers, residual flexural strength at fourteen days is evaluated per ASTM C1609‑12 and must meet the prescribed fiber‑volume‑fraction relationship. Finally, emulsified‑asphalt‑stabilized CRABC material used in overlays is cured for a minimum of two weeks or until average moisture content falls below two percent, as confirmed by core moisture tests, to ensure long‑term durability.
