Average Daily Traffic (ADT)

Part of: Traffic Loading is-subtopic-of
Average Daily Traffic (ADT) is a topic under Traffic Loading.
current-adttwo-way-adtinitial-adttwo-lane-aadt
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Average Daily Traffic (ADT) is a 24‑hour, two‑directional vehicle count taken when a roadway first opens to traffic, providing the core traffic‑volume metric for pavement design and project analysis. By supplying the initial, design‑year and class‑specific traffic mix, ADT allows engineers to compute equivalent single axle loads (ESALs), select appropriate Skid Resistance Levels for asphalt wearing courses, evaluate the suitability of cold‑recycled base mixes, and quantify user‑delay costs in life‑cycle cost studies. The count is further adjusted with directional, lane‑distribution and growth factors to reflect traffic conditions over the design period, ensuring that structural capacity and safety requirements are matched to expected loading.[1]

Purpose and design objectives

Average Daily Traffic (ADT) provides the traffic volume needed to determine how many vehicles are delayed during construction or maintenance activities, allowing user delay costs to be quantified and included in a life‑cycle cost analysis; by supplying the initial ADT, design‑year ADT and related traffic mix, engineers can calculate delayed vehicle counts for each year, direction and vehicle class, which then feed the total user delay cost calculation for the project. ADT is employed as a traffic‑volume metric that drives the selection of an appropriate Skid Resistance Level for the coarse aggregate in asphalt wearing courses, thereby addressing the engineering need to maintain adequate surface friction throughout the pavement’s service life and ensure safe driving conditions under expected traffic loads. ADT is used in cold‑recycling project selection to ensure the pavement’s early‑stage strength and abrasion resistance are sufficient for the expected traffic loads; projects carrying significantly heavy truck traffic (i.e., 200 or more daily ESALs) should not be selected, and these mixtures are not suitable for high ADT roads unless detours can be established until adequate structural strength is obtained, while polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended where traffic volumes exceed 3,000 two‑lane AADT to obtain quicker stability. ADT is collected as a 24‑hour, two‑directional vehicle count at the time a roadway first opens to traffic so that engineers can compute the total 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads for the project’s performance period; feeding this ADT value into the rigorous ESAL calculation method allows designers to determine the cumulative loading and stress that the pavement must withstand over its design life, thereby addressing the need to size pavement layers to achieve the required serviceability. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (p. 76), Ch. 5 (pp. 110–123), Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157)

Scope and applicability

In the context of asphalt wearing courses, Average Daily Traffic is employed when a new construction, overlay, or resurfacing project is planned; the anticipated initial ADT on a new facility or the current ADT for a resurfacing determines which Skid Resistance Level (SRL) designation shall be applied to the coarse aggregate, and that SRL must be shown on the pavement design approval forms. Higher‑than‑necessary SRL grades are prohibited on non‑wearing courses, leveling courses, and shoulders. For cold recycled asphalt base courses, ADT is relevant only when traffic can be controlled or detoured during construction; projects carrying significantly heavy truck traffic—defined as 200 or more daily ESALs—should not be selected for cold recycling, and the mixtures are not suitable for high‑ADT roads unless detours can be established until adequate structural strength is obtained. When two‑lane AADT exceeds 3,000, the use of polymer‑modified emulsions is recommended to improve early strength. In pavement design calculations, ADT serves as the required 24‑hour two‑directional vehicle count for determining total 18‑kip ESALs and therefore applies to all functional classifications of highways when rigorous ESAL calculations are performed. For new construction or rehabilitation on collectors and local roads, ADT may be used with the Simple Method provided that the resulting layer thicknesses do not exceed the minimum limits for those roadway types; however, the Simple Method must not be used on any roadways other than collectors and local roads. On cross routes that interchange with limited‑access freeways, if the design‑year ADT exceeds 8,000 vehicles per day, the Rigorous Method must be employed instead of the simple approach. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 110–123), Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157)

In Section 5.8, when the asphalt layer functions as a non‑wearing course, a leveling course, or a shoulder, the usual practice of selecting the Skid Resistance Level on the basis of anticipated initial ADT for new facilities or current ADT for resurfacing may be set aside on a project‑by‑project basis; consequently a design approach other than an ADT‑derived SRL must be used for those layers. Section 5.13 specifies that cold‑recycled base courses should not be chosen for projects that carry significantly heavy truck traffic, defined as 200 or more daily ESALs, and that roads with traffic volumes over 3,000 two‑lane AADT are recommended to employ polymer‑modified emulsions or an alternative design because the recycled mixes are less resistant to abrasion; additionally, the method may be used only where traffic can be controlled or detoured during base construction and curing, and if such control is not possible another pavement design should be preferred. Section 7.1 states that for collector or local roads the Simple Method may replace the rigorous ADT‑based ESAL calculation provided that the layer thicknesses obtained from the Simple Method do not exceed the minimum thickness limits for that roadway type, and that on rehabilitation projects the Simple Method is acceptable only when the required structural capacity does not exceed the existing pavement’s capacity; the Simple Method must not be applied to any roadways other than collectors and local roads, and if either the thickness limit for new construction or the capacity limit for rehabilitation is exceeded, the rigorous method must be used. For cross routes intersecting limited‑access freeways, a simple approach may be considered only when the projected design‑year ADT is 8,000 or less; once the design‑year ADT exceeds 8,000, the rigorous method becomes required. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 110–123), Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157)

Design inputs and requirements

PennDOT requires that the Skid Resistance Level assigned to the coarse aggregate in an asphalt wearing course be selected on the basis of the anticipated initial Average Daily Traffic for new facilities or the current Average Daily Traffic for resurfacing projects, following the guidelines in Table 5.4 of Chapter 5; the SRL designation shall be indicated on pavement design approval forms, typical sections, and contract proposals, and the design of inappropriately high SRL designations will be prohibited on non‑wearing courses, leveling courses, and shoulders, although a project may request an exception (section 5.8). Cold‑recycling projects must follow the Chapter 5 Subsection 5.13 guidelines in the Asphalt Concrete Pavement Guidelines and the design procedures of Publication 27 (Bulletin 27) for asphalt concrete mixtures; projects carrying significantly heavy truck traffic—i.e., 200 or more daily ESALs—should not be selected for cold recycling, polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended when traffic volumes exceed 3,000 two‑lane AADT, and only select projects where traffic can be controlled or detoured during base construction and curing may be selected (section 5.13). The Bureau of Planning and Research supplies the initial ADT, annual growth rates, truck percentages, and directional factor for pavement design; for ESAL calculation the ADT must be a 24‑hour, two‑directional count taken at project opening with a default directional distribution of 50 % unless BPR specifies otherwise, lane‑distribution factors are 100 % for two‑lane, 90 % for four‑lane and 80 % for six‑or‑more‑lane highways, the Simple Method may be used only on collectors and local roads and must not cause layer thicknesses to exceed the minimums in Table 9.4, and cross routes with a design‑year ADT that exceeds 8,000 require the Rigorous ESAL method (section 7.1). [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 110–123), Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157)

Design methods and criteria

The user‑delay cost analysis begins by assembling the initial ADT, the design‑year ADT and the traffic mix by vehicle class; a directional factor and the total days of the activity are also required. From these inputs the Traffic Growth Factor is calculated, after which an adjusted ADT is produced for each activity year, for each direction, and for the portion of the ADT that is delayed, ultimately providing the Total Number of Vehicles Delayed During the Activity broken out by vehicle class. For pavement design the conversion of ADT to design traffic loads employs the Rigorous ESAL Calculation method implemented in DARWin. The method requires the two‑way ADT, the number of lanes in the design direction and a lane distribution factor that is set at 100 % for a two‑lane highway, 90 % for four lanes, and 80 % for six or more lanes. A directional distribution factor of normally 50 % is used unless the BPR directs otherwise or traffic data indicate a different value, and a factor of 100 % is applied for one‑way ADT counts. Vehicle classification percentages are entered together with annual growth rates for truck volume and for the truck ESAL factor; the compound growth rate calculation type selected in DARWin governs how these rates are combined. Where the project involves collectors or local roads, the Simple Method may be used provided that the resulting layer thicknesses do not exceed minimum limits; this alternative assumes a zero‑percent annual truck‑factor growth and applies truck‑volume growth as specified in Table 7.2. Both the Rigorous and Simple approaches rely on empirically derived ESAL factors for each vehicle class. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (p. 76), Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157)

In cold‑recycled base‑course projects the selection guidelines require that a project with significantly heavy truck traffic—defined as 200 or more daily ESALs—should not be selected for cold recycling, and they recommend using polymer‑modified emulsions when the two‑lane average daily traffic exceeds 3,000 vehicles, especially where there is a high percentage of trucks. For pavement design the average daily traffic (ADT) required for ESAL calculations must be a 24‑hour, two‑directional vehicle count taken for the project in the year it first opens to traffic; the directional factor applied to that ADT is 50% unless the BPR directs otherwise or traffic data shows this not to be the case, and a directional factor of 100% is used for one‑way ADT counts. The percent of trucks assigned to the design lane depends on lane configuration—100% for two‑lane highways, 90% for four‑lane highways, and 80% for highways with six or more lanes. For cross routes that interchange with limited‑access freeways, if the projected design‑year ADT exceeds 8,000 then the Rigorous Method must be used to calculate daily 18‑kip ESALs. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 121–123), Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157)

Structural section and dimensions

For cold‑recycled base courses, the specification calls for a minimum compacted depth of 3 inches and a maximum of 5 inches, and it requires that the existing pavement contain at least 2 inches of asphalt so that, with an additional 1 inch of reclaimed or virgin aggregate, the total thickness meets the 3‑inch minimum. These thickness limits apply to the recycled base layer selected under the ADT criteria in Section 5.13. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 121–123)

Materials and mixture design

When a new asphalt wearing course or a resurfacing operation is planned, the anticipated initial ADT for a new facility or the current ADT for an existing surface is used to determine the Skid Resistance Level (SRL) of the coarse aggregate in accordance with Table 5.4; the selected SRL must correspond to the traffic level so that the polishing resistance required to maintain adequate friction throughout the pavement’s service life is provided, and a design that specifies an SRL higher than required is prohibited on non‑wearing courses, leveling courses, and shoulders, although the contractor may supply aggregate with the specified SRL or better or an equivalent blend. For cold‑recycled asphalt base courses the ADT governs both the suitability of the recycling treatment and the type of binder that should be employed; projects that carry significantly heavy truck traffic, defined as 200 or more daily ESALs, should be excluded from a cold‑recycling approach, while when the two‑lane AADT exceeds 3,000 (or when there is a high percentage of trucks) polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended because they provide improved initial strength and durability, allowing the mixture to achieve sufficient structural capacity during curing, meet durability expectations despite higher abrasion susceptibility, and remain constructible by permitting faster early traffic reopening. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 110–123)

Structural details and interfaces

In cold‑recycled base courses the interface to the wearing layer is required to have a minimum one‑week curing period under low humidity and high temperature before the wearing course is placed, and because the mix has higher void content a wearing surface is imperative; the recycled layer shall be compacted to a depth between three and five inches. When traffic volumes exceed 3,000 two‑lane AADT or truck traffic is high, polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended to improve initial strength at this interface, while projects carrying significantly heavy truck traffic (200 or more daily ESALs) should not be selected for cold recycling. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 121–123)

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

When selecting a cold‑recycled base course, ADT is used together with environmental factors; projects carrying significantly heavy truck traffic (i.e., 200 or more daily ESALs) should not be selected for cold recycling and any road that has deteriorated because of poor drainage conditions should be excluded. Polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended where early or late season recycling may impair initial curing, especially when traffic volumes exceed 3,000 two‑lane AADT or a high percentage of trucks is present, thereby linking ADT thresholds to moisture and climate considerations. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 121–123)

Verification and expected performance

The completed ADT should be verified against the selection criteria in Section 5.13, confirming that the project does not exceed the heavy‑truck threshold of 200 or more daily ESALs and that any traffic volume over 3,000 two‑lane AADT triggers the use of polymer‑modified emulsions to meet performance expectations; additionally, it must be shown that traffic can be controlled or detoured during base construction and curing so that constructability and consistency with project requirements are maintained. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 121–123)

The expected performance of a pavement wearing course, as driven by the anticipated ADT, is to maintain an adequate level of surface friction for the entire service life so that safe driving conditions are preserved. This friction requirement is satisfied by selecting a coarse‑aggregate Skid Resistance Level (SRL) appropriate to the initial or current ADT and confirming it with friction testing; PennDOT uses the AASHTO T 242 test method to generate the friction values that support the SRL designation and its periodic reevaluation. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (p. 110)
