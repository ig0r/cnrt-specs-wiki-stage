Shrinkage Cracking

Part of: Cracking is-subtopic-of
Shrinkage Cracking is a topic under Cracking.
shrinkage-cracking
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Shrinkage cracking is a distress that manifests in Portland cement concrete pavements, most commonly observed when high‑early‑strength (HES) concrete is used for full‑depth repairs or patches and in the chemically stabilized layer of a Full Depth Reclamation (FDR) base whose unconfined compressive strength exceeds nine hundred pounds per square inch [1][2]. The distress is attributed to material‑related deficiencies rather than traffic loading, climate, moisture, drainage, or design and construction shortcomings, with the use of high early‑strength (HES) concrete—identified as a materials‑selection issue that may cause excessive shrinkage cracking—and chemically stabilized material whose unconfined compressive strength exceeds 900 psi both linked to this distress [1][2]. When shrinkage cracking occurs it compromises the intended structural capacity and durability of the pavement, triggering rework to satisfy density and strength acceptance criteria as stipulated in the relevant specifications [2].

Where it occurs

Shrinkage cracking is reported as a distress associated with Portland cement concrete work, particularly when high‑early‑strength (HES) concrete is employed for full‑depth repairs or patches; the guidance advises avoiding HES concrete because of its potential for excessive shrinkage cracking and recommends using accelerated or precast concrete mixes that are not subject to this problem [1]. In addition, shrinkage cracking is noted to develop in the chemically stabilized layer of a Full Depth Reclamation (FDR) base when the unconfined compressive strength of that material exceeds nine hundred pounds per square inch, as specified in the acceptance criteria for chemically stabilized FDR [2]. Neither source mentions shrinkage cracking occurring in asphalt surface courses, composite pavements, overlays, or shoulders [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 227–229)

Causes and mechanisms

Shrinkage cracking is attributed to material‑related deficiencies rather than traffic loading, climate, moisture, drainage, or design and construction shortcomings [1][2]. The Pavement Policy Manual advises that the use of high early strength (HES) concrete should be avoided because it may cause excessive shrinkage cracking, indicating a materials‑selection issue [1]. Similarly, the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that chemically stabilized material that develops an unconfined compressive strength exceeding 900 psi may result in shrinkage cracking, linking the distress to excessive material strength and thus to a construction or materials deficiency [2]. Neither source includes a tabular classification of distresses by cause, so no table entry for shrinkage cracking is provided [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 227–229)

Effects on performance

According to the Full‑Depth Reclamation specifications, chemically stabilized material that develops an unconfined compressive strength greater than 900 psi may develop shrinkage cracking; when this occurs the pavement must be reworked to satisfy the density and strength acceptance criteria, indicating a loss of structural capacity and durability if left untreated. The need for rework reflects that shrinkage cracking compromises the intended performance of the reclaimed base, although the source does not comment on ride quality, safety, or service‑life impacts beyond the requirement to correct the distress. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 227–229)

When chemically stabilized full‑depth reclamation attains a compressive strength greater than 900 psi, shrinkage cracking may occur and the affected lot is treated as a failure that triggers rework under Section 344.3(g); the failed area must be identified, cores taken to delineate the extent of damage, a new mix design approved, and the portion reclaimed again with additional stabilizing material before any core holes are filled with an approved repair material per Bulletin 15. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 227–229)

Repairs and treatments

The Pavement Policy Manual advises that high early strength concrete should be avoided because it may cause excessive shrinkage cracking[1]. When an early opening to traffic is required, the manual recommends accelerated‑concrete pavement patching as a treatment for shrinkage cracking concerns (Section B Accelerated Concrete Pavement Patching)[1]. In situations where minimum project time or work‑zone safety are of paramount concern, precast concrete paving systems may be used as an alternative to accelerated concrete in patching applications that could otherwise be susceptible to shrinkage cracking (Section C Precast Concrete Paving)[1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications prescribe reworking a failed area by reclaiming it again with additional stabilizing material as the repair for shrinkage cracking[2]. This re‑reclamation is required when chemically stabilized full‑depth reclamation attains an unconfined compressive strength greater than 900 psi, because such high strength may result in shrinkage cracking (Section 344.3(g) Acceptance)[2]. The specification further states that failed areas must be reclaimed again with the additional stabilizing material as necessary to achieve the required acceptance criteria[2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 227–229)

In situations where the pavement distress indicates a potential for excessive shrinkage cracking, the Pavement Policy Manual specifies that high‑early‑strength concrete shall be avoided for patching under section 4.2 A of the concrete pavement patching guidelines [1]. For those same conditions, the manual permits the use of accelerated concrete when an early traffic opening is required because it is not identified as being prone to excessive shrinkage cracking, and also cites precast concrete pavement as an alternative that is not subject to excessive shrinkage cracking [1]. In full‑depth reclamation work, the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that if a chemically stabilized reclaimed material attains an unconfined compressive strength greater than 900 psi at seven days, shrinkage cracking may develop and the prescribed remedy is rework of the failed area with additional stabilizing material in accordance with section 344.3(g) Acceptance [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 227–229)

Prevention in design and construction

The Pavement Policy Manual advises that high early‑strength (HES) concrete should be avoided because it may cause excessive shrinkage cracking; this recommendation applies whenever HES concrete would otherwise be selected for a patch [1]. If an early traffic opening is required, the designer should consider accelerated concrete, which can achieve opening‑to‑traffic strength in 4 to 7 hours and does not carry the same shrinkage‑cracking risk [1]. As another alternative that is not subject to excessive shrinkage cracking, precast concrete pavement may be employed in place of HES concrete for patch work [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require that chemically stabilized full‑depth reclaimed material be limited to an average unconfined compressive strength below 900 psi; exceeding this threshold may result in shrinkage cracking and therefore triggers rework as stipulated in the acceptance criteria [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 227–229)

In the Concrete Pavement Patching guidance (Section 4.2), the source advises avoiding high early‑strength (HES) concrete because it may cause excessive shrinkage cracking; when a rapid traffic opening is required, accelerated concrete is recommended as an alternative that does not carry the same shrinkage risk; and precast concrete pavement is also presented as a material option that is not subject to excessive shrinkage cracking, serving as another way to mitigate this distress. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89)

Specification limits and acceptance

In Section 344.3(g) Acceptance for Full Depth Reclamation, shrinkage cracking is limited by the compressive‑strength test of chemically stabilized material; the material shall be tested for unconfined compressive strength using specimens prepared according to ASTM D1633 and if the 7‑day strength is greater than 900 pounds per square inch, shrinkage cracking may occur and rework will be required. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 227–229)

When chemically stabilized material tests above 900 psi and shrinkage cracking is indicated, the specification requires rework of the affected lot by reclaiming it with additional stabilizing material as outlined in Section 344.3(e); the source does not specify a particular party or a time limit for completing that remedial action. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 227–229)
