Crack Sealing

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Crack Sealing is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
crack-sealing
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-23

Crack sealing is a routine‑maintenance treatment applied to pavement surfaces where visible cracks have formed but before the distress has progressed to require more extensive rehabilitation. By placing a sealant into the fissures—after cleaning and preparing the substrate under ambient temperatures of at least about 50 °F—the method blocks water and debris infiltration, thereby retarding further deterioration such as raveling or loss of structural integrity and preserving functional condition[1]. The treatment is scheduled on a three‑to‑five‑year cycle, identified through automated condition surveys and visual inspections, and must be completed at least twenty‑four hours before any subsequent paving to allow proper curing[1].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Crack sealing is a routine‑maintenance treatment applied to address surface cracking identified in pavement condition surveys. By sealing cracks, water and debris are kept out of the fissures, which helps retard further deterioration such as raveling or loss of structural integrity. The practice is emphasized on the Interstate system and performed cyclically to preserve functional condition. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Crack sealing is listed under routine‑maintenance activities, which are defined as actions that repair and/or retard existing distress. Because it is a repair‑oriented treatment, the primary purpose of crack sealing is to fix damage in the pavement surface while also slowing further deterioration, thereby helping preserve the pavement’s functional condition. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Distress characterization and causes

Crack sealing targets distress that originates from water and debris infiltrating pavement cracks, a problem that is intensified when the supporting layers are deficient; for instance, premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements may be caused by inadequate subbase material. Aggregate‑related deficiencies such as Vanport Limestone concerns or alkali‑silica reaction also generate hidden deterioration that accelerates crack formation. By applying sealants on a regular 3 to 5 year cycle for asphalt surfaces, the maintenance program blocks infiltration and slows further damage. [1]

Condition assessment and timing

The need for crack sealing is identified through the annual automated pavement condition survey (videologging) that records cracking and other distresses; the resulting condition data are entered into the treatment‑matrix system, which flags sections where routine maintenance—specifically crack sealing—is indicated. When the matrix signals a crack‑related treatment or when visual field inspections reveal cracks not captured by the survey, those locations are scheduled for sealant application according to the prescribed 3‑to‑5‑year cycle. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Crack sealing is applied as a routine preservation measure when visible cracks appear in an asphalt surface but the pavement has not yet progressed to a condition that would require more extensive preservation, rehabilitation, or reconstruction. The treatment follows a three‑to‑five‑year cycle, with particular emphasis on sections that have reached five years of service without having been sealed; these sections are reviewed closely and prioritized for sealing. Intervening at this early stage—once cracks are evident but before they deepen or widen—retards further deterioration and aligns with the prescribed maintenance schedule for the resurfacing network. [1]

Materials and repair details

Crack‑sealing performance is sensitive to ambient temperature, requiring a minimum of about 50 °F for effective placement, and it must be done well before any new paving—at least a full day in advance—to allow the sealant to set. The existing pavement surface also matters: all thermoplastic or preformed markings should be stripped, epoxy and polyester paints need to be abraded to expose a clean, roughened substrate, and the area must be thoroughly cleaned so that no debris interferes with bond strength. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 108–109)

Treatment procedures

Crack sealing must be completed at least twenty‑four hours before any paving work begins, and the sealant should be applied neatly so that no excess material remains on the surface, which is essential for the performance of the subsequent ultra‑thin wearing course. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 108–109)

Crack sealing must be scheduled at least a day before any paving work, and it can only be performed when the ambient temperature is 50 °F or higher and within the seasonal windows set by the standard specifications. The source does not specify particular equipment for crack sealing nor impose explicit traffic restrictions during the sealing operation. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 108–109)

Quality and workmanship

A satisfactory crack seal is confirmed by meeting both timing and visual‑appearance criteria: the sealing must be finished at least twenty‑four hours before any paving activity to allow proper curing, and the treated area must look neat with no large amount of sealant remaining on the pavement surface. Meeting these conditions indicates the seal will protect the pavement without compromising the subsequent UTWC performance. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 108–109)

Performance and service life

Crack sealing is treated as routine maintenance because it repairs or slows existing distress, helping to keep the pavement in good condition and maintain its functional performance. When applied to asphalt surfaces on a three‑to‑five‑year cycle, crack sealing preserves the pavement’s durability and delays more severe deterioration, thereby extending the expected service life of the roadway. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Monitoring and follow-up

Signs that a crack‑sealed area is no longer performing adequately include the appearance of potholes or loss of aggregate (raveling) especially where reflective cracks are present. When such distress becomes evident, it indicates that the seal has failed and additional maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation should be considered. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 108–109)
