Crack Sealing

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Crack Sealing is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
crack-sealing
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-27

Crack sealing is a routine‑maintenance treatment applied when surface cracking is identified in pavement condition surveys, aimed at keeping water and debris out of fissures that would otherwise accelerate deterioration such as raveling or loss of structural integrity. The method targets non‑structural cracks—plastic shrinkage, drying shrinkage, temperature‑related cracking, finishing tears, joints and random cracks—whose openings typically exceed about 0.007 in to 0.016 in in width and a depth of at least one‑eighth inch (up to a quarter‑inch for some specifications). After thorough cleaning and drying, the fissure is filled with a high‑modulus hot‑mix methacrylate sealant (or epoxy resin for structural concrete cracks) forced to flush level, thereby restoring surface continuity and retarding further moisture‑induced distress. [1][2]

Purpose and treatment objectives

Crack sealing is a routine‑maintenance treatment applied when surface cracking is identified in pavement condition surveys, with the purpose of keeping water and debris out of the fissures that can otherwise accelerate deterioration such as raveling or loss of structural integrity. The treatment targets non‑structural cracks—including plastic shrinkage, drying shrinkage, temperature‑related cracking, finishing‑induced tears, longitudinal and transverse joints, and random cracks—whose openings are large enough to admit moisture and particulate matter. In flexible pavements the cracks of concern typically have depths exceeding roughly one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch and widths between about 0.007 in and 0.016 in, while in concrete surfaces the acceptable range extends up to an inch wide or at least 1/16 in for joint and crack sealing. By cleaning, drying, and filling these fissures with a high‑modulus hot‑mix methacrylate sealant (or epoxy resin for structural concrete cracks), the surface continuity is restored and further moisture‑induced distress is mitigated. Across the Interstate system and other major networks, cyclic crack sealing—often combined with joint sealing—is emphasized as a key activity to preserve functional condition and prevent progression of cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, and related distresses. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 314), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 464), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (pp. 1075–1192)

Crack sealing is classified among routine‑maintenance actions whose dual aim is to repair existing surface distress and to retard its further development, thereby maintaining the pavement’s functional condition [1]. The treatment is applied to non‑structural cracks that become visible when they exceed prescribed dimensions—typically deeper than one‑eighth inch, or deeper than one‑quarter inch if their width lies between 0.007 and 0.016 inch [2]. Before sealant placement the crack must be thoroughly cleaned and kept dry so that the material can fully penetrate the fissure [2]. The guides specify the use of a high‑modulus hot‑mix penetrating sealant or filler, supplied by the same manufacturer as the associated overlay system, which is forced into the void until it completely fills the crack and leaves the surface flush with the surrounding pavement [2]. By filling the fissure to flush level the surface condition is restored, satisfying the repair objective while simultaneously limiting water and debris ingress that would otherwise accelerate deterioration [1][2]. The combined effect of damage removal and retardation of further distress contributes to extending the service life of the pavement structure [1].

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (pp. 1075–1110)

Distress characterization and causes

The distress is located by measuring each crack’s ends against permanent reference points, and its extent is quantified through width, depth, and length measurements. Severity is classified as non‑structural cracks that exceed a depth of one‑quarter inch and have widths between 0.007 in and 0.016 in, which are considered defects in material or workmanship. The specification requires these cracks to be sealed with a penetrating filler before further deterioration, but the excerpt does not provide information on how quickly the cracks progress or any direct safety or operational impacts. [2]

[2] — SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (pp. 1075–1076)

Crack sealing is employed because water and debris can enter pavement through cracks, a process that accelerates deterioration when the supporting layers are deficient [1][2]. In jointed concrete pavements, premature mid‑slab cracking may arise from an inadequate subbase material, providing a conduit for moisture infiltration [1]. Aggregate‑related deficiencies such as concerns with Vanport Limestone and the occurrence of alkali‑silica reaction can produce hidden deterioration that further promotes crack development [1]. Non‑structural cracking mechanisms intrinsic to cementitious layers include plastic shrinkage and drying shrinkage, which generate fine cracks as moisture is lost from the material [2]. Temperature‑induced expansion and contraction create movement‑related fissures that likewise open pathways for water entry [2]. Surface tears introduced during finishing or texturing operations add additional openings that facilitate infiltration [2]. Defects associated with materials or workmanship—such as rain exposure before an overlay has cured, an improper finish, inadequate curing, honeycombing of the concrete, and premature loading of a latex surface before it reaches minimum strength—exacerbate existing cracks and allow deeper water penetration [2]. When these mechanisms produce visible surface openings deeper than one‑eighth inch and wider than 0.007 to 0.016 inch, subgrade saturation, loss of bond, and progressive pavement distress are accelerated [2]. For asphalt‑surfaced pavements, applying a sealant on a regular three‑to‑five year cycle blocks infiltration and slows further damage [1].

Applicability and treatment selection

Crack sealing is deemed appropriate on rigid‑type pavements—including Portland cement concrete overlays, bridge decks, approach slabs, and other concrete surfaces—when the observed cracking is non‑structural, such as plastic or drying shrinkage, temperature‑induced cracks, or surface tears produced during finishing. The individual cracks must meet dimensional limits: for thin surface cracks the depth must exceed one‑eighth inch and the width must lie between approximately 0.007 in and 0.016 in; for longitudinal cracks or joints that are to be routed, a finished slot of about three‑quarters of an inch wide by one inch deep (or up to one inch wide after sawing or grinding) is required. Prior to sealant placement the cracks must be thoroughly cleaned, free of dust, dirt and debris, kept dry, and protected from moisture; joint seals and scuppers on bridge decks must also be shielded against sealer infiltration. Environmental conditions dictate that ambient air and pavement surface temperatures be at least 40 °F for most concrete work, while the high‑modulus water‑modified (HMWM) sealant used on bridge decks performs best when substrate temperature is between 50 °F and 100 °F, preferably during the coolest part of the day when cracks are widest. A Corrective Action Plan confirming that ambient and substrate temperature and moisture conditions satisfy the sealant data sheet must be approved before work begins. Traffic must be prohibited from the treated area until the HMWM sealer has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 424–464), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (pp. 1110–1191)

Crack sealing is only appropriate for shallow, narrow non‑structural cracks that meet a series of dimensional, material, and environmental criteria; when any of these limits are exceeded a more extensive repair such as removal and replacement must be used. Structural cracking in a Portland cement concrete overlay automatically disqualifies the crack from sealing and requires the affected area to be removed and replaced. For non‑structural cracks the sealant can be applied only if the depth does not exceed about one‑eighth inch and the width falls between roughly 0.007 in and 0.016 in; cracks deeper than a quarter‑inch or wider than 0.016 in are considered unsuitable for sealing and must be repaired by removal and replacement. In addition, the crack (or longitudinal joint) must be routed to at least three‑quarters of an inch wide and one inch deep before sealant placement; if this preparation cannot be achieved, another maintenance technique should be selected. The substrate surface must be thoroughly cleaned and kept dry, and sealing may not proceed until a Corrective Action Plan or Quality Control plan—including the appropriate crack‑filler data sheet and verified ambient/substrate temperature and moisture conditions—has been reviewed and approved by the Representative or System Provider. Ambient air or pavement surface temperatures below forty degrees Fahrenheit prevent proper adhesion of conventional sealants, while epoxy injection systems require temperatures of at least fifty degrees Fahrenheit for cure; under colder conditions a different repair method must be employed. Transverse joints and transverse cracks are excluded from cross‑stitching and sealing procedures and must be treated by an alternative repair approach. Finally, if the wearing surface exhibits severe deficiencies—such as inadequate tolerance, improper air content, insufficient compressive strength, failure to bond to the substrate, bonding to unsound concrete, rain damage, honeycombing, or other serious defects—the sealant will not perform adequately and removal and replacement of the surface is required. [2]

Condition assessment and timing

Determining the need for crack sealing begins with an annual automated pavement condition survey—often called videologging—that records cracking along with other distresses such as rutting and edge deterioration, and the resulting data are entered into a treatment‑matrix system that flags those sections where routine maintenance, specifically crack sealing, is indicated [1]. Because the automated survey may miss some surface cracks, a qualified inspector or project representative also performs a visual field inspection of the pavement overlay to identify any additional cracking or surface tears that were not captured in the survey data [1][2]. When a crack is identified, its geometry must be quantified; the width, depth and length of each crack are measured and the end points are recorded relative to permanent reference points on the project site [2]. Depth is normally obtained with a mechanical probe, but if the probe cannot provide a reliable measurement, coring is required; two cores are taken at the location, one is sent to an independent laboratory and the other to the Laboratory Testing Services or the representative for detailed analysis to determine whether the cracking is structural or non‑structural [2]. The guides specify that only visible, non‑structural cracks that exceed a depth of one‑eighth inch and have widths between 0.007 in and 0.016 in meet the threshold for high‑modulus water‑based membrane penetrating crack sealing, provided a corrective action plan has been approved [2]. Once the thresholds are satisfied, the treatment is scheduled according to the prescribed three‑to‑five‑year cycle for routine crack sealing of asphalt‑surfaced pavements [1].

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (pp. 1075–1192)

Crack sealing is applied as a routine preservation measure when visible cracks appear in an asphalt surface but before the pavement has deteriorated to require more extensive rehabilitation, and it is also limited to non‑structural cracks such as plastic shrinkage, drying shrinkage, temperature‑related cracking, or surface tears caused by finishing [1][2]. The guides specify that the treatment should be performed on a three‑to‑five‑year cycle, with particular attention given to sections that have reached five years of service without prior sealing and are therefore reviewed closely for priority sealing [1]. For cement‑concrete pavements the trigger is when a crack opens to at least one‑sixteenth of an inch (≈0.062 in) and its width falls between roughly 0.007 in and 0.016 in, with depth thresholds of one‑eighth inch or, in some specifications, one‑quarter inch [2]. Longitudinal cracks or joints must be routed to a geometry of about three‑quarters of an inch wide and one inch deep before the sealant is placed [2]. The sealant used is the high‑modulus water‑based (HMWM) penetrating crack filler supplied by the same manufacturer as the overlay system, and it may only be applied when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at least 40 °F to ensure proper curing [2]. In all cases the crack faces must be thoroughly cleaned, kept dry, and covered if necessary until sealing is performed [2]. Consequently, the condition for applying crack sealing combines the presence of non‑structural surface cracks that meet these dimensional limits with a service age reviewed on a three‑to‑five‑year cycle (especially after five years without prior treatment) and suitable environmental temperatures [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 11 (pp. 193–194), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 424–464), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (pp. 1075–1110)

Materials and repair details

Crack sealing begins with thorough cleaning of each crack or joint, removing debris, moisture and contaminants and confirming cleanliness with a wipe test in the presence of the Representative. The opening is then routed to create a reservoir whose dimensions depend on the existing size: longitudinal joints are typically widened to three‑quarters of an inch and deepened to one inch, larger openings may be enlarged to up to one inch wide by 1¼ inches deep, and surface cracks deeper than one‑eighth inch (or one‑quarter inch where specified) with widths between .007 and .016 inch are also prepared. For concrete pavements the minimum crack width is 1/16 inch. When required a backer rod and a bond‑breaker tape are placed on the bottom of the reservoir, the tape not extending more than 1/8 in up the face; epoxy‑bonded tie bars may be installed by injecting epoxy into drilled holes. Six dowel bars are positioned in each treated joint or crack as part of the configuration for bridge decks and approach slabs.

The sealant material is selected according to project specifications. Approved hot‑pour or silicone sealants are used where indicated, while a high‑modulus water‑modified (HMWM) penetrating filler/sealer supplied by the same manufacturer as the Portland cement concrete overlay is required for many surface cracks. The HMWM system is a low‑odor wet‑mix containing a methacrylate resin, an initiator that includes a metal drier and peroxide, and a promoter; it may also be used on bridge decks and approach slabs. Asphalt‑based sealants listed in Bulletin 15 or 41 are applied to cracks from one‑quarter inch to one inch wide, and openings wider than one inch are filled with a 4.75 mm asphalt wearing course. Preformed neoprene seals are installed with the manufacturer’s adhesive lubricant when specified, and epoxy resin systems are mixed for at least 180 seconds on a low‑speed (400–600 rpm) drill using an approved paddle before injection.

Placement is permitted only when ambient air and pavement surface temperatures are at least 40 °F; HMWM applications require substrate temperatures between 50 °F and 100 °F, and epoxy injections are allowed only above 50 °F. The sealant is worked into the crack until it is flush with or slightly recessed (approximately one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the surface). A thin film of sealant about 1/32 to 1/16 inch thick and extending one to three inches wide is then wiped level. For HMWM sealants sand meeting AASHTO T‑27 specifications—no less than 85 % passing a No. 8 sieve and at least 90 % retained on a No. 200 sieve, kiln‑dried and kept dry—is broadcast over the still‑tacky surface at a minimum rate of one pound per square yard, providing an abrasive finish; coverage must be at least 100 ft² per gallon. Injection ports are glued in place at manufacturer‑recommended spacings, sealed with a compatible surface seal, and entry ports are plugged as flow appears; any remaining holes are patched after cure.

All repair actions—including cleaning, routing, placement of backer rod or tape, installation of dowel bars, application of the selected sealant, sand broadcast, and port sealing—must be documented in a Corrective Action Plan or Quality Control and Action Plan that includes the material data sheet, allowable temperature and moisture conditions, and receives Representative approval prior to placement. Traffic is prohibited until the sealant has fully cured, and excess sand is removed before reopening the pavement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 314), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–464), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (pp. 1075–1192)

Ambient temperature requirement for placement of crack sealants is a critical factor; epoxy‑based systems should not be placed when the ambient air or concrete temperature is below 50 °F, while high‑modulus moisture‑resistant (HMWM) penetrating fillers require both air and pavement surface temperatures of at least 40 °F and are generally limited to no higher than 90 °F, although bridge decks may accept substrate temperatures up to 100 °F. [1][2] In addition, the sealant must be applied well before any subsequent paving operation, with a minimum interval of one full day (24 hours) to allow the material to cure and achieve bond strength. [1] The existing pavement surface must be prepared by removing all thermoplastic or preformed markings, abrading epoxy and polyester paints to expose a clean, roughened substrate, and thoroughly cleaning the area so that no debris, dust, rust, scale, grease, or moisture remains; cleaning may be accomplished with compressed air at a pressure of at least 100 psi or by water flushing followed by complete drying and a wipe test. [1][2] Cracks to be sealed must be open, dry, and free of foreign material, with widths ranging from 0.007 in to 0.016 in and depths generally exceeding one‑eighth inch (some specifications call for at least a quarter‑inch depth); the joint faces and reservoirs should be cleared laterally 4–6 in and routed to create a half‑inch deep reservoir before sealant placement. [2] For flexible pavements, the preferred material is an HMWM penetrating filler that consists of a low‑odor resin, an initiator (typically a metal drier together with peroxide) and a promoter formulated exactly as specified by the manufacturer; the sealant must be obtained from an approved source, stored according to supplier recommendations, heated within the temperature limits printed on its container, and kept at pouring temperature for no longer than six hours. [2] When an epoxy system is selected, it must conform to the manufacturer’s data sheet, be mixed for a minimum of 180 seconds at a low speed of 400–600 rpm with a proportioning tolerance of ±5 %, and be applied only when ambient or concrete temperatures are at least 50 °F. [2] Preformed neoprene seals may be used provided the adhesive prescribed by the maker is applied, and the seal is not stretched more than three percent nor compressed longitudinally more than two percent, otherwise bond failure can occur. [2] For rigid pavements, an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant must be selected and installed precisely according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the specific joint type, ensuring chemical compatibility with both transverse and longitudinal joints. [2] Compatibility between the crack‑sealing material and any Portland‑cement overlay is achieved by using a sealant from the same manufacturer as the overlay system, employing pre‑mixed resin‑initiator blends supplied by that manufacturer, and preventing leakage or spillage of foreign substances during placement. [2] When HMWM sealants are placed, broadcast sand must be applied at a minimum rate of one pound per square yard; the sand shall meet gradation limits of at least 85 % passing a No. 8 sieve and no less than 90 % retained on a No. 200 sieve, be kiln‑dried, kept dry until use, and any excess removed before traffic returns. [2] Adhering to these material properties, compatibility requirements, and pavement‑condition criteria enables the sealant to achieve proper adhesion, fill the crack to a depth of typically one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the surface, and provide long‑term durability that extends the service life of the pavement. [1][2]

Treatment procedures

Crack sealing is scheduled to be finished no less than twenty‑four hours before any subsequent paving activity so that the sealant can set and will not interfere with the ultra‑thin wearing course. The process begins by marking all cracks identified for treatment, after which each crack is thoroughly cleaned of scale, dirt, curing compounds, rust, grease or other foreign material using methods such as media‑blasting, sweeping, vacuuming or a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi that will not damage the substrate. The cleaned faces must be completely dry and pass a quality‑control wipe test before any sealant is placed. When specified, bond‑breaker tape and appropriately sized backer rod are positioned at the bottom of the joint reservoir to provide a clean break and support for the sealant. Cracks are then routed or sawed to the prescribed dimensions—typically three‑quarters of an inch wide by one inch deep for longitudinal joints, or a half‑inch deep reservoir for narrower cracks—and immediately flushed with water to remove loose debris. The water is removed and the crack is dried with compressed air or a hot‑air lance, taking care not to overheat the surrounding pavement. All joint seals and scuppers are protected from infiltration prior to sealant application, and a detailed quality‑control and corrective‑action plan that includes the manufacturer’s data sheet, ambient and substrate temperature limits, and moisture conditions must be submitted and approved before work proceeds. The selected sealant—whether a hot‑pour silicone, a high‑modulus water‑based (HMWM) penetrating sealer, an asphalt crack sealant, or an epoxy resin system—is heated or mixed according to the producer’s instructions and kept within the specified pouring temperature range (generally 40 °F to 90 °F for asphalt sealants and 50 °F to 100 °F for HMWM products) and must be placed within its allowable use window, typically no more than six hours at temperature. Sealant is then introduced into the prepared crack until its surface lies one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the pavement, or level with the surface when a flush finish is required, and any excess material on the surrounding pavement is brushed off to achieve a neat job that leaves no large amount of material on the surface. For HMWM and asphalt sealants, sand is broadcast immediately after placement at a minimum rate of one pound per square yard (or sufficient to provide an abrasive finish) and later removed before traffic returns; typical coverage rates are about 100 ft² per gallon. The pavement may not be reopened to traffic until the sealant has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations, which for epoxy systems includes allowing the surface sealer to harden before injection, mixing the resin for at least 180 seconds at 400–600 rpm, and curing at temperatures above 50 °F. Throughout the operation cracks are kept covered, dry, and protected until the sealant has cured to the satisfaction of the representative. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 108–109); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 314), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–464), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (pp. 1075–1192)

Crack sealing must be scheduled at least 24 hours before any paving work and performed only within the seasonal windows prescribed by standard specifications. [1] Prior to mobilizing crews a pre‑approved quality‑control plan, often called a Corrective Action or Quality Control and Action Plan, must be submitted and accepted, including the manufacturer’s data sheet and the required ambient and substrate temperature and moisture conditions. [2] The first field step is to locate each crack, measure its width (0.007 in. to 0.016 in.) and depth (minimum 1⁄8 in., with some specifications calling for at least ¼ in.), and core it when necessary to verify that it meets the size limits for non‑structural repair. [2] Cracks are then cleaned of debris, dried, and protected from contamination; cleaning may be done with hand brushes or with a high‑pressure compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi, and when moisture persists a hot‑compressed‑air lance can be employed. [2] For new concrete joints the surface is inspected with a quality‑control wipe test, backer‑rod or bond‑breaker tape is installed as required, and any second‑stage saw cuts are completed before sealant placement. [2] The sealing material selected depends on pavement type, and the associated equipment reflects that choice: hot‑pour or silicone joint sealants for concrete are heated in an indirect double‑boiler system; high‑modulus water‑based (HMWM) penetrating sealer is mixed according to the manufacturer’s instructions and applied with a broom or squeegee; epoxy anchoring systems require a low‑speed electric drill equipped with an approved mixing paddle for at least 180 seconds, followed by injection from a hand‑held gun, pressure pot, or calibrated machine. [2] When cross‑stitching is required on rigid pavements, a hydraulic powered drill—or a gang‑mounted rig—drills angled holes after a full‑depth saw cut and the joint is routed to approximately three‑quarters of an inch wide and one inch deep before sealant application. [2] Temperature limits constitute the primary environmental constraint; all sealants must be placed when both ambient air and pavement surface temperatures are at least 40 °F, and placement above 90 °F is prohibited unless a written waiver is obtained. [2] Certain materials impose higher minima: hot‑pour sealants may not be used below 40 °F, epoxy systems cannot be mixed or injected if the ambient or concrete temperature falls below 50 °F, and HMWM penetrating sealer on bridge decks requires substrate temperatures between 50 °F and 100 °F, preferably during the coolest part of the day when cracks are most open. [2] Some specifications further restrict work to periods when the ambient temperature is 50 °F or higher, reinforcing the need to coordinate sealing activities with seasonal windows. [1][2] After sealant placement the material is leveled with the pavement surface, excess is removed, and a sand coat of at least one pound per square yard is broadcast while the sealer remains fluid, after which the sand is swept off. [2] The sealed section may not be opened to traffic until the sealant or any anchoring material has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations; for new concrete slabs this also requires that the initial concrete strength has been achieved and all joints have been sealed before construction equipment or vehicles are permitted on the slab. [2] No explicit traffic restriction is imposed by the other guide, but the overall practice therefore follows the curing‑based opening criteria described above. [1][2]

Quality and workmanship

Inspection of crack‑sealing operations begins with verification that the sealant material conforms to the manufacturer’s data sheet and originates from an approved source such as Bulletin 15 or 41. The inspector confirms that the product possesses the required viscosity, low‑odor HMWM composition (resin, initiator, promoter), and meets the specified coverage rate of at least 100 ft² per gallon. All components must be stored according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with containers kept leak‑free, and the broadcast sand used for the abrasive finish must satisfy AASHTO T27 gradation limits, be kiln‑dried and protected from moisture until placement.

Prior to any application the cracks or joints are measured for width, depth and length, and their end locations are recorded relative to permanent reference points. Cracks deeper than ¼ in and between 0.007 in and 0.016 in wide (or surface cracks exceeding 1⁄8 in depth) are identified for sealing with the HMWM filler. Where required, the crack is routed to a reservoir typically three‑quarters of an inch wide by one inch deep (or up to one inch wide by 1¼ inches deep for larger cracks), flushed with water, and then thoroughly cleaned of scale, dirt, curing compound, temporary sealant or any foreign material. The faces must be completely dry; if compressed air is used it shall be at least 100 psi measured at the source. A quality‑control wipe test prescribed by PTM 643 is performed immediately before placement and repeated until a pass is documented.

The preparatory stage also includes checking that backer‑rod and tape bond‑breaker are installed within tolerance (no more than 1⁄8 in of tape projection) and that any joint seals or scuppers are protected. The contractor’s quality‑control plan, including the corrective‑action plan that references the HMWM data sheet and specifies allowable ambient and substrate temperature and moisture conditions, must be reviewed and approved before work begins.

During installation the inspector verifies that ambient air and pavement surface temperatures are at least 40 °F (with many specifications requiring 50–100 °F for hot‑pours) and that no single batch of material remains at pouring temperature for longer than six hours. The sealant is placed directly into open cracks with a broom or squeegee, filled to the level shown on the drawings—generally leaving the surface finish between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the pavement—and excess material is removed. Sand is broadcast over the fresh seal at a minimum rate of one pound per square yard, then swept clean before traffic returns.

After placement the work is inspected for proper cure; the representative from the sealer manufacturer must confirm that mixing and application procedures were followed, that the sealant has fully cured, and that all loose sand has been cleared. Final acceptance is recorded only when the seal meets the documented fill level, surface finish, temperature, moisture and cleanliness criteria, and any deviations are noted as defects. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 314), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–464), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (pp. 1075–1191)

The treatment is deemed satisfactory only when it is completed at least twenty‑four hours prior to any subsequent paving operation, allowing the sealant sufficient curing time [1]. In addition, the finished crack must present a neat appearance with no visible excess material remaining on the pavement surface, which signals that the seal will protect the underlying structure without impairing later overlay performance [1]. A strict cleaning protocol precedes placement; all scale, dirt, curing compound, temporary joint‑sealing material and any other foreign matter must be removed, the faces dried, and both surfaces verified free of dust, debris or moisture by a QC wipe test performed in the presence of the Representative at two locations on each joint, with cleaning repeated until the visual standard is achieved [2]. Placement may not occur unless ambient air temperature is forty degrees Fahrenheit or higher and pavement surface temperature meets the specified limits—generally not below forty degrees for any sealant, while hot‑poured or high‑modulus water‑modified (HMWM) materials require a substrate temperature between fifty and one hundred degrees, never exceeding ninety degrees without special approval, and epoxy systems demand at least fifty degrees [2]. The dimensions of the crack or joint dictate the sealant type: visible non‑structural surface cracks or tears deeper than one‑eighth inch (or where specified deeper than one‑quarter inch) with widths between .007 in and .016 in are treated with an HMWM penetrating sealant supplied by the same manufacturer as the Portland cement concrete overlay, whereas joints routed to approximately three‑quarters of an inch wide and one inch deep (up to 1¼ in deep for larger openings) also qualify for HMWM treatment; cracks whose widths range from .25 in to 1 in receive a conventional asphalt sealant, and openings wider than 1 in are filled with a 4.75‑mm Asphalt Wearing Course [2]. The sealant is placed either flush with the pavement surface or recessed between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below it, and care must be taken to prevent any material from spilling onto surrounding pavement [2]. A minimum application coverage of one hundred square feet per gallon of sealant is required to ensure adequate fill [2]. After the HMWM resin has been worked into the crack and the surface wetted, a sand finish is broadcast at no less than one pound per square yard, with excess sand removed to provide abrasion resistance [2]. For asphalt‑based sealants a thin film remains after wiping flush, typically between one‑thirty‑second and one‑sixteenth inch thick and extending one to three inches beyond the crack edges, which serves as a protective layer [2]. All sealing operations must be supported by an approved Quality Control and Action Plan that includes the material data sheet, ambient and substrate temperature limits, moisture conditions and mixing tolerances, and traffic may not resume until the sealant has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications, confirming Representative satisfaction [2]. Epoxy‑based sealants specifically require a mixing time of at least one hundred eighty seconds on a low‑speed drill, with component proportions held within plus or minus five percent of the prescribed ratios [2].

Performance and service life

Crack sealing is treated as routine maintenance because it repairs or slows existing distress, helping to keep the pavement in good condition and maintain its functional performance. When applied to asphalt surfaces on a three‑to‑five‑year cycle, crack sealing preserves the pavement’s durability and delays more severe deterioration, thereby extending the expected service life of the roadway. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Crack‑sealing treatments on both flexible and rigid pavements are most frequently compromised by non‑structural cracking such as plastic shrinkage, drying shrinkage, temperature‑induced cracks, and surface tears that arise from finishing or texturing operations. When these distresses exceed the dimensional limits set in the specifications—generally a depth greater than one‑eighth inch for polymer‑modified concrete (PPC) overlays and greater than one‑quarter inch for latex overlays, with widths ranging from about 0.007 in to 0.016 in—they must be addressed with a high‑modulus water‑resistant (HMWM) penetrating sealant supplied by the same manufacturer as the overlay material. The repair procedure requires that each crack be thoroughly cleaned and kept dry, often covered until the sealant is applied, and that a corrective or quality‑control action plan be approved before work proceeds. Application must occur under controlled ambient and substrate temperatures; if either the air temperature or pavement surface temperature falls below 40 °F, curing of the sealant is inhibited and the treatment should be postponed. The sealant is placed flush with the pavement face and filled to a level between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the surface, avoiding over‑fill or runoff onto the surrounding pavement. On rigid pavements, improper drilling that penetrates through the slab bottom creates a more serious failure requiring full‑depth patching. Thus, the primary factors governing successful crack sealing are the nature and geometry of the distress, material compatibility, cleanliness and dryness of the substrate, temperature conditions, correct fill depth, and careful execution of preparatory drilling. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 424–425), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (pp. 1075–1110)

Monitoring and follow-up

After crack sealing is applied, the sealed cracks or joints must be inspected with a QC wipe test performed in the presence of the Representative to confirm that the surfaces are clean and dry before sealant placement; this test is repeated at two locations on each of the first three saw‑cut joints per day and then at two locations for every joint and every twenty joints thereafter, with any failure requiring re‑cleaning until acceptable.
