Crack Sealing

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Crack Sealing is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
crack-sealing
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-30

Crack sealing is a routine‑maintenance treatment applied to pavement surfaces that exhibit visible surface cracking—such as longitudinal, transverse or random fissures at least one‑sixteenth of an inch wide—in order to repair existing distress and retard further deterioration [1][2]. The process fully fills the identified cracks with a temperature‑controlled sealant after thorough cleaning and routing, creating a watertight barrier that blocks water, debris and corrosive agents from infiltrating the slab and thereby slows freeze‑thaw damage, reinforcement corrosion and loss of structural integrity [1][2]. Successful application requires ambient and pavement surface temperatures of at least 40 °F (50 °F for epoxy systems), proper crack preparation to standardized dimensions, use of approved sealant materials, and reopening to traffic only after the sealant has fully cured [2][1].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Crack sealing is a routine‑maintenance treatment applied to pavement surfaces that exhibit surface cracking identified during condition surveys [1][2]. The distress targeted consists of visible fissures—such as longitudinal joints, transverse joints, and random cracks—in cement‑concrete pavements, with openings of at least one‑sixteenth of an inch [2]. These cracks constitute pathways for water, debris, and unsound material to infiltrate the slab, which can accelerate freeze‑thaw damage, corrosion of reinforcement, raveling, loss of structural integrity, and other forms of progressive deterioration [1][2]. By fully filling the fissures with a suitable sealant that creates a watertight barrier, crack sealing prevents further ingress of moisture and particles and thereby mitigates the underlying deterioration mechanisms [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 322), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 475), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (p. 1219)

Crack sealing is listed under routine‑maintenance activities, which are defined as actions that repair and/or retard existing distress. Because it is a repair‑oriented treatment, the primary purpose of crack sealing is to fix damage in the pavement surface while also slowing further deterioration, thereby helping preserve the pavement’s functional condition. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Distress characterization and causes

Crack sealing targets distress that originates from water and debris infiltrating pavement cracks, a problem that is intensified when the supporting layers are deficient; for instance, premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements may be caused by inadequate subbase material. Aggregate‑related deficiencies such as Vanport Limestone concerns or alkali‑silica reaction also generate hidden deterioration that accelerates crack formation. By applying sealants on a regular 3 to 5 year cycle for asphalt surfaces, the maintenance program blocks infiltration and slows further damage. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Applicability and treatment selection

Crack sealing is appropriate for longitudinal cracks or joints in rigid concrete pavements when the ambient air and pavement surface temperatures are at least 40 °F, the crack has been routed to roughly three‑quarters of an inch wide and one inch deep, cleaned and dried, and the sealant can be placed before traffic returns. The procedure must not be used on transverse cracks or joints, and traffic may only be reopened after the anchoring material has fully cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 434–435)

Crack sealing is unsuitable whenever the temperature conditions required for proper cure are not met or when the preparation of the crack does not satisfy the standards for a reliable seal. If either the ambient air temperature or the pavement surface temperature falls below forty degrees Fahrenheit, the sealant will not cure adequately; under those cold‑weather circumstances an alternative maintenance or rehabilitation technique must be chosen. Epoxy injection has an even higher temperature requirement: it should not be applied when the ambient air or concrete temperature is below fifty degrees Fahrenheit, because the epoxy will also fail to cure properly and a different repair method—such as a hot‑mix seal coat, surface patching, or another appropriate rehabilitation approach—should be used instead. In addition, sealing cannot proceed unless the crack faces are thoroughly cleaned and dried to the satisfaction of the supervising representative; if this level of cleanliness cannot be achieved, another preservation or repair strategy is required. Finally, the cross‑stitching method that is prescribed for longitudinal joints must not be employed on transverse cracks or joints; when a transverse crack is present, a full‑depth patch or other suitable repair technique should replace crack sealing. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 434–435), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (p. 1219)

Condition assessment and timing

The need for crack sealing is identified through the annual automated pavement condition survey (videologging) that records cracking and other distresses; the resulting condition data are entered into the treatment‑matrix system, which flags sections where routine maintenance—specifically crack sealing—is indicated. When the matrix signals a crack‑related treatment or when visual field inspections reveal cracks not captured by the survey, those locations are scheduled for sealant application according to the prescribed 3‑to‑5‑year cycle. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Crack sealing is employed as a routine preservation activity when cracks become visible on pavement surfaces, before they have progressed to deeper or wider conditions that would demand more extensive rehabilitation [1]. For asphalt pavements the practice follows a three‑to‑five‑year cycle, with particular attention given to sections that have reached five years of service without having been sealed, which are then prioritized for treatment [1]. The timing aligns with an early‑intervention philosophy, intervening at the stage when cracks first appear so as to retard further deterioration and maintain the resurfacing network according to the prescribed maintenance schedule [1]. On cement‑concrete pavements the trigger for sealing is a measured crack opening of at least one‑sixteenth of an inch; any crack meeting or exceeding this width should be cleaned and sealed to prevent water and debris intrusion [2].

Materials and repair details

Crack sealing on both rigid concrete and flexible asphalt pavements begins with a thorough cleaning of the crack surface to remove rust, scale, grease, loose particles and any unsound material; the cleaned joint is then flushed with water and dried, typically with a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi. For concrete joints the work proceeds only when the opening is at least 1/16 in wide; for larger cracks the preparation includes routing or grinding to a prescribed reservoir that is generally three‑quarters of an inch wide and one inch deep, although cracks between three‑quarters and one inch may be widened to one inch with a depth of 1¼ in, and a half‑inch deep reservoir may be created when required. The backer rod and bond‑breaker tape are placed on the bottom of the reservoir, the tape being limited to no more than one‑eighth inch above the face. When using preformed neoprene seals they are installed with equipment that avoids cutting or twisting the seal. Sealants are selected from approved hot‑pour or silicone materials for concrete joints, asphalt‑based sealants listed in the applicable bulletins for cracks ¼ in to 1 in wide, and a 4.75 mm Asphalt Wearing Course for openings greater than one inch. Epoxy bonding compound is injected into drilled holes that are sized no more than three‑eighths of an inch larger than the tie bar, after which six dowel bars or the specified tie bars are placed. For epoxy‑resin crack sealing a surface seal is first applied around glue injection ports spaced as recommended by the manufacturer; the resin system is mixed for at least 180 seconds with a low‑speed drill (400–600 rpm) and injected from the lowest point upward once the surface seal has cured. All placement operations are temperature controlled: ambient air and pavement temperatures must be 40 °F or higher for hot‑pour, silicone, neoprene and asphalt sealants, while epoxy injection requires at least 50 °F. The sealant is placed level with the pavement surface, leaving a film about one‑thirty‑second to one‑sixteenth inch thick that sits approximately one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the surface and spreads one to three inches beyond the joint; excess material is removed. During service the seal may not be elongated more than three percent or compressed longitudinally more than two percent. Traffic is prohibited until the anchoring or epoxy material has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidance, and a quality‑control plan that includes wipe tests and representative test sections must be approved before full‑scale sealing proceeds. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 322), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–475), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (p. 1219)

One set of guidelines specifies that crack sealing should only be attempted when ambient air and pavement surface temperatures are at least 50 °F, establishing a firm lower limit for effective placement. [1] Another set of recommendations permits placement of most sealants once both ambient and surface temperatures reach 40 °F, while still requiring epoxy injections to wait until the concrete or surrounding material is no colder than 50 °F; placements above 90 °F are prohibited unless written approval is obtained. [2] The treatment must be completed well in advance of any subsequent paving, with a minimum interval of one full day to allow the sealant to cure and achieve bond strength. [1] All thermoplastic or preformed pavement markings should be stripped from the surface, and epoxy and polyester paints must be abraded to eliminate glass beads and produce a clean, roughened substrate before sealant application. [1] Cracks or joints are required to be routed to standardized dimensions, typically a width of three‑quarters of an inch and a depth of one inch, although a minimum reservoir depth of one‑half inch is acceptable in some cases. [2] The crack or joint must then be thoroughly cleaned and dried, removing rust, scale, grease, oil, loose particles, unsound concrete, mortar, curing compounds, and any other foreign material by methods such as media blasting, water flushing, or compressed air at a pressure of at least one hundred pounds per square inch, followed by a QC wipe test to verify cleanliness. [2] Immediately before placing a bond‑breaker tape or the sealant itself, the joint must be inspected and confirmed free of moisture and other contaminants. [2] When backer rods are employed, they should be selected at a size roughly 150 % of the measured crack width, and any tape‑type bond breaker must not exceed a thickness of one‑eighth inch. [2] Sealants must be sourced from agencies’ approved lists—such as those identified in Bulletin 15 for asphalt sealants or Bulletin 41 for epoxy systems—and must be handled within the manufacturer‑specified temperature range; hot‑pour materials require indirect heating, batch times under six hours, and proper agitation, and they may only be heated to the safe pouring temperature indicated on the product label. [2] Epoxy systems require a minimum mixing time of 180 seconds using a low‑speed (400–600 rpm) drill equipped with an approved paddle, and the resulting mixture must fall within a viscosity tolerance of plus or minus five percent. [2] After placement the sealant reservoir is filled so that the material terminates one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface, producing a final film thickness of roughly one‑thirty‑second to one‑sixteenth inch that is flush with the wheel path. [2] For epoxy injections, the bonding compound must completely wet the drilled hole and extend at least one inch beyond any embedded tie bar to ensure adequate adhesion. [2] The combined preparation steps—including removal of thermoplastic markings, abrasion of painted surfaces, precise crack routing, exhaustive cleaning and drying, appropriate backer‑rod sizing, and strict temperature control—are essential to achieve the bond strength required for long‑term performance of crack sealing treatments. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 108–109); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 322), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–475), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (p. 1219)

Treatment procedures

Crack sealing begins with a systematic identification and marking of all longitudinal cracks or joints that are to be treated. The required sealant is obtained from an approved source, either an asphalt‑based material listed in the relevant bulletins or a two‑part epoxy system for concrete, and must meet the specifications indicated on its shipping container. The sealing operation must be completed at least twenty‑four hours before any paving work begins, ensuring that the subsequent ultra‑thin wearing course will not be adversely affected by residual sealant. A neat application that leaves no excess material on the surface is essential for optimal performance of the later pavement layer. The cracks are prepared by routing, sawing or grinding them to a width of approximately three‑quarters of an inch and a depth of one inch, with larger openings permitted up to one inch wide and 1¼ inches deep; this preparation is followed immediately by flushing the opening with water. After flushing, the crack is thoroughly cleaned and dried, all rust, scale, grease, loose particles, unsound concrete or mortar are removed, and a final blow‑off is performed with compressed air at not less than one hundred pounds per square inch measured at the source or with a hot‑air lance. For epoxy applications injection ports are glued in at the manufacturer‑recommended spacing, the crack is beveled as directed, and a surface sealant is applied and allowed to harden before the resin is introduced. The asphalt sealant is heated within the temperature range printed on its container and must not be held at pouring temperature for more than six hours; the epoxy resin is mixed according to the manufacturer’s directions for at least one hundred eighty seconds using a low‑speed drill (four hundred to six hundred revolutions per minute) while maintaining the mix ratio within plus or minus five percent. Placement of sealant proceeds only when ambient air and pavement surface temperatures are at least forty degrees Fahrenheit for asphalt, or when concrete and ambient temperatures are not below fifty degrees Fahrenheit for epoxy. The heated asphalt material is poured into the prepared joint until it reaches a level flush with the pavement, leaving an under‑fill of roughly one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the surface; excess material is then wiped away so that a thin film remains on the surface about one‑thirty‑second to one‑sixteenth inch thick and extending one to three inches beyond the crack edges. In epoxy work the resin is injected beginning at the lowest point of the crack, with ports being plugged as the material emerges until the crack is completely filled. Any surplus sealant or epoxy is removed, leaving only the specified thin film, and traffic is prohibited until the anchoring material has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations; no additional curing period beyond maintaining the temperature limits (generally forty to ninety degrees Fahrenheit for asphalt unless written approval is obtained) is required. The process also calls for careful avoidance of spilling sealant onto surrounding slabs and for keeping the material within its specified temperature range throughout handling. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 108–109); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 322), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 434–475), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (p. 1219)

Implementation of crack sealing is scheduled to occur at least one day before any subsequent paving activity, respecting the seasonal windows defined by standard specifications. [1] The work begins with a quality‑control plan and marking of the cracks, after which the joint is prepared to the prescribed geometry—typically sawing or grinding to approximately three‑quarters of an inch wide and one inch deep. [2] The prepared crack is then flushed with water and all loose debris removed before drying; a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi measured at the source, or a hot compressed‑air lance applied carefully, is used to eliminate remaining moisture without overheating the surrounding pavement. [2] For rigid pavements a hydraulic powered drill is employed to route the joint at the proper angle while minimizing damage to the concrete surface. [2] Once dry, glue‑in injection ports are installed and the crack faces are beveled; an anchoring compound or surface seal is applied and allowed to cure in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. [2] Epoxy resin systems are mixed using a low‑speed electric drill operating between 400 and 600 rpm equipped with an approved paddle for a minimum of 180 seconds, then injected with a hand‑held gun, pressure pot, or dedicated injection machine, starting at the lowest point of the crack and progressing upward until fully filled. [2] After the epoxy has cured, the ports are removed and any necessary patching is performed before the final sealant is placed on clean, dry crack faces. [2] Ambient air temperature must be 50 °F or higher for the operation to proceed, and the sealant material may only be applied when both ambient and pavement surface temperatures are at least 40 °F and not exceed roughly 90 °F unless special departmental approval is obtained; the sealant also cannot remain at pouring temperature for more than six hours. [1][2] Traffic may not re‑enter the treated area until the anchoring or epoxy material has fully cured as required by the product specifications, but no additional traffic restrictions are imposed beyond this curing period. [2][1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 108–109); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 322), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 434–475), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (p. 1219)

Quality and workmanship

The inspector must verify that the sealant material originates from an approved source such as those listed in Bulletins 15 or 41 and that it is the type specified by the manufacturer—hot‑pour, silicone, epoxy, etc. The material shall be heated within the temperature range prescribed by the producer, not held at pouring temperature for more than six hours, and any reheating must follow the manufacturer’s directions. Prior to placement the crack or joint faces must be completely free of scale, rust, grease, dirt, curing compound, temporary sealing material, loose particles, and any unsound concrete or mortar. Cleaning shall be accomplished with methods that will not damage the substrate, typically using a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi measured at the source or a hot lance applied without overheating. A representative‑witnessed QC wipe test must be performed immediately before sealing to confirm cleanliness and dryness. The crack geometry shall be routed to the required dimensions: generally a width of three‑quarters inch and a depth of one inch, with larger cracks widened to one inch and deepened to 1 ¼ inches as needed; for longitudinal joints the routing is specified at ¾ in wide by 1 in deep, and tie bars must receive at least one inch of surface coverage. The sealant shall be placed only when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at or above 40 °F (or within the broader 40‑90 °F range unless a special waiver is granted). Bond‑breaker tape, backer rod, injection ports, or other backing must be positioned as recommended, with epoxy injection ports glued at manufacturer‑specified spacing. The sealant must be filled to the prescribed level—typically leaving the reservoir surface between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface—and wiped flush so that a thin film remains 1/32 in to 1/16 in thick and 1–3 in wide, without spilling onto the deck. Acceptance is achieved when the QC wipe test passes, the joint is clean and dry, the correct sealant type and temperature were used, and the fill depth meets the specified criteria. For epoxy systems the resin must be mixed with a minimum 180‑second mixing time on a low‑speed drill (400–600 rpm) maintaining component proportions within ±5 %, and an initial cure must be verified before removing ports. Any drilled holes that penetrate the slab bottom must be repaired with a full‑depth patch, and traffic may be allowed only after the sealant or anchoring material has fully cured according to manufacturer recommendations. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 322), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–475), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (p. 1219)

A satisfactory crack‑sealing operation must be completed at least twenty‑four hours before any subsequent paving activity to allow the sealant to cure properly, and the treated surface should appear neat with no visible excess material remaining on the pavement [1][2]. Prior to placement, the crack or joint faces must be completely free of scale, dirt, curing compound, temporary sealing material, and any other foreign substance; cleaning is typically achieved by media‑blasting or by a compressed‑air stream that registers at least one hundred pounds per square inch at the source [2]. Cleanliness is verified through a representative‑witnessed wipe test, and if the test fails the joint must be re‑cleaned until it is dry to the representative’s satisfaction [2]. Sawed or ground cracks are routed to prescribed dimensions, generally not exceeding three‑quarters of an inch in width and one inch in depth, although wider cracks may be allowed up to one inch wide and one‑and‑a‑quarter inches deep [2]. When a backer rod is required, it must be oversized by fifty percent of the joint width before sealant placement [2]. Sealant may only be applied when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at least forty degrees Fahrenheit; for epoxy‑based systems the minimum rises to fifty degrees Fahrenheit, and asphalt‑based sealants should not be placed above ninety degrees Fahrenheit unless special approval is obtained [2]. The sealant is introduced so that its surface lies between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface, it must be level with the surrounding slab, and any excess material is wiped flush, leaving a thin film typically ranging from one‑thirty‑second to one‑sixteenth inch thick that extends one to three inches beyond the crack edges [2][1]. For epoxy resin systems the components must be mixed according to manufacturer instructions for a minimum of one hundred and eighty seconds, with component ratios maintained within plus or minus five percent tolerance before injection [2]. Injection proceeds only after any surface sealer has hardened and continues until the crack is completely filled [2]. Traffic or construction equipment may not re‑enter the sealed area until the anchoring material or sealant has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s specifications [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 108–109); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 322), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–475), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (p. 1219)

Performance and service life

Crack sealing is treated as routine maintenance because it repairs or slows existing distress, helping to keep the pavement in good condition and maintain its functional performance. When applied to asphalt surfaces on a three‑to‑five‑year cycle, crack sealing preserves the pavement’s durability and delays more severe deterioration, thereby extending the expected service life of the roadway. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Monitoring and follow-up

Signs that a crack‑sealed area is no longer performing adequately include the appearance of potholes or loss of aggregate (raveling) especially where reflective cracks are present. When such distress becomes evident, it indicates that the seal has failed and additional maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation should be considered. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 108–109)
