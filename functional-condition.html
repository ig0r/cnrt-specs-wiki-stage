Functional Condition

Part of: Pavement Condition Assessment is-subtopic-of
Functional Condition is a topic under Pavement Condition Assessment.
functional-condition
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Functional Condition is an assessment of a pavement’s serviceability that combines the surface‑distress observations recorded in the annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (STAMPP)—cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and faulting—with nondestructive field measurements such as skid‑resistance tests and Falling Weight Deflectometer data to capture both user‑level friction and structural capacity [1]. The resulting condition data feed treatment matrices that assign the segment to routine maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction, while also triggering scheduled crack sealing (every 3–5 years for asphalt) and joint sealing (every 5 years for concrete) to keep the functional service life within acceptable bounds [1]. Because the STAMPP surveys can miss specific defects—premature mid‑slab cracking, aggregate‑related problems like Vanport Limestone or Alkali Silica Reaction, and ramp pavement conditions—field visual inspections and targeted testing are required to verify true condition and ensure that patching beyond ten percent of the pavement area, which is uneconomical for preservation, prompts a higher‑level treatment [1].

Purpose and investigation objectives

The annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (STAMPP) generate functional‑condition data such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and faulting; these observations are entered into the pavement condition/treatment matrices which then dictate whether a segment receives routine maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction. Because crack sealing of asphalt surfaced pavements should be done every 3 to 5 years and joint sealing of concrete pavements should be done every 5 years, the functional‑condition findings trigger those scheduled treatments and also guide the timing of preservation actions before distress exceeds the point where patching greater than 10% of the pavement area is generally not cost-effective or permitted for Pavement Preservation projects. In addition, FWD data is used to determine pavement and subgrade characteristics necessary to perform structural design, while skid‑resistance test outcomes add points on project prioritization forms, ensuring that maintenance and rehabilitation plans reflect both user serviceability and structural capacity. [1]

Scope and applicability

Functional Condition assessments derived from the Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (STAMPP) provide a broad view of cracking, rutting and other surface distress, yet the source notes that this condition data and its treatment matrices do not always properly account for specific conditions that may be present. As a result, distresses such as premature mid‑slab cracking caused by inadequate subbase material, aggregate‑related problems like Vanport Limestone concerns or Alkali Silica Reaction, and ramp pavement conditions are often missed. When any of these issues are suspected, field visual inspections and complementary investigations—such as skid‑resistance testing requested from BOMO or Falling Weight Deflectometer testing—to verify the true pavement condition should be employed. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Existing information and records

A Functional Condition review should examine skid resistance test results from BOMO for any roadway sections with a verified problem, Falling Weight Deflectometer data that define pavement and subgrade characteristics and indicate concrete joint efficiency (noting that patching greater than 10% of the pavement area is generally not cost‑effective for preservation projects), Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) traffic loading figures used to set priorities, and the annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (Videologging) from the STAMPP program which provide data on cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and faulting; it should also incorporate maintenance history such as routine crack sealing every 3 to 5 years, joint sealing every 5 years, and any preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction actions recorded for the segment. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

The functional condition assessment must address several gaps in the existing records because automated Pavement Condition Surveys (STAMPP) can miss critical distress. Specifically, premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements—often linked to inadequate subbase material—is not reliably captured, and aggregate‑related problems such as Vanport Limestone concerns or Alkali Silica Reaction are undetectable in the STAMPP data set. In addition, ramp pavement condition is omitted from the STAMPP program, and skid‑resistance issues on concrete pavements with Vanport Limestone may be exacerbated after diamond grinding, requiring targeted field testing. Consequently, field investigations are needed to verify these conditions, confirm any required corrective actions, and compensate for the noted limitations in data reliability. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Visual inspection and condition assessment

Functional Condition assessments should look for visible distresses such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling or weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting, which are the condition data collected in the annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys. In addition, premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements—often caused by inadequate subbase material—should be identified as a specific defect that may not be captured by standard survey data. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Field measurements and testing

Functional Condition assessments rely on several nondestructive field methods: Skid Resistance Testing quantifies surface friction to identify sections with verified skid‑friction problems; Falling Weight Deflectometer testing provides pavement and subgrade stiffness data that support structural design, reveal concrete joint efficiency, and indicate the extent of required concrete patching; Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (videologging) are conducted annually to record distress types such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and faulting, thereby supplying the condition data used in treatment matrices; and Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) traffic‑loading data is considered when prioritizing projects. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Testing that supports functional‑condition assessment relies on skid‑friction testing performed by BOMO, Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) measurements, and annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys conducted via videologging; the source specifies only that condition surveys are performed annually and does not provide a test configuration, spacing interval, or site/environmental requirements for these tests. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Structural and functional performance

Evaluating a pavement’s functional condition relies on several field measurements: skid resistance testing provides the friction values that affect ride safety; Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing supplies deflection data and concrete joint efficiency, which together indicate load‑transfer capability and structural capacity; Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) traffic loading figures are used to gauge cumulative stress on the structure; and Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (videologging) record surface distresses such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and faulting that serve as proxies for roughness and ride quality. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

The annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys provide data on cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and faulting, which are then cross‑referenced with the traffic loading (ESAL) information and the structural characteristics derived from Falling Weight Deflectometer tests to see whether the observed distress stays within the limits set by the treatment matrices. When patching greater than 10% of the pavement area is exceeded, preservation is generally not cost-effective and a higher‑level rehabilitation or reconstruction is triggered; likewise, if the functional pavement life has been exceeded, reconstruction is prescribed. Routine maintenance actions such as crack sealing every 3 to 5 years for asphalt and joint sealing every 5 years for concrete are used to keep the functional condition within acceptable bounds while the remaining service‑life needs are evaluated against these cycles. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Data interpretation and diagnosis

Functional Condition is evaluated by combining the annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (videologging) with targeted field measurements such as Skid Friction tests and Falling Weight Deflectometer data; the FWD results supply pavement and subgrade characteristics for structural design while skid resistance testing triggers corrective actions when problems are verified. The condition data feed the treatment matrices that assign routine maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction, but analysts must also record observations in field views because some distress—e.g., premature mid‑slab cracking or aggregate‑related issues—not captured by STAMPP may require earlier intervention. When patching is required, quantities exceeding 10% of the pavement area are generally not cost-effective for preservation projects and therefore guide the selection of a more extensive treatment. This integrated approach ensures that visual, measurement, and performance information together define the functional serviceability of the pavement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

The observed functional condition may stem from weak support because an inadequate subbase material can cause premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements, and it may also arise from material deterioration such as aggregate‑related problems including Vanport Limestone concerns and Alkali Silica Reaction, which are not always detectable in the STAMPP data. No other causes such as drainage deficiencies, overloading, environmental exposure or aging are mentioned in this section. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Findings, confidence, and next actions

Functional Condition data derived from annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (STAMPP) enable the program to identify distress such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and faulting, and to match those observations with the treatment matrices that assign sections to routine maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction; this supports conclusions about which treatment category is appropriate and when actions like cyclic crack sealing (every 3‑5 years for asphalt) or joint sealing (every 5 years for concrete) should be scheduled. However, the source notes that STAMPP data may not capture specific problems—premature mid‑slab cracking caused by inadequate subbase, aggregate issues such as Vanport Limestone or Alkali Silica Reaction, and ramp pavement condition are omitted—and that despite ongoing quality‑assurance efforts, limitations and imperfections in existing data remain, requiring field observations to supplement the matrix triggers. Additionally, patching quantities exceeding greater than 10 % of the pavement area are generally not cost-effective for preservation projects, indicating a threshold beyond which functional condition alone may not justify preservation. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Following a functional‑condition assessment, any segment flagged for skid resistance problems must be submitted to BOMO for skid friction testing and the resulting corrective actions incorporated into the District Interstate plan; structural deficiencies identified by Falling Weight Deflectometer data should be used to verify pavement design parameters and may trigger joint efficiency checks or concrete patching, noting that patching greater than 10% of the pavement area is generally not cost-effective for preservation projects. Annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (STAMPP) continue to monitor cracking, rutting, edge deterioration and other distresses, and when premature mid‑slab cracking, Vanport Limestone or ASR issues are observed, targeted monitoring and prompt skid resistance testing are required. Routine maintenance such as cyclic crack sealing of asphalt pavements should be done every 3 to 5 years and joint sealing of concrete pavements should be done every 5 years is emphasized to retain functional condition, while preservation treatments—including micro‑surfacing, diamond grinding, base repair or concrete patching—are selected before extensive deterioration according to the treatment cycle charts. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)
