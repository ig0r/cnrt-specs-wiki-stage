Flexible Pavement

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Flexible Pavement is a topic under Pavement Design.

Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Flexible pavement, represented by asphalt‑concrete surfacing, is a layered system designed to distribute traffic loads through appropriate thicknesses while providing a level, stable riding platform and sufficient surface friction for safety [1]. Its performance depends on highly permeable base and subbase materials that quickly remove surface and subsurface water, preserving subgrade support and limiting load‑induced deflections; structural adequacy is demonstrated by meeting or exceeding the required Design Structural Number using calculated layer thicknesses, coefficients and non‑destructive testing [1]. The system also incorporates scratch or leveling courses, binder and wearing layers (including FB‑1/FB‑2 where traffic permits), shoulders and safety edges to support lateral loads, reduce edge stresses, and extend service life to at least an eight‑year design period [1].

PennDOT publications covering “Flexible Pavement” also include the following subtopics: Asphalt Concrete (HMA) and Full-Depth Asphalt Pavement.

Asphalt Concrete (HMA) serves as the primary surfacing material that distributes traffic loads through its layered composition and characteristics. [1] Full‑Depth Asphalt Pavement applies this system by accounting for frost heave losses in its design inputs, ensuring terminal serviceability is maintained despite freezing-induced subgrade movements. [1]

Purpose and design objectives

Flexible pavement, represented by asphalt concrete surfaces, is intended to provide adequate structural capacity through appropriate layer thicknesses—often verified by non‑destructive testing—to distribute loads, while also ensuring effective removal of surface and subsurface water, a level and stable riding platform via scratch or leveling courses, sufficient surface friction for safety, and proper shoulder support to reduce edge stresses. The need to improve the removal of surface and subsurface water is addressed because most pavement problems can be attributed, at least in part, to excessive water within or beneath the pavement layers; subsurface drainage serves two primary purposes by increasing pavement life through removal of water from beneath the pavement surface, which reduces loss of subgrade support and increases resistance of the aggregate base to traffic‑induced deflections, and by limiting distress types such as rutting of a softened subgrade and surface roughness caused by lack of support from a fluid base. Because of its flexibility, FB‑1 is recommended for use on low‑volume roads that have highly flexible existing pavement structures, and FB wearing courses may be used independently on roadways having an ADT of 1,500 or less; because of the high void content in the FB‑1 and FB‑2 surfaces, a seal coat or surface treatment must be placed on the FB surface, and a minimum of 3 months of warm weather traffic densification of the FB surface is recommended before the application of either a seal coat or surface treatment. Overlays are used to correct structural deficiencies, surface deterioration, and/or rideability on existing pavements; an overlay of flexible pavement seeks to solve the engineering problem of an existing roadway that lacks sufficient structural capacity or does not provide acceptable ride quality, including low‑friction surface conditions, by applying new asphalt layers designed with accurate traffic inputs so that the restored load‑bearing ability and improved smoothness meet a required service period of at least a minimum 8‑year structural design life. Long‑life flexible pavement is intended to combat bottom‑up fatigue cracking in the structural layers, thereby reducing the frequency of costly full‑depth patch repairs and limiting maintenance to periodic resurfacing of the wearing surface. The Roadbed Soil Resilient Modulus, Mr, is used to represent subgrade support characteristics; it is important to test samples at the same density and moisture content they will develop in service, and because the procedures were created to use average Mr values, it is not necessary to purposely input low modulus values to represent possible worst‑case scenarios. The design seeks to supply the structural strength required to carry traffic loads, expressed by a Design Structural Number that must be met or exceeded by the calculated SN from the chosen layer thicknesses; the Calculated SN from the thickness design must be greater than the Design SN to be structurally adequate, and pavement course adjustments should be made so that the least total pavement cost is incurred. Within the scope of section 10.1, flexible pavement design is intended to ensure that an existing pavement can sustain anticipated future traffic loads by providing adequate structural capacity; it requires determining the current effective structural capacity (SN_eff) and comparing it with the structural capacity needed for future traffic (SN_f), using condition surveys or component analysis, non‑destructive deflection testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer data, or remaining‑life calculations to obtain SN_eff, thereby addressing the performance need of maintaining serviceability under increasing loads. Within the scope of section 10.2, the flexible‑pavement design embodied in an AC overlay is intended to ensure that the reconstructed structure can sustain the anticipated future traffic demand; the designer must compute a Structural Number for Future Traffic and compare it with the Effective Existing SN using component or non‑destructive testing approaches based on core data or RMS history and applying flexible pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 for ESAL calculations, and the overlay thickness is then selected so that the calculated SN of the new layers exceeds the required Overlay (Design) SN, thereby providing structural adequacy while observing subbase inclusion rules, such as omitting subbase when the design 18‑kip ESALs exceed 5,000,000 on non‑interstates unless its quality and drainability have been verified. The Pavement Policy Manual states that flexible pavement wearing courses may be used alone only where the average daily traffic is 1,500 or less; for roadways with an ADT greater than 1,500 a combined binder and wearing course system must be employed [1]. The same manual requires that design address drainage to improve removal of surface and subsurface water so that the subgrade retains its support capacity and limits traffic‑induced deflections, thereby extending service life [1]. It further mandates provision of base repair or replacement to achieve adequate structural capacity, inclusion of scratch or leveling courses or milling to create a level platform, and specification of binder‑course thickness that adds structural layer thickness as determined by testing [1]. When a deflection‑based design method is applied, the overlay depth for flexible pavement must be adjusted using a structural coefficient of 0.20 for FB courses [1]. A leveling course of at least 1 inch may be included in the structural calculations, and thin asphalt overlays shall not be placed thinner than 1½ inches unless special conditions are met [1]. For AC overlays on existing AC pavement, the calculated Structural Number must be greater than the Overlay Design Structural Number to satisfy the structural adequacy objective [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require that the subbase provide a structurally sound foundation capable of supporting traffic loads by placing No. 2A aggregate to at least 3 inches compacted depth and No. OGS aggregate to at least 4 inches, constructing each lift no more than 8 inches (or no more than 4 inches when granulated slag is used), and achieving the non‑movement compaction condition [2]. Durability is ensured by preparing a sound subgrade free of soft, muddy or frozen areas, maintaining adequate moisture to prevent segregation, protecting the subbase from winter exposure by covering it with an asphalt base course or proof rolling before pavement placement, and correcting any depth deficiency of ½ inch or more through scarifying, blending, and recompacting [2]. When cold‑recycled base courses are incorporated, the design shall achieve sufficient structural strength after at least one week of curing under favorable weather, limit abrasion susceptibility by avoiding high ADT traffic unless detours can be established until adequate strength is obtained, and be placed to a compacted thickness between 3 inches minimum and 5 inches maximum [1]. Because of the high void content in FB‑1 and FB‑2 surfaces, a seal coat or surface treatment must be placed on the FB surface after at least three months of warm‑weather traffic densification, with postponement permissible up to three years if the underlying pavement is sound; FB Modified surfaces may forego such treatment for at least four years [1]. The interlayer material must be compatible with the asphalt, thermally stable and physically durable so it can maintain a strong internal bond and resist performance breakdown over time, thereby mitigating reflective cracking and moisture intrusion [1]. Safety considerations require a Safety Edge on the outside edge of wearing or binder courses with a depth of 1½ inches or greater but not exceeding 5 inches to give drivers a durable tapered transition back onto the roadway [1]. The specifications prohibit traffic on No. OGS subbase, limit traffic on No. 2A subbase when used as a haul road or exposed for more than 60 calendar days, and require repair of any damaged geotextile at no cost [2]. Ride quality is addressed through overlay selection to remedy surface deterioration, improve rideability, and provide sufficient friction when low‑friction values are present; the design shall be capable of improving ride quality, address any suspected structural inadequacy, and provide sufficient friction [1]. The finished subbase depth must be kept within ½ inch of the specified elevation to maintain serviceability [2]. An overlay design shall achieve at least a minimum 8‑year structural design life but shall not exceed a maximum of 20 years, using current traffic data—including ADT, projected growth and truck percentages—to size the overlay appropriately [1].

Scope and applicability

Flexible pavement design is applied to projects that have an asphalt concrete wearing surface regardless of the underlying base type. The approach is appropriate when a project requires improvements such as drainage, base repair, scratch‑and‑leveling or milling, addition of a binder course, enhancement of surface friction, or shoulder work. It also accommodates the use of reclaimed asphalt pavement in hot mix, warm mix, or cold‑recycled base courses and can be combined with full‑depth reclamation techniques that stabilize the recycled layer mechanically, chemically, or with asphalt. For surface courses, FB‑1 cold mixes are recommended for low‑volume roads that have highly flexible existing pavement structures, while FB‑2 hot‑mix specifications permit mixing at a stationary plant and allow PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 binder. Wearing courses of either type may be used independently on roadways having an average daily traffic of 1,500 vehicles or less; for higher traffic volumes a combination of binder and wearing courses is required. In widening projects, partial lane widening of less than 10 feet must match the existing pavement structure, whereas full‑width lane additions of ten feet or more should also match the existing structure unless the new lane experiences different loading conditions, in which case the design should reflect those loads; drainage layers should be kept consistent with the existing pavement. Thin asphalt overlays on non‑Interstate facilities are limited to pavements whose surface is asphalt, that are structurally sound with less than 2 % patching required, have good or upgraded surface and subsurface drainage, and do not need a structural upgrade; Superpave 6.3 mm thin overlay courses must be placed at the minimum depths shown in the tables, and the 9.5 mm fine‑grade wearing course may not use PG 64E‑22 binder. Cold‑recycled base courses are most appropriate for cracked asphalt pavements that have structurally sound, well‑drained bases and subgrades such as collector and local access highways or shoulder reconstructions and widenings; the existing road must contain at least 2 inches of asphalt material, traffic volumes should stay below 200 daily ESALs, and projects must allow traffic control or detour during base construction and curing. Flexible pavement design is also suitable for conventional flexible pavements, asphalt overlays of fractured Portland cement concrete pavement, and flexible overlays over existing flexible pavements, using an overall standard deviation of 0.45; when placed over a rigid pavement the recommended overall standard deviation changes to 0.40. When the approved structure calls for a flexible system that incorporates either an aggregate/cement or an aggregate/lime/pozzolan base course, those two base materials must be offered as alternatives in the bid. The design must achieve a structural number greater than the required value using either the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method, while adhering to the minimum and maximum course depths specified in the relevant tables, and asphalt material selection should follow suitability guidance based on highway ADT as shown in Table 10.3. For an AC overlay of an existing AC pavement, the Component Method or Non‑Destructive Testing Method is used to obtain the effective structural number, and flexible‑pavement average initial truck factors are applied for ESAL calculations; the subbase is excluded from the structural evaluation on Interstates because it may be in poor condition, and on non‑Interstate roads the subbase is omitted when design 18‑kip ESALs exceed 5 million unless a specific investigation confirms its quality and drainability. Flexible pavement (including shoulders) is appropriate when a subbase is constructed using No. 2A or No. OGS aggregate placed to the positions and depths specified for the pavement structure, with each No. 2A layer compacted to at least 3 inches and each No. OGS layer compacted to at least 4 inches, and when the subbase is built in layers not more than 8 inches thick (or not more than 4 inches thick if granulated slag is used). A Class 4, Type A geotextile must be installed as required, and an asphalt base course must be placed over the prepared subbase before the construction‑season shutdown; if the subbase remains over winter without a protective layer it must be proof‑rolled prior to placing the flexible pavement and any unstable sections must be removed and repaired. When an existing asphalt pavement cannot provide adequate structural capacity—such as when a thicker binder course is required or the base must be repaired to achieve sufficient support—a bonded concrete structural overlay or total reconstruction may be preferred. The Superpave Restricted Performance Specification also limits flexible‑pavement use to projects where the surface‑course thicknesses meet the minimum requirements, the existing pavement is stable under the roller, and proper leveling and sealing have been completed; if those conditions are not met another design approach must be considered. Traffic traveling over intermediate layers and cold longitudinal paving joints can degrade performance, so detours that keep vehicles off the work zone are preferred when feasible, otherwise a more durable pavement type may be selected. An alternate pavement design becomes preferable when the life‑cycle cost comparison shows a difference of at least ten percent for agency costs alone or at least twenty percent when user delay costs are included. In addition, the District Pavement Type Selection Committee may justify selecting a single pavement type—such as a rigid alternative—if engineering judgment identifies a unique, extraordinary circumstance based on subgrade condition, local materials, right‑of‑way limits or significant non‑pavement items, provided the justification is fact‑based and defensible; projects with substantial non‑pavement items may also be deemed unsuitable for alternate pavement type bidding. If the existing roadway exhibits mainly surface‑related distresses rather than deficiencies in the support layers, a treatment other than full‑depth reclamation of a flexible pavement is generally more effective. The practicality of using full‑depth reclamation also depends on having sufficient existing pavement thickness and suitable subgrade material to be incorporated into the reclaimed layer, so thin pavements or inadequate subgrade conditions favor an alternative design approach. When subgrade CBR values are below five, or low CBR values are widespread across the project, subgrade strengthening or an alternative pavement structure becomes more cost‑effective than a standard flexible design. Frost‑susceptible soils that present significant frost‑heave potential may lead to a preference for a rigid pavement with an adequate granular foundation because flexible designs rely on increasing base thickness alone. CBR values greater than twenty cannot be used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation, indicating that the assumed flexible pavement modulus would be unrealistic and prompting consideration of another design approach. If back‑calculated resilient modulus values exceed fifteen thousand psi, they must be verified before being applied to a flexible pavement; otherwise alternative treatments such as rock capping or geogrid reinforcement should be evaluated. When an overlay involves a bonded concrete layer over existing asphalt or AC/PCC composite pavement, the standard flexible‑pavement overlay design is not suitable because the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures do not consider the bonding between the concrete and the underlying asphalt; in such cases the design must shift to the bonded concrete overlay mechanistic‑empirical procedure. Design inputs and requirements

In the design of a flexible pavement that incorporates cold‑recycled base courses (section 5.13), a comprehensive site assessment is required to evaluate drainage conditions and to verify that the existing road contains at least 2 inches of asphalt material. Traffic data are gathered because projects carrying significantly heavy truck traffic (i.e., 200 or more daily ESALs) should not be selected for cold recycling. The selection process must include sampling and testing of pavement, base, subbase and subgrade materials to characterize material and geotechnical properties, together with a review of the pavement’s construction and maintenance history and current traffic volumes.

For flexible pavements designed under full‑depth reclamation techniques (section 5.15), design must be based on a reliable estimate of future traffic loading and on a recent pavement condition survey that documents current distress. It is important to assess subgrade strength using in‑situ testing such as falling weight deflectometer (FWD), dynamic cone penetrometer (DCP) or light weight deflectometer (LWD). Proper sampling of the existing pavement and subgrade materials plays a vital role in defining material properties and layer composition. Determination of appropriate layer thicknesses must consider both the anticipated traffic and environmental conditions together with any identified drainage problems on the site, and existing drainage issues should be corrected before construction to ensure a well‑drained pavement structure. [1]

Design of flexible pavement must comply with Federal‑aid Highway Program policy and the Temporary Traffic Control Guidelines (Publication 213) for any required detours or traffic protection. When a Restricted Performance Specification is selected, the project must meet Publication 408 Section 413 minimum surface‑course thicknesses and the density acceptance range of 92.0 % to 98.5 % for Standard and RPS wearing courses as governed by the Percent Within Limits provisions. Recycling requirements in a Restricted Performance Specification dictate that at least 15 % of the estimated milled material be retained by the contractor, with full provision required if the total milled quantity is less than 200 tons, per Publication 408 Section 703.2(a)7, and any use of reclaimed asphalt or recycled concrete aggregate must follow the guidance in Publications 408 and 611. Continuous pavement base drains shall be provided along the outside edge of the pavement as required by Publication 72M, with outlet sizing and spacing designed according to Publication 13M Chapter 10 Section 10.3.D, and all outlets within mowable areas must be protected with appropriate headwalls; base‑drain outlets must also be protected from crushing or plugging. Life‑cycle cost analysis for flexible pavement projects shall use the price‑adjustment rules in Publication 408 Section 110.04, applying the semiannual Asphalt Adjustment Multiplier to all asphalt items, and resurfacing thicknesses are prescribed as 1.5 inches when a 9.5 mm Wearing Course mix is used or 2 inches when a 12.5 mm mix is used; shoulder construction must match the mainline pavement type. Selection of pavement type shall be based on cost‑difference thresholds of at least 10 % in agency‑only life‑cycle costs or at least 20 % when user delay costs are included, with guidance from FHWA Technical Advisory, NCHRP Report 703 and the Alternate Bidding for Pavement Type Selection process. Design of asphalt overlays shall follow Chapter 10 of the design manual and full‑depth reconstruction shall follow Chapter 9; base repairs must use either a Base Course/Superpave Base Course or AASHTO #1 aggregate, include a leveling course with a minimum thickness of one inch and cross‑slope correction, and require full‑depth asphalt shoulders. FB surface courses are governed by the FB‑1, FB‑2 and FB Modified specifications; FB‑1 mixes may be placed by a mobile plant, whereas FB‑2 mixes must be produced at a stationary mixing plant and may use PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 binder, which must be mixed hot and placed hot. When a deflection‑based design program is applied to an FB overlay, the structural coefficient shall be set to 0.20, and a seal coat or surface treatment shall follow at least three months of warm‑weather traffic densification (or may be postponed up to three years if conditions are satisfactory), while FB Modified surfaces may defer seal coating for at least four years. FB wearing courses may be used independently only on roadways with an ADT of 1,500 or less; for ADT greater than 1,500 a binder and wearing‑course combination is required, and contracts must bid FB‑1, FB‑2 and FB Modified as equivalent alternatives but not as alternatives to Superpave courses, with FB Modified possibly excluded from Federal‑aid projects. Partial lane widening of less than ten feet shall match the existing pavement structure by using an equal depth of asphalt material and keeping drainage layers consistent with those of the existing pavement; full‑width lane additions of ten feet or more should also match the existing structure unless traffic loading conditions dictate otherwise, and in either case the modified cross section must be checked to ensure adequate drainage throughout. Flexible pavement overlays are governed by Chapter 5 overlay guidelines and Publication 408 Section 413.3(b) for thickness and weather limits, as well as Publication 408 Section 466 for interlayer specifications; Superpave mixes of 25 mm, 19 mm, 12.5 mm and 9.5 mm may be used at the appropriate minimum depths, a 6.3 mm wearing course shall use PG 64E‑22 binder while a 9.5 mm fine‑grade wearing course shall not, thin asphalt overlays on Interstates shall not be placed with a thickness of less than 1½ inches without prior PDAU approval, and on non‑Interstates they may be thinner only when the existing surface is asphalt, structurally sound, requires less than 2 % patching, has good or upgraded drainage and needs no structural upgrade. The Safety Edge shall be used as a standard edge treatment with a 30° taper, a depth of at least 1½ inches and not more than 5 inches, adding roughly one percent to material quantities. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses are designed in accordance with Publication 27 (Bulletin 27) mix‑design procedures, test methods and allowable emulsified asphalts, supplemented by Publication 408 Section 344 and Bulletin 27 Chapter 2 Section 7 for full‑depth reclamation; performance requirements call for a minimum compacted depth of three inches and a maximum of five inches, and at least one week of curing under favorable weather before the wearing course is placed. Projects that carry significantly heavy truck traffic (200 or more daily ESALs) shall not be selected for cold recycling, and only projects where traffic can be controlled or detoured during base construction and curing may be considered; polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended when traffic exceeds 3,000 two‑lane AADT, the truck proportion is high, or early/late season recycling may impair initial strength. Full‑depth reclamation techniques are governed by standard traffic analysis procedures used in conventional pavement design and condition‑survey specifications set out in PennDOT Publication 336 or 343; required in‑situ testing includes Falling Weight Deflectometer testing before rehabilitation and post‑construction testing with dynamic cone penetrometer and light‑weight deflectometer as referenced in Appendix J, and sampling protocols prescribed in Appendix J must be followed. Subgrade soils used in flexible pavement design shall have any CBR value greater than 10 approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, and no CBR may exceed 20 when applying the Heukelom and Klomp correlation (the product of 1 500 and the CBR cannot surpass 30 000 psi); subgrade improvement plans must be coordinated with the District Geotechnical Engineer and documented in the PS&E. Design methods and criteria

Under the Life‑Cycle Cost Analysis guidelines the flexible‑pavement design process follows a life‑cycle cost analysis procedure in which an Asphalt Adjustment Multiplier is applied to both initial construction and future maintenance costs; the multiplier is computed semiannually with the equation AAM = 1 + Adjustments / Total of Asphalt Payments. Resurfacing specifications require that all flexible‑pavement overlays be 1.5 inches thick when a 9.5 mm Wearing Course mix is used or 2 inches thick when a 12.5 mm Wearing Course mix is used. When a deflection‑based design program is employed for FB surface courses the analyst must adjust the overlay depth because the program assumes Superpave material, and a structural coefficient of 0.20 shall be used for FB courses in designing FB overlays; FB‑1 wearing and binder mixes are cold mixes usually mixed and placed by a mobile plant, whereas FB‑2 mixes must be produced at a stationary mixing plant using hot mix with PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement. Cold‑recycled base courses are designed according to the mix‑design procedure described in Publication 27, Chapter 2, and projects carrying significantly heavy truck traffic—defined as 200 or more daily ESALs—should not be selected for cold recycling; the constructed layer must have a minimum compacted depth of 3 inches and may not exceed 5 inches. The Heukelom and Klomp correlation is used to convert California Bearing Ratio values to resilient modulus, multiplying CBR by 1 500 except that the factor shall be reduced to 1 000 when in‑situ density is significantly below maximum dry density; any CBR value greater than 10 must be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and no CBR value greater than 20 may be used in the correlation. When field data are available a Falling Weight Deflectometer back‑calculated resilient modulus must be adjusted with a correction factor of 0.25, and the dynamic subgrade modulus is halved to obtain a static value. The AASHTO pavement design process requires an overall standard deviation of 0.45 for flexible pavements over flexible pavements and 0.40 for flexible pavements over rigid pavements. The roadbed soil resilient modulus M_r represents subgrade support; the value used in the AASHTO design process should be the average of project tests, and if seasonal values are known they may be entered in the secondary dialog box to compute an effective roadbed soil resilient modulus. Structural adequacy is expressed through a Design Structural Number calculated as SN = a₁d₁ + a₂d₂m₂ + a₃d₃m₃ + …, where the structural coefficients aᵢ are taken from Table 9.3 and all drainage coefficients mᵢ shall be set to 1.0; designers may use either the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method in DARWin, while the Layered Analysis Thickness Design method is not permitted. Overlay designs must begin with adequate traffic data—including current ADT, projected growth and truck percentage—and the daily 18‑kip ESALs must be determined in accordance with Chapter 7; all overlay designs are performed in DARWin implementing the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures, and bonded concrete overlays on asphalt or composite pavements shall be designed using the BCOA‑ME mechanistic‑empirical procedure. All overlay designs shall have a minimum structural design life of eight years and a maximum of twenty years, and when first overlaying cement‑concrete or brick surfaces a minimum binder thickness of 2½ inches shall be provided together with the minimum wearing‑course thickness indicated in the tables.

Materials and mixture design

Flexible pavement specifications require that the granular base and sub‑base layers be composed of highly permeable materials so that water can drain by gravity; permeability is assessed through aggregate gradation because larger voids reduce upward moisture movement, and the slope of the sub‑base should be maximized to increase drainage rate, although a modest reduction in permeability may be tolerated to provide a stable work platform during construction (source: Pavement Policy Manual) [1]. The subbase material shall be either No. 2A aggregate or No. OGS aggregate, with each placed at its required minimum compacted depth of 3 inches for No. 2A and 4 inches for No. OGS; construction must build the subbase in layers that do not exceed 8 inches of compacted depth, and when granulated slag is used the layer thickness shall be limited to no more than 4 inches (or a maximum compacted layer of 6 inches only if the full layer depth is compacted as prescribed elsewhere) (source: Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATION Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS) [2]. All subbase material must be mixed with water using acceptable methods so that it contains enough moisture to prevent segregation during stockpiling, hauling and placing, and it shall be kept in a well‑moistened condition from before placement through compaction (source: Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATION Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS) [2]. The design shall identify the nominal aggregate size of the wearing‑course mix as either 9.5 mm or 12.5 mm and apply the corresponding overlay thickness—1.5 inches for a 9.5 mm mix and 2 inches for a 12.5 mm mix, with shoulders required to match the mainline pavement type (source: Pavement Policy Manual) [1]. For low traffic volumes (ADT ≤ 1 500) cold‑mix FB‑1 surface mixtures—characterized by high void content and softer binders than PG 64S‑22—may be used independently on flexible pavements that already exhibit high flexibility; for higher volumes (ADT > 1 500) a combination of binder and wearing courses is required. FB‑2 must be mixed at a stationary plant and may incorporate either PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement, requiring hot mixing and placement. Because the void content makes FB surfaces prone to early distress, a seal coat or surface treatment must be placed after a minimum of three months of warm‑weather traffic densification; if the underlying pavement is structurally sound this application may be postponed for up to three years, while FB Modified surfaces are exempt from seal coating for at least four years. Design of FB overlays uses a structural coefficient of 0.20 to reflect their lower stiffness compared with Superpave materials. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses are limited to projects in which heavy truck traffic does not exceed 200 daily ESALs and where construction traffic can be controlled; the mix shall be compacted to a thickness between three inches minimum and five inches maximum, incorporating at least two inches of existing asphalt plus either one inch of underlying reclaimed aggregate or one inch of virgin coarse aggregate when the RAP is fine‑graded. Polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended where traffic exceeds 3 000 two‑lane AADT, truck percentages are high, recycling occurs early or late in the season, or rapid strength development is desired. A minimum curing period of one week under low humidity and high temperature is required before placing the wearing surface so that structural strength can develop. Full‑depth reclamation begins with an evaluation of traffic levels, existing distress and in‑situ subgrade strength, followed by determination of a layer thickness that meets the structural requirement for the anticipated traffic and environmental conditions; material design may follow pulverization, mechanical stabilization or chemical stabilization. The type and content of any chemical stabilizer shall be determined by laboratory testing because increasing the amount generally raises strength but may cause brittleness if excessive. Proper drainage must also be identified and corrected before construction to protect durability and performance. For flexible‑pavement subbase the material mix must be prepared with enough moisture to prevent segregation during stockpiling, hauling and placement, and it shall remain well‑moistened from mixing through compaction. Each aggregate layer is limited in thickness: No. 2A aggregate layers shall be compacted to a minimum depth of three inches, OGS aggregate layers to a minimum depth of four inches. Subbase layers shall not exceed eight inches of compacted depth, or not more than four inches when granulated slag is used, with a possible maximum of six inches if the full layer is compacted as specified. Compaction shall be achieved to a non‑movement condition by overlapping passes from the sides toward the centre. A Subbase Delivery Plan must be prepared and submitted for quantities greater than 1 500 tons before subbase operations may begin. [1][2]

Structural details and interfaces

Flexible pavement rehabilitation guidelines require that all longitudinal and transverse joints in existing concrete pavements be cleaned and sealed, and that any asphalt joints and cracks also receive sealing; heavy‑duty membranes may be applied where necessary [1]. Existing transverse and patch joints must be sawed and the overlay sealed over them, and a continuous pavement base drain shall be provided [1]. Paved shoulders are to be constructed as Type 6 or Type 7 shoulders, or as full‑depth asphalt shoulders; when concrete shoulders or lane widenings of one to two feet exist they should be retained together with accompanying asphalt shoulders, and guide rail and drainage structures should be adjusted or replaced as needed [1]. A Safety Edge is required on the outside edge of asphalt pavements and shoulders, with a minimum depth of 1 ½ inches and a total depth not exceeding 5 inches, which adds roughly 1 % to material quantities [1]. When an overlay is placed, an asphalt paving interlayer may be installed between the existing and new asphalt layers to mitigate reflective cracking; the interlayer must be compatible with the asphalt mix, thermally stable, physically durable, and may be a geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grid, or composite reinforcing grid as defined in Publication 408 Section 466 [1]. For subbase construction in flexible pavement, if the subbase is placed in widths less than the full pavement width, the edge of the completed subbase shall be neatly trimmed before the abutting subbase is placed; this excerpt provides no additional requirements for longitudinal or transverse joints, interlayers, membranes, notches, transitions, or connections [2].

At every interface the system is arranged to remove water quickly, using highly permeable base and subbase materials that allow gravity‑drainable water to pass rapidly, maximizing subbase slope, providing continuous edge drains along the outside edge and on the low side of superelevated sections, installing lateral underdrains at cut‑to‑fill transitions and other critical locations identified by maintenance staff, and protecting drain outlets in mowable areas with appropriate headwalls. A continuous pavement base drain is provided to manage water at layer boundaries, and guide rail and drainage structures are adjusted or replaced as needed to maintain proper alignment and function. Where bonding between asphalt layers is required, an asphalt tack coat shall be applied, and all longitudinal and transverse joints shall be cleaned and sealed using heavy‑duty membranes as required. Prior to placing an overlay a leveling course of at least one inch minimum, including cross‑slope correction, may be placed to ensure a smooth transition between existing and new surfaces; the leveling course shall be counted as a paving layer in structural calculations but shall not be combined with a binder course. Because FB‑1 and FB‑2 surface courses have high void content, a seal coat or surface treatment shall be placed after at least three months of warm‑weather traffic densification (or up to three years if the underlying pavement is sound). When an overlay is designed using a deflection‑based program, the overlay depth shall be adjusted because the program assumes Superpave material, and a structural coefficient of 0.20 must be used for FB courses in the overlay design. Partial lane widenings less than ten feet shall be built so that new asphalt layers have the same depth as existing asphalt and any concrete layers have the same thickness as existing concrete, while keeping drainage layers consistent with those already in place; full‑width lane additions of ten feet or more should also match the existing pavement structure unless the added lane will carry a different traffic loading, in which case layer thicknesses shall be designed for the actual traffic and drainage verified throughout the cross‑section. When milling asphalt over concrete or brick, at least one and one‑half inches of the existing asphalt material shall remain to retain structural integrity and provide an adequate bond, and coring shall verify that bond exists before removal. The Safety Edge shall be incorporated into the wearing or binder course with a depth of one and a half inches or greater but not exceeding five inches, adding roughly a one percent increase in material quantity. If an interlayer is specified to mitigate reflective cracking, the selected material shall be compatible with the asphalt, thermally stable and physically durable, and shall be installed according to the applicable interlayer specifications. The cold‑recycled base course shall be allowed to cure for at least one week under low humidity and high temperature before the wearing surface is placed; because the mix retains a higher void content, a wearing surface shall be provided, and the compacted thickness of the recycled layer shall be no less than three inches and not exceed five inches. When reclaimed asphalt pavement consists of a wearing course or fine‑graded material, virgin coarse aggregate No. 8, 57, or 67 shall be added to satisfy Publication 27 specifications. Geogrids may be used at the interface between subgrade and aggregate layers to stabilize weak subgrades; a Class 4 Type A geotextile shall be installed between subgrade and subbase across the full lane width (eight feet or greater) to maintain material integrity, while a Class 4 Type C geotextile shall be used where the subgrade CBR is less than three to provide lateral restraint at the base‑subgrade interface. In frost‑prone areas a stronger base course shall be provided and pavement base drains installed at layer interfaces. When rock cuts are required, treatment shall follow the depth of subbase or other treatment defined in the Soil Survey Report, and lateral benches shall be retained in rock/soil transition zones. At the interface between the subgrade and the flexible‑pavement system, preparation of the subgrade is required and a Class 4 Type A geotextile must be installed before any subbase material is placed. The subbase is then spread over the geotextile to a uniform depth, with aggregate courses compacted to at least three inches for No. 2A material or at least four inches for No. OGS material, and each layer limited to not more than eight inches in compacted thickness (or not more than four inches when granulated slag is used). Compaction proceeds from the sides toward the centre with overlapping passes to achieve non‑movement as defined in Section 206.3(b), and depth and elevation are controlled according to Section 210.3(c); any deficiency of a half inch or more triggers scarifying, blending additional material, and recompacting before the surface is covered with the required asphalt base course prior to winter shutdowns. Drainage, environment, and accessibility

A flexible pavement design must incorporate a well‑drained system because excess water in any pavement layer, including the subgrade, can degrade material integrity and reduce structural capacity [1]. The design therefore calls for a subsurface drainage system in which base and subbase materials are highly permeable so that gravity‑drainable water is removed rapidly, and the slope of the subbase is maximized to increase the rate of gravity drainage; spacing between drainage pipes and outlets should be kept small to enhance removal [1]. Continuous pavement base drains shall be provided along the outside edge of the pavement and on the low side of superelevations, lateral underdrains are required at cut‑to‑fill transitions, and all outlets within mowable areas must be protected by appropriate headwalls [1]. A leveling course of at least a one‑inch minimum that includes cross‑slope correction is incorporated to promote water shedding [1]. When moisture intrusion is a concern an interlayer such as geotextile paving fabric or composite reinforcing grid may be used because it provides a pavement moisture barrier, and the selection of the interlayer shall also consider climate and traffic conditions, including freeze‑thaw cycles that may require a thicker overlay (minimum 1 ½ inches) to delay cracking in harsh environments [1]. For partial widening of less than ten feet the drainage layers are kept consistent with those of the existing pavement, whereas for full‑width lane additions of ten feet or more the designer must verify that drainage is adequately provided throughout the pavement cross section, especially when altering layer thicknesses [1]. In thin asphalt overlays the design shall verify that surface drainage is good or will be upgraded to good and that subsurface drainage is good or will be upgraded to good; otherwise the overlay cannot be placed [1]. Frost‑susceptible soils shall be removed or insulated with a non‑frost‑susceptible granular layer, and a stronger base course is used to resist reduced bearing support while pavement base drains and positive slopes are provided to remove water and mitigate frost heave [1]. Subbase material must not be placed on soft, muddy, or frozen areas, and it shall contain enough moisture to prevent segregation during stockpiling, hauling, and placing while minimizing additional water added during compaction [2]. The subbase is required to be maintained in a well‑moistened condition from before placement through compaction [2]. If a constructed subbase is exposed to weather for more than sixty calendar days, it shall be retested for surface irregularities and depth and any deficiencies corrected before paving [2]. Subbase or subbase covered with treated permeable base course must not be left exposed during winter shutdowns; the required asphalt base course shall be placed over all prepared subbase for flexible pavements before the shutdown of paving operations for the construction season [2].

In flexible pavement design, accessibility, safety, geometric and user requirements are prescribed across several sections of the specification. Under section 2.1 GENERAL, the design must address drainage to improve removal of surface and subsurface water, must provide base repair to replace areas of obvious failure with materials that will provide adequate structural capacity and facilitate drainage, must include scratch or leveling courses when a level platform is required to correct the existing surface, may add binder‑course thickness where increased structural capacity is needed as determined by non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures, must ensure sufficient surface friction to maintain safe vehicle traction, and must incorporate shoulders that support lateral loads and reduce edge stresses. Section 3.3 GUIDELINES FOR DEVELOPING INITIAL COSTS > A specifies that for flexible‑pavement rehabilitation the design must provide appropriate paved shoulders—type 6 or type 7 where applicable—and a continuous pavement base drain; guide rail and drainage structures should be adjusted or replaced as necessary to preserve safety, and a leveling course of at least 1‑inch minimum with cross‑slope correction ensures geometric conformity, while full‑depth asphalt shoulders are also specified for certain overlay treatments. In the context of widening (section 5.11), any partial lane widening that is less than 10 feet must match the existing pavement structure, meaning the asphalt layers are matched by equal depth and concrete layers by equal thickness, and as much as possible the drainage layers should be kept consistent with those of the existing pavement; for full‑width lane additions of ten feet or more, the new lane should also match the existing pavement structure unless circumstances dictate heavier or lighter traffic loadings for the additional lane, and it is important when changing the pavement layer thicknesses to check that drainage is adequately provided throughout the pavement cross section. Section 5.12 OVERLAYS requires a uniform cross‑slope established by a leveling course that is at least 1 inch thick, providing a consistent working platform for the binder or wearing layer; when a safety edge is incorporated it shall be applied on the outside edge of asphalt pavements and shoulders, formed as a 30° taper with a construction tolerance of 26° to 40°, and must have a depth of at least 1½ inches but not exceed 5 inches, adding roughly a 1 % increase in material quantity; for Superpave wearing‑course overlays on Interstates and non‑Interstates the thickness shall not be less than 1½ inches unless prior approval is obtained. Finally, section 5.13 COLD RECYCLED ASPHALT BASE COURSES states that projects carrying significantly heavy truck traffic—defined as 200 or more daily ESALs—should not be selected for cold recycling, only projects where traffic can be controlled or detoured during base construction and curing period should be selected, adequate drainage is required before recycling, and the base layer must be placed to a minimum compacted depth of 3 inches and may not exceed 5 inches. Verification and expected performance

The completed flexible pavement is verified by the Representative after each density test by digging a full‑depth test hole and measuring the subbase depth; any deficiency of ½ inch or more makes the layer defective and requires scarifying, blending additional material, recompacting and retesting. Compaction must achieve the “non‑movement” condition required in Section 206.3(b) with overlapping passes from side to centre. Surface tolerances are checked against the limits in Section 210.3(c), and if the subbase has been left exposed over winter a proof roll per Section 206.3(a)1 must be performed before placing the asphalt base course for flexible pavements, with any unstable areas repaired prior to final paving.
