Performance Grade (PG) Binder

Part of: Asphalt Binder is-subtopic-of
Performance Grade (PG) Binder is a topic under Asphalt Binder.
pg-binderpg-binderspg-binderpg-binderspg-64e-22
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Performance Grade (PG) Binder is an asphalt cement that must meet a defined performance grade—such as PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22 or PG 64E‑22—and satisfy the Standard Specifications for Performance‑Graded Asphalt Binder verified by the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery (MSCR) test in AASHTO M 332[1]. It serves as the binding matrix that holds aggregate together, delivering temperature‑dependent stiffness that enables pavement layers to resist permanent deformation (rutting) and low‑temperature thermal cracking while providing flexibility for low‑volume or high‑void surface courses[1]. The binder may be polymer‑modified (e.g., with styrene block copolymers or rubber) to increase viscosity, elastic recovery and durability, and must be sourced from an approved producer, accompanied by quality‑control testing and certification as required by the Pennsylvania specifications[2].

Material purpose and applicability

Performance Grade (PG) Binder is an asphalt cement that meets a defined performance grade such as PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22 or PG 64E‑22 and must satisfy the Standard Specifications for Performance‑Graded Asphalt Binder verified by the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery (MSCR) test in AASHTO M 332. In the FB surface courses described in Section 5.3 of the Pavement Policy Manual, the PG binder serves as the binding matrix that holds aggregate together and, because it can be softer than PG 64S‑22, provides the flexibility needed for low‑volume roads or as a wearing layer. For Superpave paving courses covered in Section 5.9 of the same manual, the selected PG binder together with the project’s ESAL count enables designers and contractors to formulate an asphalt mixture that can resist permanent deformation (rutting) and low‑temperature thermal cracking, thereby tailoring performance to site climate and traffic conditions. Section 5.10 of the Pavement Policy Manual specifies that only three standard PG‑Binder grades—PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22 and PG 64E‑22—are to be used in both conventional and Superpave pavement courses; the binder provides the binding matrix for the aggregate mixture and, through its temperature‑dependent stiffness, helps the pavement resist deformation such as rutting under summer heat. When higher high‑temperature resistance is required, designers may “bump” the binder to a higher grade to obtain a stiffer binder at elevated temperatures, improving rut resistance. The PG 64E‑22 grade is a polymer‑modified asphalt binder that incorporates styrene block copolymers (SB or SBS); it is specified because its short‑term construction performance and long‑term durability of 5 to 10 years have been documented to give good performance under Pennsylvania’s climate, traffic loads and various project types. A conventional PG 64S‑22 binder can also be modified with rubber; the resulting high‑viscosity binder provides good elastic recovery, increased durability, improved long‑term resistance to rutting and cracking, enhanced resistance to aging and oxidation, reduction of roadway noise, and the added rubber further stiffens the binder to improve rut resistance. In SECTION 413—SUPERPAVE MIXTURE DESIGN, STANDARD AND RPS CONSTRUCTION OF PLANT‑MIXED ASPHALT COURSES WITH PERCENT WITHIN LIMITS AND LTS TESTING of the PennDOT Specifications, a PG binder is supplied for virgin mixes, mixes containing reclaimed asphalt pavement or recycled shingles, and for blends with warm‑mix additives, providing the binding material that holds aggregate together in flexible pavement courses while delivering the required performance characteristics of the specified grade. In SECTION 412—SUPERPAVE MIXTURE DESIGN, CONSTRUCTION OF PLANT‑MIXED 6.3 MM THIN ASPHALT OVERLAY COURSES, a PG binder such as PG 64E‑22 must conform to the same specifications and is furnished as the asphalt component of a virgin mix for thin‑asphalt overlay courses; its function is to meet the specified performance grade while being subject to quality‑control testing and certification. In SECTION 419—STONE MATRIX ASPHALT MIXTURE DESIGN, RPS CONSTRUCTION OF PLANT‑MIXED WEARING COURSES, the PG binder must also meet the MSCR‑based specifications and may be supplied either as a virgin mix or in mixes containing up to 10 % reclaimed asphalt pavement, where it serves as the binding component that satisfies the required performance criteria for flexible pavements. In SECTION 341—COLD RECYCLED ASPHALT BASE COURSE, COLD‑IN‑PLACE, the binder must be one of the grades PG 64S‑22, PG 64V‑22, PG 64E‑22, PG 58S‑28 or PG 58V‑28 and must conform to AASHTO M 332; within a cold‑recycled asphalt base course it functions as the binding agent that, together with emulsified or foamed asphalt, coats reclaimed material and aggregates according to the mix design, providing cohesion and structural stability for the pavement. Finally, in SECTION 489—ULTRA‑THIN BONDED WEARING COURSE, the PG binder required for the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course must meet the Standard Specification for Performance‑Graded Asphalt Binder, be sourced from an approved producer listed in Bulletin 15 for the specified grade, and is supplied with quality‑control testing and certification; it serves as the binding component of the plant‑mixed asphalt concrete wearing course, providing cohesion between fine aggregate, coarse aggregate and filler to form a durable surface. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 103–131); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 220), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 245–344)

PG‑Binder selection for Superpave paving courses is linked to the projected 20‑year design life ESALs and applies to wearing, binder, and base layers. When the estimated ESAL range exceeds 10 million, a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder should be specified for wearing (or leveling or binder) courses where severe rutting conditions are existing or expected from stopping conditions or steep grades. For an ESAL estimate of 0.3 to < 3.0 million, the district may lower the range to < 0.3 million only for the wearing and binder courses if asphalt cracking is a problem in the region, the average truck speed is above 30 mph, and rutting is not a concern, but frequent stopping conditions, intersections, or steep hills with slow truck traffic are reasons to retain the higher range. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 111–113)

Composition and characteristics

Performance Grade (PG) binders are polymer‑modified asphalt materials whose composition is defined by the addition of specific polymers to a base binder, such as styrene block copolymers in SB or SBS formulations, while provisional approvals may rely on other polymer systems like natural latex (SBR formulation) or additional modifiers [1]. The resulting high‑viscosity asphalt matrix provides good elastic recovery and increased durability, contributing to long‑term resistance to rutting, cracking, aging and oxidation [1]. When rubber is incorporated into a conventional PG 64S‑22 binder the stiffness of the binder increases, improving rut resistance; however such rubber‑modified binders are not eligible for testing and grading under AASHTO M 320 or M 332 performance‑graded requirements [1]. The definition of a Performance‑Grade asphalt binder in the specifications is tied to compliance with the Standard Specifications for Performance‑Graded Asphalt Binder evaluated through the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery (MSCR) test prescribed in AASHTO M 332 [2]. In Section 412 the specification calls for a virgin PG binder identified as PG 64E‑22 that must meet those requirements and may be supplied either as an unmodified base binder or blended with an approved warm‑mix additive or modifier at the refinery, terminal, or plant, provided that the resulting blend continues to satisfy the performance‑grade criteria [2]. Section 419 permits the same performance‑grade verification but allows the binder to be a virgin mix or a mixture containing up to 10 % reclaimed asphalt pavement; it also may incorporate an approved WMA technology additive or modifier blended at the refinery/terminal or at the plant, on condition that the additive originates from an approved manufacturer and source listed in Bulletin 15 and that the final blend still meets the specified performance grade [2]. Section 341 specifies that PG binder must be supplied as one of the designated grades—PG 64S‑22, PG 64V‑22, PG 64E‑22, PG 58S‑28, or PG 58V‑28—and must conform to AASHTO M 332 with MSCR evaluation; it must be obtained from a source listed in Bulletin 15 and accompanied by the required quality‑control testing and certification [2]. The polymer‑modified composition described in the Pavement Policy Manual underlies the high‑viscosity matrix that enables the binder to satisfy the MSCR‑based performance criteria set forth in the Pennsylvania specifications [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 113–128); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 220), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 245–289)

In FB‑1 surface courses, using asphalt materials softer than PG 64S‑22 produces a highly flexible surface because the binder’s lower stiffness contributes to a high void content; consequently FB‑1 is recommended for low‑volume roads with highly flexible existing pavement structures. For FB‑2 mixes the specification permits either PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22, and both grades must be mixed and placed hot, so processing temperature directly influences the binder’s performance in the overlay. Adding crumb‑rubber modifiers to a conventional PG 64S‑22 binder changes its composition and proportioning, raising the binder’s viscosity and stiffness; this yields better elastic recovery, greater durability, enhanced long‑term resistance to rutting, cracking, aging, oxidation, and also reduces roadway noise. Because the resulting asphalt‑rubber binder cannot be tested and graded according to the performance‑graded asphalt binder requirements in AASHTO M 320 or M 332, it is not assigned a PG rating under those specifications. Variations in crumb‑rubber composition and fineness directly change the binder’s particle size and viscosity; a lower crumb rubber content combined with a finer gradation (#30 mesh or smaller) yields a CRMAB that has smaller particles and lower viscosity, which enables it to be placed into dense‑graded aggregates that have limited void space while still satisfying the required performance grade. The binder is produced by blending ground tire rubber with hot asphalt at a refinery or an asphalt‑binder storage and distribution terminal, creating a “terminal blend” that can be shipped to the mixing plant without altering the need to meet the physical requirements of AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22). Adding crumb rubber to a conventional PG 64S‑22 binder changes the composition and raises the performance grade to PG 64E‑22, indicating altered temperature susceptibility. The wet‑process requirement means the rubber chemically modifies the binder, whereas a dry process would leave the binder unchanged and treat the rubber mainly as aggregate; this rubber addition increases stiffness, so the mixture should not be used for projects where significant handwork or raking is required. In Section 413, changes in the binder’s composition, source, or processing can shift the performance grade that must be used: mixes that contain between 5 % and 15 % reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) or up to 5 % recycled shingles must still meet the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery (MSCR)‑based PG specifications and must come from a Bulletin 15 listed source, whereas mixes with more than 15 % RAP (or both RAP and recycled asphalt shingles) require the Laboratory Testing Services (LTS) to evaluate the reclaimed binder and then assign the appropriate grade. Adding warm‑mix asphalt (WMA) technology additives—whether blended at the refinery/terminal or at the plant—also demands that the base binder conform to the same PG requirements, be sourced from an approved list, and be covered by a detailed quality‑control plan; any material not specifically specified, such as unapproved additives or rejuvenators, must not be incorporated into the mixture. In Section 419, variations such as using a virgin mix or a mix that contains up to 10 % RAP, selecting an asphalt source listed in Bulletin 15, or incorporating WMA technology additives either at the refinery/terminal or at the plant are allowed only when the resulting binder still conforms to the performance‑grade requirements defined by the MSCR test and the applicable specifications; therefore quality‑control testing, certification, and documented dosage rates and blending methods must be provided to verify that these compositional, source, processing, or proportioning changes do not cause the binder to fall outside its specified grade. In Section 341, the specification requires that PG binder be obtained from a listed source for the chosen grade, and any change in source or grade obligates the contractor to develop a new mix design because the binder’s performance characteristics may differ; quality‑control testing and certification are then used to confirm compliance with the required properties. Additionally, when RAP gradations and composition vary sufficiently, field adjustments must be made according to the QC plan to achieve satisfactory coating and the specified compaction, indicating that variations in material composition can affect the mix’s behavior. In Section 489, because PG binder must meet the Standard Specification for Performance‑Graded Asphalt Binder and be sourced from an approved producer listed in Bulletin 15, any change in composition or source triggers a requirement to submit a revised Job Mix Formula before the new material can be used; failure to keep the binder within the JMF tolerances for asphalt content by mass and sieve gradations results in the mixture being classified as defective, and if compliance cannot be restored within 150 tons of production operations must be suspended. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 103–131); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 220), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 258–344)

Key material properties

Within asphalt rubber gap‑graded wearing courses, a Performance Grade binder’s high viscosity is identified as a key physical attribute because it supplies good elastic recovery and increased durability[1]. The same high viscosity contributes to mechanical stiffness that underpins long‑term resistance to rutting and cracking[1]. Chemical stability of the binder against aging and oxidation further enhances its durability in service[1]. When rubber is added to a conventional PG 64S‑22 binder, the resulting increase in binder stiffness is reported to improve rut resistance[1]. The modified binder also exhibits improved resistance to reflective, fatigue and low‑temperature cracking[1]. In addition, the use of rubber leads to a reduction in rutting[1]. Construction specifications for Performance Grade binders emphasize only the thermal condition during placement: the liquid asphalt must be kept at a temperature no lower than 290 °F and must not exceed 320 °F to ensure proper flow without damaging the interlayer system[2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 128–130); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 317–319)

In the Pavement Policy Manual, section 5.10 of the Superpave pavement‑course specifications, a Performance Grade binder is identified by a two‑number designation that couples a high‑temperature value with a low‑temperature value—for example PG 58S‑28—and only the three standard grades PG 58S‑28, 64S‑22, and 64E‑22 may be specified for conventional and Superpave courses [1]. The manual also permits a “bumping” practice that raises the high‑temperature component of the designation to obtain a stiffer binder for hot summer conditions, while any grade not listed among those three is treated as nonstandard and may be used only after prior approval from the Materials Division Laboratory Testing Section [1]. In dense‑graded courses modified with crumb rubber (section 5.17), the PG binder’s properties continue to be classified by its PG designation, and the addition of crumb rubber to a conventional PG 64S‑22 binder can bump the grade to PG 64E‑22 [1]. Section 413 of the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications requires that the rheological properties of a Performance‑Grade asphalt binder be measured with the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery (MSCR) test prescribed in AASHTO M 332, as amended by Bulletin 25, and the resulting data are used to classify the binder into the grade required by the project [2]. The same MSCR‑based measurement and classification are mandated in Section 412 for binders identified by their PG label (for example PG 64E‑22), with compliance demonstrated through quality‑control testing and certification documented in the contract, and the criteria also apply when a warm‑mix additive or modifier is blended with the base binder [2]. Section 419 likewise obliges that the binder satisfy the Standard Specifications for Performance‑Graded Asphalt Binder by evaluation of its rheological properties with the MSCR test defined in AASHTO M 332 (as revised by Bulletin 25), after which classification into a specific PG grade follows from those results [2]. In Section 341, the performance‑grade binder must be supplied in one of the specified PG designations (e.g., PG 64S‑22 or PG 58V‑28) and it has to conform to AASHTO M 332, which requires characterization by the MSCR test except where revised by Bulletin 25; its grade is therefore classified by its PG label and verified through that laboratory test [2]. Section 489 also references the Standard Specification for Performance‑Graded Asphalt Binder (AASHTO M 332) and requires that the binder be sourced from an approved producer listed in Bulletin 15, with performance properties verified through quality‑control testing and certification as mandated by Sections 106.03(b) and 702.1(b)1 [2]. Finally, Section 466 specifies a measurable property of a performance‑grade liquid asphalt binder—the allowable application temperature range—which must be at least 290 °F and must not exceed 320 °F to ensure the binder remains tacky without damaging the interlayer system [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 113–130); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 220), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 245–344)

Behavior and mechanisms

In FB‑1 mixes, the use of softer asphalt than PG 64S‑22 together with a high void content makes the binder highly flexible, which influences how it accommodates loading on low‑volume roads that have highly flexible existing pavement structures. The same high void content also renders the surface prone to moisture ingress, so a seal coat or surface treatment must be placed on FB surfaces after a minimum of 3 months of warm‑weather traffic densification; if the underlying pavement is sound the seal may be postponed for up to three years and may not be required on an FB Modified surface for at least four years. When PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 cement is used in FB‑2 mixes, the material must be mixed hot and placed hot, reflecting sensitivity to temperature during construction. FB wearing courses can be used independently only where average daily traffic does not exceed 1,500 vehicles. For Superpave paving courses, Performance Grade binders are selected together with the projected ESAL loading so that the mixture can resist permanent deformation (rutting) and thermal cracking; when the estimated ESAL range is greater than 10 million ESALs, a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder should be specified for wearing or binder courses to avoid pavement rutting. All ARBC materials are designed with less than 0.3 million ESAL (50 gyrations) mix designs and design air voids of 2.5%, which promotes better resistance to bottom‑up cracking and moisture damage resistance of the bottom pavement layer during its service life. Approved PG 64E‑22 binders, polymer‑modified with SB or SBS (and provisionally other polymers), have shown good performance under actual Pennsylvania climatic conditions and a range of traffic loads, and documentation indicates durability across temperature and moisture variations typical of the region for both short‑term construction behavior and long‑term service of 5 to 10 years. High‑viscosity PG binders provide good elastic recovery and increased durability, leading to improved long‑term resistance to rutting and cracking under traffic loading; they also exhibit better resistance to aging and oxidation, indicating enhanced performance when exposed to chemical degradation over time. Adding rubber to a conventional PG 64S‑22 binder can increase the binder stiffness, improve rut resistance, and raise its grade to PG 64E‑22, while crumb‑rubber modification further yields good elastic recovery, increased durability, improved resistance to reflective, fatigue and low‑temperature cracking, reduction in rutting compared with conventional mixes, and a reduction in roadway noise. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 103–130)

In FB surface courses the behavior of PG binders is governed primarily by the mix’s high void content, which imparts flexibility, and by the relative softness of the asphalt cement compared with PG 64S‑22; these physical characteristics make the FB‑1 mixes highly flexible. The requirement to use PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 in FB‑2 mixes and to mix and place the material hot introduces a temperature‑dependent mechanism that controls binder viscosity and workability, and design of FB overlays incorporates a structural coefficient of 0.20 reflecting the mechanical stiffness contribution of the binder‑asphalt system in deflection‑based analysis. For Superpave paving courses the performance of a PG binder is controlled by its ability to resist permanent deformation (rutting) and thermal cracking at low temperatures, which are addressed through selection of an appropriate PG binder based on projected ESALs; when the 20‑year design predicts more than 10 million ESALs the District should specify a polymer modified binder such as PG 64E‑22 for wearing courses to avoid pavement rutting, indicating that the polymer chemistry modifies stiffness and deformation resistance. Aramid (aromatic polyamide) fibers, known for strong tensile and heat‑resistance properties, may prevent or delay crack initiation and propagation, thereby influencing the mechanical response of the binder–aggregate system. The high viscosity of a PG binder provides elastic recovery and increased durability, which are mechanical mechanisms that help the pavement resist deformation over time; its ability to improve long‑term resistance to rutting and cracking reflects both physical stiffness and mechanical stability, while improved resistance to aging and oxidation represents a chemical mechanism that slows degradation. Adding rubber to a conventional PG 64S‑22 binder can increase binder stiffness, further enhancing rut‑resistance through a mechanical stiffening effect. In crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder (CRMAB) the behavior is governed primarily by the physical characteristics imparted by the rubber particles and resulting viscosity, the chemical interaction created when reclaimed tire rubber is blended with hot asphalt, and the mechanical context of the dense‑graded aggregate skeleton; CRMAB uses a finer gradation (#30 mesh or smaller) and a lower crumb‑rubber content, so its smaller particle size and lower viscosity allow incorporation into the limited void space of a dense‑graded mixture. The blending of ground tire rubber with hot asphalt at a refinery or an asphalt binder storage and distribution terminal produces a binder that must meet the physical requirements of AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22). When rubber is added by the wet process, the chemical interaction modifies the binder’s properties, whereas rubber used in the dry process acts more like an aggregate replacement; this modification raises mixture stiffness so that the resulting mechanical resistance to deformation can limit hand‑work or raking on projects, and if warm‑mix asphalt is employed a chemical warm‑mix additive must be used (foaming is not allowed). The behavior of a Performance‑Grade binder is controlled by its visco‑elastic response to repeated loading, which is quantified with the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery (MSCR) test that measures how the binder creeps under stress and how much it recovers, thereby governing deformation resistance. In addition, the binder must meet the physical property requirements set out in AASHTO M 332 for the specified grade (e.g., PG 64S‑22 or PG 58V‑28) and be subject to quality‑control testing and certification. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 103–131); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 220)

Durability and deterioration

The high void content of FB‑1 and FB‑2 wearing courses makes a PG binder vulnerable to loss of integrity unless protected, so a seal coat or surface treatment must be placed; before that at least three months of warm‑weather traffic densification is recommended to consolidate the mix. When the underlying pavement remains structurally sound and the FB surface stays satisfactory, the seal coat can be postponed for up to three years, while a FB Modified surface may go without a seal coat for at least four years. PG binders are selected so that the resulting asphalt mixture can resist the two primary durability concerns identified in Superpave design: permanent deformation (rutting) and low‑temperature thermal cracking. The specification process ties the PG‑binder choice to the projected ESAL loading because higher traffic levels increase the risk of rutting, while regional cracking problems or high truck speeds highlight the need for resistance to thermal cracking; when the estimated ESAL range exceeds 10 million ESALs, a polymer‑modified (PG 64E‑22) binder should be specified to avoid pavement rutting. The durability of a PG‑grade binder can be undermined by age oxidation of the existing asphalt, which may generate alligator cracking that can reflect through new overlays. Water intrusion into the structural section can cause stripping—the separation of asphalt and aggregate—loss of subgrade support, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation, and pavement deterioration; freeze‑thaw cycles further aggravate damage by expanding and contracting trapped moisture. Because these processes are linked to moisture movement, specifications call for interlayer systems that act as a moisture barrier and require appropriate binder application rates. The durability of a Performance Grade binder is also challenged primarily by aging and oxidation, which degrade the binder over time, and by mechanical distress such as rutting and cracking that develop under traffic loading. High‑viscosity binders can mitigate these mechanisms by providing elastic recovery and greater stiffness, especially when rubber is added to a conventional PG 64S‑22 binder; however, asphalt‑rubber binders cannot be tested and graded according to the performance‑graded asphalt binder requirements in AASHTO M 320 or AASHTO M 332. For performance‑grade liquid asphalt binders used in interlayer systems, the material shall be at least 290 °F and shall not exceed 320 °F during placement because higher temperatures can cause damage to the interlayer system. Before installation the product must remain dry, wrapped, and stored off the ground under cover to shield it from outdoor weather elements, ultraviolet light, precipitation, chemicals and sparks; if the outer wrap is damaged before installation the compromised layer must be discarded, indicating that physical damage to packaging also threatens binder integrity. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 103–128); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 317–319)

In FB‑1 and FB‑2 wearing courses, the high void content makes the surface more vulnerable to deterioration, so a seal coat or surface treatment must be placed on the FB surface (increase susceptibility) [1]. A minimum of three months of warm‑weather traffic densification is recommended before applying either a seal coat or surface treatment to mitigate that vulnerability (reduces susceptibility) [1]. When the underlying pavement is structurally sound and the FB surface is in satisfactory condition, the application of seal coat or surface treatment may be postponed for up to three years without substantially increasing deterioration risk (reduces susceptibility) [1]. For design traffic exceeding ten million ESALs, a polymer‑modified binder such as PG 64E‑22 is recommended for wearing or binder courses to avoid rutting, thereby lowering the binder’s tendency to deteriorate (reduces susceptibility) [1]. When estimated traffic lies between 0.3 and less than three million ESALs, the district may specify an ESAL range of less than 0.3 million for the wearing and binder courses if asphalt cracking is a regional problem, average truck speed exceeds thirty miles per hour, or rutting is not a concern; these conditions are intended to reduce susceptibility (reduces susceptibility) [1]. Conversely, roadway features such as frequent stopping conditions, intersections, and steep hills with slow‑moving trucks are cited as reasons for not adopting the lower ESAL specification because they could increase deterioration risk (increases susceptibility) [1]. Incorporating aramid fibers into the mix can prevent or delay crack initiation and propagation provided the fibers are well distributed, which reduces the binder’s vulnerability to cracking (reduces susceptibility) [1]. The high‑viscosity nature of a PG binder provides good elastic recovery and increased durability, lessening its tendency to deteriorate (reduces susceptibility) [1]. Adding rubber to a conventional PG 64S‑22 binder raises the binder stiffness, improves resistance to rutting, cracking, aging and oxidation, and cuts rutting potential compared with conventional mixes, thereby reducing susceptibility to performance loss over time (reduces susceptibility) [1]. Rubber modification also introduces elasticity that enhances durability and lowers the tendency to deteriorate; the crumb rubber gives good elastic recovery, improves resistance to reflective, fatigue and low‑temperature cracking, and converting PG 64S‑22 to PG 64E‑22 by adding rubber raises the performance grade and provides higher temperature stability, further reducing susceptibility (reduces susceptibility) [1]. In an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course, using fly ash as filler when design traffic is greater than or equal to three million ESALs increases the binder’s susceptibility to deterioration; omitting fly ash under such high‑traffic exposure reduces that risk (increases and reduces susceptibility) [2]. Requiring the binder to meet the Standard Specification for Performance‑Graded Asphalt Binder (AASHTO M 332) and to be obtained from an approved producer helps ensure its inherent properties are suitable for the intended temperature and loading conditions, thereby reducing susceptibility (reduces susceptibility) [2]. Maintaining asphalt content and aggregate gradation within the tolerances set out in the Job Mix Formula prevents defects that could accelerate deterioration, thus lowering susceptibility (reduces susceptibility) [2]. In the asphalt paving interlayer system, allowing the binder temperature to exceed 320 °F can cause damage to the interlayer system and increase susceptibility; the specification therefore mandates that the temperature must not exceed 320 °F (increases susceptibility if exceeded) [2]. Temperatures below the minimum of 290 °F can impair proper application, which may also raise the risk of deterioration (increases susceptibility if too low) [2]. Exposure to moisture, ultraviolet light, precipitation, chemicals or sparks during shipping and storage increases the binder’s susceptibility to damage; keeping the binder dry and wrapped helps reduce that risk (reduces susceptibility) [2]. Storing the binder elevated off the ground and covered protects it from outdoor weather elements, ultraviolet radiation, precipitation, chemicals and sparks, thereby decreasing its susceptibility to deterioration (reduces susceptibility) [2].

Material interactions and compatibility

In gap‑graded wearing courses, the high‑viscosity PG binder provides elastic recovery and durability, and when rubber is blended with a conventional PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder the addition of rubber can increase the binder stiffness to improve rut resistance. Because the presence of rubber changes the binder’s characteristics, asphalt‑rubber binders are not eligible for performance grading under AASHTO M 320 or M 332 specifications. In AR‑GG mixtures the rubber‑modified binder is first blended with crumb‑rubber modifier using the wet process before any aggregate is added, which ensures that the CRM is compatible with the PG binder; the mixture may also contain RAP or RAS material as allowed by the SSP, allowing reclaimed binder to be combined with virgin binder without additional steps. In dense‑graded courses, crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder contains fine crumb rubber particles (30 mesh or smaller) that lower the binder’s viscosity, which allows the PG 64E‑22 binder to be incorporated into aggregates with very limited void space while maintaining adequate adhesion. CRMAB must meet the physical requirements of the AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) specification as modified and specified in Bulletin 25 or AASHTO M 332, indicating that the rubber‑modified binder can satisfy the same performance‑grade criteria as a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder. Section 413 of the Pennsylvania specifications requires that Performance‑Grade binders may only be combined with materials expressly specified; no additives, rejuvenators or other substances may be incorporated unless they are listed, and for mixes containing RAP or RAS the LTS evaluates the binder present in the reclaimed material and then determines the PG class that the contractor must use, ensuring that the blended virgin and reclaimed binders together satisfy the required grade. Any warm‑mix asphalt technology additive or modifier may be used only if it is supplied by an approved manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15, with its dosage rate, blending method and quality‑control plan recorded on the bill of lading and in the producer QC plan to confirm compatibility with the base PG binder. Section 412 permits the PG 64E‑22 virgin binder to be combined with approved WMA technology additives either at the refinery/terminal or at the plant producing the mixture, and in both cases the additive is blended according to a documented dosage rate and method while a producer quality‑control plan must verify that the resulting blend still satisfies the performance‑grade requirements defined by the MSCR test; certification and bill‑of‑lading documentation are required to confirm that the modified binder meets the specified PG criteria. According to Section 419, the PG binder must satisfy the Standard Specifications for Performance‑Graded Asphalt Binder (MSCR test, AASHTO M 332) and may be supplied as a virgin mix or blended with up to ten percent RAP, showing compatibility with RAP binder within that limit; when warm‑mix asphalt technology is employed an approved WMA additive or modifier can be introduced either at the refinery/terminal or at the plant, but in both cases the final blend must still meet the specified performance grade. Section 341 specifies that Performance‑Grade binders such as PG 64S‑22 and PG 58V‑28 must be furnished conforming to AASHTO M 332 and the MSCR test, then combined with reclaimed asphalt pavement, aggregates, and either emulsified or foamed asphalt according to a mix design established from testing samples of each reclaimed material; if RAP gradations or composition vary sufficiently, field adjustments are made as detailed in the QC plan to obtain satisfactory coating and the required compaction, and any change in the binder source or grade requires a new mix design, ensuring proper blending of virgin PG binder with reclaimed material and adequate interaction with aggregates. When a Performance Grade (PG) binder is employed in an overlay, moisture intrusion can cause stripping of the asphalt‑aggregate bond; consequently any interlayer must be compatible with the binder to provide a strong internal adhesive or mechanical bond within the asphalt matrix and must also be thermally stable for long‑term performance [1]. The construction guidance further requires that the existing pavement surface be dry and free of dirt, gravel, vegetation, and oil, explicitly prohibiting placement of the interlayer system on wet pavement, which underscores a moisture‑related compatibility risk for PG binders [2]. For overlays in which the existing asphalt layer over a concrete or brick base is milled away, retaining at least the preferred minimum of 1 1/2 inches of the original asphalt is advised; reducing the remaining thickness below this value can diminish structural integrity and lead to debonding between layers, creating additional compatibility concerns [1]. Temperature susceptibility is addressed by specifying that performance‑grade liquid asphalt binders must be heated to no less than 290 °F and must not exceed 320 °F during placement in order to avoid damage to the interlayer system [2].

Influence of production and placement

For FB‑2 surfaces that incorporate PG 58S-28 or PG 64S-22 asphalt cement, mixing must be done at a stationary plant and both mixed hot and placed hot to ensure the binder attains the temperature needed to properly coat the high‑void, highly flexible mix; this temperature control is critical for achieving the intended performance of the PG binder in the FB overlay. The high void content inherent to FB mixes imparts pronounced flexibility, which together with proper hot mixing/placement supports durability. A seal coat or surface treatment should be delayed until at least three months of warm‑weather traffic densification have occurred to allow adequate curing. For performance‑grade liquid asphalt binders used in the interlayer system, plant mixing and production temperatures must be kept at or above 290 °F so the material remains tacky and can be sprayed uniformly. The temperature may not exceed 320 °F because higher heat would damage the asphalt paving interlayer system; maintaining this window protects binder integrity and ensures proper bonding during placement. Selection, specification, and improvement

For FB‑1 wearing or binder layers the manual specifies binders softer than PG 64S‑22 to provide the high flexibility needed in these high‑void mixes that are typically produced on mobile plants, whereas for FB‑2 the specification permits either PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement and, because the mix must be produced at a stationary plant and placed hot, PG 64S‑22 is more commonly used [1]. For Superpave paving courses the choice of a PG binder is primarily governed by the 20‑year design life ESAL total; when the estimated ESAL range is greater than 10 million ESALs the District should specify a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder for wearing (or leveling or binder) courses to avoid rutting, while an ESAL range of 0.3 to < 3.0 million may permit using a lower‑grade binder and the < 0.3 million range is allowed only for wearing and binder courses, especially if cracking is a regional problem, average truck speed exceeds 30 mph, or rutting is not a concern [1]. Specification is first limited to the three standard grades—PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22 and PG 64E‑22—that are authorized for both conventional and Superpave pavement courses, with the appropriate grade chosen according to the project type and pavement layer as listed in Table 5.5; when higher summer temperatures are expected a “bumping” of the high‑temperature grade may be applied to obtain a stiffer binder that improves rut resistance, and any PG binder outside the standard list is considered nonstandard and can be specified only after obtaining prior approval from BOCM’s Materials Division (Laboratory Testing Section) and completing a Superpave Special Study Project [1]. For overlay applications the specification requires PG 64E‑22 for standard Superpave 6.3 mm thin wearing courses, whereas PG 64E‑22 shall not be used for Superpave 9.5 mm fine‑grade wearing courses [1]. The use of polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 as the Performance Grade binder for asphalt‑rubber gap‑graded (AR‑GG) mixtures is guided by whether the project would normally employ a gap‑graded wearing or binder course, and such projects may be considered only when traffic exceeds 25,000 ADT, cumulative design ESALs exceed 10 million over a 20‑year life, and the area is greater than 50,000 square yards so that the premium AR‑GG mix remains cost‑effective; it shall not be used where significant handwork or raking is required due to stiffness, nor in shoulders unless lane and shoulder are paved together, nor in temporary lanes or crossovers, and layer thickness must not exceed 2 ½ in [1]. Selection of crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder (CRMAB) for dense‑graded courses is guided by project type, performance expectations, cost considerations, and physical constraints; CRMAB binders should be considered for projects that would normally use dense‑graded wearing or binder courses with a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder and may be used for new construction or overlays on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements, but they should not be used where significant handwork or raking is required because of mixture stiffness [1]. Because CRMAB is a premium, more expensive mix it should only be specified when the benefits justify the cost, typically in the upper wearing and binder layers of high‑traffic Interstates or Interstate look‑alikes that exceed > 25,000 ADT, > 10 million ESALs (20‑year design life), and > 50,000 square yards; it should not be placed in shoulders unless lane and shoulder are paved in one pass, nor used in temporary lanes or crossovers, and additional constraints include limiting layer thickness to no more than 2 ½ in, using the wet process only for binder modification, and employing a chemical warm‑mix additive if warm‑mix asphalt is used (foaming not allowed) [1]. The selection of a Performance‑Grade (PG) binder for any pavement application is driven by compliance with the Standard Specification for Performance‑Graded Asphalt Binder, which must be verified by the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery (MSCR) test in accordance with AASHTO M 332 and any revisions contained in Bulletin 25; in every case the binder must be obtained from a supplier or producer listed in Bulletin 15 for the required grade, and it must be accompanied by quality‑control testing and certification as prescribed in Sections 106.03(b) and 702.1(b)1 [2]. For cold‑recycled asphalt base courses (Section 341) the allowable grades are limited to PG 64S‑22, PG 64V‑22, PG 64E‑22, PG 58S‑28 or PG 58V‑28; any change in source or grade requires a new mix design and a signed Bill of Lading must be provided on the first day of paving and whenever the batch number changes [2]. For mixes that contain more than 15 % reclaimed asphalt pavement, or a combination of at least 5 % recycled asphalt shingles with 5 % or greater RAP (Section 413), Laboratory Technical Services evaluates the binder present in the RAP (and RAS if applicable) and determines the specific PG class that must be used [2]. For projects that specify a particular performance grade such as PG 64E‑22 (Sections 412, 419, 489), the binder must meet the same MSCR compliance, be sourced from Bulletin 15, and when a warm‑mix asphalt additive or modifier is employed the additive and its manufacturer also must be approved and listed in Bulletin 15; the dosage rate, blending method, QC testing, corrective‑action procedures, storage, handling, shipping documentation and bill of lading information must be incorporated into the producer’s quality‑control plan to demonstrate that the modified binder still satisfies the specified performance grade [2].

Performance evaluation and implications

The evaluation of PG‑Binder performance incorporates documented short‑term construction observations and long‑term service records extending five to ten years, as evidenced by pre‑construction manual pavement distress surveys, additional asphalt binder sampling, loose box sampling and core sampling performed by the Long‑Term Service (LTS) program and the Northeast Center of Excellence for Pavement Technology (NECEPT), followed by post‑construction manual pavement distress surveys and core sampling over a three to five year period after construction. [1] Laboratory verification of a Performance‑Grade binder is required through the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery (MSCR) test specified in AASHTO M 332 as revised in Bulletin 25, which provides the primary rheological evidence of high‑ and intermediate‑temperature performance. [2] The results of the MSCR test must be documented in accordance with the quality‑control testing and certification procedures outlined in Sections 106.03(b) and 702.1(b)1. [2] When reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) or shingles are incorporated, a Long‑Term Performance (LTS) assessment of the binder is also performed, and the LTS result is used to assign the PG class that the contractor must use in the mixture. [2] The same laboratory and quality‑control requirements for MSCR testing apply to Superpave mixture design projects covered by Sections 413, 412, 419 and to cold‑recycled asphalt base courses described in Section 341, where compliance is demonstrated solely through the MSCR test and its associated certification. [2]

The selection of a Performance‑Grade (PG) binder is tied directly to the projected traffic loading so that its high‑temperature stiffness and low‑temperature flexibility can be used to design mixes that resist permanent deformation (rutting) and thermal cracking. For projects with an estimated 20‑year design life ESALs greater than 10 million, the guidelines require a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder in wearing courses to avoid rutting, whereas for an estimated range of 0.3 to < 3.0 million ESALs the district may specify lower‑ESAL specifications for wearing and binder layers when cracking is a concern. Only three standard PG binders—PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22, and PG 64E‑22—are to be specified in conventional and Superpave pavement courses, which limits the range of binder stiffness and low‑temperature cracking resistance available on a project. When higher summer temperatures are expected, “bumping” the high‑temperature grade (for example from PG 58S‑28 to PG 64S‑28) makes the binder stiffer at those temperatures and therefore improves rutting resistance. Nonstandard grades may be used only after prior approval and a Superpave Special Study Project. Approved PG 64E‑22 binders are polymer‑modified by adding styrene block copolymers (SB or SBS), and their performance both in the short term (construction) and long term (5 to 10 years) has been documented as providing good elastic recovery, increased durability, improved resistance to aging and oxidation, and lower roadway noise levels. A high‑viscosity PG binder delivers elastic recovery that translates into greater pavement durability, while its inherent stiffness contributes to long‑term resistance against both rutting and cracking. When a crumb‑rubber modified (CRMAB) binder is used, the lower rubber content and finer gradation produce a smaller particle size and lower viscosity that allow incorporation into dense‑graded aggregates with limited void space, enabling dense‑graded courses without excessive air voids and providing performance similar to polymer‑modified binders. CRMAB must meet the physical requirements of AASHTO M 320 (PG 64E‑22) as modified in Bulletin 25 or AASHTO M 332, and its use is recommended only in the upper wearing and binder courses where its superior performance can be best utilized; layer thickness should not exceed 2½ inches. For FB‑2 mixes that permit PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement, hot mixing and hot placement at a stationary plant are required, and because of the high void content a seal coat or surface treatment is required after a minimum of three months of warm‑weather traffic densification, although it may be postponed up to three years if the underlying pavement is sound. FB‑Modified surfaces may not require such treatment for at least four years. When a deflection‑based design program is used, the overlay depth for FB courses must be adjusted by applying a structural coefficient of 0.20 because the program otherwise assumes a Superpave binder. The use of an asphalt rubber binder that replaces a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder increases stiffness and therefore should not be used on projects where significant handwork or raking is required; its layer thickness also must not exceed 2½ inches, and structural design must employ the Superpave layer coefficients from Table 10.6 for the AR‑GG layer. In cold‑recycled base courses, a Performance‑Grade binder that meets PG 64S‑22, PG 64V‑22, PG 64E‑22, PG 58S‑28 or PG 58V‑28 and conforms to AASHTO M 332 with Multiple Stress Creep Recovery testing ensures the required performance characteristics, and any change in source or grade triggers a new mix design to maintain proper coating, compaction and long‑term durability. The contract obligates the contractor to obtain the binder from a Bulletin 15 listed source, provide quality‑control testing and certification as specified, and incorporate the exact grade into the mix design. During interlayer installation the performance‑grade liquid asphalt binder must be kept hot enough to stay tacky but not so hot that it degrades the system, with a required temperature minimum of 290 °F and a maximum of 320 °F; maintaining this window allows a uniform spray pattern, proper saturation of the reinforcing fabric, and prevents damage to the interlayer, thereby achieving the intended performance and durability.
