Traffic Loading

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Traffic Loading is a topic under Pavement Design.
traffic-loadingtraffic-loadingsfuture-traffictraffic-loading-conditionsanticipated-traffic
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Traffic Loading is the engineering analysis that converts projected vehicle traffic—average daily traffic, growth rates, and truck composition—into equivalent single‑axle loads (ESALs) so that pavement structures can be sized to sustain those loads without premature failure or excessive cost[1]. The process requires a recent condition survey and a summary table of ADT and truck percentage, then applies the 1993 AASHTO design procedures via AASHTOWare® DARWin® 3.01—using the rigorous 18‑kip ESAL calculation unless the Simple Method is permitted—to determine the structural number or layer thickness for concrete, RCC, asphalt overlays, and ramp pavements[1][2]. Ongoing verification through periodic traffic‑classification counts monitors cumulative ESALs, and when they exceed the projected 20‑year design‑life values the contractor’s warranty liability is released, ensuring that excessive loading is identified and addressed[2].

Purpose and design objectives

Traffic Loading is incorporated into pavement design to provide the vehicle‑load data required to address the engineering problem of pavements that are structurally inadequate or lack sufficient capacity, thereby enabling designs that can sustain service without premature failure or excessive cost. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements the required compressive strength is selected so that the pavement can support the expected average daily traffic and speed limits without failure. Whenever field observations indicate a change in pavement condition, updated traffic data shall be obtained and a new design performed, with a summary table of ADT and truck percentage included for each design, to verify structural adequacy. Within an interchange the design of all ramp pavement structures shall be based on the ramp that experiences the highest traffic loadings; if heavy‑truck traffic may develop, the ramp shall be designed accordingly, and the acceleration and deceleration portions must have the same pavement type and thickness as the adjoining roadway to withstand stresses from acceleration, deceleration, and heavy vehicles. The total 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) for a project’s performance period are calculated using the Rigorous ESAL Calculation method, and when daily ESALs exceed the limits of the Simple Method the Rigorous Method must be used to provide the quantitative basis for determining structural thickness and material requirements that keep the pavement serviceable throughout its design life. For overlay projects undertaken to improve ride quality or address suspected structural inadequacy, accurate traffic information—including ADT, projected growth, and truck percentage—is employed to compute daily 18‑kip ESALs and to satisfy a minimum eight‑year and a maximum twenty‑year structural design life. In an AC overlay of existing AC pavement the Structural Number for Future Traffic and the Effective Existing Structural Number are calculated, and the calculated SN must be greater than the Overlay (Design) SN so that the overlay remains structurally adequate under future loads; similar procedures using rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors determine pavement thickness for future traffic in overlays of jointed plain concrete pavement and AC/PCC composite pavement. Traffic Loading is also used to verify that a pavement is subjected only to the traffic volumes for which it was designed, and when Department traffic counts indicate that cumulative ESALs have exceeded 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life ESALs, the warranty provisions relieve the contractor of responsibility for repairing rutting or base‑condition distresses for the remainder of the warranty period. This verification ensures that excessive vehicle loading, which would otherwise cause premature pavement failure, is identified and accounted for as a performance need. [1][2]

For an overlay, the functional objective is to improve ride quality and to address any suspected structural inadequacy, while safety is addressed by requiring designs for low‑friction conditions; durability is ensured by designing each overlay for a minimum 8-year structural design life and a maximum of 20 years, which also satisfies serviceability expectations tied to ride performance. Scope and applicability

Traffic‑loading considerations are appropriate for roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements when the structure will serve as a concrete pavement, concrete shoulder, or concrete base course on roadways that have an average daily traffic (ADT) of less than or equal to 2,000 and a speed limit of 55 mph or less; under these conditions RCC may be used as the final wearing surface on shoulders adjacent to reinforced or plain cement‑concrete pavements, as a temporary pavement such as median crossovers or widening areas, or as either a final wearing course or a base course beneath an asphalt wearing course or surface treatment (section 4.11). The RCC mix must be a very dry concrete with a low water‑cement ratio—​as low as 0.30 and typically 0.40—a dense aggregate gradation with a maximum size less than 1 inch, and may include limited admixtures or air entrainment; when the exposed RCC surface will support speeds of 35 miles per hour or greater, diamond grinding is required to provide texture (section 4.11). Traffic loading is also appropriate for the design of ramp pavements on limited‑access highway interchanges, whether the pavement is concrete or asphalt; the ramp shall use the same pavement type and thickness as the higher‑traffic‑volume roadway it merges with, and its classification and minimum depth are taken from that higher‑volume road (section 6.6). Design is based on the ramp that experiences the highest traffic loadings, and if there is a potential for heavy truck traffic the design shall reflect those loads (section 6.6). Traffic‑loading analysis via equivalent single axle load (ESAL) calculation is required for all functional classifications of highways; for collectors and local roads the Simple Method may be used for new construction or rehabilitation provided that the resulting layer thicknesses do not exceed the minimum values for that road type, otherwise the Rigorous Method must be applied (section 7.1). On cross routes that interchange with limited‑access freeways a minimum design daily 18‑kip ESAL value from Table 7.3 may be used only when the projected ADT is 8,000 or less; if the ADT exceeds 8,000 the Rigorous Method must be employed (section 7.1). The Simple Method is expressly prohibited on any roadways other than collectors and local roads (section 7.1). The AC‑overlay of jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) design approach, which incorporates traffic loading through the use of rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors for ESAL calculations, may be applied when an asphalt concrete overlay is being placed over existing JPCP, jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP), or continuously reinforced concrete pavement (CRCP); it is also appropriate when the existing surface is a concrete slab that currently has an asphalt overlay that will be stripped before the new AC overlay is installed (section 10.4). Design inputs and requirements

Traffic loading design must be based on a reliable estimate of future traffic levels and a recent pavement condition survey that records current distress, and it requires site information such as a location map showing the project’s beginning and ending segments and any intersecting roadways. The required traffic data are to be presented as a summary table of average daily traffic (ADT) and truck percentage together with all breakdowns, source and date of the traffic data. Material and geotechnical inputs include representative material sampling performed according to the prescribed number, size, depth and handling criteria, a CBR summary table, Falling Weight Deflectometer data, soils testing results, in‑situ testing such as dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer to evaluate subgrade stiffness, and a frost‑heave probability. Environmental details are reflected in the cover letter, for example the environmental approval date. Existing‑condition information must comprise an assessment of existing drainage conditions so that any water problems can be identified and corrected before construction, as well as the pavement history for the project limits obtained from RMS. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 6 (pp. 134–136)

Traffic loading on Pennsylvania pavements is governed by a series of standards, specifications, performance requirements, and project constraints that define allowable traffic volumes, speed limits, design procedures, analysis tools, data collection, warranty criteria, and remedial actions. For roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavement the PennDOT criteria limit the allowable average daily traffic to less than or equal to 2,000 vehicles and require a speed limit of 55 mph or less when RCC is used as a final wearing or base course, as specified in Publication 408 specifications sections 502, 658, and 323 [1]. The pavement thickness and compressive‑strength requirements for RCC must follow the current PennDOT concrete pavement design procedures in Chapter 8 Section 8.15, which may range from 4 inches to 18 inches and allow compressive strengths from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi [1]. Control joint spacing for RCC pavements shall be as designated in Publication 72M RC‑27M, with joints spaced 15 feet to 20 feet when the pavement is less than 8 inches thick and, for thicknesses of 8 inches or greater, spaced at a distance equal to the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet [1]. Full‑depth reclamation projects must obtain a reliable estimate of future traffic loading before construction and the overall pavement design must be consistent with standard pavement design traffic analysis procedures [1]. Traffic‑loading inputs for PennDOT pavement designs are governed by the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures as amended by the Department, and all analyses must be performed using the required AASHTOWare® DARWin® 3.01 software together with a summary table of average daily traffic and truck percentage that includes source and date information [1]. An analysis is required whenever a new, reconstructed or rehabilitated pavement segment exceeds 500 continuous feet on divided roadways, 1,000 continuous feet on undivided roadways, or when bridge approaches total more than 1,000 feet, and any design‑year shift of more than three years also triggers a new design; project schedules further constrain traffic‑related work by imposing specific let‑out and approval timelines for preservation, moderate‑complexity, and most‑complex projects [1]. Ramp pavement designs on limited‑access highways shall conform to the same pavement type as the higher‑traffic‑volume roadway in the interchange, the acceleration and deceleration sections must match that roadway’s pavement type and thickness, all ramps are classified using the higher‑traffic‑volume classification, and the design of every ramp structure is based on the ramp experiencing the highest traffic loadings with minimum depths appropriate to that higher‑traffic‑volume roadway applied throughout; if a development scenario could generate heavy truck traffic, the ramp shall be designed accordingly [1]. Traffic data for pavement design will be supplied by the Bureau of Planning and Research (BPR), and the rigorous ESAL calculation method of DARWin must be used unless the Simple Method may be applied only on collectors and local roads and only when the resulting pavement layer thicknesses do not exceed the minimum layer thicknesses for the appropriate type of roadway; otherwise the Rigorous Method must be used, and for cross routes that interchange with limited‑access freeways, if the projected design‑year ADT is greater than 8,000 the Rigorous Method must be employed [1]. All overlay designs will be designed for a minimum eight‑year structural design life and a maximum of twenty years and must be based on the best available traffic data; the minimum information needed is current average daily traffic, projected traffic growth, and the truck percentage, with a full truck distribution required unless the roadway is a collector or local road, in which case the Simple Method may be used to calculate the 18‑kip ESALs for the project (see Chapter 7) [1]. If the required traffic information is not available in the District, it shall be requested from BPR; if BPR cannot supply it, the district may perform counts following Appendix B with all collected data submitted back to BPR for refinement [1]. Each design also requires completion of Form D‑4332 for PDAU concurrence and FHWA approval where applicable [1]. For an asphalt‑concrete overlay on existing AC pavement, traffic loading is governed by Chapter 10 Section 10.2 which requires using Table 10.7 to select appropriate input values for the future‑traffic structural number and applying the flexible‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 for ESAL calculations; the effective existing structural number must be obtained with either the Component Method or the Non‑Destructive Testing Method, and subbase material is excluded from the structural evaluation on Interstates and non‑Interstate roads when the design 18‑kip ESAS exceeds 5,000,000 unless a specific investigation confirms its condition; the calculated overlay structural number must be greater than the Overlay (Design) structural number for adequacy [1]. Similar procedures apply to an AC overlay of fractured PCC slab, where the structural number for future traffic and the existing effective structural number are calculated using the Component Method and subbase guidance from Section 10.2 before sizing thickness according to Table 10.7 with ESAL values derived from the flexible‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors listed in Table 7.1 [1]. For an asphalt‑concrete overlay on jointed plain concrete pavement, traffic loading is governed by the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors listed in Table 7.1 for ESAL calculations and the designer must reference Table 10.8 for appropriate input values while calculating pavement thickness for future traffic [1]. For an asphalt‑concrete overlay on an AC/PCC composite pavement, the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic shall be obtained from Table 10.8, the Effective Existing Thickness must be calculated using the Condition Survey Method as directed in Table 10.9, and ESAL calculations shall use the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors listed in Table 7.1 [1]. For a bonded PCC overlay on a PCC pavement, traffic loading also uses the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 for ESAL calculations; the resulting concrete thickness shall be rounded up to the nearest half‑inch while staying within the minimum and maximum depths specified in Table 10.10, and bonded overlays should only be utilized where the existing concrete pavement does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting [1]. Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) data may be considered to determine district priorities, although it does not need to be addressed in District Interstate plans [1]. The 60‑month asphalt concrete pavement warranty governs traffic loading by requiring the Department to conduct routine traffic classification counts that verify ESAL estimates do not exceed the values projected in the contract documents [2]. Performance of the paved segment is evaluated against distress limits set out in Table A; if a year‑5 survey shows that 30% of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, microsurfacing or an approved alternative must be applied, and if combined deviations exceed a remedial‑action area greater than 20% of the segment surface, the entire segment must be removed and replaced [2]. Additionally, repair patches whose locations are less than 100 feet apart must be merged into one continuous repair [2].

Design methods and criteria

Traffic loading analysis for Pennsylvania Department of Transportation pavement designs is performed with the required AASHTOWare® DARWin® 3.01 software, which applies the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures as amended by the department and incorporates traffic inputs such as average daily traffic (ADT) and truck‑percentage tables. The analysis may employ either the Rigorous ESAL Calculation method in DARWin or, where permitted, the Simple Method; the rigorous approach computes total 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads for the performance period by entering the design ADT, lane and directional distribution factors, vehicle classification percentages, annual growth rates for traffic volume and truck factor, and the average initial truck factor from Table 7.1 using the compound growth rate calculation type mandated in DARWin. For collectors and local roads the Simple Method may be applied provided that the resulting layer thicknesses do not exceed the minimum layer thicknesses for the appropriate roadway type (see Table 9.4), otherwise the rigorous method must be used, and for cross‑routes serving limited‑access freeways a minimum daily 18‑kip ESAL value from Table 7.3 is prescribed unless the projected ADT exceeds 8,000, which then also forces use of the Rigorous Method. For overlay designs traffic loading begins with collection of the minimum required data—current ADT, projected growth, and truck percentage—and, unless the road is a collector or local street, a truck distribution that permits the Simple Method to compute design daily 18‑kip ESALs as prescribed in Chapter 7; these ESAL values are entered into DARWin, which applies the 1993 AASHTO procedures for the seven overlay types. When the overlay is a bonded concrete over asphalt or composite pavement the BCOA‑ME mechanistic‑empirical procedure is used, with designs performed for a minimum eight‑year structural design life and may extend to a maximum of twenty years. In designing an AC overlay on existing AC pavement traffic loading is accounted for by first determining the Structural Number required for future traffic using the values in Table 10.7 together with flexible‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 to compute ESALs, then calculating the effective existing SN with DARWin’s Component Method (or the Non‑Destructive Testing Method) which applies the structural number equation of Section 9.8 using layer depths and coefficients (all drainage coefficients equal to 1.0); the overlay SN is obtained as the difference between future‑traffic SN and effective existing SN, after which a thickness design—either specified or optimized—is performed in DARWin ensuring that the calculated SN exceeds the design SN, while subbase layers are excluded from the structural evaluation on Interstates and all other roadways when the design 18‑kip ESALs exceed 5,000,000 unless subbase quality has been verified. The AC overlay of a fractured PCC slab follows the same sequence: calculate the SN for future traffic, determine the existing effective SN with the Component Method, and compute an overlay SN that is used in a thickness design referencing Table 10.7 and Section 10.2, with subbase inclusion following the guidance of Section 10.2 and flexible‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 employed to obtain ESAL values. For an AC overlay of jointed plain concrete pavement traffic loading is addressed by first applying rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 to compute ESALs, then using the secondary screen in DARWin—which mirrors the rigid‑pavement design process—to determine the required overlay thickness for future traffic, with input values drawn from Table 10.8. In designing an asphalt concrete overlay for an AC/PCC composite pavement, Traffic Loading first requires determining the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic according to the guidance in Table 10.8; the Effective Existing Thickness must be calculated using the Condition Survey Method as shown in Table 10.9, and these two inputs are entered into DARWin which returns the actual asphalt overlay thickness required rather than a structural number. For a bonded Portland cement concrete overlay of a PCC pavement Traffic Loading also begins with the same average initial truck factors to compute ESALs; the design proceeds in DARWin by first calculating the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic per Table 10.8, then computing the Effective Existing Thickness using the Condition Survey evaluation method described in Table 10.9, after which selecting the Calculate button yields the required concrete overlay thickness rounded up to the nearest half‑inch. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements the department requires application of the current PennDOT concrete pavement design procedures set out in Chapter 8, Section 8.15 to establish the necessary structural thickness, treating RCC as conventional concrete and permitting compressive strengths from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi; an empirical control‑joint rule then governs joint spacing, requiring joints every 15 ft to 20 ft for pavements less than 8 in thick and, for pavements 8 in or greater, a spacing equal to the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 ft. Ramp pavement design follows a procedure that requires the ramp to use the same concrete or asphalt type and thickness as the higher‑traffic roadway it merges with, classifying the ramp by that roadway’s classification; the design of all ramp structures within an interchange is based on the ramp with the highest traffic loadings, and if heavy‑truck traffic is anticipated the ramp shall be designed accordingly. Under the warranty specification traffic loading is monitored by conducting periodic traffic classification counts that are used to calculate cumulative Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) values and verify they remain within the projected ESAL amounts set out in the contract documents; if those counts show that estimated cumulative ESALs have exceeded 100% of the estimated 20‑year design life ESALs, or increase enough to require a mix‑design change, the contractor is not held responsible for repairing rutting or base‑condition distresses for the remainder of the warranty period. [1][2]

[1] When the projected design‑year ADT of a cross route that interchanges with a limited‑access freeway exceeds 8,000 vehicles, the rigorous method must be used; the simple method may be applied only on collectors and local roads and only when the pavement layer thicknesses obtained do not exceed the minimum values prescribed for the roadway type. [1] Overlay designs are required to have a structural design life of at least eight years but not more than twenty years. Materials and mixture design

For roller compacted concrete pavements that will be subjected to traffic loading, the specification must include cement, aggregate and water; the mix design shall employ a lower water‑cement ratio as low as 0.30 but typically 0.40, shall use a dense aggregate gradation with a maximum aggregate size less than 1 inch, may or may not incorporate air entrainment, may have limited use of admixtures, and the resulting compressive strength can range from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi. For ramp pavement design, traffic loading must specify the pavement type—concrete or asphalt—and require that it match the type used on the higher‑traffic roadway in the interchange; the acceleration and deceleration sections of a ramp are required to have the same pavement type and thickness as the adjoining mainline roadway, and the design shall apply minimum depths that are appropriate to the higher‑traffic volume roadway. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 6 (p. 152)

When roller‑compacted concrete pavement is selected for traffic loading, the mixture is proportioned with a water‑cement ratio as low as 0.30 (typically 0.40) and a dense aggregate gradation whose maximum size is less than 1 inch; air entrainment may be used and admixtures are limited, producing compressive strengths that can range from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi and making the pavement suitable for roads with an ADT of less than or equal to 2,000 and a speed limit of 55 mph or less. The required pavement thickness is then chosen between 4 inches and 18 inches using the PennDOT concrete design procedures, and the underlying subgrade and subbase must be prepared to support compaction, thereby meeting structural, durability, constructability and performance goals. For an overlay, traffic loading requires using current ADT, projected growth and truck percentage to compute daily 18‑kip ESALs, after which Tables 10.1‑10.3 are applied to select the appropriate asphalt material—leveling, wearing or base—for that traffic level; Table 10.4 then dictates the minimum and maximum layer thicknesses, with a minimum of 2 ½ inches of binder when first overlaying concrete or brick and the wearing course required to meet its listed minimum thickness, while the appropriate Superpave mixture size from Table 10.5 completes the mix design. All of these steps together satisfy structural requirements of a minimum 8‑year and maximum 20‑year design life, as well as durability, constructability and performance requirements, and any special cases such as curb taper in parking lanes must be reviewed with the District Pavement Management Engineer. Structural details and interfaces

According to the Roller Compacted Concrete (RCC) guidelines in Chapter 4, RCC pavement is designed without forms, steel reinforcing, or dowel bars, so reinforcement and load‑transfer devices are not required. Control joints may be omitted, but the Department requires them in shoulders so that they line up with the adjacent mainline concrete pavement; when joints are used their spacing must follow the prescribed rules. For RCC sections thinner than 8 inches the control joints should be placed every 15 feet to 20 feet, while for sections 8 inches or greater the joint spacing is calculated by multiplying the pavement thickness by 2.5 feet. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99)

Traffic loading dictates that interface treatments be tailored to the expected vehicle stresses for each pavement configuration. In roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements that are placed adjacent to reinforced or plain cement‑concrete pavements, the control joints in the RCC shoulder shall be aligned with the spacing and alignment of the adjacent mainline concrete joints (section 4.11). When RCC is used as a base course beneath an asphalt wearing surface, diamond grinding is not required before placing the overlay, but the subgrade and subbase should be able to support the compaction of the RCC during construction and shall be separate construction items (section 4.11). For temporary median crossovers or roadways where the average daily traffic does not exceed 2,000 vehicles and the speed limit does not exceed 55 mph and RCC forms the final wearing course, control joint spacing follows Publication 72M or the rule that pavements less than 8 inches thick have joints spaced 15 feet to 20 feet and pavements 8 inches or greater use a spacing equal to thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet (section 4.11). Any exposed RCC surface on roads with a speed limit of 35 miles per hour or greater must be textured by diamond grinding (section 4.11). When asphalt is placed over a cement‑concrete or brick surface for the first time, the design shall include at least 2½ inches of binder material to provide an adequate load‑transfer layer and the wearing course shall meet the minimum thickness specified for the selected material; where a parking lane abuts a curb, the wearing course may be tapered to a 1‑inch depth at the curb while ensuring proper compaction and sealing of the pavement‑curb joint (section 10.1). Accurate traffic data such as ADT, projected growth, and truck percentage are required early in the design to determine these thicknesses (section 10.1). For bonded Portland cement concrete (PCC) overlays, the interface between the new overlay and the existing concrete pavement is designed to be a direct bond so that the two layers function as a single monolithic slab; this approach should only be used where the underlying concrete does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting, otherwise distress will transmit through the overlay unless repaired (section 10.6). The required overlay thickness is obtained from DARWin calculations, rounded up to the nearest half‑inch, and the final depth must fall within the minimum and maximum limits given in Table 10.10 (section 10.6). [1]

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Traffic loading design must incorporate drainage and moisture considerations by first evaluating existing drainage conditions as part of the layer‑thickness determination. Any identified drainage problems must be corrected before the reclaimed pavement layer is placed, with wet subgrade locations specifically identified and effective drainage installed prior to full‑depth reclamation. Other water‑related damage within the existing pavement layers should be evaluated to determine the source of water and remedied before reclamation because excess moisture degrades material integrity and reduces structural capacity. The overall structural requirement for the pavement is based on anticipated traffic and environmental conditions, ensuring that climate effects are accounted for in the design. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126)

In the roller‑compacted concrete pavement design described in section 4.11, traffic loading must respect an average daily traffic of less than or equal to 2,000 vehicles and a posted speed limit of 55 mph or less for roadways, with shoulders subject to the same criteria. If the RCC surface is exposed and the speed limit is 35 mph or greater, the pavement surface must be textured by diamond grinding before it is placed in service. For RCC pavements thinner than 8 inches, control joints should be spaced between 15 ft and 20 ft; for RCC pavements eight inches or thicker, joint spacing equals the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 ft, and shoulder joints should align with adjacent concrete mainline joints. In the ramp pavement design outlined in section 6.6, the pavement type—concrete or asphalt—shall be the same as that used on the higher‑traffic‑volume roadway within the interchange. The acceleration and deceleration portions of the ramps must have the same pavement type and thickness as the adjoining mainline roadway. Ramps should be designed using the same roadway classification as the higher‑traffic‑volume roadway, with design based on the ramp experiencing the highest traffic loadings and applying minimum depths appropriate to that higher‑volume road. If heavy‑truck traffic is anticipated, the ramp shall be designed accordingly. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 6 (p. 152)

Verification and expected performance

The Department verifies the completed traffic loading by conducting annual performance surveys and routine traffic‑classification counts; the surveys, performed according to Publication 336, compare observed distress levels against the limits set out in Table A, while the counts confirm that cumulative ESAL estimates stay within the values projected in the contract documents. If the fifth‑year survey shows that thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment must be applied; if deviations beyond Table A thresholds affect more than twenty percent of a segment (with medium cracks counted as six inches on each side), the entire segment is required to be removed and replaced, and any repair patches spaced less than one hundred feet apart are combined into a single continuous repair. Should traffic counts reveal that cumulative ESALs have exceeded one‑hundred percent of the estimated 20‑year design life ESALs, the contractor is relieved of responsibility for rutting or base‑condition repairs. This combination of distress surveys, threshold limits, and ESAL verification ensures compliance, constructability, performance, and consistency with project requirements. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351)

Under the Roller Compacted Concrete pavement guidelines, the expected service life for RCC pavements is typically estimated at 20 years, reflecting a life expectancy that depends on construction materials, environmental conditions, and traffic volumes. Its high compressive strength of 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi enables it to withstand traffic volume loadings, indicating resistance to distress under repeated loads. Ride quality is acknowledged to vary with the equipment used during placement, and when ride quality must be quantified the International Roughness Index (IRI) is used; therefore RCC should not be selected for projects where IRI will be measured for payment incentives. Under the 60‑month asphalt concrete pavement warranty, the expected performance is that distress levels remain within the limits set in Table A, with the year‑5 automated survey not permitted to show more than 30 % of a segment’s area containing two or more low‑severity distresses. In addition, the combined remedial‑action area for any deviation must not exceed 20 % of the segment surface. These outcomes are measured annually by automated surveys that identify and quantify distresses according to Publication 336, supplemented by manual surveys for flushing and potholes, rut depth checks using a 12‑ft straight edge across the lane width, and traffic classification counts to confirm ESAL estimates.
