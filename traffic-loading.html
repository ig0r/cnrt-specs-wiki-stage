Traffic Loading

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Traffic Loading is a topic under Pavement Design.
traffic-loadingtraffic-loadingsfuture-traffictraffic-loading-conditionsanticipated-traffic
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Traffic Loading is the analytical process by which vehicle‑load data—primarily average daily traffic, growth rates and truck percentages—are translated into equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) to size pavement structures so that they possess sufficient structural capacity for their intended service life. The methodology, implemented in AASHTOWare® DARWin® using either the rigorous or, where permitted, the simple ESAL calculation, drives decisions on layer thicknesses, material selection and overlay design for both new and rehabilitated concrete and asphalt pavements, including ramps and roller‑compacted concrete sections, and is re‑evaluated whenever field observations indicate a change in condition. Ongoing performance verification through annual traffic classification counts and distress surveys ensures that cumulative ESALs remain within the projected 20‑year design values, at which point contractual responsibility for rutting or base failures may shift away from the contractor [1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Traffic Loading” also include the following subtopics: Average Daily Traffic (ADT), Traffic Growth Rate, and Vehicle Classification.

Purpose and design objectives

Traffic Loading is incorporated into pavement design to provide the vehicle‑load data required to assure that a pavement structure has sufficient capacity to resist the stresses generated by traffic and thereby achieve structural adequacy for its intended service life [1]. When field observations indicate a change in pavement condition, updated traffic data—including a summary table of average daily traffic and truck percentage—must be obtained and a new design performed to verify that the redesigned section remains structurally adequate [1]. For ramp pavements within an interchange, the design is based on the ramp experiencing the highest traffic loadings, and if heavy‑truck traffic may develop, the ramp shall be designed accordingly; the acceleration and deceleration portions must have the same pavement type and thickness as the adjoining roadway to withstand stresses from acceleration, deceleration, and heavy vehicles [1]. The total 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) for a project’s performance period are calculated with the Rigorous ESAL Calculation method, and when daily ESALs exceed the limits of the Simple Method, the Rigorous Method must be used to determine structural thickness and material requirements that keep the pavement serviceable throughout its design life [1]. Overlay designs intended to improve ride quality or address suspected structural inadequacy rely on accurate traffic information—ADT, projected growth, and truck percentage—to compute daily 18‑kip ESALs and are required to meet a minimum eight‑year and a maximum twenty‑year structural design life [1]. In an asphalt concrete overlay of existing AC pavement, the Structural Number for Future Traffic and the Effective Existing Structural Number are calculated, and the calculated Structural Number must be greater than the Overlay (Design) Structural Number so that the overlay remains structurally adequate under future loads; analogous procedures using average initial truck factors determine thickness for overlays of jointed plain concrete pavement and AC/PCC composite pavement [1]. The overarching engineering problem addressed by Traffic Loading is the potential for pavements to be structurally inadequate or to lack sufficient capacity, which could lead to premature failure or excessive cost if not properly designed for anticipated vehicle loads [1]. Under the 60‑month warranty in the Pennsylvania specifications, traffic classification counts are used to verify that estimated cumulative ESALs remain within the projected values, and if cumulative ESALs exceed 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life ESALs, the contractor is relieved of responsibility for rutting or base‑condition repairs, reflecting the performance need to prevent load‑induced deterioration such as rutting and base failure [2].

For an overlay, the functional objective is to improve ride quality and to address any suspected structural inadequacy, while safety is addressed by requiring designs for low‑friction conditions; durability is ensured by designing each overlay for a minimum 8-year structural design life and a maximum of 20 years, which also satisfies serviceability expectations tied to ride performance. Scope and applicability

Traffic‑loading considerations are appropriate for roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements when the structure will serve as a concrete pavement, concrete shoulder, or concrete base course on roadways that have an average daily traffic (ADT) of less than or equal to 2,000 and a speed limit of 55 mph or less; under these conditions RCC may be used as the final wearing surface on shoulders adjacent to reinforced or plain cement‑concrete pavements, as a temporary pavement such as median crossovers or widening areas, or as either a final wearing course or a base course beneath an asphalt wearing course or surface treatment (section 4.11). The RCC mix must be a very dry concrete with a low water‑cement ratio—​as low as 0.30 and typically 0.40—a dense aggregate gradation with a maximum size less than 1 inch, and may include limited admixtures or air entrainment; when the exposed RCC surface will support speeds of 35 miles per hour or greater, diamond grinding is required to provide texture (section 4.11). Traffic‑loading analysis via equivalent single axle load (ESAL) calculation is required for all functional classifications of highways; the Simple Method may be used for new construction or rehabilitation on collectors and local roads provided that the resulting layer thicknesses do not exceed the minimum values for that road type, otherwise the Rigorous Method must be applied (section 7.1). On cross routes that interchange with limited‑access freeways a minimum design daily 18‑kip ESAL value from Table 7.3 may be used only when the projected ADT is 8,000 or less; if the ADT exceeds 8,000 the Rigorous Method must be employed (section 7.1). The Simple Method is expressly prohibited on any roadways other than collectors and local roads (section 7.1). Traffic‑loading considerations are also appropriate for ramp pavements on limited‑access highway interchanges, whether the pavement is concrete or asphalt; the ramp shall use the same pavement type and thickness as the higher‑traffic‑volume roadway it merges with, and its classification and minimum depth are taken from that higher‑volume road (section 6.6). Design for ramps shall be based on the ramp that experiences the highest traffic loadings, and if there is a potential for heavy truck traffic the design shall reflect those loads (section 6.6). The AC‑overlay of jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) design approach, which incorporates traffic loading through the use of rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors for ESAL calculations, may be applied when an asphalt concrete overlay is being placed over existing JPCP, jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP), or continuously reinforced concrete pavement (CRCP); it is also appropriate when the existing surface is a concrete slab that currently has an asphalt overlay that will be stripped before the new AC overlay is installed (section 10.4). Traffic‑loading considerations are applied to asphalt concrete pavement projects that fall under Section 496 60‑month warranty; the Department will routinely schedule and perform traffic classification counts to confirm that Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) estimates remain within the projected values in the contract documents (section 496). When estimated cumulative ESALs have exceeded 100% of the estimated 20‑year design life ESASL​s during the warranty period, the contractor is not held responsible for rutting or base‑condition distresses for the remainder of the warranty period (section 496). RCC pavement is limited to roadways with an average daily traffic of less than or equal to 2,000 and a speed limit of 55 mph or less; when either the ADT exceeds this threshold or the posted speed is higher, another pavement design should be considered, and RCC should not be chosen for locations where the International Roughness Index will be measured because its typically rougher surface can affect the IRI result, making smoother‑ride designs preferable. When a project’s pavement length exceeds the thresholds that trigger a DARWin analysis—more than 500 continuous feet on divided roadways or 1,000 continuous feet on undivided roadways—a traffic‑loading based design must be performed; for shorter sections that do not require such analysis the design is limited to matching the existing structure and meeting minimum thicknesses, but if the existing composite pavement well exceeds the minimum pavement thickness or contains elements not reasonable to replicate such as crack‑and‑seat, a full DARWin analysis (i.e., an alternative design approach) is recommended, and PennDOT Oversight projects are advised to consider one rigid and one flexible design alternative rather than relying solely on traffic‑loading assumptions. For collectors and local roads the Simple Method may be used instead of the rigorous traffic‑loading calculation provided that the pavement layer thicknesses it produces do not exceed the minimum thicknesses for that roadway type, and similarly on rehabilitation projects the Simple Method is acceptable when the required structural capacity does not exceed what the existing pavement can provide; if either of those limits is exceeded, or if the facility is anything other than a collector/local road, the Rigorous ESAL method must be employed, and for cross‑routes that intersect limited‑access freeways the Rigorous Method becomes mandatory whenever the design‑year ADT is greater than 8,000. When an overlay project lacks the minimum traffic inputs—current ADT, projected growth, truck percentage, and a truck distribution—the standard traffic‑loading based design cannot be used; the required traffic information shall be requested from BPR or conduct its own counts before proceeding, and any bonded concrete overlay placed over asphalt pavement or over an AC/PCC composite slab requires a different design procedure (the BCOA‑ME method) because the 1993 AASHTO procedures do not consider the bonding effect. When an existing asphalt overlay on a composite pavement is suspected of having material problems or deficiencies, the AC‑Overlay of AC/PCC design method should not be used; cores must be taken and analyzed, and if deficiencies are confirmed the old asphalt must be fully removed and the project re‑designed using the AC Overlay of PCC Pavement approach because deficient material invalidates the assumptions underlying the traffic‑loading based AC/PCC overlay method. Bonded PCC overlays are only suitable when the existing concrete pavement does not exhibit a great extent of cracking and/or faulting; if there is a significant amount of distress in the existing concrete pavement, it will be reflected up through the bonded overlay unless it is repaired, making an alternative design approach—such as an unbonded overlay—preferable. When the Department’s traffic counts during the warranty period show that cumulative ESALs have exceeded 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life ESALs—or increase enough to warrant a change in the mix design—the original traffic‑loading based pavement design is considered insufficient, making an alternative design approach preferable because the contractor will no longer be responsible for rutting or base‑condition repairs and responsibility shifts away from the contractor. Design inputs and requirements

Traffic loading design must be based on a reliable estimate of future traffic levels and a recent pavement condition survey that records current distress, and it requires site information such as a location map showing the project’s beginning and ending segments and any intersecting roadways. The required traffic data are to be presented as a summary table of average daily traffic (ADT) and truck percentage together with all breakdowns, source and date of the traffic data. Material and geotechnical inputs include representative material sampling performed according to the prescribed number, size, depth and handling criteria, a CBR summary table, Falling Weight Deflectometer data, soils testing results, in‑situ testing such as dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer to evaluate subgrade stiffness, and a frost‑heave probability. Environmental details are reflected in the cover letter, for example the environmental approval date. Existing‑condition information must comprise an assessment of existing drainage conditions so that any water problems can be identified and corrected before construction, as well as the pavement history for the project limits obtained from RMS. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 6 (pp. 134–136)

For roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements, PennDOT criteria limit the allowable average daily traffic to less than or equal to 2,000 vehicles and require a speed limit of 55 mph or less when RCC is used as a final wearing or base course, as specified in Publication 408 specifications sections 502, 658, and 323. The pavement thickness and compressive‑strength requirements must follow the current PennDOT concrete pavement design procedures in Chapter 8, Section 8.15 of this manual, which may range from 4 inches to 18 inches and allow compressive strengths from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi, and control joint spacing shall be as designated in Publication 72M, Roadway Construction Standards, RC‑27M, with RCC pavements less than 8 inches thick spaced 15 feet to 20 feet and for thicknesses of 8 inches or greater using the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet[1]. For full‑depth reclamation projects a reliable estimate of future traffic loading shall be obtained before construction, and the overall pavement design must be consistent with standard pavement design traffic analysis procedures; traffic loading inputs for PennDOT designs are governed by the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures as amended by the Department, and all analyses must be performed using the required AASHTOWare® DARWin® 3.01 software together with a summary of average daily traffic and truck percentage, including source and date information[1]. An analysis is required whenever a new, reconstructed or rehabilitated pavement segment exceeds 500 continuous feet on divided roadways, 1,000 continuous feet on undivided roadways, or when bridge approaches total more than 1,000 feet, and any design‑year shift of more than three years also triggers a new design; project schedules further constrain traffic‑related work by imposing specific let‑out and approval timelines for preservation, moderate‑complexity, and most‑complex projects[1]. Ramp pavement designs on limited‑access highways shall conform to the same pavement type as the higher‑traffic‑volume roadway in the interchange, and the acceleration and deceleration sections must match that roadway’s pavement type and thickness; all ramps are classified using the higher‑traffic‑volume roadway classification, and the design of every ramp structure is based on the ramp experiencing the highest traffic loadings with minimum depths appropriate to that higher‑traffic‑volume roadway applied throughout, and if a development scenario could generate heavy truck traffic the ramp shall be designed accordingly[1]. Traffic data for pavement design will be supplied by the Bureau of Planning and Research (BPR); the rigorous ESAL calculation method of DARWin must be used unless the Simple Method may be applied only on collectors and local roads and only when the resulting pavement layer thicknesses do not exceed the minimum layer thicknesses for the appropriate type of roadway, and for cross routes that interchange with limited‑access freeways, if the projected design‑year ADT is greater than 8,000 the Rigorous Method must be employed[1]. All overlay designs will be designed for a minimum eight‑year structural design life and a maximum of twenty years and must be based on the best available traffic data; the minimum information needed is current average daily traffic, projected traffic growth, and the truck percentage, with a full truck distribution required unless the roadway is a collector or local road, in which case the simple method may be used to calculate the 18‑kip ESALs for the project[1]. If the required traffic information is not available in the District, it shall be requested from BPR; if BPR cannot supply it, the district may perform counts following Appendix B with all collected data submitted back to BPR for refinement, and each design also requires completion of Form D‑4332 for PDAU concurrence and FHWA approval where applicable[1]. For an asphalt‑concrete (AC) overlay on existing AC pavement, traffic loading is governed by Chapter 10 Section 10.2 which requires using Table 10.7 to select appropriate input values for the future‑traffic structural number and applying the flexible pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 for ESAL calculations; the effective existing structural number must be obtained with either the Component Method or the Non‑Destructive Testing Method, and subbase material is excluded from the structural evaluation on Interstates and on non‑Interstate roads when the design 18‑kip ESAS exceed 5,000,000 unless a specific investigation confirms its condition, while the calculated overlay SN must be greater than the Overlay (Design) SN for adequacy[1]. The AC overlay of fractured PCC slab design requires calculating the structural number for future traffic and the existing effective SN, applying the Component Method and subbase guidance from Section 10.2, then using the resulting overlay SN to size the thickness according to Table 10.7, with ESAL values derived using the flexible pavement Average Initial Truck Factors listed in Table 7.1[1]. In the design of an asphalt‑concrete overlay on jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP), traffic loading is governed by the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors listed in Table 7.1 for ESAL calculations and the designer must reference Table 10.8 for the appropriate input values while calculating pavement thickness for future traffic[1]. For an asphalt‑concrete overlay on an AC/PCC composite pavement, the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic shall be obtained from Table 10.8, the Effective Existing Thickness shall be calculated with the Condition Survey Method as directed in Table 10.9, and ESAL calculations shall use the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors listed in Table 7.1[1]. In a bonded PCC overlay on a PCC pavement, traffic loading must also use the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 for ESAL calculations; the resulting concrete thickness shall be rounded up to the nearest half‑inch while staying within the minimum and maximum depths specified in Table 10.10, and bonded overlays should only be utilized where the existing concrete pavement does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting[1]. Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) data will be considered to determine priorities and may be used to set district priorities, although it does not need to be addressed in District Interstate plans[1]. Under Section 496 of the asphalt‑concrete pavement warranty, performance criteria are tied to distress limits in Table A and to verification of ESAL estimates; the Department must conduct annual traffic classification counts to confirm that ESAL estimates remain within the projected values in the contract documents, and if cumulative ESALs exceed 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life ESALs the Contractor is not responsible for rutting or base‑condition repairs[2]. Remedial action is triggered when a year‑5 performance survey shows that 30 % of a segment’s area has two or more low‑severity distresses, and any repair areas spaced less than 100 feet must be treated as one continuous repair; all repairs also must meet the surface tolerance specified in Section 413.3(l)[2].

Design methods and criteria

Traffic loading analysis for PennDOT pavement designs is performed with AASHTOWare® DARWin® 3.01 software, which implements the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures as amended by the department [1]. The required traffic inputs include current average daily traffic (ADT), projected growth rates, and truck‑percentage tables, and these data are entered into DARWin to compute equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) for the design period [1]. Two alternative ESAL calculation approaches are provided: the Rigorous ESAL Calculation method, which determines total 18‑kip ESALs by applying design ADT, lane and directional distribution factors, vehicle classification percentages, annual growth rates, and the average initial truck factor from Table 7.1 using a compound growth rate calculation type mandated in DARWin [1]. The Simple Method may be used on rehabilitation projects when the resulting layer thicknesses do not exceed the minimum layer thicknesses for the appropriate roadway type as listed in Table 9.4, otherwise the Rigorous method must be applied [1]. For collectors and local roads the Simple Method is permissible, but for cross‑routes serving limited‑access freeways a minimum daily 18‑kip ESAL value from Table 7.3 is required unless the projected ADT exceeds 8,000, in which case the Rigorous method is mandatory [1]. Overlay designs begin with collection of the minimum required data—current ADT, projected growth, and truck percentage—and, except for collectors or local streets, a truck distribution that permits the Simple Method to compute design daily 18‑kip ESAS as prescribed in Chapter 7; these ESAL values are then entered into DARWin which applies the AASHTO procedures for each of the seven overlay types [1]. When the overlay is a bonded concrete over asphalt or composite pavement, the BCOA‑ME mechanistic‑empirical procedure is used, with designs performed for a minimum eight‑year structural design life and may extend to a maximum of twenty years [1]. For an AC overlay on existing AC pavement the future‑traffic Structural Number (SN) is calculated using Table 10.7 together with the flexible‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1, while the effective existing SN is obtained in DARWin by the Component Method or the Non‑Destructive Testing Method applying the structural number equation of Section 9.8 with all drainage coefficients equal to 1.0; the overlay SN equals the difference between future‑traffic SN and effective existing SN and a thickness design is performed in DARWin ensuring that the calculated SN exceeds the required SN, with subbase layers excluded from the structural evaluation when design 18‑kip ESALs exceed 5,000,000 unless subbase quality has been verified [1]. The same sequence of future‑traffic SN calculation, effective existing SN determination, and overlay SN computation is followed for an AC overlay of a fractured PCC slab, with subbase inclusion governed by the guidance in Section 10.2 [1]. For an AC overlay of jointed plain concrete pavement, rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 are used to compute ESALs and a secondary screen in DARWin that mirrors the rigid‑pavement design process determines the required overlay thickness using input values referenced in Table 10.8 [1]. In designing an AC/PCC composite pavement overlay, the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic is obtained from Table 10.8, the Effective Existing Thickness is calculated with the Condition Survey Method shown in Table 10.9, and DARWin returns the actual asphalt overlay thickness required rather than a structural number [1]. For a bonded Portland cement concrete overlay of a PCC pavement, average initial truck factors are again used to compute ESALs, the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic is taken from Table 10.8, the Effective Existing Thickness is evaluated with the Condition Survey method in Table 10.9, and DARWin calculates the required concrete overlay thickness rounded up to the nearest half‑inch [1]. Roller‑compacted concrete pavements are designed using the current PennDOT concrete pavement design procedures set out in Chapter 8, Section 8.15, permitting compressive strengths from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi and applying an empirical control‑joint rule that requires joints every 15 ft to 20 ft for pavements less than 8 in thick and a spacing equal to the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 ft for pavements 8 in or greater [1]. Ramp pavements are required to use the same concrete or asphalt type and thickness as the higher‑traffic roadway they merge with, the ramp is classified by that roadway’s classification, and the design of all ramp structures within an interchange is based on the ramp with the highest traffic loadings; if heavy‑truck traffic is anticipated the ramp shall be designed accordingly [1]. Under the asphalt concrete pavement warranty, periodic traffic classification counts are performed to verify that ESAL estimates remain within the projected values set out in the contract documents, and when cumulative ESALs exceed 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life ESALs the contractor is relieved of responsibility for rutting or base condition repairs [2]. Annual performance and distress surveys are conducted using automated and manual methods prescribed by Publication 336, which define rating procedures, extent estimation techniques, and measurement protocols applied to evaluate pavement response to traffic loads [2].

The first step in establishing traffic loading requirements is to collect the minimum information of current average daily traffic, projected growth and truck percentage [1]. Design daily 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) shall be determined in accordance with Chapter 7 [1]. For general pavement analysis the loading model shall be based on the rigorous 18‑kip ESAL calculation unless a threshold permits otherwise [1]. The default directional distribution factor is 50 % (or 100 % for one‑way counts) and lane distribution factors are set to 100 % for two‑lane highways, 90 % for four‑lane highways and 80 % for six or more lanes [1]. When the projected design‑year ADT of a cross route that interchanges with a limited‑access freeway exceeds 8,000 vehicles, the rigorous method must be used; the simple method may be applied only on collectors and local roads and only when the pavement layer thicknesses obtained do not exceed the minimum values prescribed for the roadway type [1]. Overlay designs shall have a structural design life of at least eight years but not more than twenty years [1]. In flexible‑pavement overlays ESAL calculations shall use the average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 and the overlay is considered adequate only when the calculated Structural Number exceeds the Overlay (Design) Structural Number [1]. For Interstates the existing subbase shall be excluded from the structural evaluation because it may be in poor condition; on non‑Interstate roads the subbase shall also be omitted when the design 18‑kip ESALs exceed 5,000,000 unless a specific investigation verifies its quality and drainability [1]. For AC overlays of jointed plain concrete pavement and for AC overlays of AC/PCC composite pavement the traffic‑loading representation likewise uses the rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1; the design process begins with determining the pavement thickness for future traffic using the secondary screen in DARWin that follows the rigid‑pavement design procedure and references Table 10.8 for appropriate input values [1]. Ramp pavements on limited‑access highways shall be designed whenever a ramp is proposed or existing; the pavement type—concrete or asphalt—shall match the higher‑traffic‑volume roadway within the interchange, and both the acceleration and deceleration portions shall have the same pavement type and thickness as the roadway they merge with [1]. The design of all ramp structures in an interchange is based on the ramp that experiences the highest traffic loadings; minimum depths appropriate to the higher‑traffic‑volume roadway shall be applied, and if heavy truck traffic is anticipated the ramp shall be designed accordingly [1]. Roller Compacted Concrete pavements shall be used only where the average daily traffic is less than or equal to 2,000 vehicles and the speed limit is 55 mph or less; when the surface is exposed and the speed limit is 35 mph or greater the pavement must be textured by diamond grinding [1]. Design thickness for RCC may range from 4 inches to 18 inches with typical compressive strengths between 4,000 psi and 10,000 psi; control joint spacing is governed by thickness: sections less than 8 inches thick require joints spaced 15 feet to 20 feet, whereas sections 8 inches or greater use a spacing equal to the thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet [1]. Under the 60‑month asphalt concrete pavement warranty traffic loading is assumed to follow the cumulative ESAL values projected in the contract and the Department must verify these values each year through traffic classification counts [2]. If those counts show that the estimated cumulative ESALs have exceeded 100 % of the 20‑year design life estimate, the contractor is relieved of responsibility for repairing rutting or base‑condition distresses for the remainder of the warranty [2]. Performance criteria also require that by the fifth‑year survey no more than 30 % of a segment’s area may contain two or more low‑severity distresses; otherwise remedial treatment such as microsurfacing must be performed [2].

Materials and mixture design

For roller‑compacted concrete pavements that will be subjected to traffic loading, the specification must list cement, aggregate and water as constituent materials, require a water‑cement ratio as low as 0.30 but typically 0.40, call for a dense aggregate gradation with a maximum aggregate size less than 1 inch, allow air entrainment to be either included or omitted, permit only limited use of admixtures, and target a compressive strength that can range from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi. [1] In ramp pavement design, traffic loading must specify the pavement type—concrete or asphalt—and require that it be the same type as the higher‑traffic roadway in the interchange; the acceleration and deceleration portions of the ramps must have the same pavement type and thickness as the adjoining mainline roadway, and minimum depths appropriate to the higher‑traffic volume roadway shall apply. [1] Under the asphalt‑concrete pavement warranty, any material placed for remediation or replacement shall be plant‑mixed asphalt concrete unless another material is accepted in writing by the District Executive, and that asphalt concrete must satisfy the contractor’s mix‑design tolerances, which include a density greater than 92 % of the theoretical maximum density and percent‑asphalt and gradation values that fall within the limits indicated in the approved mix design. [2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 6 (p. 152); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

When roller‑compacted concrete pavement is selected for traffic loading, the mixture is proportioned with a water‑cement ratio as low as 0.30 (typically 0.40) and a dense aggregate gradation whose maximum size is less than 1 inch; air entrainment may be used and admixtures are limited, producing compressive strengths that can range from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi and making the pavement suitable for roads with an ADT of less than or equal to 2,000 and a speed limit of 55 mph or less. The required pavement thickness is then chosen between 4 inches and 18 inches using the PennDOT concrete design procedures, and the underlying subgrade and subbase must be prepared to support compaction, thereby meeting structural, durability, constructability and performance goals. For an overlay, traffic loading requires using current ADT, projected growth and truck percentage to compute daily 18‑kip ESALs, after which Tables 10.1‑10.3 are applied to select the appropriate asphalt material—leveling, wearing or base—for that traffic level; Table 10.4 then dictates the minimum and maximum layer thicknesses, with a minimum of 2 ½ inches of binder when first overlaying concrete or brick and the wearing course required to meet its listed minimum thickness, while the appropriate Superpave mixture size from Table 10.5 completes the mix design. All of these steps together satisfy structural requirements of a minimum 8‑year and maximum 20‑year design life, as well as durability, constructability and performance requirements, and any special cases such as curb taper in parking lanes must be reviewed with the District Pavement Management Engineer. Structural details and interfaces

According to the Roller Compacted Concrete (RCC) guidelines in Chapter 4, RCC pavement is designed without forms, steel reinforcing, or dowel bars, so reinforcement and load‑transfer devices are not required. Control joints may be omitted, but the Department requires them in shoulders so that they line up with the adjacent mainline concrete pavement; when joints are used their spacing must follow the prescribed rules. For RCC sections thinner than 8 inches the control joints should be placed every 15 feet to 20 feet, while for sections 8 inches or greater the joint spacing is calculated by multiplying the pavement thickness by 2.5 feet. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99)

Traffic loading dictates that interface treatments be tailored to the expected vehicle stresses for each pavement configuration. In roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements that are placed adjacent to reinforced or plain cement‑concrete pavements, the control joints in the RCC shoulder shall be aligned with the spacing and alignment of the adjacent mainline concrete joints (section 4.11). When RCC is used as a base course beneath an asphalt wearing surface, diamond grinding is not required before placing the overlay, but the subgrade and subbase should be able to support the compaction of the RCC during construction and shall be separate construction items (section 4.11). For temporary median crossovers or roadways where the average daily traffic does not exceed 2,000 vehicles and the speed limit does not exceed 55 mph and RCC forms the final wearing course, control joint spacing follows Publication 72M or the rule that pavements less than 8 inches thick have joints spaced 15 feet to 20 feet and pavements 8 inches or greater use a spacing equal to thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet (section 4.11). Any exposed RCC surface on roads with a speed limit of 35 miles per hour or greater must be textured by diamond grinding (section 4.11). When asphalt is placed over a cement‑concrete or brick surface for the first time, the design shall include at least 2½ inches of binder material to provide an adequate load‑transfer layer and the wearing course shall meet the minimum thickness specified for the selected material; where a parking lane abuts a curb, the wearing course may be tapered to a 1‑inch depth at the curb while ensuring proper compaction and sealing of the pavement‑curb joint (section 10.1). Accurate traffic data such as ADT, projected growth, and truck percentage are required early in the design to determine these thicknesses (section 10.1). For bonded Portland cement concrete (PCC) overlays, the interface between the new overlay and the existing concrete pavement is designed to be a direct bond so that the two layers function as a single monolithic slab; this approach should only be used where the underlying concrete does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting, otherwise distress will transmit through the overlay unless repaired (section 10.6). The required overlay thickness is obtained from DARWin calculations, rounded up to the nearest half‑inch, and the final depth must fall within the minimum and maximum limits given in Table 10.10 (section 10.6). Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Traffic loading design must incorporate drainage and moisture considerations by first evaluating existing drainage conditions as part of the layer‑thickness determination. Any identified drainage problems must be corrected before the reclaimed pavement layer is placed, with wet subgrade locations specifically identified and effective drainage installed prior to full‑depth reclamation. Other water‑related damage within the existing pavement layers should be evaluated to determine the source of water and remedied before reclamation because excess moisture degrades material integrity and reduces structural capacity. The overall structural requirement for the pavement is based on anticipated traffic and environmental conditions, ensuring that climate effects are accounted for in the design. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126)

For roller‑compacted concrete pavements the traffic loading must be limited to an average daily traffic of less than or equal to 2,000 vehicles and a posted speed limit of 55 mph or less, with shoulders subject to the same criteria. If the RCC pavement will have an exposed surface and the speed limit is 35 mph or greater, the pavement surface must be textured by diamond grinding before it is placed in service. When RCC pavements are less than 8 inches thick, control joints should be spaced between 15 ft and 20 ft; for RCC pavements eight inches or thicker, joint spacing equals the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 ft, and shoulder joints shall align with adjacent concrete mainline joints. In ramp design the pavement type—concrete or asphalt—shall be the same as that used on the higher‑traffic‑volume roadway within the interchange, and the acceleration and deceleration portions of the ramps must have the same pavement type and thickness as the adjoining mainline roadway. Ramps should be designed using the same roadway classification as the higher‑traffic‑volume roadway, with design based on the ramp experiencing the highest traffic loadings and applying minimum depths appropriate to that higher‑volume road; if heavy‑truck traffic is anticipated, the ramp shall be designed accordingly. Traffic loading must also be verified by routine traffic classification counts that confirm the Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) estimates stay within the projected values set out in the contract documents. Any remedial work must meet the surface tolerance specified in Section 413.3(l). Rut depth shall be checked with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width to assess severity, and all remediation operations must provide and maintain traffic control that follows an approved traffic control plan complying with Publications 212 and 213, the MUTCD, and the contract special provisions. Verification and expected performance

The completed Traffic Loading is verified by the Department’s annual performance analysis that uses automated surveys (and manual surveys per Publication 336 for flushing and potholes) to measure distresses and compares them with the limits in Table A; if a year‑5 survey shows 30% of a segment area having two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action must be taken. Constructability is confirmed by ensuring all repairs meet the surface tolerance of Section 413.3(l) and that any repair patches spaced less than 100 feet are combined into one continuous repair. Performance compliance also requires traffic classification counts to confirm that ESAL estimates remain within the projected values set out in the contract documents. Consistency with project requirements is maintained by following the warranty specifications for materials, traffic control plans, and notification procedures before surveys or remediation work. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Under the Roller Compacted Concrete pavement guidelines, the expected service life for RCC pavements is typically estimated at 20 years, reflecting a life expectancy that depends on construction materials, environmental conditions, and traffic volumes. The high compressive strength of RCC (4,000 psi to 10,000 psi) enables it to withstand traffic volume loadings, indicating resistance to distress under repeated loads. Ride quality for RCC is acknowledged to vary with the equipment used during placement, and when ride quality must be quantified the International Roughness Index (IRI) is used; therefore RCC should not be selected for projects where IRI will be measured for payment incentives. For asphalt concrete pavements covered by the 60‑month warranty, performance under traffic loading is judged by whether distresses remain within the limits set for each segment, with annual automated surveys (per Publication 336) recording and rating all identified distresses and manual surveys capturing flushing and potholes. If the year‑5 survey determines that 30% of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial treatment such as microsurfacing is required. Rut depth is checked when necessary using a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width to assess severity and extent. Annual traffic classification counts are performed to verify that the accumulated Equivalent Single Axle Loads (ESALs) stay within the projected values specified in the contract. [1][2]
