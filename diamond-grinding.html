Diamond Grinding

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Diamond Grinding is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
diamond-grinding
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-26

Diamond grinding is a preservation treatment applied to structurally sound Portland‑cement concrete pavements that exhibit only minor surface defects such as high spots, warped or curled slabs, and wheel ruts shallower than about 0.5 in.[1][2] The process removes a thin sacrificial layer—typically at least one‑quarter inch—using self‑propelled machines equipped with stacked diamond blades to cut longitudinal grooves that re‑profile the slab, eliminate the high points and minor rutting, and restore proper cross‑slope and transverse drainage.[1][2] By creating a uniform texture that meets skid‑resistance and smoothness criteria (e.g., IRI ≤ 70 in/mi for speeds > 45 mph), the treatment improves ride quality, reduces hydroplaning risk, and extends service life while avoiding structural rehabilitation.[1][2]

Purpose and treatment objectives

Diamond grinding is applied to Portland‑cement concrete pavements that are structurally sound but exhibit surface profile deficiencies such as high spots, warped or curled slabs, and minor wheel ruts not deeper than 0.5 in. The treatment also targets excessive roughness manifested as ridges larger than 1/8 in, irregularities exceeding 3/16 in measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge, and vertical differentials across joints greater than ¼ in (up to ½ in). Cross‑slope deviations beyond a quarter‑inch in twelve feet and longitudinal texture falling below the 98 % NGCS requirement are additional performance problems that diamond grinding is intended to correct. When the International Roughness Index exceeds 70 in/mi for speeds over 45 mph (or 90 in/mi for lower speeds), the pavement fails smoothness criteria and diamond grinding removes a thin sacrificial layer to bring IRI within limits. The process also restores transverse drainage compromised by rutting or uneven surface, thereby reducing hydroplaning risk and improving skid resistance. For exposed roller‑compacted concrete decks with posted speed limits of 35 mph or higher, the lack of sufficient macro‑texture is a distress that must be remedied by diamond grinding to meet texture requirements. Pavements constructed with Vanport Limestone aggregate are excluded because removal of the mortar layer exposes limestone that can polish rapidly and lose skid resistance. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 233–234), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–435), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (pp. 1107–1110)

Diamond grinding is limited to concrete pavements that are structurally sound and exhibit only minor distress such as wheel ruts under about 0.5 inches, high spots, warped slabs, or localized bumps exceeding the tolerances set in specifications[1][2]. The treatment removes a thin surface layer with diamond‑saw blades to re‑profile the slab, eliminate high points and minor rutting, restore transverse drainage, and provide a uniform texture that meets skid‑resistance requirements[1][2]. Across the guides the chief purpose is to return the pavement to its intended functional condition—smoothness, appropriate cross‑slope, traction, and safety—so that ride quality is restored[1][2]. This focus on functional restoration distinguishes diamond grinding from repairs aimed at structural damage; it is classified as a preservation treatment rather than a structural rehabilitation[1][2]. Because the restored surface reduces distress progression, the guides note that the procedure also helps to slow further deterioration and can extend the service life of the pavement, although those outcomes are considered secondary to restoring functionality[1][2].

Distress characterization and causes

Diamond grinding is applied to Portland‑Cement Concrete (rigid) pavements where the exposed surface exhibits high points that exceed the allowable vertical deviation of one‑quarter inch and may also show curled or warped slabs as well as minor rutting; the distress can be present anywhere on the pavement deck but wheel ruts must be less than 0.5 inches deep for the treatment to be considered appropriate [1][2]. The extent of the defect is defined by high spots greater than 0.25 inch and, when present, minor rutting that does not exceed the 0.5‑inch threshold, while warped or curled slabs are also addressed as part of the profile correction [1][2]. Both guides characterize the condition as relatively minor in severity, noting that it primarily affects ride quality and vehicle control rather than indicating structural failure, although any deviation beyond the tolerance is deemed defective and must be corrected before traffic reopening [1][2]. Neither source provides a quantified progression rate for the distress, indicating that the speed at which the surface irregularities deteriorate is not specified in the specifications [1][2]. By removing the high points and restoring a smoother, uniformly textured surface, diamond grinding temporarily raises skid‑friction resistance, reduces the potential for hydroplaning, and improves safety for motorists; for roadways with speed limits of 35 mph or higher, the restored texture is required to maintain safe operation at those speeds [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 370–374)

Diamond grinding is applied when a concrete pavement’s surface condition has deteriorated because several interrelated mechanisms have compromised its texture and structural integrity. The primary trigger is the loss of the thin mortar film that normally protects the aggregate; once this layer is stripped, the underlying aggregate—particularly Vanport limestone—is exposed and can polish rapidly, causing a swift decline in skid resistance and ride quality. Heavy traffic loads exacerbate the problem by inducing surface faulting, wheel ruts, and general wear that diminish smoothness. Inadequate cross‑slope prevents effective transverse drainage, allowing water to linger on the deck and further reducing friction. Water infiltration, often linked to poor drainage or subgrade weaknesses, together with climate stresses such as freeze‑thaw cycles, accelerates material aging and can lead to surface cracking. Insufficient or low‑quality subbase material provides inadequate support beneath the slab, contributing to premature mid‑slab cracking and indicating a lack of structural capacity. Soft, wet, or otherwise unsuitable subbase conditions also produce slab settlement and edge‑differential height that exceed allowable tolerances, creating vertical differentials greater than the permissible limits and necessitating removal of a thin surface layer to bring the slab back within elevation requirements. Chemical and aggregate degradation—including alkali‑silica reaction and the inherent polishing susceptibility of Vanport limestone—further erodes durability and can be aggravated after grinding. These load‑related, climate/materials‑related, drainage‑related, and support‑deficiency mechanisms collectively drive the need for diamond grinding to restore a smooth profile, reestablish positive crown and drainage, eliminate wheel ruts, and improve friction. [1][2]

Applicability and treatment selection

Diamond grinding is indicated for concrete pavements that remain structurally sound but display only minor surface defects such as high spots, curled or warped slabs, and wheel ruts shallower than half an inch [1]. The technique is also appropriate for precast concrete slabs that incorporate a built‑in sacrificial thickness of one‑quarter inch when the finished vertical edge differential across any joint exceeds one‑quarter inch but does not exceed one‑half inch and the slab cannot be removed for re‑grading, with grinding continued until the edge differential is reduced to less than the allowable quarter‑inch tolerance [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements that will serve as an exposed wearing surface, diamond grinding is required when the posted speed limit on the roadway is thirty‑five miles per hour or greater, and it may be applied on low‑volume roads where the average daily traffic does not exceed roughly two thousand vehicles and the speed limit does not exceed about fifty‑five miles per hour [1][2]. The guides advise that diamond grinding should not be used on concrete pavements containing aggregates such as Vanport Limestone or other materials known to polish rapidly, for which alternative surface treatments like an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or a high‑friction surface treatment are recommended instead [1]. No additional environmental exposures or site constraints beyond the structural soundness of the pavement and the aggregate considerations are stipulated for the use of diamond grinding [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 370–435)

Diamond grinding is appropriate only for concrete pavements that are structurally sound, display shallow wheel ruts of less than half an inch, and have joint vertical edge differentials that do not exceed the built‑in sacrificial thickness of one‑quarter inch [1][2]. When the pavement exhibits deeper rutting, significant structural distress, extensive cracking, joint spalling, or any other severe deficiency, preservation by grinding should be abandoned in favor of more robust interventions such as transverse or longitudinal grooving, concrete overlay, micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, high‑friction surface treatments, or other rehabilitation methods [1][2]. Pavements constructed with Vanport Limestone aggregate are specifically contraindicated for grinding because the process removes the mortar layer and rapidly exposes limestone that loses skid resistance; in those cases an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or a high‑friction surface treatment is preferable [1]. If a concrete slab is slated for an overlay, the existing surface should be left unground, any spalled areas must be cleaned and filled with asphalt material and a leveling course applied before the new layer is placed [1]. Roller‑compacted concrete used solely as a base beneath an asphalt wearing course does not require grinding, whereas RCC that will remain exposed on roads with speed limits of 35 mph or greater must be textured by diamond grinding to provide adequate skid resistance [1]. When the amount of patching required exceeds roughly ten percent of the pavement area, or when widespread cracking and joint failures indicate deterioration beyond the early preservation stage, rehabilitation or reconstruction treatments such as base repair, concrete patching, or full‑depth replacement should be selected instead of grinding [1]. Diamond grinding is also unsuitable when defects would require removal beyond the thin sacrificial layer, such as minor depressions that can only be corrected by extra‑depth grinding; in those situations a different repair method must be employed [2]. All concrete repairs—including patching, spall repair, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization, and slab jacking—must be completed before any grinding can commence, so the presence of unfinished repairs dictates an alternative rehabilitation technique [2]. Equipment that tends to cause spalling at joints or cracks or that fractures the surface aggregate also makes grinding inappropriate [2]. Joint vertical edge differentials greater than one‑quarter inch cannot be corrected by grinding beyond the sacrificial thickness; if the differential lies between one‑quarter and one‑half inch only minimal grinding is allowed, while any differential exceeding one‑half inch requires removal of the slab and regrading of the subbase or bedding before another repair can be applied [2]. Finally, diamond grinding is limited to ride‑quality surfaces that satisfy prescribed smoothness limits; if the measured roughness index exceeds the maximum allowable value, if any individual bump measured with a 12‑foot straightedge is larger than one‑quarter inch, or if an adjacent ridge or valley exceeds one‑eighth inch, the surface is classified as defective and must be removed and replaced rather than ground [2].

Condition assessment and timing

Determining the need for diamond grinding begins with confirming that the concrete slab retains sufficient structural capacity, which is typically verified through falling‑weight deflectometer testing and examination of core extracts for adequate thickness, integrity, and subbase/subgrade condition. A comprehensive visual survey follows to locate surface profile defects such as high spots, warped or curled slabs, minor rutting, failed joints, rough ride quality, cracking, spalling, and other distress, with wheel‑rut depth measured and required to be less than 0.5 in before grinding is considered. Non‑destructive investigations—including deflection or faulting measurements, ground‑penetrating radar scans, and specialized scans for dowel‑bar alignment and slab thickness—are employed to pinpoint problem areas and assess underlying support conditions. Skid‑resistance testing, often performed with a BOMO skid‑friction device, identifies sections where traction has deteriorated and thus may benefit from grinding to restore macrotexture. Automated pavement condition surveys or videologging provide systematic data on cracking, rutting, joint conditions, and overall ride quality that feed into preservation treatment matrices used to decide whether grinding is appropriate. Cross‑slope uniformity is checked at approximately 100‑foot intervals along the lane, and any deviation exceeding one‑quarter inch over a twelve‑foot length is recorded as a candidate for grinding. Straight‑edge or crown‑surface template testing across each slab locates high spots, and an individual bump showing an irregularity greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch (or one‑quarter inch where specified) triggers removal by diamond grinding. For precast or RCC slabs the vertical differential at joints is measured with a depth gauge; differentials between one‑quarter and one‑half inch are corrected by milling away excess material, relying on a sacrificial quarter‑inch overlay to achieve tolerance. Ride quality is quantified using an International Roughness Index test, with acceptance limits of 70 in/mi for posted speeds above 45 mph and 90 in/mi for speeds at or below 45 mph; values exceeding these thresholds indicate the need for grinding. The Mean Roughness Index (MRI) is compared to maximum values in the applicable tables, and when the MRI exceeds those limits diamond grinding is prescribed to bring the surface within acceptable roughness. Macrotexture depth measured according to ASTM E965 must be at least 0.040 in; if the mean texture depth falls below this value additional testing at fifty‑foot intervals is performed to delineate deficient areas that may require grinding. For federally funded projects a smoothness verification test using an inertial lightweight profiler is conducted on at least ten percent of lane miles (covering a minimum of one day) to confirm IRI and MRI values before corrective grinding is specified. When all inspections, measurements, and tests collectively indicate a structurally sound pavement with surface smoothness or skid‑resistance deficiencies that fall within the preservation treatment matrix thresholds, diamond grinding is selected as the remedial action. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 233–234), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–435), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (pp. 1107–1110)

Diamond grinding is applied to concrete pavements that are still structurally sound but exhibit early‑stage surface distress such as high spots, warped or curled slabs, and only minor rutting typically less than about 0.5 inches deep [1]. The treatment is also required when measured surface tolerances exceed the limits established for joint alignment and ride quality, for example a finished vertical edge differential across any joint greater than ¼ inch (but not more than ½ inch) or individual bumps that exceed 3/16 inch (or ¼ inch where specified) on a 12‑foot straightedge, ridges or valleys over 1⁄8 inch, and overall pavement variation greater than ¼ inch [2]. In addition, if the roughness index of a lot surpasses the maximum acceptable value, grinding is prescribed to bring the texture within specification before any further mechanical texturing or grooving is performed [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete that will serve as an exposed wearing surface on roads with posted speed limits of 35 mph or higher, diamond grinding must be completed prior to service to provide the required skid resistance and texture [1][2]. The method is also specified as the final step when a concrete patching project proceeds without an accompanying asphalt overlay, ensuring the desired ride quality and friction are achieved without adding overlay thickness [1]. Preservation programs typically schedule the first grind after about fifteen years of service on roughly half the roadway width, followed by a full‑width grind at twenty‑five to thirty years to reestablish a uniform profile and frictional texture [1]. Diamond grinding is not recommended for pavements constructed with Vanport Limestone aggregate because that material tends to polish rapidly; in such cases an ultra‑thin wearing course or high‑friction surface treatment is preferred instead [1].

Materials and repair details

Diamond grinding on concrete pavements removes a thin sacrificial layer of at least one‑quarter inch, which must be shown on shop and working drawings; this thickness is omitted when the operation is performed solely as preparation for an overlay. [1][2] The treatment is normally applied across the full width of the pavement, requiring 100 % coverage of the lane and approximately a quarter of each shoulder to accommodate tapering. [1] A power‑driven, self‑propelled grinding machine weighing no less than 40 000 lb and equipped with a grinding head at least four feet wide is specified for the operation. [2] The grinding head carries a stacked set of diamond blades, the texturing blades being 1/8‑inch thick and separated by 1/32‑inch spacers, while the grooving blades are ¼‑to‑⅜ inch larger in diameter to produce longitudinal grooves. [2] The longitudinal grooves are spaced about one‑half inch apart and cut to a depth between one‑eighth and three‑sixteenths of an inch; the resulting surface texture consists of parallel grooves 0.09–0.13 inch wide with land areas of 0.060–0.110 inch, giving an overall groove depth of roughly one‑sixteenth inch. [2] The blades may be applied in a single pass by stacking both blade types or in two passes—first the texturing blades then the deeper grooving blades—to achieve the desired texture. [2] Overlap between adjacent machine passes is limited to two inches, and a feather‑edge strip at least two feet wide extending a minimum of one hundred feet onto the shoulders must be left to preserve proper drainage. [2][1] Prior to grinding all concrete pavement defects must be corrected, including patching spalled areas, retrofitting dowels, slab stabilization or jacking, and resealing any joint that becomes broken during grinding. [2][1] When a precast slab exhibits a vertical differential across a joint greater than one‑quarter inch but not exceeding one‑half inch, it may remain in place provided it was delivered with the required sacrificial thickness of one‑quarter inch that can be removed by grinding; removal beyond this limit is prohibited. [2] The scope generally calls for grinding the entire lane width unless an alternative extent is approved, and any defective portion of pavement requires removal of at least ten feet of reinforced concrete between existing transverse joints or replacement of an entire plain‑concrete panel, with new or rebuilt joints constructed in kind according to standard joint‑drawing methods. [2][1] For roller‑compacted concrete surfaces intended for traffic speeds of 35 mph or higher, diamond grinding is mandated as a distinct construction item separate from any asphalt wearing course or other surface treatment. [1] Design documents must clearly show the grinding limits, including transition or stop lines at bridges and ramps, and the finished surface must provide a positive cross‑slope to ensure proper drainage of both pavement and shoulder areas. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–73), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 386–435)

Diamond grinding can only be applied when the concrete slab retains structural integrity and wheel‑rut depth does not exceed half an inch. The treatment is intended to correct surface irregularities such as high spots, curled or warped slabs, minor rutting, and isolated low spots, provided that correcting them would not require excessive removal of surrounding material. A minimum sacrificial concrete thickness of one‑quarter inch must be present; for precast units the delivered elevation is typically at least a quarter inch higher so that grinding can restore cross‑slope, profile and IRI after installation[1][2]. The concrete’s compressive strength and air content influence the durability of the ground surface and must meet the specifications for the existing pavement[1]. Aggregate composition is a critical factor; aggregates that polish rapidly such as Vanport Limestone or other polishing‑susceptible mixes are excluded because grinding removes the mortar layer and quickly degrades skid resistance, requiring alternative treatments like ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses or high‑friction surface treatments[1]. Hard coarse aggregates increase the effort required for material removal and limit the amount that can be removed during grinding[1]. Existing distress such as a high percentage of patching, alkali‑silica reaction, premature mid‑slab cracking linked to inadequate subbase, frost damage or other durability problems reduces the likelihood of successful grinding and may necessitate alternative preservation actions[1]. For roller‑compacted concrete, a textured finish obtained by grinding is required when the exposed surface will serve traffic at speeds of 35 mph or higher[1]. Joints and cracks must be smooth with a maximum differential of one‑sixteenth inch between adjacent faces before grinding, and any joint that has been cut must be rebuilt in kind[2]. Surface aggregate must possess sufficient strength to resist spalling or fracture when engaged by the diamond blades, and equipment used shall be self‑propelled machines equipped with blades that do not induce spalling at joints or fractures of the aggregate[2]. Geometric tolerances are critical; slope misalignment greater than one‑eighth inch over a twelve‑foot span, deviations from established cross‑slope, or high points exceeding an allowable ridge/valley height of one‑eighth inch at lot edges must be corrected before grinding can proceed[2]. All concrete repairs—including patching, spall repair, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization and slab jacking—must be completed and the surface fully hardened prior to grinding[2]. The vertical differential across a joint may range between one‑quarter and one‑half inch; grinding can be employed to bring the edge differential within the allowable one‑quarter inch, provided that no more than the sacrificial quarter‑inch layer is removed[1][2]. For reinforced cement concrete pavements a minimum of ten feet of intact pavement between transverse joints must remain after grinding, whereas plain‑cement panels may be treated in their entirety[2]. Ride quality is evaluated before grinding and the finished surface must meet International Roughness Index limits of 70 in/mile for facilities with posted speeds above 45 mph or 90 in/mile for those at 45 mph or lower; when these limits are satisfied no additional texturing is required[2]. Joint cleaning and resealing must follow grinding to preserve the sealant reservoir shape and prevent damage to the sealant[1].

Treatment procedures

Diamond grinding begins after all necessary concrete repairs—such as patching, spall repair, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization or jacking—have been completed and the existing ride quality has been recorded. A test section is first cut with a self‑propelled, power‑driven machine that weighs at least 40 000 lb and carries a four‑foot wide diamond‑blade head; this verifies that the equipment can achieve the required texture. The grinding unit is then positioned parallel to the roadway centreline using its built‑in guide, and set up so that it remains at least two feet from curbs, bridges or other barriers. Grinding proceeds either in a single pass or a two‑pass sequence. A stack of 1/8‑inch diamond blades separated by 1/32‑inch spacers (or taller grooving blades when deeper cuts are needed) creates longitudinal grooves whose depth typically ranges from one‑sixteenth to three‑sixteenths of an inch, with spacing of roughly half an inch. The resulting texture consists of parallel grooves about 0.09–0.13 in wide, leaving lands of 0.060–0.110 in and a peak‑to‑valley height near 1/16 in. Overlap between adjacent passes is limited to less than two inches, and a feather pass extending at least two feet onto the shoulders for a minimum length of one hundred feet preserves drainage and cross‑slope. During grinding the slurry generated by the blades is removed continuously, and the surface is cleaned before traffic is allowed back. After the work is finished the pavement must exhibit a smoothness index no greater than 70 in/mi where posted speeds exceed 45 mph, or no greater than 90 in/mi at lower speeds, and texture coverage of at least 98 % with no ridges exceeding one‑eighth inch. Cross‑slope is restored to within a quarter‑inch over twelve feet, and any joints or cracks are kept within a 1/16‑inch differential. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 389–396)

Diamond grinding is performed with a self‑propelled, power‑driven grinder equipped with diamond blades, and one guide specifies that the machine should weigh at least 40,000 lb and have a grinding head no narrower than four feet to achieve the required smoothness without inducing joint spalling or aggregate fracture[2]. The other guide does not prescribe particular equipment, noting only that equipment specifications are not mandated[1]. All concrete repairs—including patching, spall repair, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization, and slab jacking—must be completed before grinding can commence, and a test section is prepared and approved prior to full‑scale work[2][1]. For precast slabs the operation follows placement and any necessary grouting, and it may only proceed when the vertical joint differential lies between one‑quarter inch and one‑half inch, with each slab providing a built‑in sacrificial thickness of one‑quarter inch that limits how much material can be removed[2]. The sequencing placed by the guides positions grinding after slab‑stabilization measures, partial‑depth repairs, load‑transfer restorations, cross‑stitching, full‑depth patching and base‑drain installations, and before work on shoulders or joints; joint cleaning and sealing are required after grinding to preserve sealant shape[1]. A full‑depth grind of the entire surface is preferred, while isolated low spots may be left unground if correcting them would require excessive removal, and any grooving is performed only after the grind has been completed[1][2]. No explicit environmental constraints such as temperature or moisture limits are provided in either source, indicating that implementation does not depend on specific weather conditions according to the guides[1][2]. Traffic management follows the Maintenance and Protection of Traffic guidelines, requiring temporary control devices, detours where feasible, coordination with the District Traffic Unit, and adherence to work‑hour limits to protect motorists during grinding[1]. Additional traffic restrictions prohibit grinding within two feet of existing curbs, bridge barriers or roadway barriers, mandate continuous extraction of slurry so that it never flows across occupied lanes or into gutters, and require thorough cleaning of the pavement before reopening to traffic[2].

Quality and workmanship

Before any grinding begins the inspector must verify that the concrete pavement is structurally sound and that existing wheel ruts do not exceed a depth of 0.5 inch, while also checking for high spots, curled or warped slabs, and minor rutting that can be corrected by grinding [1]. The equipment review requires confirming that a self‑propelled, power‑driven machine equipped with diamond blades is used, the unit must weigh at least 40 000 lb and provide a minimum cutting width of four feet, and the blades must be straight, correctly spaced and checked with a four‑foot straightedge [2]. For precast concrete slabs the contractor must ensure that each slab was delivered with a sacrificial layer of one‑quarter inch to allow removal without exceeding that limit, and any vertical joint differential greater than ¼ inch but not more than ½ inch must be reduced by grinding so that the final edge differential is below ¼ inch [2]. Prior to grinding all concrete repairs shall be completed and the existing ride quality recorded; pre‑grind longitudinal IRI values are required to be no greater than 70 in/mi for posted speeds over 45 mph or 90 in/mi for speeds of 45 mph or less, and a 300‑ft test section must be approved before full production begins [2]. During grinding the inspector watches blade condition to prevent spalling of joints, aggregate fracture, or creation of ridges exceeding one‑eighth inch, while ensuring that the machine produces a longitudinal grooved texture whose parallel grooves are between 0.09 inch and 0.13 inch wide, with land areas of 0.060 inch to 0.110 inch and a groove‑to‑ridge depth of approximately one‑sixteenth inch, covering at least 98 % of the surface [2]. Cross‑slope is surveyed at roughly 100‑ft intervals and any deviation greater than ¼ inch over a 12‑ft span is corrected, and the grinding passes must include feathering sufficient for drainage [2]. After grinding the pavement is cleaned of slurry, damaged joints are resealed, and the finished surface is inspected to confirm that joint differentials do not exceed one‑sixteenth inch, slope deviations are no larger than one‑eighth inch in 12 ft, ridges remain under one‑eighth inch, and groove spacing remains uniform [2]. The completed work is further verified by measuring ride‑quality characteristics; the mean roughness index for each lot must stay within the maximum values prescribed, individual bumps greater than one‑quarter inch on a 12‑ft straightedge are unacceptable, and adjacent‑lot ridges or valleys exceeding one‑eighth inch must be reworked [2]. An independent inertial profiler confirms that the final International Roughness Index does not exceed the applicable limit of 70 in/mi for speeds above 45 mph or 90 in/mi for lower speeds, and the verification MRI must be within 6.1 in/mi of the acceptance MRI on at least 90 % of the tested lots before traffic opening is permitted [2]. When these material, preparation, installation, and post‑construction criteria are satisfied the diamond‑grinding job is accepted as having achieved the intended profile improvement, smoother texture, and enhanced skid resistance [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 386–435)

A satisfactory diamond‑grinding treatment is only undertaken on concrete slabs that are structurally sound and whose existing wheel ruts do not exceed one‑half inch in depth. Under those conditions the grinding removes high spots, curled or warped areas and minor rutting to restore a proper profile and increase skid resistance [1][2]. The work must be performed in accordance with current specifications for Portland cement concrete surface restoration [1]. Joint and crack edges are required to be level within one‑sixteenth inch [2]. Where precast slabs are used, the vertical differential across any joint may not exceed a quarter inch and the grinding depth is limited to the quarter‑inch sacrificial layer provided with the slab [2]. The surface profile measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge shall contain no bump or high spot greater than one‑quarter inch, and individual irregularities larger than three‑sixteenths inch must be removed; transverse deviations exceeding one‑quarter inch over the same length are also corrected, while slope variations are limited to one‑eighth inch [2]. Ridges or valleys on the transverse profile may not exceed one‑eighth inch (approximately three millimetres) [2]. The texture required after grinding consists of parallel longitudinal grooves whose width ranges from 0.09 to 0.13 inch, with land areas between grooves of 0.060 to 0.110 inch and a groove depth of about one‑quarter inch, and at least ninety‑eight percent of the pavement surface must be so textured [2]. Ride quality is verified by the International Roughness Index, which shall not exceed seventy inches per mile on roads with posted speeds greater than forty‑five miles per hour and shall not exceed ninety inches per mile where the speed limit is forty‑five miles per hour or less; these limits must be met for each 528‑foot lot [2]. The Mean Roughness Index of a lot must remain within the maximum value specified in the applicable table, and verification profiling must show that the measured MRI does not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than six point one inches per mile for at least ninety percent of the 528‑foot lots and for the lane average; any area failing these tolerances must be reground before traffic is allowed [2].

Performance and service life

Diamond grinding is applied to concrete pavements that are structurally sound and have wheel ruts shallower than 0.5 inches. By removing a thin surface layer, the treatment re‑profiles the slab, eliminating high spots, low spots, curled or warped sections, and minor rutting, while restoring proper cross‑slope for transverse drainage. The smoother texture raises skid resistance, reduces hydroplaning potential, improves ride quality, enhances safety and lowers pavement noise. When an asphalt overlay is not planned, grinding provides the required smooth riding surface without adding structural material, thereby preserving the existing condition. However, on pavements constructed with Vanport Limestone aggregate, removal of the mortar layer exposes the limestone and its friction can deteriorate rapidly, limiting the durability of the skid‑resistance benefit. As a preservation treatment applied before extensive distress develops, diamond grinding helps maintain functional performance, retards further deterioration, and supports pavement durability, extending the expected service life relative to an untreated slab, although no specific lifespan increase is quantified. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 93–94), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Diamond grinding of concrete pavements is susceptible to a suite of recurring distresses that can undermine the treatment’s intended improvements in ride quality, friction, and durability [1][2]. The most frequently reported loss of skid resistance occurs when the grinding process removes the effective mortar layer and exposes aggregates such as Vanport limestone, which polish rapidly and cause a sharp decline in texture; specifications therefore exclude diamond grinding on pavements constructed with this aggregate and recommend an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or high‑friction surface treatment instead [1]. Premature mid‑slab cracking may develop after grinding if the supporting subbase is deficient, because material removal can reveal underlying weaknesses that propagate as cracks through the slab [1]. Aggregate‑related pathologies, notably the susceptibility of Vanport limestone and the presence of alkali‑silica reaction, often remain undetected in routine condition surveys yet become apparent following grinding operations [1]. Damage at joints and existing cracks is another common failure, where inappropriate equipment or blade configurations can cause spalling or fracture surface aggregates, necessitating resealing of sealed joints at no additional cost to the owner [2]. Dimensional irregularities such as ridges, valleys, or bumps that exceed three‑sixteenths of an inch when measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge (or one‑quarter inch in more severe cases) are considered defects and must be removed by further grinding [2]. Transverse deviations greater than one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes also constitute unacceptable surface unevenness [2]. Slope depressions or misalignments beyond the permitted tolerance of one‑eighth inch over a twelve‑foot span for longitudinal slope, and up to one‑quarter inch for cross‑slope, trigger rejection of the treated lot [2]. Joint differentials that surpass the maximum allowable one‑sixteenth inch between adjacent sides, as well as edge differentials greater than one‑quarter inch on newly placed precast slabs, require corrective grinding or slab removal and re‑grading before the pavement can be accepted [2]. Ride‑quality criteria are monitored through the macro‑roughness index; any lot whose MRI exceeds the specified limit or where the verification MRI differs from the acceptance value by more than 6.1 inches per mile for at least ninety percent of the tested sections must be re‑grinded to meet performance standards [2]. The development of these distresses is strongly influenced by aggregate composition, particularly the presence of rapidly polishing limestone or reactive silica that affect friction loss and may not be identified during standard inspections [1]. Subbase quality governs the likelihood of post‑grinding cracking, while equipment selection, blade type, excessive grinding depth (single passes must not exceed one‑eighth inch), uncontrolled overlap beyond two inches, insufficient pre‑grinding repairs, lack of feather‑pass drainage, and failure to observe straightedge tolerance requirements all contribute to surface irregularities, joint damage, and slope deviations [2]. Consequently, when any of these influencing factors are present, the treatment must be re‑grinded until joint differentials fall within one‑sixteenth inch, ridges remain below one‑eighth inch, slope deviations stay under one‑eighth inch over twelve feet, texture coverage exceeds ninety‑eight percent, and MRI criteria are satisfied [2].

Monitoring and follow-up

After diamond grinding, the treated section should be inspected for any loss of skid resistance—particularly on concrete pavements containing Vanport Limestone—by arranging prompt skid‑friction testing through the Bureau of Materials Operations (BOMO) [1]. Ongoing monitoring can also be performed with the annual automated pavement condition surveys (STAMPP) to verify that no new cracking, joint faulting, or other distress has developed, and any adverse test results must be addressed in the district’s interstate plan [1]. The contractor and agency Representative must then confirm that the restored surface meets all grade, texture, cross‑slope, and ride‑quality criteria established for diamond grinding projects [2]. A 12‑foot straightedge (or crown template) is placed perpendicular to the centerline and moved across the lane width; any depression, ridge or valley greater than one‑eighth inch over the 12‑foot length is unacceptable, and any individual bump exceeding three‑sixteenths inch also requires correction [2]. A 4‑foot straightedge is used to verify blade alignment and to ensure that ridges do not exceed one‑eighth inch [2]. Cross‑slope uniformity is inspected at 100‑foot intervals, and any deviation greater than a quarter inch over a 12‑foot segment must be corrected by additional grinding [2]. The finished pavement must achieve at least ninety‑eight percent textured surface and contain no ridges exceeding one‑eighth inch [2]. Ride quality is measured with an International Roughness Index (IRI) test, where the IRI may not exceed seventy inches per mile on facilities with posted speeds greater than forty‑five miles per hour and may not exceed ninety inches per mile where the speed limit is forty‑five miles per hour or less [2]. In parallel, smoothness verification is performed using an inertial lightweight profiler on a minimum of ten percent of lane miles; the mean‑roughness index must not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than six point one inches per mile for at least ninety percent of the evaluated lots, and overall length differences must remain below two tenths of a percent [2]. All straightedge readings, cross‑slope measurements, IRI results, and MRI data are documented and submitted to the Representative, and any failure triggers additional corrective grinding before final acceptance [2].
