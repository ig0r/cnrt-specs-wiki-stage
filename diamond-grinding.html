Diamond Grinding

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Diamond Grinding is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
diamond-grinding
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-30

Diamond grinding is a preservation treatment applied to structurally sound Portland‑cement concrete pavements that removes a thin sacrificial layer using diamond‑saw blades. By cutting away high spots, warped or curled slabs, and wheel ruts shallower than 0.5 in, the process re‑profiles the cross‑slope and longitudinal surface, restores transverse drainage, and creates a macro‑texture that temporarily raises skid‑resistance and reduces the International Roughness Index to meet ride‑quality tolerances. The method is limited to pavements whose surface irregularities exceed specified limits (e.g., bumps larger than ¼ in or edge differentials between ¼ and ½ in) but do not require structural repair, and it is avoided on aggregates such as Vanport Limestone that polish rapidly.[1][2]

PennDOT publications covering “Diamond Grinding” also include the following subtopics: Grooving.

Grooving serves as a specialized application of this treatment by employing self‑propelled diamond‑cutting machines to cut longitudinal channels that direct water away from the tire–pavement interface while preserving the existing cross‑slope. [2]

Purpose and treatment objectives

Diamond grinding is employed on Portland‑cement concrete pavements that remain structurally sound in order to remedy surface profile deficiencies such as high spots, warped or curled slabs, and minor wheel ruts whose depth does not exceed 0.5 in. The treatment is also specified when the longitudinal roughness of the deck exceeds the tolerances established for ride quality, namely an International Roughness Index greater than 70 in/mi on facilities with posted speeds above 45 mph or greater than 90 in/mi on lower‑speed roads. In addition, any overall surface roughness measured by the Mean Roughness Index that surpasses its maximum allowable value is considered a condition warranting diamond grinding. Localized texture defects are addressed as well; individual bumps or ridges larger than one‑quarter inch (or three‑sixteenths of an inch when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge) and valleys or depressions deeper than one‑eighth inch are treated by removing the offending high points. Joint alignment problems that produce a vertical edge differential greater than one‑quarter inch but not exceeding one‑half inch are corrected to bring the edge height back within the required quarter‑inch tolerance. By cutting away a thin layer of concrete with diamond‑saw blades, the process restores the intended cross‑slope and longitudinal profile, eliminates wheel ruts, reestablishes transverse drainage, and reduces the International Roughness Index, thereby improving ride quality and driver comfort. The grinding also creates a macro‑texture that temporarily raises skid‑resistance friction and lowers the risk of hydroplaning, which is required on exposed roller‑compacted concrete where the posted speed limit is 35 mph or higher. However, the guides advise against using diamond grinding on pavements constructed with Vanport Limestone aggregate because removal of the mortar layer exposes limestone that can polish rapidly and lose skid resistance. [1][2]

Diamond grinding is applied only to structurally sound concrete pavements where wheel ruts are limited and surface irregularities exceed allowable limits, indicating that the treatment targets existing functional deficiencies rather than structural repair. By removing a thin sacrificial layer with diamond‑saw blades, the process re‑profiles the slab, eliminates high spots, restores transverse drainage and cross‑slope, and provides a uniform texture that meets prescribed ride‑quality criteria. The primary objective is to restore the pavement’s functional performance—smoothness, traction, safety, and proper drainage—so that ride quality specifications such as an IRI not exceeding 70 in/mile for higher‑speed roads are achieved. Because it is classified as a preservation treatment, this functional restoration also slows further distress and contributes to extending the service life of the pavement, though extension is a secondary benefit rather than the chief intent. The treatment does not address structural damage; it is limited to correcting surface profile and texture on pavements that are otherwise sound. [1][2] [1][2] [1][2] [1] [1][2]

Distress characterization and causes

Diamond grinding is applied to Portland‑cement concrete pavements where the surface profile exhibits high spots, curled or warped slabs, and minor rutting, as well as to locations where vertical edge differentials at joints exceed the allowable ¼ inch and where straight‑edge testing shows high points or depressions greater than ¼ inch across a slab width [1][2]. The distress is identified primarily at joint edges and anywhere on the slab surface where the elevation deviates beyond the tolerance, with measured differentials commonly ranging from the ¼‑inch limit up to about ½ inch, which represents the upper bound for corrective grinding; wheel ruts are considered acceptable only when they are less than 0.5 inches deep [1][2]. Across the guides the severity of these irregularities is regarded as minor or defect‑level rather than structural failure, yet they can produce an uneven ride and may precipitate spalling or other surface failures if left unaddressed [1][2]. Neither guide provides a quantified rate at which the high points, edge differentials, or rutting progress, so the progression speed of the distress remains unspecified [1][2]. From an operational perspective, the excessive vertical differentials and surface roughness compromise vehicle stability, reduce ride quality, and on roadways with posted speeds of 35 mph or higher diminish skid‑friction resistance, increasing the potential for hydroplaning; diamond grinding restores the profile to within the ¼‑inch tolerance, reestablishes smoothness, and temporarily raises texture to maintain safe operating conditions [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 382–447)

Diamond grinding is applied when the surface condition of a concrete pavement has deteriorated because several interrelated mechanisms have compromised its texture and structural integrity.[1] The loss of the thin mortar film that normally protects the aggregate exposes the underlying aggregate, especially Vanport limestone, whose inherent polishing susceptibility causes a rapid decline in skid resistance once the film is stripped.[1] Heavy traffic loads further accelerate distress by inducing surface faulting, wheel ruts and general wear that reduce smoothness.[1] Inadequate cross‑slope prevents effective transverse drainage, allowing water to linger on the deck and lowering friction.[1] Water infiltration, often linked to poor drainage or subgrade weaknesses, together with climate stresses such as freeze‑thaw cycles, speeds material aging and can lead to surface cracking.[1] Insufficient or low‑quality subbase material provides inadequate support beneath the slab, contributing to premature mid‑slab cracking that signals a lack of structural capacity.[1] Chemical and aggregate degradation, manifested by alkali‑silica reaction and the polishing tendency of Vanport limestone, further erodes durability and may be aggravated after grinding.[1] In precast concrete pavements, vertical edge differentials develop when slabs settle or are not properly supported by the prepared subbase and bedding material, especially if dowel and joint grouting is incomplete.[2] When these differentials exceed a quarter‑inch, the surface must be ground back to meet tolerance, indicating that inadequate bedding preparation, settlement of ungrouted slabs, and resulting uneven joint elevations are key mechanisms accelerating distress.[2] Collectively, load‑related stresses, drainage deficiencies, material aging, support inadequacies, and edge differential development drive the need for diamond grinding to restore a smooth profile, reestablish positive crown and drainage, eliminate ruts, and improve friction.[1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–94), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Applicability and treatment selection

Diamond grinding is suitable for concrete pavements that are structurally sound but display only minor surface defects such as high spots, curled or warped slabs, and wheel ruts shallower than half an inch.[1] The treatment may also be applied to both cast‑in‑place roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) slabs and precast concrete units when the surface is intended to remain exposed to traffic.[1][2] For RCC or other rigid pavements that will carry vehicles traveling at speeds of 35 mph or higher, grinding is required to provide texture and bring profile irregularities within the allowable quarter‑inch tolerance, provided the irregularities do not exceed one‑half inch.[1][2] The method is appropriate when measured vertical edge differentials across joints are greater than one‑quarter inch but not more than one‑half inch, allowing grinding to reduce the differential below the quarter‑inch limit rather than removing the slab.[2] Traffic volumes should be modest; diamond grinding is recommended for roads with an average daily traffic of approximately 2,000 vehicles or less.[1] The guides advise avoiding diamond grinding on concrete pavements that contain aggregates such as Vanport Limestone or other rapid‑polishing materials, for which alternative surface treatments are preferred.[1] No specific environmental exposures or site constraints beyond the aggregate limitation and structural soundness are stipulated; suitability is therefore governed primarily by pavement type, exposure, traffic speed, volume, and surface condition.[1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 382–447)

Diamond grinding is appropriate only when the concrete pavement remains structurally sound and exhibits only shallow surface distress such as wheel ruts that are less than half an inch deep. [1] When rut depths exceed this limit, or when the pavement shows deeper rutting, significant structural distress, extensive cracking, joint spalling, or other severe deficiencies, preservation by grinding should be abandoned in favor of more robust interventions such as transverse or longitudinal grooving, concrete overlay, micro‑surfacing, or other rehabilitation methods. [1] The guides also agree that irregularities measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge greater than one‑quarter inch (or greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch according to some specifications) render grinding ineffective and require removal and replacement of the affected concrete. [2] Likewise, transverse profile deviations—ridges or valleys—exceeding one‑eighth inch trigger the use of alternative treatments rather than diamond grinding. [2] For precast concrete slabs, grinding is permitted only when the finished vertical differential at a joint falls between one‑quarter and one‑half inch; differentials larger than one‑half inch demand regrading or full replacement of the slab. [2] When localized defects are short in length—generally fifteen feet or less—or confined to an area of ten feet between transverse joints that would otherwise require panel replacement, carbide grinding or milling and resurfacing is prescribed instead of diamond grinding. [2] Pavements constructed with Vanport Limestone aggregate are specifically contraindicated for grinding because the process removes the mortar layer and rapidly exposes limestone that loses skid resistance; in such cases an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or a high‑friction surface treatment is preferred. [1] If a concrete slab is slated for an overlay, the existing surface should be left unground; any spalled areas must be cleaned, filled with asphalt material, and a leveling course applied to provide a uniform base before the new layer is placed. [1] Roller‑compacted concrete used as a base beneath an asphalt wearing course does not require diamond grinding; the specified preservation treatment is a uniform, void‑free application of emulsified asphalt over the RCC surface. [1][2] However, when roller‑compacted concrete will serve as the exposed pavement surface and the design speed is thirty‑five miles per hour or greater, the surface must be textured by diamond grinding to achieve adequate skid resistance. [1] Diamond grinding also becomes unsuitable once deterioration has progressed beyond the early preservation stage, which is indicated by patching needs exceeding roughly ten percent of the pavement area or by widespread cracking and joint failures; at that point rehabilitation or reconstruction such as base repair, concrete patching, or full‑depth replacement should be selected. Condition assessment and timing

Determining the need for diamond grinding begins with confirming that the concrete pavement retains sufficient structural capacity, which is typically verified through falling‑weight deflectometer testing after any joint repairs and by reviewing core extracts for adequate thickness and integrity. A comprehensive visual survey follows to locate surface profile defects such as high spots, warped or curled slabs, minor rutting, failed joints, rough ride quality, cracking, spalling, and other distress that may affect smoothness. Wheel‑rut depth is measured and must be less than 0.5 in before grinding can be specified. Cross‑slope is checked at 100‑foot intervals along the pavement length, and any segment deviating more than one‑quarter inch over a twelve‑foot run must be corrected prior to authorizing grinding. The vertical differential across each joint is measured with a straight‑edge or depth gauge; if the edge difference exceeds one‑quarter inch but does not exceed one‑half inch, grinding is required until the differential is reduced to less than one‑quarter inch, and the slab must contain a prescribed one‑quarter‑inch sacrificial layer to accommodate that removal. Surface irregularities are evaluated with a twelve‑foot straightedge or crown surface template moved across the width in five‑foot stages, and any high spot or depression greater than one‑quarter inch (or three‑sixteenths of an inch where that limit applies) is deemed defective and must be ground. The transverse profile is inspected visually for ridges or valleys exceeding one‑eighth inch, which also triggers corrective grinding. Friction tests such as BOMO skid‑friction measurements are performed to confirm loss of traction that would warrant a preservation treatment. Roughness surveys and automated pavement condition assessments provide systematic data on cracking, rutting, joint conditions, and overall smoothness, feeding into treatment matrices used to decide on grinding. Longitudinal roughness is measured with the prescribed profilometer to obtain an International Roughness Index; the IRI must not exceed 70 in/mi on facilities posted above 45 mph and must not exceed 90 in/mi where the speed limit is 45 mph or less. The Mean Roughness Index for each lot is calculated and compared with maximum values listed in the applicable tables, and any lot whose MRI exceeds those limits requires grinding. Non‑destructive investigations—including deflection or faulting testing, ground‑penetrating radar, and specialized scans such as MIT Scan 2 for dowel‑bar alignment and MIT Scan T2 for slab thickness—help locate problem areas and evaluate underlying support conditions. Representative core extracts are obtained near deteriorated joints and at random locations to assess subbase or subgrade quality and concrete material characteristics, including the presence of polishing‑susceptible aggregate that could affect skid resistance. Finally, smoothness verification testing using an inertial lightweight profiler is performed on a representative sample—at least ten percent of lane miles and for a minimum one day—to confirm that recorded IRI and MRI values are within the acceptance tolerances before grinding is approved. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 238–239), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 382–447)

Diamond grinding is recommended for concrete pavements that remain structurally sound but have begun to exhibit early‑stage surface distress such as high spots, warped or curled slabs, and only minor rutting, with wheel ruts typically less than about 0.5 inches deep[1]. The treatment is also specified when measured surface deviations exceed the tolerances established for ride quality, including individual bumps greater than one‑quarter inch using a 12‑foot straightedge, ridges or valleys exceeding one‑eighth inch, and overall surface variations over one‑quarter inch that must be reduced to within those limits[2]. For precast concrete slabs, grinding is applied when the finished vertical edge differential at a joint exceeds one‑quarter inch but does not surpass one‑half inch, allowing the slab to remain in place while bringing the edge back into the allowable tolerance[2]. In addition, if an exposed roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) surface will serve as a wearing course on any roadway with a posted speed limit of 35 mph or higher, diamond grinding must be performed before the pavement is placed in service to provide the required texture and skid resistance[1][2]. The method may also be employed when the measured roughness index (MRI) surpasses the project‑specified maximum, indicating that the surface friction has deteriorated beyond acceptable levels[2]. Within a preservation program, the first grind is commonly scheduled after roughly fifteen years of service on about half the roadway width, followed by a full‑width grind at twenty‑five to thirty years to reestablish a uniform profile and texture before more severe distress develops[1]. The guides note that diamond grinding should not be used on pavements containing Vanport Limestone aggregate because that material polishes rapidly; in such cases alternative treatments such as an ultra‑thin wearing course or high‑friction surface treatment are preferred[1].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–73), Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 382–447)

Materials and repair details

Diamond grinding of concrete pavements is a surface‑restoration operation that removes a thin layer from the existing slab and is normally applied across the full width of the lane, with total grinding preferred [1][2]. The treatment also extends onto roughly one‑quarter of each shoulder to accommodate tapering and includes a feathered transition of at least two feet onto adjacent shoulders for a minimum length of one hundred feet [1][2]. Design documents must show grinding limits, including transition or stop lines at bridges and ramps, and the finished surface must maintain a positive cross‑slope so that both pavement and shoulder drain properly [1]. A sacrificial concrete thickness of no less than a quarter‑inch is required in the shop and working drawings for new or precast slabs; grinding may not remove more than this amount, although the sacrificial layer is omitted when grinding is performed solely as preparation for an overlay [1][2]. The equipment used must be a self‑propelled, power‑driven grinder equipped with diamond blade stacks, and the machine must weigh at least 40,000 lb when fitted with a grinding head that is no narrower than four feet [2]. The blade stack consists of 1/8‑inch thick diamond blades separated by 1/32‑inch spacers, and the grooving blades are selected to be one‑quarter to three‑eighths inch larger in diameter than the grinding blades [2]. Grinding creates longitudinal grooves spaced roughly half an inch apart; each groove is between 0.09 in and 0.13 in wide with land areas of 0.060 in to 0.110 in, and the depth of cut ranges from about one‑sixteenth inch up to three‑sixteenths inch depending on the required texture [2]. Overlap between successive passes is limited to two inches, and the grinder must stay at least two feet away from curbs, bridge barriers, or other roadway edges [2]. Before grinding begins, all concrete repairs—including patching, spall repair, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization, and slab jacking—must be completed, and any joint damaged during grinding must be resealed in accordance with contract specifications [2]. When vertical joint differentials exceed the quarter‑inch tolerance but are not greater than a half inch, diamond grinding is used to bring them within allowable limits without exceeding the sacrificial thickness [2]. High spots on the pavement surface that exceed the ¼‑inch tolerance are removed with the same self‑propelled grinder or comparable cutting tools until straightedge testing shows compliance, at no additional cost to the owner [2]. All slurry generated during grinding must be removed before traffic is restored [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete surfaces that will carry traffic at speeds of 35 mph or higher, diamond grinding is mandated as a distinct construction item separate from any asphalt wearing course or other surface treatment [1][2]. The process is not specified for pavements containing Vanport Limestone aggregate, and where aggregates are prone to rapid polishing an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or High Friction Surface Treatment may be prescribed instead of diamond grinding [1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–73), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 219), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 238–239), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 382–447)

Diamond grinding can only be applied when the concrete slab retains structural integrity and wheel‑rut depth does not exceed half an inch [1]. The treatment is intended to correct surface irregularities such as high spots, curled or warped slabs, minor rutting and isolated low spots, provided that correcting them does not require excessive removal of surrounding material [1][2]. A minimum sacrificial thickness of one‑quarter inch must be present in the slab, joint differentials are limited to 1/16 inch while vertical edge differentials may range from 0.25 to 0.5 inch, and grinding beyond the quarter‑inch layer is prohibited [1][2]. The existing transverse profile must be free of depressions or slope misalignments greater than one‑eighth inch over a twelve‑foot straightedge, and any individual bump exceeding a quarter inch must be removed to bring the surface within tolerance [2][1]. The original cross‑slope of the roadway must be preserved throughout the operation [2]. Concrete compressive strength and air content influence durability, and aggregates that polish rapidly—such as Vanport Limestone—or mixes susceptible to rapid polishing limit the effectiveness of grinding because the mortar layer is removed and skid resistance declines quickly [1]. Hard coarse aggregates increase the effort required and restrict the amount of material that can be removed, and equipment must be selected to avoid spalling at joints or fracturing surface aggregate [1][2]. Pavements exhibiting significant patching percentages, alkali‑silica reaction, premature mid‑slab cracking linked to inadequate subbase, frost damage or other durability problems reduce the likelihood of successful grinding and often require alternative preservation actions [1]. Both reinforced and plain cement concrete slabs as well as exposed roller‑compacted concrete may be treated, but when surface irregularities exceed the stated limits or joint conditions are unacceptable, removal and replacement of the pavement may be required instead of grinding [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements that will serve traffic at speeds of 35 mph or higher, a textured finish produced by diamond grinding is mandatory [1][2]. All required repairs—including concrete patching, spall repair, joint sealing, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization, and slab jacking—must be completed before grinding so that no untreated cracks or joints are exposed to the blades; joint faces must be closely matched within the specified differentials [2][1]. After grinding, joints should be cleaned and resealed to preserve sealant reservoir shape and prevent damage to the sealant [1]. If a protective coating was applied before grinding it must be reapplied after the cut, and unless an exception is granted the entire lane width is normally ground [2]. The finished texture consists of parallel grooves 0.09–0.13 inch wide with land areas of 0.060–0.110 inch and a depth of about one‑sixteenth inch, producing a maximum International Roughness Index of 70 in/mile for posted speeds above 45 mph or 90 in/mile for speeds at or below 45 mph [2]. The cost of grinding is primarily influenced by the amount of material to be removed and the hardness of the coarse aggregate, so accurate knowledge of the original aggregate type aids in realistic unit price development [1].

Treatment procedures

Before any grinding can begin, all concrete repairs—including patching, spall repair, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization and slab jacking—must be completed and the existing ride quality documented. A 300‑ft test section is then constructed and approved, and the remainder of the pavement is inspected with a 12‑foot straightedge moved in short passes across the width to locate any deviations greater than one‑quarter inch; any high spots are removed by grinding until the surface falls within the allowable tolerance. The contractor must use a self‑propelled diamond grinder weighing at least 40,000 lb and equipped with a minimum four‑foot grinding head that carries a stack of 1/8‑in diamond blades spaced 1/32‑in apart (or a two‑pass configuration). Grinding is performed longitudinally, parallel to the centerline, either in a single pass that also creates the texture or in two passes—the first removing a thin layer and the second cutting longitudinal grooves 1/8 in wide, 1/8‑to 3/16 in deep and spaced about one‑half inch apart. Throughout the operation slurry is continuously removed and the pavement surface is cleaned before traffic is allowed to return.

The resulting texture consists of parallel grooves between 0.09 in and 0.13 in wide (approximately 1/8 in) with a ridge‑to‑valley depth of about 1/16 in, leaving a land area of 0.060 in to 0.110 in between grooves. A feather pass of at least two feet onto the shoulders for a minimum length of 100 ft is required to provide proper drainage. The finished surface must be true to grade with ridges no greater than 1/8 in, at least 98 % of the area textured, and an International Roughness Index not exceeding 70 in/mile (90 in/mile for lower‑speed roads). Cross‑slope, longitudinal roughness, joint differentials (limited to 1/16 in) and other tolerances are checked, and re‑grinding is performed if necessary. Finally, the pavement is protected from vehicular traffic for the entire curing period and is reopened only after the prescribed cure time has elapsed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 382–405)

Diamond grinding is limited to concrete slabs that are structurally sound and have wheel ruts no deeper than half an inch, and it must follow any slab‑stabilization measures, partial‑depth repairs, load‑transfer restorations, cross‑stitching, full‑depth patching, or base‑drain installations before work on shoulders or joints begins; joint cleaning and sealing are required after grinding to preserve sealant shape [1]. The operation is performed with a power‑driven, self‑propelled grinder that weighs at least 40,000 lb and carries a grinding head of minimum four‑foot width equipped with diamond blades, which may be set up for either a single‑pass configuration combining flat grinding and taller grooving blades or a two‑pass sequence that first produces a flush surface and then adds longitudinal grooves 1/8 to 3/16 in deep [2]. Prior to grinding all concrete repairs—including patching, spall repair, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization, and slab jacking—must be completed and a straight‑edge test performed, after which the preferred method is a full‑depth grind of the entire surface, leaving isolated low spots unground only when correcting them would require excessive removal [1][2]. Any grooving required for texture or drainage is carried out after the grinding pass, and the pavement must be textured as specified in the project specifications to meet the requirements for traffic traveling at 35 mph or higher [1]. Grinding proceeds longitudinally across each lane with the cutter parallel to the centreline; start‑stop lines are set perpendicular to it, overlap between successive passes is limited to no more than one inch, and a feather pass of at least 100 ft extending a minimum of two feet onto the adjacent shoulder, gore area, or auxiliary lane is required to preserve drainage [2]. The equipment must not operate within two feet of existing curbs, bridge barriers, or roadway barriers, and unground areas may not be left between passes [2]. During grinding the slurry generated by the diamond blades is removed continuously so that it does not cross occupied traffic lanes or enter gutters or other drainage structures, and the pavement surface is cleaned before traffic is returned [2]. Traffic management follows the Maintenance and Protection of Traffic guidelines, employing temporary control devices, detours where feasible, coordination with the District Traffic Unit, and adherence to work‑hour limits to protect motorists while grinding proceeds [1]. The guides do not prescribe specific temperature or moisture constraints for grinding, but they require that the pavement remain protected from vehicular traffic throughout any curing period and may only be opened after that period has elapsed and all slurry has been cleared [2].

Quality and workmanship

When planning a diamond‑grinding job the inspector first confirms that the concrete pavement is structurally sound and that existing wheel ruts do not exceed a depth of 0.5 inch, because grinding is only appropriate under those conditions [1]. The equipment itself is then examined to verify compliance with specifications: it must be a power‑driven, self‑propelled machine equipped with diamond blades, weigh at least 40,000 lb and have a grinding head no narrower than four feet [2]. Blade straightness and spacing are checked so that the parallel grooves produced fall within the required width of 0.09–0.13 in, land areas of 0.060–0.110 in, and a groove‑to‑ridge depth of roughly one‑sixteenth inch [2]. Any protective coating applied before grinding must be stripped off and reapplied after the work is completed [2]. Prior to grinding all concrete defects such as cracks or spalls must be repaired, slurry removed continuously, and a test section approved by the overseeing agency [2]. Unless an explicit waiver is issued the contractor must grind the entire lane width, limiting joint and crack differentials during grinding to one‑sixteenth inch and ensuring that post‑grind vertical edge differential does not exceed one‑quarter inch; an initial differential up to one‑half inch may be reduced by grinding but removal of more than a quarter‑inch from a precast slab is prohibited [2][1]. Cross‑slope uniformity is verified at regular intervals, and deviations greater than one‑eighth inch over a twelve‑foot straightedge are unacceptable, while any deviation exceeding one‑quarter inch must be corrected before proceeding [2]. The grinding operation itself is performed to correct high spots, curled or warped slabs, and minor rutting, producing a smoother, more uniform texture that enhances skid resistance [1][2]. Texture coverage must reach at least ninety‑eight percent of the pavement area using the specified NGCS pattern, with groove dimensions remaining within the limits noted earlier [2]. After grinding the finished surface is examined with a twelve‑foot straightedge or crown template to locate high spots or depressions; any irregularity greater than a quarter‑inch (or three‑sixteenths inch in more stringent cases) is removed by additional grinding until tolerances are met [2]. Transverse profile measurements must show ridge or valley deviations no more than one‑eighth inch, and individual bumps larger than three‑sixteenths to one‑quarter inch are corrected [2]. Ride quality is evaluated by measuring the International Roughness Index, which may not exceed 70 in/mile on roads with posted speeds above 45 mph or 90 in/mile on slower routes, and by confirming that the MRI does not surpass the acceptance limit by more than 6.1 inches per mile on at least ninety percent of each 528‑ft lot [2]. Final smoothness is verified with an inertial profiler to ensure compliance with all tolerance criteria before the work is accepted [2]. The completed work is then confirmed to have achieved the intended profile improvement and increased friction, satisfying the objective of enhanced skid resistance [1].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 238–239), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 382–447)

A diamond‑grinding operation is only considered satisfactory when the underlying concrete slab is structurally sound and any existing wheel ruts are shallower than half an inch, because grinding is intended to correct high spots, curled or warped areas, and minor rutting rather than repair severe distress [1]. The treatment must also restore the deck to its original grade and re‑establish the designed cross‑slope uniformly across the lane [2]. Across all joints the finished vertical differential may not exceed a quarter inch, while the differential between the adjacent sides of any joint or crack is limited to one‑sixteenth inch [2]. When a 12‑foot straightedge is placed either parallel or perpendicular to traffic, no ridge, valley or high spot shall be greater than one‑eighth inch in transverse profile, and the overall deviation across the lane width shall not exceed three‑sixteenths inch; any irregularity exceeding these limits is deemed defective and must be removed by further grinding [2]. Individual bumps that measure more than three‑sixteenths inch with the straightedge trigger corrective action, and any departure greater than one‑quarter inch is considered a defect requiring removal [2]. The depth of material removed during grinding is limited to a quarter inch; precast slabs must provide an equivalent sacrificial thickness to accommodate this removal, and grinding beyond that limit is prohibited [2]. The resulting texture shall consist of longitudinal grooves spaced about half an inch apart, each groove being between 0.09 and 0.13 inch wide with land areas of 0.060 to 0.110 inch, and a depth ranging from one‑sixteenth to three‑sixteenths inch; overlap between adjacent passes shall not exceed one inch and at least ninety‑eight percent of the surface must be textured [2]. Ride quality is verified through roughness indices: the International Roughness Index may not exceed seventy inches per mile on roads posted above forty‑five miles per hour and ninety inches per mile on lower‑speed facilities, while macro‑roughness index measurements must stay within acceptance limits, allowing an excess of no more than six point one inches per mile on at least ninety percent of the 528‑foot test sections and on the average for all sections in a lane [2]. Acceptance is demonstrated by successive straightedge inspections and roughness surveys, with grinding continued until no observable departures remain and all listed tolerances are satisfied [1][2].

Performance and service life

Diamond grinding is applied to concrete pavements that are structurally sound and exhibit wheel ruts shallower than 0.5 inches, ensuring the treatment targets slabs before extensive distress develops [1]. The process removes a thin surface layer to re‑profile the slab, eliminating high spots, low spots, curled or warped sections, and minor rutting while restoring proper cross‑slope for transverse drainage [1]. By creating parallel grooves that are 0.09 to 0.13 inches wide with a land area of 0.060 to 0.110 inch and a depth of approximately 1/16 inch, the finished surface meets texture requirements and provides a uniform riding plane [2]. The resulting texture limits longitudinal roughness to a maximum International Roughness Index of 70 in/mile on roads with posted speeds above 45 mph and 90 in/mile on lower‑speed facilities, thereby improving ride quality [2]. The smoother surface raises skid resistance, reduces the potential for hydroplaning, lowers pavement noise, and enhances overall safety [1][2]. When an asphalt overlay is not planned, diamond grinding supplies the required smooth riding surface without adding structural material, preserving the existing condition of the slab [1]. However, on pavements constructed with Vanport Limestone aggregate, removal of the mortar layer exposes the limestone whose friction can deteriorate rapidly, limiting the durability of the skid‑resistance benefit [1]. The guides agree that diamond grinding eliminates joint or crack faults and provides a uniform finished surface, contributing to improved functional performance [2][1]. Because it is a preservation treatment applied before extensive distress, the process helps maintain functional performance and retards further deterioration, supporting pavement durability and extending the expected service life relative to an untreated slab, although no specific lifespan increase is quantified [1]. Conversely, the specifications do not address durability or projected service‑life extensions, leaving those outcomes undocumented in the technical criteria [2].

Diamond grinding frequently suffers a rapid decline in skid resistance when the exposed aggregate polishes quickly, a condition especially noted with Vanport limestone because the grinding removes the effective mortar layer that previously contributed to friction. Monitoring and follow-up

Following diamond grinding, the contractor conducts a comprehensive acceptance inspection before reopening the section to traffic. [2] The surface grade and continuity are checked with a 12‑foot straightedge or crown‑surface template, advancing no more than five feet at a time; any deviation greater than one‑quarter inch over that length requires additional grinding. [2] A transverse profile taken with the same straightedge must show ridges or valleys not exceeding one‑eighth inch and individual bumps larger than three‑sixteenths inch are also ground down. [2] Cross‑slope is measured at 100‑foot intervals along each lane, and any segment where the deviation exceeds one‑quarter inch over a twelve‑foot length is corrected. [2] Joint edge differentials are verified and must be reduced to the allowable one‑quarter‑inch limit; joints exceeding this tolerance are re‑ground until compliant. [2] At least ninety‑eight percent of the area must retain the specified longitudinal grooved texture, confirming that the required texture coverage has been achieved. [2] Ride quality is evaluated by measuring the International Roughness Index in accordance with Section 507.3, with a maximum IRI of 70 in/mi where posted speeds exceed 45 mph and a maximum of 90 in/mi where speeds are 45 mph or less. [2] For federally funded projects, a Department‑conducted smoothness verification test using an inertial lightweight profiler is performed on a minimum of ten percent of lane miles; the verification mean roughness index may not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches per mile on at least ninety percent of the 528‑foot lots and on average for all lots, with testing coordinated at least fourteen days in advance. [2] In addition to these geometric checks, the treated section is inspected for loss of skid resistance—particularly on concrete pavements containing Vanport Limestone—by arranging prompt skid‑friction testing through BOMO, and ongoing monitoring can be accomplished through annual automated pavement condition surveys such as STAMPP to confirm that no new cracking, joint faulting, or other distress has appeared, with any adverse results addressed in the district’s interstate plan. After diamond grinding, the pavement is inspected for any condition that exceeds the tolerances established for smoothness, profile, or joint integrity; when such exceedances are observed the section is considered deficient and requires further work [2]. Specific surface‑profile limits include ridges or valleys between adjacent ride‑quality sections greater than one‑eighth inch, depressions or high spots measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge exceeding one‑eighth inch, and isolated bumps larger than three‑sixteenths to one‑quarter inch [2]. Joint, crack, or vertical‑edge differentials larger than one‑sixteenth inch are unacceptable, and any differential that persists above one‑quarter inch signals the need for additional treatment; differences between one‑quarter and one‑half inch may be corrected by another round of grinding, whereas differences exceeding one‑half inch typically require slab regrading or replacement [2]. Roughness criteria are also monitored, with an International Roughness Index above seventy inches per mile on roads posted for speeds greater than forty‑five miles per hour, or above ninety inches per mile on slower routes, indicating that the grinding has not achieved the required ride quality [2]. Cross‑slope deviations greater than a quarter‑inch over any twelve‑foot segment and ride‑quality index values that surpass the maximum values set out in the applicable tables likewise denote a deficient condition [2]. In addition to geometric thresholds, performance trends such as persistent low‑friction readings, increasing cracking, joint spalling or faulting, rutting, edge deterioration, and raveling or weathering are interpreted as signs that the preservation stage has been exceeded and that further maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation is needed [1]. When concrete patching required after grinding exceeds roughly ten percent of the pavement area, the extent of damage is considered beyond what grinding alone can remedy, prompting more extensive rehabilitation measures [1]. Particular aggregate types, for example Vanport Limestone, may experience a reduction in skid resistance after grinding; ongoing friction testing is therefore essential to determine whether additional treatment is warranted [1]. The combined presence of any of these geometric exceedances or performance degradations triggers corrective actions that can range from additional diamond or carbide grinding to slab removal and replacement, depending on the severity of the observed condition [1][2].
