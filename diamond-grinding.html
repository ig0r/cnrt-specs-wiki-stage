Diamond Grinding

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Diamond Grinding is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
diamond-grinding
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-23

Diamond grinding is a preservation treatment applied to Portland‑cement concrete pavements that remain structurally sound, in which a thin surface layer—typically at least a quarter‑inch thick—is removed with diamond‑saw blades to correct minor profile defects such as high spots, warped or curled slabs, and wheel ruts shallower than 0.5 in.[1] By re‑profiling the slab, restoring intended cross‑slope and longitudinal grade, and improving macro‑texture, the process lowers the International Roughness Index, enhances ride quality, and provides a temporary increase in skid resistance that reduces hydroplaning risk, especially on exposed roller‑compacted concrete where speeds exceed 35 mph.[1] The technique is limited to pavements without rapidly polishing aggregates such as Vanport Limestone, because removal of the mortar layer would quickly diminish friction, and it is typically specified after concrete patching when no asphalt overlay will follow.[1]

Purpose and treatment objectives

Diamond grinding is applied to Portland‑cement concrete pavements that remain structurally sound in order to correct surface profile deficiencies such as high spots, warped or curled slabs, and minor wheel ruts whose depth does not exceed 0.5 in. By removing a thin layer of the deck with diamond‑saw blades, the treatment restores the intended cross‑slope and longitudinal profile, eliminates rutting, reestablishes transverse drainage, and lowers the International Roughness Index, thereby improving ride quality and driver comfort. The process also increases macro‑texture and friction, providing a temporary boost in skid resistance and reducing the risk of hydroplaning; this is required on exposed roller‑compacted concrete where the posted speed limit is 35 mph or higher. Diamond grinding is not recommended for pavements constructed with Vanport Limestone aggregate because removal of the mortar layer exposes limestone that can polish rapidly and lose skid resistance. The technique is frequently specified after concrete patching when no asphalt overlay will be placed, to achieve a uniform texture, smoother ride, and enhanced traction. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99)

Diamond grinding is applied only to structurally sound concrete pavements where wheel ruts are less than about 0.5 inches and where high spots, warped slabs or minor rutting have developed. By removing a thin surface layer with diamond‑saw blades, the treatment re‑profiles the slab, restores transverse drainage and texture, and returns a smooth riding surface with higher skid friction. Specifications explicitly cite improvement of ride quality as the purpose, indicating that the chief objective is to restore the pavement’s functional performance—smoothness, traction and safety—rather than to repair structural damage. Because it is classified as a preservation treatment, this functional restoration also slows further distress and helps extend the service life of the pavement, but the primary intent remains restoring functionality. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 93–94), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Distress characterization and causes

Diamond grinding is applied to Portland Cement Concrete (rigid) pavements where the surface profile shows high spots, curled or warped slabs, and minor rutting; it is only used when the pavement is structurally sound and wheel ruts are less than 0.5 inches deep. The distress is generally considered minor in severity, and while the source does not discuss how quickly it worsens, the treatment restores a smoother profile and temporarily raises skid‑friction resistance, thereby lowering the risk of hydroplaning and improving safety for motorists. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57)

Diamond grinding is employed when a concrete pavement’s surface condition has deteriorated because several interrelated mechanisms have compromised its texture and structural integrity. The primary trigger is the loss of the thin mortar film that normally protects the aggregate; once this layer is stripped, the underlying aggregate—particularly Vanport limestone—is exposed and can polish rapidly, causing a swift decline in skid resistance and ride quality. Heavy traffic loads exacerbate the problem by inducing surface faulting, wheel ruts, and general wear that diminish smoothness, while inadequate cross‑slope prevents effective transverse drainage, allowing water to linger on the deck and further reducing friction. Water infiltration, often linked to poor drainage or subgrade weaknesses, together with climate stresses such as freeze‑thaw cycles, accelerates material aging and can lead to surface cracking. Inadequate support beneath the slab, for example an insufficient or low‑quality subbase, contributes to premature mid‑slab cracking, indicating a lack of structural capacity. Chemical and aggregate degradation—manifested by alkali‑silica reaction (ASR) and the inherent polishing susceptibility of Vanport limestone—also erodes durability and can be aggravated after grinding. These load‑related, climate/materials‑related, and support‑deficiency mechanisms collectively drive the need for diamond grinding to restore a smooth profile, reestablish positive crown and drainage, eliminate ruts, and improve friction. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–94), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Applicability and treatment selection

Diamond grinding is appropriate for concrete pavements that remain structurally sound but exhibit only minor surface imperfections. It may be applied to Portland‑cement concrete slabs where high spots, curled or warped sections, and wheel ruts shallower than half an inch are present, correcting the profile and providing a temporary increase in skid resistance. The treatment is also required for roller‑compacted concrete that will serve as the exposed wearing surface on low‑volume roads; specifically, when the posted speed limit is 35 mph or higher (and not exceeding about 55 mph) and the average daily traffic does not exceed roughly 2,000 vehicles, the RCC must be textured by diamond grinding before it is opened to traffic. Diamond grinding should be avoided on concrete pavements that contain Vanport Limestone or other aggregates known to polish rapidly; in those cases alternative surface treatments such as an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or a high‑friction surface treatment are recommended. No additional environmental exposures or site constraints are stipulated beyond the structural soundness and aggregate considerations. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99)

Diamond grinding is only suitable for concrete pavements that remain structurally sound and exhibit only shallow wheel ruts—specifically, rut depths less than half an inch. When the pavement shows deeper rutting, significant structural distress, extensive cracking, joint spalling, or any other severe deficiency, preservation by grinding should be abandoned in favor of more robust interventions such as transverse or longitudinal grooving, a concrete overlay, micro‑surfacing, or other rehabilitation methods. The technique is also contraindicated on pavements constructed with Vanport Limestone aggregate because the grinding process removes the mortar layer and rapidly exposes limestone that loses skid resistance; in those cases an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or a high‑friction surface treatment is preferable. When a concrete slab is slated for an overlay, the existing surface should be left unground; any spalled areas must be cleaned and filled with asphalt material and a leveling course applied to provide a uniform base before the new layer is placed. Similarly, roller‑compacted concrete used as a base beneath an asphalt wearing course does not require grinding, since the overlying asphalt supplies the necessary texture, and grinding is unnecessary on RCC sections that will serve only low‑speed roads (below 35 mph). Finally, diamond grinding becomes unsuitable once deterioration has progressed beyond the early preservation stage—evidenced by patching needs exceeding roughly ten percent of the pavement area or widespread cracking and joint failures—at which point rehabilitation or reconstruction treatments such as base repair, concrete patching, or full‑depth replacement should be selected. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Condition assessment and timing

To determine if diamond grinding is appropriate, engineers first verify that the concrete slab retains structural capacity, typically using falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD) testing after any joint repairs and by reviewing core samples for adequate thickness and integrity. A detailed visual survey follows to identify surface profile defects such as high spots, curled or warped slabs, minor rutting, failed joints, rough ride quality, cracking, spalling, and other distress; wheel‑rut depth is measured and must be less than 0.5 in. before grinding can be specified. Quantitative measurements are then taken: friction tests (e.g., BOMO skid‑friction) and roughness surveys confirm loss of traction or smoothness, while automated pavement condition surveys provide systematic data on cracking, rutting, and joint conditions that feed into treatment matrices. Non‑destructive investigations—including deflection or faulting testing, ground‑penetrating radar, and specialized scans such as MIT Scan 2 for dowel‑bar alignment and MIT Scan T2 for slab thickness—help locate problem areas and evaluate underlying support conditions. Representative core extracts are obtained near deteriorated joints and at random locations to assess subbase or subgrade quality and concrete material characteristics, including the presence of polishing‑susceptible aggregate. When these inspections, measurements, and test results collectively indicate a structurally sound pavement with surface smoothness or skid‑resistance deficiencies that fall within the preservation treatment matrix, diamond grinding is selected as the remedial action. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Diamond grinding is indicated when a concrete pavement remains structurally sound but has begun to show early‑stage surface distress such as high spots, curled or warped slabs, and only minor rutting—specifically wheel ruts that are less than about 0.5 inches deep. The treatment is used to restore smooth ride quality and improve skid resistance before severe cracking, extensive rutting, or loss of friction occurs. It is appropriate for pavements that do not contain Vanport Limestone aggregate, because that aggregate tends to polish rapidly; in those cases an ultra‑thin wearing course or high‑friction surface treatment would be preferred. In a preservation program the first grind is typically scheduled around fifteen years of service, treating roughly half the width of the roadway, followed by a full‑width grind at about twenty‑five to thirty years to reestablish a uniform profile and frictional texture. For roller‑compacted concrete that will serve as an exposed wearing surface on any road with a posted speed limit of 35 mph or higher, diamond grinding must be performed before the pavement is placed in service. The method is also specified when concrete patching is undertaken without an accompanying asphalt overlay, serving as the final step to achieve the desired ride quality and skid resistance while avoiding additional overlay thickness. [1]

Materials and repair details

Diamond grinding is applied to concrete pavements as a surface‑restoration operation that removes a thin layer of the existing slab. The treatment is normally executed over the entire width of the pavement, with total grinding preferred, and at the 30‑year point it must cover 100 % of the full‑width area while also treating roughly one‑quarter of each shoulder to accommodate tapering. A sacrificial concrete thickness of at least a quarter‑inch is required by Publication 408 Section 529 and must be shown on the shop and working drawings; this layer is omitted when the grinding is being performed solely as preparation for an overlay. The process is not specified for pavements that contain Vanport Limestone aggregate, and where aggregates are prone to rapid polishing an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or a High Friction Surface Treatment is prescribed instead. For roller‑compacted concrete surfaces that will be exposed to traffic at speeds of 35 mph or higher, diamond grinding is mandated as a distinct construction item separate from any asphalt wearing course or other surface treatment. The design documents must display the grinding limits, including transition or stop lines at bridges and ramps, and the finished surface must provide a positive cross‑slope so that both pavement and shoulder drain properly. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–73), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99)

Diamond grinding can be applied only when the concrete slab retains structural integrity and its wheel‑rut depth does not exceed half an inch. The treatment is intended to correct surface irregularities such as high spots, curled or warped slabs, minor rutting, and isolated low spots provided that correcting them would not require excessive removal of surrounding material. A minimum sacrificial thickness of one‑quarter inch must be present in the slab; precast units are typically placed with an elevation about a quarter inch higher than adjacent pavement so that grinding can restore cross‑slope, profile, and IRI after installation. The concrete’s material properties also govern performance: compressive strength and air content influence durability, while the aggregate composition is critical. Pavements containing Vanport Limestone or other aggregates that polish rapidly are excluded because grinding removes the mortar layer and quickly degrades skid resistance; similarly, mixes susceptible to rapid polishing require alternative treatments such as ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses or high‑friction surface treatments. Hard coarse aggregates increase the effort required and limit the amount of material that can be removed. Additional distress factors—such as significant patching percentages, alkali‑silica reaction, premature mid‑slab cracking linked to inadequate subbase, frost damage, or other durability problems—reduce the likelihood of successful grinding and may necessitate alternative preservation actions. For roller‑compacted concrete, a textured finish obtained by grinding is required when the exposed surface will serve traffic at 35 mph or higher. Finally, joint cleaning and resealing must follow grinding to preserve sealant reservoir shape and prevent damage. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–99), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Treatment procedures

In practice diamond grinding can only be applied to concrete slabs that remain structurally sound and whose wheel ruts do not exceed half an inch. The operation is placed in the construction schedule after any slab‑stabilization measures, partial‑depth repairs, load‑transfer restorations, cross‑stitching, full‑depth patching and base‑drain installations, and before work on shoulders or joints; joint cleaning and sealing must follow grinding to preserve sealant shape. The preferred method is a full‑depth grind of the entire surface, leaving isolated low spots unground when correcting them would require excessive removal, and any grooving is performed only after the grind. For reinforced cement concrete pavements that will carry traffic at 35 mph or higher, grinding is mandatory to provide texture, and it must be treated as a distinct construction item separate from asphalt wearing courses or other surface treatments. Traffic management follows the Maintenance and Protection of Traffic guidelines; temporary control devices, detours where feasible, coordination with the District Traffic Unit, and adherence to work‑hour limits are required to protect motorists while grinding proceeds. The guidelines do not prescribe particular diamond‑saw equipment nor specify environmental constraints such as temperature or moisture. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–99)

Quality and workmanship

When planning diamond grinding the inspector must confirm that the concrete pavement is structurally sound and that existing wheel ruts do not exceed the 0.5‑inch limit, then evaluate the surface profile for high spots, curled or warped slabs, and any minor rutting that can be corrected; the grinding operation should be carried out to achieve a smoother, more uniform texture that enhances skid resistance, and the completed work is verified to ensure the profile improvement and increased friction have been achieved. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57)

A satisfactory diamond‑grinding treatment on Portland cement concrete pavement is achieved only when the slab is structurally sound and any existing wheel ruts are shallower than half an inch. Under those conditions the grinding removes high spots, curled or warped areas, and minor rutting to restore a proper profile and increase skid resistance, thereby reducing hydroplaning potential. The work must follow the current specifications for PCC surface restoration. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57)

Performance and service life

Diamond grinding is applied to concrete pavements that are structurally sound and have wheel ruts shallower than 0.5 inches. By removing a thin surface layer, the treatment re‑profiles the slab, eliminating high spots, low spots, curled or warped sections, and minor rutting, while restoring proper cross‑slope for transverse drainage. The smoother texture raises skid resistance, reduces hydroplaning potential, improves ride quality, enhances safety and lowers pavement noise. When an asphalt overlay is not planned, grinding provides the required smooth riding surface without adding structural material, thereby preserving the existing condition. However, on pavements constructed with Vanport Limestone aggregate, removal of the mortar layer exposes the limestone and its friction can deteriorate rapidly, limiting the durability of the skid‑resistance benefit. As a preservation treatment applied before extensive distress develops, diamond grinding helps maintain functional performance, retards further deterioration, and supports pavement durability, extending the expected service life relative to an untreated slab, although no specific lifespan increase is quantified. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 93–94), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Diamond grinding of concrete pavements is prone to several recurring distresses. The most frequent is a rapid loss of skid resistance, especially when the pavement contains aggregates such as Vanport limestone that polish quickly once the effective mortar layer is removed by the grinding blades. This loss can be severe enough that specifications (e.g., Publication 408, Section 514) exclude its use on Vanport‑limestone concrete and recommend an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or a high‑friction surface treatment instead. In addition to friction problems, premature mid‑slab cracking may develop after grinding when the underlying subbase is inadequate; the removal of material can expose weaknesses that propagate as cracks through the slab. Underlying aggregate pathologies—particularly Vanport limestone susceptibility and alkali‑silica reaction—may also become apparent post‑grinding because they are not readily identified in routine condition surveys such as STAMPP data collection. Consequently, the occurrence of these failures is driven primarily by the aggregate composition of the pavement, the quality of its supporting subbase, and the limited detectability of certain distress mechanisms during standard inspections. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Monitoring and follow-up

After diamond grinding, the treated section should be inspected for any loss of skid resistance—especially on concrete pavements containing Vanport Limestone—by arranging prompt skid‑friction testing through BOMO. Ongoing monitoring can also use the annual automated pavement condition surveys (STAMPP) to confirm that no new cracking, joint faulting, or other distress has appeared, and any adverse test results must be addressed in the district’s interstate plan. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

If post‑grind inspections reveal persistent low‑friction readings, growing cracking, joint spalling or faulting, rutting, edge deterioration, or raveling/weathering, the diamond‑ground section is considered to have exceeded the preservation stage and requires additional maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation. The need for extensive concrete patching—generally more than ten percent of the pavement area—also signals that grinding alone is no longer sufficient. Particular aggregate concerns, such as Vanport Limestone, can worsen skid resistance after grinding and must be closely monitored with friction testing to determine if further treatment is warranted. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)
