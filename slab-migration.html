Slab Migration

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Slab Migration is a topic under Distress.
slab-migration
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Slab migration is the visible movement or rocking of concrete slabs—or slab fragments—under normal traffic loads, producing unstable support for any subsequent overlay. It occurs exclusively in Portland cement concrete pavements (jointed slabs, continuously reinforced sections, bridge‑end slabs and longitudinal cracks) where deteriorated joint performance and loss of subgrade support allow slabs to shift, a condition identified by joint faulting that exceeds greater than 1/4‑inch (requiring milling before a geotextile interlayer) or greater than 1/2 inch (necessitating a scratch course or grinding prior to an asphalt interlayer) [2]. The distress is triggered by traffic loading and compromised load transfer at joints, and the specification (Section 548.3) mandates that any area exhibiting such unstable slabs be stabilized or repaired—via slab stabilization, slab‑jacking, flexible‑base replacement, or shallow patch repairs—before interlayer placement [2][1].

Description and identification

Slab migration is the movement of concrete slabs or slab fragments that rock visibly under normal traffic loads, creating unstable support for an overlay. It shows up as visibly moving slabs and joint faulting; joints that have greater than 1/4‑inch faulting are identified for geotextile interlayer placement, and when joint faulting exceeds 1/2 inch a scratch course or milling of the joint is required. The specification (Section 548.3) mandates that any area with such unstable slabs be stabilized or repaired—using slab stabilization (Section 679), slabjacking (Section 681), flexible‑base replacement (Section 316), or shallow patch repairs—before the interlayer material is placed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Within the slab‑stabilization specification (Section 679—Slab Stabilization, 679.3 Construction) slab migration is first recognized through loaded‑slab corner deflection testing; any measured deflection that exceeds 0.02 inch signals the need for corrective action. When the joint efficiency is at least 65 % the required response is full stabilization, whereas a joint efficiency of less than 65 % calls for a patch‑and‑stabilize approach. If temperature conditions preclude formal testing, visual inspection for pumping problems or poor joint performance serves as an alternative indicator of migration. During grout injection the crew must stop pumping when any vertical movement is noted, and upward pavement lift greater than 0.05 inch is not permitted, a limit that separates true slab migration from ordinary cracking or surface defects.

For high‑density polyurethane grout work (Section 679—Slab Stabilization, 679.3(d)) the identification of migration relies on vertical‑movement testing that measures lift at the pavement edge or at two outside slab corners adjacent to a joint and the adjoining shoulder; the equipment must be capable of detecting lift changes as small as 0.001 inch. The specification again limits allowable uplift to not exceed 0.05 inch, thereby distinguishing migration from other distresses that are not evaluated by lift magnitude.

The same vertical‑movement testing protocol is prescribed for asphalt grout stabilization (Section 679—Slab Stabilization, 679.3(c)). Lift is measured at the pavement edge or at two opposite slab corners adjacent to a joint and shoulder with equipment able to detect 0.001 inch variations, and any upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited, indicating migration rather than normal settlement.

In the unbonded concrete overlay provisions (Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfaced Pavements, 548.3 Construction) slab migration is recognized by the visible movement or rocking of slabs or slab fragments under normal traffic loads. This visual symptom differentiates migration from static cracking. Joint faulting is quantified, and joints showing greater than 1/4‑inch faulting must be milled before a geotextile interlayer is placed; if faulting exceeds 1/2 inch a scratch course or milling/grinding of the joint is required prior to an asphalt interlayer. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–605)

Where it occurs

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), slab migration is identified as a distress that appears in Portland cement concrete pavements, specifically within the concrete slabs and their longitudinal cracks or joints; the manual makes no reference to its occurrence in asphalt pavements, composite structures, overlays, or shoulders. [1] The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications likewise limit slab migration to concrete slab pavements, noting that it is observed on concrete‑surfaced (rigid) pavements where slabs or fragments move or rock under normal traffic loads and that the phenomenon is addressed only for concrete pavements in the stabilization, slab‑jacking, and overlay sections. [2] Both documents therefore associate slab migration exclusively with various forms of concrete pavement—including Portland cement concrete slabs, continuously reinforced concrete pavement, jointed concrete slabs, bridge‑end slabs, and concrete‑surfaced rigid pavements—while excluding asphalt, composite, overlay, and shoulder structures from its occurrence.

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–612)

The Pavement Policy Manual states that slab migration is tied to longitudinal cracks within concrete slabs, so cross‑stitching of those cracks is recommended to restrain movement [1]. It also notes that when joint performance shows faulting or poor load transfer, inadequate support beneath the pavement can give rise to migration at joints, and slab stabilization or jacking may be employed to address the condition [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require deflection testing at every transverse joint and crack, and call for slab stabilization whenever a loaded slab‑corner deflection reaches 0.02 inch or more, indicating that migration is monitored primarily at joints, cracks, and the adjacent slab corners rather than in wheel paths, interior slabs, layer interfaces, base, or subgrade [2]. The high‑density polyurethane grout stabilization portion of Section 679 monitors vertical lift at the pavement edge and at any two outside slab corners adjacent to a joint and adjoining shoulder, limiting upward movement to less than 0.05 inch, which further confirms that migration is observed at edges and corner locations near joints [2]. Section 681 on slabjacking specifies jack‑up procedures for jointed concrete slabs (including bridge‑end slabs) and for continuously reinforced concrete pavements, indicating that movement can be seen both at slab joints and across the interior of a continuous slab [2]. The asphalt grout stabilization clause also mandates measurement of vertical slab lift at the pavement edge and at any two outside slab corners adjacent to a joint, and notes that asphalt may seep from joints or cracks during injection, signaling movement at those discontinuities [2]. Finally, Section 548 describes slab migration as visible rocking of entire concrete slabs or fragments on the surface, especially at joints that exhibit faulting; any joint with greater than 1/4‑inch faulting is identified for treatment and if the fault exceeds 1/2 inch additional corrective actions are required [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–612)

Causes and mechanisms

The specification identifies slab migration as the visible movement or rocking of concrete slabs and fragments when subjected to normal traffic loads; therefore traffic loading is recognized as a cause. The text does not attribute slab migration to climate, moisture, drainage, material properties, or design/construction deficiencies, nor does it include any table that classifies distresses by cause. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Slab migration initiates when joint performance deteriorates—faulting or poor load transfer—so that either the support beneath the slab is lost or the ability of adjacent slabs to share loads is compromised, prompting the need for slab stabilization or jacking to restore support and dowel‑bar retrofits or cross‑stitching to reestablish load transfer [1]. The process accelerates when the subgrade is unstable, drainage is deficient, or water, chlorides, and incompressible materials infiltrate the pavement structure, all of which undermine joint sealing and promote spalling and loss of aggregate interlock [1]. In the context of high‑density polyurethane grout stabilization, slab migration manifests as an upward lift of the pavement that occurs when grout is injected beneath it; the lift progresses as injection continues and must be limited to less than 0.05 inch [2]. Progression is controlled by regulating the injection rate and continuously monitoring slab rise with equipment capable of detecting movement to 0.001 inch, so any detectable vertical movement triggers a stop in pumping [2]. Conditions that accelerate migration include an uncontrolled injection rate or inadequate pressure/temperature control, which allow grout pressure to exceed the allowable lift limit [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 604–605)

Severity levels and rating

In Section 679—Slab Stabilization, the construction portion defines that any joint or crack exhibiting a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more triggers remedial action; when the joint efficiency is at least 65 % the specification calls for stabilization, whereas if the joint efficiency is less than 65 % the required treatment is patching and stabilization, both subject to Representative approval. Additionally, vertical movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited, upward movement during grout injection that exceeds 0.05 inch is also prohibited, and pressure in the grout line must not exceed a maximum of 200 psi unless otherwise directed. In the asphalt grout stabilization sub‑section (Section 679.3(c)2.d), slab migration severity is limited to a single threshold: any upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is considered unacceptable, and equipment capable of detecting slab lift to measurements of 0.001 inch shall be used. In Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfaced Pavements, slab migration is governed by fault‑measurement limits rather than a named severity scale; joints that exhibit greater than one‑quarter inch of faulting trigger the placement of a geotextile interlayer, and when joint faulting exceeds one‑half inch the specification requires either a scratch course at the joint or milling/grinding of the joints to reduce the fault before an asphalt interlayer is placed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–604)

The source uses the slab‑stabilization construction procedure in Section 679—Slab Stabilization, subsection 679.3 Construction to rate Slab Migration; it requires stabilization when a loaded slab corner deflection is 0.02 inch or more together with a joint efficiency of 65% or more, which serves as the severity criterion for treatment. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Measurement, survey and extent

In Section 679—Slab Stabilization, slab migration is quantified by two complementary measurements. Deflection testing, performed according to ASTM D4695, records the loaded corner deflection of each joint; any joint that exhibits a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more triggers stabilization. In addition, vertical‑movement testing uses equipment capable of detecting slab lift with a resolution of 0.001 inch. The lift is measured simultaneously at the pavement edge or at any two outside slab corners adjacent to the joint and the adjoining shoulder, and upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is not permitted during grout injection (Section 679.3(d) for high‑density polyurethane grout and Section 679.3(c) for asphalt grout). In Section 681—Slabjack​ing, slab migration is expressed as the vertical deviation of a concrete slab from a reference string‑line grade, reported in feet of rise or faulting. The deviation is obtained from roadway profiling surveys that produce existing and post‑jacking profile drawings showing the existing grade, the proposed grade line, and the achieved grade after stabilization. The contractor must adjust jacking so that the raised pavement falls within ±0.02 foot of the string‑line grade for continuously reinforced pavement or within ±0.03 foot for jointed slabs. In Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay, slab migration is evaluated by measuring vertical faulting at concrete joints. When a geotextile interlayer is placed, any joint showing faulting greater than 1/4‑inch must be milled; when an asphalt interlayer is used, faulting exceeding 1/2 inch requires a scratch course or milling/grinding of the joint. The specification does not prescribe a particular instrument, so the observed amount of faulting is compared directly to these limits. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–612)

In Section 679—Slab Stabilization, subsection 679.3 Construction the survey for slab migration is performed by deflection testing in accordance with ASTM D4695, supplemented by visual verification when air temperature limits (below 40 °F or above 70 °F) prevent testing; each transverse joint and crack is tested before work and retested after grouting as directed, so the frequency is essentially a per‑joint inspection at the time of stabilization. The unit of measurement is each individual joint or crack, with the key criteria being a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more and a calculated joint efficiency (unloaded slab corner divided by loaded slab corner deflection ×100) that must meet or exceed 65 percent. Extent of migration is therefore expressed as the percentage joint efficiency for each surveyed joint, with joints exceeding the thresholds identified for stabilization. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Effects on performance

When slab migration is allowed to continue, joint performance deteriorates and appears as faulting and reduced load‑transfer across transverse joints or cracks; the loss of support beneath the slab then requires remedial actions beyond simple resealing, such as slab stabilization or jacking (Specification Section 681) to restore bearing and a dowel‑bar retrofit (Specification Section 527) to reestablish load transfer when drainage is functional and the subgrade is stable. If these measures are not applied, the distress can progress to more extensive spalling that may require full‑depth concrete pavement patching of the joint. Consequently, resealing should be performed as soon as joints begin to show distress, even though serviceability may still be satisfactory. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90)

Repairs and treatments

Cross‑stitching is a repair that strengthens longitudinal cracks or joints that are in reasonably good condition and thereby prevents slab migration while preserving aggregate interlock (source [1] section 4.3). Slab stabilization or slab jacking may be employed when joint performance problems such as faulting or poor load transfer are observed, provided the existing drainage is functional and the cause is not an unstable subgrade, in order to restore the support underlying slab migration (source [1] section 4.3). A dowel‑bar retrofit can be used together with slab jacking to reestablish load transfer of settled slabs that have lost support, and it is most effective when hairline cracks are present and aggregate interlock still exists on roadways subjected to heavily channeled loading (source [1] section 4.3). Cement‑grout stabilization is required for any joint or crack that exhibits a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more; if the measured joint efficiency is at least 65 % the work consists solely of stabilization, whereas if the joint efficiency is less than 65 % the joint must first be patched and then stabilized, with both actions needing representative approval (source [2] section 679.3). When pavement has risen above the allowable tolerance of 0.05 inch, the excess material shall be removed by grinding the surface back to the correct grade (source [2] section 514.3 referenced in 679.3). High‑density polyurethane grout stabilization is prescribed for upward movement of a slab; the polymer is injected beneath the slab while continuously monitoring lift, pumping must be stopped at any occurrence of vertical movement, and the specification limits upward movement to no more than 0.05 inch (source [2] section 679.3(d)). Asphalt grout stabilization provides similar control of slab migration by injecting hot asphalt into drilled holes; it is permitted only when the asphalt temperature is maintained between 380 °F and 450 °F, injection pressures are kept between 25 psi and 45 psi, and upward movement does not exceed 0.05 inch (source [2] section 679.3(c)). In slab‑jacking operations, cement grout may be used to lift depressed concrete slabs; the slab must be raised to within 0.02 foot of the string‑line grade for continuously reinforced pavement or within 0.03 foot for jointed pavement, and continuous pressures up to 200 psi are allowed (source [2] section 681.3(b)). The same elevation tolerances apply when high‑density polyurethane grout is employed in slab‑jacking, but any overjacking greater than 0.10 foot triggers removal and replacement of the slab as required (source [2] section 681.3(c)). Where unstable slabs or slab fragments move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads, pre‑overlay repairs are mandated; acceptable remedies include concrete pavement patching, slab stabilization, slab‑jacking, flexible‑base replacement, and shallow repairs using an approved patching material, all to be performed before placement of the interlayer (source [2] section 548.3). A geotextile interlayer must be installed over joints that exhibit faulting greater than 1/4 inch (source [2] section 548.3). When joint faulting exceeds 1/2 inch, an asphalt interlayer with a scratch course or milled joint is required to mitigate slab‑movement‑related faulting (source [2] section 548.3).

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–612)

According to Section 679‑3, cement grout slab stabilization may be used on joints or cracks that exhibit a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more; when the joint efficiency is at least 65 % the slab is stabilized, whereas if the joint efficiency is less than 65 % the joint must be patched and then stabilized, provided the representative approves, and the procedure requires that upward movement during grout injection not exceed 0.05 inch. High‑density polyurethane grout stabilization, described in Section 679.3(d), may be applied to slab migration only when the anticipated upward movement of the pavement does not exceed 0.05 inch; any lift greater than that is prohibited and injection must be stopped as soon as vertical movement is detected. For asphalt grout stabilization, Section 679.3(c) limits application to cases where the upward movement of the pavement does not exceed 0.05 inch; a lift greater than that precludes further grout injection under this method. Under Section 681.3, slabjacking may be employed for cement‑grout or high‑density polyurethane‑grout stabilization provided the required lift stays within the tolerance of plus or minus 0.02 foot for continuously reinforced concrete pavement or plus or minus 0.03 foot for jointed slabs or bridge‑end slabs; if the needed overjacking exceeds 0.10 foot, the treatment is ruled out and the pavement must be removed and replaced in accordance with Section 516. Finally, Section 548.3 requires that any area where slabs or slab fragments move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads be stabilized (Section 679) or repaired such as by slabjacking (Section 681) before an overlay is placed; for a geotextile interlayer the specification calls for milling joints that exhibit faulting greater than 1/4‑inch, and when an asphalt interlayer is used joint faulting exceeding 1/2‑inch mandates a scratch course or milling of the joint prior to overlay placement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–612)

Prevention in design and construction

Slab migration can be mitigated through a series of design measures that address the underlying support and load‑transfer deficiencies identified in pavement joints and cracks. Slab stabilization or jacking may be employed when joint performance problems are due to inadequate support, provided that existing drainage is functional and the subgrade is not unstable, thereby targeting a support‑related cause of slab migration [1]. Dowel bar retrofits are recommended for heavily loaded pavements where transverse joints or cracks exhibit hairline cracking but still retain aggregate interlock, restoring load transfer of settled slabs that have lost support and thus reducing migration [1]. Cross‑stitching is prescribed for longitudinal cracks or joints that are in reasonably good condition—especially when they are beginning to fault—to strengthen the crack, maintain aggregate interlock, and prevent slab migration [1]. Stabilization is required for any joint or crack that shows a loaded‑slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more and a joint efficiency of at least 65 % (or less if approved by the Representative), addressing excessive vertical movement at joints [2]. The stabilization work is performed with cement‑grout injection, drilling holes no larger than 1½ in., and lift is monitored so that upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 inch will not be allowed; pumping must cease if this tolerance is exceeded, and any panels lifted more than 0.05 inch are to be ground back to grade [2]. High‑density polyurethane grout stabilization mitigates slab migration by limiting upward slab movement, with injection stopped upon any vertical movement and a prohibition on pavement lift greater than 0.05 inch; traffic may not reopen until at least 30 minutes after grouting is finished [2]. Asphalt grout stabilization controls slab migration by limiting upward pavement movement, requiring the asphalt to be heated between 380 °F and 450 °F and pumped at 25–45 psi while lift is continuously measured, with any increase greater than 0.05 inch prohibited and traffic closure of a minimum of 30 minutes after completion [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays, any area showing unstable slabs or slab fragments that move or rock under normal traffic must be stabilized before the overlay, using either slab stabilization or slab jacking, to eliminate non‑uniform support that could promote migration [2]. Flexible‑base replacement and shallow repairs of surface defects are also prescribed to remove non‑uniform support conditions that may lead to slab migration [2]. When joint faulting exceeds 1/4‑inch, a geotextile interlayer must be placed over the repaired surface to separate the overlay and limit differential movement [2]. If joint faulting exceeds 1/2‑inch, an asphalt interlayer with a scratch course or milled/grinded joints is required to reduce the faulting that could otherwise cause slab migration [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–605)

Cross‑stitching is prescribed as a repair technique to strengthen longitudinal cracks or joints that are in reasonably good condition and thereby prevent slab migration by maintaining aggregate interlock, with the goal of achieving the nearly 100 % load‑transfer objective described in item 7 of subsection 4.3 Joints. Section 679.3 requires that only personnel who have at least five years of comparable project experience be assigned, and that work may not commence until written documentation is provided and daytime temperatures are 40 °F or higher and the subgrade or base is not frozen, a condition intended to avoid improper grout placement that could worsen slab migration. Stabilization is permitted only after each joint or crack has been deflection‑tested at air temperatures between 40 °F and 70 °F and not during spring thaw; joints exhibiting a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more and a joint efficiency of 65 % or higher (or lower if approved) are then eligible for stabilization, directly addressing the movement that characterizes slab migration. During cement‑grout injection the pump must be halted at any indication of vertical movement, and an upward pavement lift exceeding 0.05 inch is prohibited, while injection pressure may not exceed 200 psi unless specifically directed. If a panel is raised beyond the 0.05‑inch tolerance, it must be ground to restore proper grade before traffic may be reopened, and this corrective action cannot occur until at least 12 hours have elapsed following grouting. Section 679‑3(d) mandates that high‑density polyurethane grout be injected beneath the slab using a calibrated pump that controls flow rate and monitors material usage, with vertical‑movement testing equipment capable of detecting lift as small as 0.001 inch provided to stop injection immediately if movement occurs; injection holes may not exceed 5/8 inch in diameter and must be vertical and round. Traffic may not be reopened for at least 30 minutes after completion of any grout injection, allowing the slab additional time to stabilize prior to loading. Section 681.3(b) requires cement‑grout slabjacking to be carried out with an expanding rubber packer and the grout pumped until the continuously reinforced concrete surface is within ±0.02 ft of a string‑line grade (or jointed slabs and bridge end slabs within ±0.03 ft), with continuous injection pressure limited to 200 psi, short‑term pressures up to 300 psi allowed, and brief spikes to 600 psi permitted only when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade, a practice intended to halt further slab migration by reestablishing uniform elevation. Similarly, Section 681.3(c) specifies that high‑density polyurethane grout be injected beneath the slab with control of lift to remain within the same ±0.02 ft or ±0.03 ft tolerances and adherence to the identical pressure limits, thereby preventing additional slab movement. If a jacking operation raises the pavement more than 0.10 ft beyond the allowable tolerance, the excess must be removed by grinding or the pavement must be replaced in accordance with Section 516. Section 679.3(c) requires that grout‑injection holes for asphalt grout stabilization be drilled no larger than 1½ inches in diameter, vertical and deep enough to penetrate any stabilized base, and that compressed air be blown into each hole for approximately 5 to 15 seconds while the drill is withdrawn, or until water ceases, to clear moisture that could otherwise permit upward slab movement. The asphalt employed as grout must be heated to a temperature between 380°F and 450°F before pumping (any mix above 500°F is rejected), and during injection it is pumped at 25–45 psi while a lift‑monitoring device capable of detecting movement as small as 0.001 inch is used; an upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is not permitted. Section 548.3 mandates that any area exhibiting unstable slabs or slab fragments that move or rock under normal traffic loads be stabilized or repaired before placement of the interlayer material, thereby preventing non‑uniform support associated with slab migration; acceptable remedial actions include slab stabilization as described in Section 679, slabjacking per Section 681, or flexible‑base replacement according to Section 316. If joint faulting exceeds ¼ inch, a geotextile interlayer must be installed over the repaired surface, addressing joint movement that could otherwise contribute to slab migration. When joint faulting exceeds ½ inch, an asphalt interlayer must be placed after a scratch course or milling of the joints is performed prior to overlay placement, thereby reducing excessive joint displacement. Specification limits and acceptance

Under Section 679‑3(d) of the Slab Stabilization specifications, which governs High Density Polyurethane Grout Stabilization, any upward movement of a pavement slab that exceeds 0.05 inch is prohibited; compliance is verified by using vertical‑movement testing equipment that is capable of detecting slab lift to a resolution of 0.001 inch. Similarly, the Asphalt Grout Stabilization procedure in Section 679‑3(c) limits slab migration to an upward movement not greater than 0.05 inch, and demonstrates compliance with vertical‑movement testing equipment that must be able to detect lift to 0.001 inch as required by subsection 679.3(c)1.h. Within the Unbonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfaced Pavements specifications, Section 548‑3 requires that any concrete slab that moves or rocks visibly under normal traffic loads be considered unstable and must be stabilized or repaired before placement of the overlay; joint faulting is judged by measuring the vertical offset in inches on the existing pavement surface, with joints showing faulting greater than 1/4 inch required to be milled and joints where faulting exceeds 1/2 inch requiring a scratch course or milling of the joint. [2]

In the slab‑stabilization provisions, the specification distinguishes remedial actions according to the stabilization method employed. For general slab migration under Section 679.3, when the measured upward movement exceeds the allowable 0.05 inch, the work required is grinding the raised portion back to the correct grade as directed in Section 514.3; this grinding is performed by the contractor that holds the slab‑stabilization contract and must be finished before final acceptance, although no explicit time limit is set. When high‑density polyurethane grout stabilization is used (Section 679.3(d)) and an upward movement greater than 0.05 inch occurs, the specification requires that grout injection be stopped; the contractor injecting the material carries out this stop, which may also be directed by the Inspector‑in‑Charge, and the pavement must remain closed to traffic for a minimum of 30 minutes after grouting before it can be reopened. In the case of asphalt grout stabilization (Section 679.3(c)), if slab lift exceeds 0.05 inch, the operator conducting the injection must immediately halt pumping until the asphalt has congealed, with no further time delay beyond that immediate cessation. Separate from the slab‑stabilization chapter, Section 548.3 addresses unbonded concrete overlays; when slabs or slab fragments move or rock under normal traffic, the responsible party must stabilize or repair them before any interlayer material is placed, selecting one of the prescribed treatments—concrete pavement patching (Section 516), slab stabilization (Section 679), slabjacking (Section 681), flexible‑base replacement (Section 316) or shallow repairs—and these actions must be completed prior to overlay placement, though no specific time frame is provided.
