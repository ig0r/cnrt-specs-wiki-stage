Class AA Concrete

Part of: Portland Cement Concrete is-subtopic-of

Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Class AA Concrete is a high‑strength, durable Portland‑cement concrete mix that meets or exceeds the requirements of Publication 408, Specifications, Section 704 for Class AA Cement Concrete [1]. It is characterized by an average 28‑day compressive strength of about 4,925 psi (standard deviation 432 psi), a mean modulus of rupture of 631 psi and a mean elastic modulus of 4,000,000 psi, values that are used in all rigid‑pavement design calculations [1]. The material serves as the primary structural component in precast paving systems and other rigid‑pavement applications—providing load‑bearing capacity comparable to conventional jointed plain or reinforced concrete while allowing rapid opening to traffic—and is limited to a maximum water‑cement ratio of 0.42 (target 0.40, not below 0.38), a cement factor between 530 lb/yd³ and 600 lb/yd³, and a placement air content of approximately 7 % ± 1.5 % [2].

Material purpose and applicability

Class AA Concrete is a high‑strength, durable Portland cement concrete mix that meets or exceeds the requirements set out in Publication 408, Specifications, Section 704 for Class AA Cement Concrete [1]. It is characterized by an average compressive strength of 4,925 pounds per square inch (standard deviation 432 psi) and a 28‑day mean modulus of rupture of 631 psi [1]. The material’s 28‑day mean elastic modulus is taken as 4,000,000 psi for use in rigid‑pavement design calculations [1]. Class AA Concrete also refers to the cement‑concrete mixes specified in Section 704 (AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving, AA Form Paving) that are required to achieve a minimum compressive strength of 500 psi, with placement air content about 7.0 % (±1.5 % for slip‑form paving and accelerated patching) [2]. For slip‑form paving, conventional paving and accelerated patching the cement factor shall not be less than 530 lb/yd³; it shall not exceed 600 lb/yd³ for slip‑form paving and conventional paving, but the maximum does not apply to accelerated patching, and for all Class AA concrete the maximum water‑cement ratio shall be 0.42 lb/lb with a target water‑cement ratio of 0.40 lb/lb that shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb [2]. The mix is used as the primary material in precast concrete paving systems, providing structural capacity comparable to conventional jointed plain or reinforced concrete pavements and thereby supporting the full range of traffic loading conditions while offering long‑term durability [1]. Widening or lane‑addition sections on rigid pavements should be designed using Class AA cement concrete unless otherwise allowed; full lane additions (≥ 10 feet) shall be designed to the same thickness as the original pavement, and partial widenings (< 10 feet) shall match the existing pavement type and depth [1]. The material is required for all uniform‑depth rigid pavements built since 1945 unless an exemption is granted [1]. In rigid pavement work such as slip‑form paving, conventional paving, accelerated patching and bonded concrete overlays the material provides the load‑bearing surface of the pavement system [2]. For a bonded concrete overlay on an asphalt‑surfaced pavement the Class AA mix is produced and delivered to form the new rigid surface; an additional quantity not to exceed 2 % of the computed overlay volume may be compensated for concrete used to replace unsound material removed during milling [2]. In thin bonded overlays the mix incorporates synthetic or steel macrofibers adjusted to achieve the design residual strength value (f150) and meets permeability criteria for low chloride ion penetration, thereby delivering a durable overlay [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements the mix must be reinforced with virgin polypropylene fibers meeting ASTM C1116 when the overlay thickness is less than 7 in., supplying the overlay layer and all work needed to construct it while pre‑overlay repairs and transition‑area concrete are excluded [2]. For partial‑depth repair of rigid pavements Class AA cement concrete may be supplied as a Modified or an Accelerated mix; it is placed after cleaning and bonding to fill saw cuts and damaged zones, then consolidated and finished so that it matches the surrounding pavement surface and wheel ruts while being kept out of existing joints, with placement permitted only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F and rising and discontinued if temperatures fall below that threshold [2]. The Accelerated variant is intended for patching utility casting blockouts and shall achieve a minimum target compressive strength of 1,500 psi at seven hours when tested according to PTM No. 604, providing rapid early strength for restoring pavement structure [2]. In Long‑Life Concrete Pavement projects the Class AA mix is the 28‑day cementitious material required to satisfy LLCP acceptance criteria; its quality index is calculated by comparing the average sublot strength to a reference of 3,300 psi [2].

Composition and characteristics

Class AA cement concrete is described as a high‑strength, durable normal‑weight Portland cement mix that is used where rapid opening to traffic within 24–48 hours is desired. When the design compressive strength is 3500 psi the calculated 28‑day elastic modulus is about 3.4 × 10⁶ psi, while AASHTO recommends adopting a mean modulus of 4 × 10⁶ psi that corresponds with a mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi unless local historical data show significantly different strengths. The Pennsylvania specifications require the mix to be proportioned as shown in Table A of Section 704 and to consist of Portland cement, Type A fine aggregate, Type A coarse aggregate of maximum size AASHTO No. 467, 57, 67 or 8, water, an air‑entraining admixture, and may include a water‑reducing, retarding admixture or supplementary cementitious material such as fly ash or slag cement. For AA slip‑form, form or accelerated paving the coarse aggregates must not exceed 3.0 % absorption for gravels and 2.0 % absorption for all other aggregates. When the mix is designated AAAP the water‑to‑cement ratio shall not be less than 0.40 lb/lb, while the overall maximum water‑cement ratio for any Class AA concrete is 0.42 lb/lb and a target value of 0.40 lb/lb shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb. Section 530 further requires that the water‑to‑cement ratio be recorded on the plant delivery ticket as between 0.37 and 0.42, and that plastic air content meet the limits set in Section 530.3(u)2 before placement. The cement factor for AA slip‑form paving, paving and accelerated patching shall be no less than 530 lb per cubic yard and not more than 600 lb per cubic yard, although the upper limit does not apply to accelerated patching. Fresh concrete must contain 7.0 % air ±1.5 % at placement, and Section 506 specifies that the average 28‑day air content for Class AA concrete shall fall between 5.0 % and 8.5 % for lot‑by‑lot acceptance. Acceptance also requires that the percentage weight‑loss for compressive strength be at least 55 %. For modified or accelerated partial‑depth repair the mix follows Section 704 but substitutes Type A No. 8 coarse aggregate and limits the water‑cement ratio to 0.42, with the accelerated variant required to achieve a minimum compressive strength of 1,500 psi after seven hours as tested per PTM No. 604. Section 506 requires that the average 28‑day compressive strength be compared to a baseline of 3,300 psi for Class AA concrete. When used as a thin bonded overlay on asphalt‑surfaced pavement the concrete is reinforced with synthetic or steel macrofibers; if the overlay depth is less than three inches No. 67 coarse aggregate replaces No. 57 and macrofiber is added at a rate to achieve the design residual strength value (f150) according to Table A, verified by ASTM C1609‑12 at 14 days. For unbonded overlays less than seven inches deep the concrete must contain 100 % virgin polypropylene synthetic fibers that are monofilament or bundled monofilament, sized between one and 2½ inches in length with a maximum aspect ratio of 150, added in sufficient quantity to reach a residual strength ratio (R150,3) of 20.0 percent, and the dosage may not exceed five pounds per cubic yard unless field testing demonstrates workability without fiber clumping. The mixture is also designed to meet a target chloride ion penetration classified as Low, Very low or Negligible after a curing period of fifty‑six days or less, with permeability not to exceed 2,800 coulombs on AASHTO T 277 or not to exceed 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4‑inch by 8‑inch cylinders and 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6‑inch by 12‑inch cylinders on AASHTO T 358. If the maturity method is employed, degree‑hours of cure are correlated to compressive strengths at intervals of eight hours, one day, seven days, twenty‑eight days and fifty‑six days in accordance with PTM No. 640. Using high early strength concrete is avoided because it may cause excessive shrinkage cracking. Accelerated concrete achieves opening‑to‑traffic strength in 4 to 7 hours, whereas standard pavement concrete with optimized aggregate gradation reaches the required strength in 24 to 48 hours. Precast pavement units are made from a high‑strength, durable mix that typically exceeds the Class AA Cement Concrete requirements, providing superior long‑term performance. When mix composition, source material, processing steps or proportioning cause fresh concrete to fall outside the specified target range, the consistency must stay within 1 ½ inches of the selected target slump and any exceedance of the upper slump limit requires rejection of that batch. If the average 14‑day AAAP compressive strength does not reach at least 3,500 psi, the contractor may discontinue field curing and must take corrective action before acceptance. Mix designs shall be based on the actual materials used, so a lower cement factor or substitution of Portland cement with slag cement (minimum 25 %), fly ash (minimum 15 %) or silica fume (5‑10 %) directly alters strength and durability targets. Air‑entraining admixture must be adjusted to obtain a plastic‑state air content of 6.0 % for general mixes and 7.0 % for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving. Aggregates may not exceed 3.0 % absorption for gravels or 2.0 % for other aggregates; higher‑absorption coarse aggregate is permissible only if mix‑design specimens survive 300 freeze‑thaw cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation or achieve a durability factor greater than 80. Changing the fine‑aggregate fineness modulus between 2.3 and 2.5 triggers an increase of the minimum cement factor by 47 lb per cubic yard for the class of concrete, thereby raising potential compressive strength. When part of the Portland cement is replaced by supplementary cementitious material, the water‑to‑cement‑plus‑SCM ratio must be used and the AAAP mix must maintain a minimum water‑cement ratio of 0.40 lb/lb. Trial mixes for Class AAAP, AAAP LW, ASC and all concrete paving mixtures are required to meet an overdesign requirement of the specified 28‑day compressive strength plus 500 psi. For slip‑form paving the cement factor is limited to between 530 lb/yd³ and 600 lb/yd³, the water‑cement ratio shall not exceed 0.42 and targets 0.40 but shall not fall below 0.38. The air content required is 7.0 % ± 1.5 % for slip‑form and accelerated patching. If Portland cement is partially replaced by fly ash or slag cement, at least 400 lb/yd³ of Portland cement must remain and the total cementitious content may not exceed the Table A limits; slag cement is capped at 30 % of the combined cementitious weight. Provide and place concrete with a W/C ratio between 0.37 and 0.42; any deviation that causes the verified ratio to fall outside ±0.03 of the ticket calculation disqualifies the lot from the W/C incentive and can render it defective. Plastic air results that are outside specification limits cause the truck load to be rejected before placement. Both the Modified and Accelerated versions of Class AA Cement Concrete require Coarse Aggregate Type A No. 8 and limit the water‑cement ratio to 0.42; the Accelerated mix must be designed to achieve a minimum target compressive strength of 1,500 psi at seven hours, indicating that tighter proportioning raises early strength. The specification mandates a minimum durability factor of 80 % after 300 cycles and a minimum initial set time of 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F; using approved aggregate extenders or low‑expansion index aggregates directly affects durability and setting performance. When the overlay depth is less than 3 inches, specifying No. 67 coarse aggregate rather than No. 57 changes the aggregate composition and influences the resulting strength and durability of Class AA concrete. Adding synthetic or steel macrofibers at a rate that meets the design residual strength value (f150) ties fiber volume proportioning to post‑crack performance, which is verified by running ASTM C1609‑12 at 14 days and relating residual strength to fiber content. The permeability requirement links mix composition and curing processing to durability, requiring the mixture to achieve a target chloride ion penetration classified as Low, Very low or Negligible after a 56‑day or less curing period, with an optional Moderate classification limited to not exceed 2,800 coulombs (AASHTO T 277) or the specified kΩ‑cm values (AASHTO T 358). Reinforcement with virgin polypropylene fibers of length between 1.0 inch and 2½ inches, aspect ratio up to 150, dosage must achieve a residual strength ratio (R150,3) of 20.0 percent and shall not exceed 5.0 lb/yd³ unless a field demonstration shows the mix remains workable and free of fiber clumping. Key material properties

The fresh‑mix physical properties that must be controlled for Class AA concrete include slump consistency within the target range, prescribed air‑content limits, and, for AAAP LW and AA LW mixes, measured density, with mix temperature also required to be monitored. The total air content in the hardened concrete must be between 3.5 % and 7.5 %, inclusive; for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving mixes the total air content must be between 4.5 % and 8.5 % inclusive. The equilibrium density for lightweight AA concrete must lie between 110 and 117 lb/ft³, targeting 115 lb/ft³. In Section 8.4, the performance of Class AA concrete in rigid pavement is described in terms of its compressive strength and resulting modulus of rupture, which together characterize its ability to resist crushing loads and extreme fiber tension at failure; for Class AA concrete in Pennsylvania the average 28‑day compressive strength is 4,925 psi with a standard deviation of 432 psi, yielding a calculated modulus of rupture of 631 psi, and this value should be used in all rigid pavement designs unless District experience and data indicates otherwise. The mean concrete elastic modulus for Class AA concrete is recommended to be taken as 4,000,000 psi, which corresponds with a mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi. When a design compressive strength of 3500 psi is used, the ACI correlation Ec = 57,000 (f’c)^0.5 yields an elastic modulus of approximately 3.4 × 10⁶ psi, illustrating how variations in compressive strength directly affect stiffness. Mechanical performance is assessed by compressive‑strength testing of quality‑control cylinders at early ages—3‑day or 7‑day, at 14 days for AAAP mixes, and at 28 days (and 56 days when specified). Overdesign compressive strength must be at least 1,000 psi, except for AAAP, AAAP LW, AA Paving and ASC which are limited to a minimum of 500 psi. The minimum water‑cement ratio for AAAP is 0.40 lb/lb; the target water‑cement ratio for all Class AA concrete is 0.40 lb/lb and shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb, with an absolute maximum of 0.42 lb/lb. Accelerated mixes shall have a minimum target compressive strength of 1,500 psi at 7 hours. For overlays less than 7 inches thick the mechanical contribution of polypropylene fibers must be added to yield a residual strength ratio (R150,3) of 20.0 percent; fiber dosage shall not exceed 5.0 pounds per cubic yard unless field testing shows acceptable workability and no clumping. Residual tensile strength (f150) is required through appropriate macro‑fiber dosage and verified by ASTM C1609‑12 testing at 14 days. Shrinkage shall not exceed 500 microstrain (or 550 microstrain with approval). Plastic air content must lie between 5.0 % and 8.5 %. Air‑entraining admixtures are specified to support aggregates meeting 300 freeze‑thaw cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80 percent. A durability factor of at least 80 percent after 300 freeze‑thaw cycles per ASTM C666 is required for accelerated concrete. Target chloride ion penetration shall be classified as Low, Very Low or Negligible after up to 56 days and shall not exceed 2,800 coulombs or the minimum resistivity values of 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4‑by‑8 in. cylinders or 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6‑by‑12 in. cylinders. Aggregate absorption shall not exceed 3.0 % for gravels and 2.0 % for all other aggregates. Reactive aggregates may only be used if a cement‑supplementary combination reduces the 14‑day expansion to less than 0.1 %; any blended hydraulic cement must limit mortar expansion to 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at 8 weeks. Slag cement shall not exceed 30 % of total cementitious material by weight. Temperature immediately adjacent to field‑cured cylinders must be maintained in the range of 60°F to 80°F for the first 24 ± 2 hours, with a digital system recording temperature from 30°F to 120°F with an accuracy of 1°F. Placement is permitted only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40°F or higher and rising; repairs must be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours. Insulation or heating shall be provided if ambient temperature drops below 80°F during curing, and hourly monitoring must ensure that the repair area does not experience a curing temperature change in excess of 40°F within any one‑hour period; exceeding this change renders the work defective. The maturity method may be used to correlate degree‑hours of cure to compressive strengths at 8 hours, 1, 7, 28 and 56 days, thereby incorporating thermal history. [1][2]

Class AA cement concrete is evaluated through a series of laboratory and field tests that verify its compressive strength, stiffness, and other performance criteria. In Section 4.2 the properties of Class AA concrete are verified by testing its compressive strength against the minimum values set out in Publication 408, Specifications, Section 501, and the mix is classified as Class AA when it meets or exceeds the performance criteria defined in Publication 408, Section 704 for Class AA cement concrete; precast paving units are designed to be high‑strength and durable mixtures that typically exceed those Section 704 requirements [1]. The 28‑day mean modulus of rupture is characterized by first determining the average compressive strength—reported for Pennsylvania specimens as 4,925 psi with a standard deviation of 432 psi—and then applying the empirical relation S′c = K·(f′c)^0.5 where K equals 9; this calculation yields a modulus of rupture of 631 psi, and that value is to be used in all rigid‑pavement designs unless district experience suggests otherwise [1]. The stiffness of Class AA concrete is quantified by its 28‑day elastic modulus, which is derived from the measured 28‑day compressive strength using the ACI correlation Ec = 57,000 (f′c)^0.5; when a structural design compressive strength of 3,500 psi is used, the equation gives Ec = 3.4 × 10^6 psi, but the AASHTO procedure adopts a mean Ec of 4,000,000 psi that corresponds to a mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi, and unless district historical data show mean compressive strengths significantly different than 4,925 psi, that mean value is used in all rigid‑pavement designs [1]. In Section 704—CEMENT CONCRETE, subsection (d) the field evaluation begins with fresh‑mix tests on the first three consecutive trucks after a start or stoppage; slump is measured according to AASHTO T 119 and must remain within 1½ inches of the selected target range, any exceedance of the upper limit requiring rejection; air content is determined by AASHTO T 152 or T 196 without an aggregate correction factor; density for AAAP LW and AA LW mixes is measured by AASHTO T 121; and temperature is recorded using ASTM C1064 [2]. Subsequent quality‑control testing includes 3‑day or 7‑day compressive‑strength tests, a 14‑day test for AAAP mixes, and 28‑day compressive‑strength tests on molded cylinders performed in accordance with PTM No. 611; when the maturity method is employed calibrated sensors that meet PTM No. 640 are provided to estimate strength [2]. The design basis requires mix designs to be computed per ACI 211 using aggregate gradations from PTM 528 or PTM 529; trial mixes are cured for compressive‑strength testing where the Department may accept 7‑day results (or 3‑day results for high early strength) together with 28‑day tests, and the design strength shall be a minimum of 1,000 psi except for AAAP, AAAP LW, AA Paving and ASC which shall be 500 psi [2]. Plastic‑state air content must fall within ±1.5 % of the target (6.0 % for general mixes, 7.0 % for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving) as measured by AASHTO T152 or T196, while hardened concrete air content shall be between 3.5 % and 7.5 % (or 4.5 %–8.5 % for those same mixes) and may be verified by ASTM C457 [2]. Behavior and mechanisms

Under loading, the 28‑day mean tensile stress capacity of Class AA concrete is 631 psi as derived from its average compressive strength, and this value should be used in all rigid pavement designs unless district experience indicates otherwise. The rigidity of Class AA paving concrete under load is characterized by a 28‑day mean elastic modulus of 4,000,000 psi, which corresponds to a mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi and serves as the basis for structural analysis. For general design, Class AA cement concrete is required to achieve an overdesign compressive strength of at least 1,000 psi, except that mixes designated AAAP, AAAP LW, AA Paving and ASC are limited to 500 psi. Accelerated mixes intended for early loading must develop a minimum target value compressive strength of 1,500 psi at seven hours, and all mix designs must exceed the 28‑day design strength by at least 500 psi. For partial‑depth repair applications the material also must have a minimum target value of 1,500 psi at seven hours and is limited to a maximum water‑cement ratio of 0.42. The plastic‑state air content shall be 6.0 % (7.0 % for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving), and the total air content in the hardened concrete must be between 3.5 % and 7.5 %, inclusive (or between 4.5 % and 8.5 % for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving). Shrinkage is constrained to a maximum of 500 microstrain (up to 550 microstrain if approved), and for slip‑form and form paving not more than 0.05 % after the prescribed air‑storage method. Durability against moisture and temperature variations is addressed by limiting aggregate absorption and requiring that specimens satisfy 300 freeze‑thaw cycles according to ASTM C666 with less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80; the same minimum durability factor of 80 % at 300 cycles, tested in accordance with ASTM C666 (procedure B) with and without aggregate extenders, is required for partial‑depth repair material. A minimum water‑cement ratio of 0.40 for AAAP mixes containing supplementary cementitious material helps control moisture effects, while the maximum water‑cement ratio for repair mixes is limited to 0.42. Chloride ion penetration performance shall meet a target of Low, Very Low or Negligible after 56 days, not to exceed 2,800 coulombs, and the resistivity must be greater than or equal to 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑inch by 8‑inch cylinders and 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑inch by 12‑inch cylinders. Chemical exposure to sulfates is addressed by designing sulfate‑resistant concrete in accordance with ACI 201.2. Temperature‑change effects are indicated by a minimum initial set time of 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F, as tested according to AASHTO T 131, demonstrating acceptable performance under temperature variations. Accelerated concrete formulations can attain sufficient compressive strength for traffic opening in 4 to 7 hours, reflecting rapid chemical hardening. Material interactions and compatibility

Class AA concrete mix designs are required to be based on the actual cement, supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs), air‑entraining admixture, and aggregate properties that will be used in the project; therefore the proportions of binder and aggregates are adjusted to meet specified performance limits. The cement factor must include at least one SCM—slag cement at a minimum of 25 % , fly ash at a minimum of 15 % or silica fume between 5 %-10 %—and any replacement cannot exceed the maximum mass percentages listed, ensuring proper interaction between binder and aggregate. Air content is controlled by adding an air‑entraining admixture to achieve 7.0 % air in the plastic state for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving mixes, which influences both workability and durability. When lightweight aggregates are used, they must be stored and batched in a saturated condition above SSD so that their absorption does not upset the paste demand or cause unintended moisture variations. The coarse and fine aggregates are limited to a maximum absorption of 3.0 % for gravels and 2.0 % for all other aggregates; when higher‑absorption aggregate is used, the mix must still satisfy the freeze‑thaw durability requirement of 300 cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80. The batch weight of each aggregate is adjusted to offset the free water it contains, ensuring that the effective water‑to‑cement (or cement‑plus‑SCM) ratio remains within the specified limits, such as the minimum 0.40 water‑cement ratio for AAAP mixes, and if a portion of the cement is replaced by SCM the water‑to‑(cement + SCM) ratio is used to control paste demand and strength development. Durable stone or gravel must be washed before mixing, providing a clean aggregate surface for bonding; aggregates that are reactive with a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % may be used only when an approved cement‑SCM blend reduces the expansion to less than 0.1 %, while any aggregate expanding more than 0.30 % is rejected. For Class AA paving, Portland cement may be replaced with fly ash or slag cement provided that at least 400 lb/yd³ of Portland cement remains and total cementitious content stays within Table A limits, with slag cement limited to not exceed 30 % of the total cementitious material by weight, and the mix must achieve a target water‑cement ratio of 0.40 lb/lb that will not fall below 0.38 lb/lb for all Class AA concrete. In partial‑depth repair, Class AA cement concrete must be bonded to the existing pavement with a thin layer of grout or epoxy bonding compound applied immediately after final cleaning; the new concrete is placed before the grout dries or while the epoxy remains tacky, and if the bonding agent has hardened it must be removed, the surface re‑cleaned and fresh grout or epoxy reapplied. Placement is permitted only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F and rising, work must be stopped if temperatures fall below 40 °F, and a minimum of 24 hours protection from freezing is required; after placement the concrete is consolidated with a spud vibrator, worked into saw cuts, and expansion‑joint material together with preformed cellular polystyrene forms are used to keep the repair out of existing joints. For partial‑depth repair mixes, Class AA cement concrete must be mixed with Type A No. 8 coarse aggregate and cannot exceed a water‑cement ratio of 0.42, which limits paste demand and controls aggregate absorption effects; when the repair depth is greater than two inches the mix may incorporate aggregate extenders supplied with the rapid‑set patching material, otherwise only AASHTO No. 8 coarse aggregate having an expansion index less than 0.10 may be added to maintain compatibility with the cement matrix and prevent excessive expansion, and the accelerated version of Class AA concrete is required to achieve a minimum compressive strength of 1,500 psi at seven hours, reflecting the interaction between binder reactivity and the selected aggregates. All associated materials such as epoxy bonding compound, grout, curing compounds, and joint sealants must be placed or applied within the manufacturers’ specified time windows and temperature limits to preserve bond integrity with the concrete. In thin bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavement, Class AA paving cement concrete is required to incorporate macrofibers at a rate that achieves the specified residual strength (f150), and its residual strength is evaluated against fiber volume fraction using ASTM C1609‑12 after 14 days, indicating a direct fiber‑matrix interaction; when the overlay thickness is less than three inches the mix must use No. 67 coarse aggregate rather than No. 57, showing that aggregate selection depends on geometric constraints and influences paste demand, and the mix design also has to satisfy permeability criteria, targeting a low, very low or negligible chloride ion penetration after 56 days with limits of not more than 2,800 coulombs by AASHTO T 277 or not more than 15.6 kΩ‑cm (4×8 in.) and 12.3 kΩ‑cm (6×12 in.) by AASHTO T 358, reflecting the interaction between cement paste chemistry and durability requirements. For unbonded concrete overlays whose thickness is less than seven inches, Class AA cement concrete must be mixed with polypropylene fibers that are 100 % virgin polyethylene, have a length between 1.0 inch and 2½ inches, an aspect ratio no greater than 150, and are added in enough quantity to reach a residual strength ratio of 20.0 percent; the total fiber dosage may not exceed 5.0 pounds per cubic yard unless a field demonstration proves that the mix remains workable and free of clumping. Influence of production and placement

Mix design choices for Class AA Concrete influence durability: the use of high‑early‑strength (HES) concrete should be avoided because it may cause excessive shrinkage cracking, whereas standard pavement concrete meets strength requirements over a longer period [1]. Accelerated concrete can achieve traffic‑opening strength in four to seven hours, while conventional mixes typically require twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours before opening to traffic [1]. Mixing practices must keep the fresh concrete consistency within 1 ½ inches of the selected target slump value and satisfy the specified air‑content and temperature limits; if these limits are exceeded the batch shall be rejected or additional quality‑control cylinders made, which directly influences the compressive strength used for acceptance [2]. The water‑to‑cementitious ratio shall be between 0.37 and 0.42 and plastic air content must remain within the prescribed limits; failure to meet these limits can cause the lot to fail the compressive‑strength quality index [2]. At the start of construction a full‑sized batch shall be mixed using the type of mixer and the mixing procedure planned for the project, and trial mixtures shall be made for each class of concrete with test specimens molded and cured to provide the basis for achieving the required slump, air content and compressive strength that govern durability [2]. If the overlay depth is less than three inches, No. 67 coarse aggregate must replace No. 57, which influences gradation and strength potential, and macrofibers shall be added at the rate required to achieve the design residual strength value (f150) [2].

Placement quality is critical; poor placement of patches does not provide the uniform support needed and leads to performance problems [1]. For precast units incidental bedding materials may require only minimal field curing before the slab can be opened to traffic [1]. Slump, air, density (for AAAP LW and AA LW) and temperature tests shall be performed on the first three consecutive trucks at the beginning of placement operations; material control is established when all test results for those three trucks are within the established action points, after which testing may be reduced to a minimum of one test per 50 cubic yards [2]. Concrete in partial‑depth repair must be placed before grout begins to dry or while epoxy bonding compound is still tacky, and placement shall occur only when ambient air and surface temperatures are forty degrees Fahrenheit and rising; placement must be discontinued if temperatures fall below forty degrees Fahrenheit, and all repairs shall be protected from freezing for a minimum of twenty‑four hours [2]. Placement must produce an average core depth no less than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch, and rain or improper joint construction shall be avoided because surface defects from those causes render the pavement defective; insufficient compaction would cause sublot cores to fall below this allowable limit, so adequate consolidation is implicit in meeting the depth tolerance [2].

Compaction of Class AA concrete shall be achieved by using a spud vibrator or any other acceptable method to eliminate voids [2].

Finishing must be performed by ACI‑ or NRMCA‑certified flatwork finishers to maintain surface quality that underlies durability [2]. Water or other finishing aids shall not be added to the surface, and any improper final finish is listed as a defect that can disqualify the lot [2].

Curing tanks conforming to PTM No. 611 shall be provided and must maintain the temperature immediately adjacent to field‑cured cylinders in the range of sixty degrees Fahrenheit to eighty degrees Fahrenheit for the first twenty‑four ± 2 hours, after which field curing continues according to PTM No. 611 until early‑strength criteria are met [2]. Specimens shall be wet‑cured for fourteen days before beginning the twenty‑eight‑day shrinkage testing, and the concrete mixture shall be designed to meet a target chloride ion penetration of Low, Very Low, or Negligible after a fifty‑six‑day or less curing period; these controls affect permeability, shrinkage and the twenty‑eight‑day to seven‑day strength ratio needed for long‑term performance [2]. Improper curing procedures are enumerated as surface defects that lead to a defective classification and can prevent the twenty‑eight‑day compressive strength from reaching the required PWL of at least fifty‑five percent [2]. When the maturity method is used, degree‑hours of cure shall be correlated to compressive strengths according to PTM No. 640 at time intervals of eight hours, one day, seven days, twenty‑eight days and fifty‑six days, linking the curing schedule directly to strength development [2]. A white membrane‑forming curing compound shall be applied with an atomizing spray tank having an adjustable nozzle, the solution must be agitated before each application, insulation or heating shall be provided if ambient temperature drops below eighty degrees Fahrenheit, and the curing temperature must be monitored at least hourly to ensure no one‑hour change exceeds forty degrees Fahrenheit; failure to meet these requirements renders the work defective [2].

Long‑term performance of Class AA cement concrete pavement is most sensitive to construction variations that compromise load transfer and induce cracking [1]. When dowels are not bonded throughout their entire embedded length, transverse joint load transfer is lost, leading to premature distress [1]. The use of high early strength (HES) concrete should be avoided because it can cause excessive shrinkage cracking [1]. Tie bars or tie bolts are required only for patches longer than 65 feet; movements of these devices in partially set concrete can degrade short‑patch performance [1]. Poor work quality in placing the concrete patch—such as failing to remove crushed joints or uneven compaction—prevents a uniform supporting layer and accelerates deterioration [1]. Under Section 704.1(d) any construction variation that causes the slump to exceed the specified upper limits, produces air‑content or temperature readings outside the prescribed ranges during placement, or fails to keep the curing temperature between 60 °F and 80 °F for the first 24 ± 2 hours must be rejected [2]. If the average 3‑day or 7‑day compressive strength does not meet at least the minimum mix‑design value, or if the average 28‑day compressive strength falls short of the Table A minimum, additional quality‑control cylinders or corrective investigation are required [2]. Section 704.1(c) indicates that long‑term performance is compromised when the hardened concrete contains an air content outside 3.5 %–7.5 % (or 4.5 %–8.5 % for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving), a spacing factor greater than about 0.008 in., a specific surface below about 630 in²/in³, chloride ion penetration exceeding the Low/Very Low targets or more than 2,800 coulombs in AASHTO T 277, or shrinkage that surpasses 500 microstrain (or 0.05 % for slip‑form and form paving) unless a higher allowance is approved [2]. Section 545.2 specifies that raising the water‑cement ratio above the maximum of 0.42 lb/lb or allowing it to fall below the target floor of 0.38 lb/lb can degrade strength and durability, while an air content outside the design value of 7.0 % ± 1.5 % reduces freeze‑thaw resistance [2]. The use of non‑durable stone or gravel with slate texture, unwashed aggregates, any blast‑furnace‑slag aggregate, or reactive aggregates whose 14‑day expansion exceeds 0.30 % (or is not mitigated to less than 0.1 % by an approved cement‑supplementary blend) also compromises long‑term performance [2]. A cementitious mix that contains less than the required 400 lb/yd³ Portland cement or more than 30 % slag cement likewise degrades durability [2]. Section 545.4 treats core‑tested deficiencies—particularly an installed thickness short by more than ¼ inch—and the presence of defective pavement left in place as critical defects affecting acceptance and durability of the bonded concrete overlay [2]. In partial‑depth repair work, Section 525.3(f) requires that grout or epoxy bonding compound not be allowed to run or puddle in low spots, that repair concrete be placed before grout has dried or epoxy has hardened, that placement be avoided when ambient air and surface temperatures are below 40 °F, and that the repair be protected from freezing for at least 24 hours [2]. Section 525.2 adds that a water‑cement ratio above 0.42, a durability factor below 80 % after 300 cycles, an initial set time less than the required 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F, omission of prescribed aggregate extenders when repair depth exceeds two inches, placement of grout later than 60 minutes after mixing, or application of epoxy beyond its shelf life or outside manufacturer’s pot‑life and temperature limits can adversely affect long‑term behavior [2]. Section 525.3(g) further states that a change in curing temperature exceeding 40 °F within any one‑hour period renders the work defective, that insulation or heating must be provided if ambient air drops below 80 °F during curing, and that any repaired area that becomes delaminated, cracked, spalled or otherwise delaminated is considered defective and must be removed and replaced at no cost to the Department [2]. Section 530.3(t) lists defective‑work conditions most strongly affecting long‑term performance, including an average core depth less than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch (or any sublot core depth similarly deficient), a compressive‑strength quality index (PWL) under 55 %, surface problems such as rain damage, improper joint construction, improper curing procedures, improper final finish, cracking or honeycombing that cannot be satisfactorily repaired, depressions or high points exceeding the tolerances of Section 506.3(o) and not correctable, and edge‑slumping that fails to meet the requirements of Section 501.3(g) [2]. Section 506.3(u) reinforces that variations in slab depth, compressive strength and air content most affect durability; a lot is rejected if the average core depth is not more than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch or any sublot core depth falls below that limit, if the 28‑day compressive strength is lower than 3,300 psi as reflected in the Q_L calculation, or if air content lies outside the 5.0 %–8.5 % limits (expressed through Q_L and Q_U) especially when less than 55 % of the material meets those limits [2].

Selection, specification, and improvement

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, Class AA cement concrete serves as the default material for widening and lane‑addition projects on rigid pavements, but its use is limited to uniform‑depth rigid pavements constructed since 1945, whether or not they have been overlaid [1]. The same manual requires that full lane additions that are ten feet wide or greater retain the original pavement thickness unless heavier traffic loads dictate a different design, in which case the design shall reflect the actual traffic [1]. For partial widenings less than ten feet wide, the existing pavement type and depth must be matched [1]. When the existing structure is jointed plain cement pavement, the widening must be plain cement concrete; for jointed reinforced cement pavement either reinforced or plain cement concrete may be used [1]. Transverse joint spacing shall follow the original rules: if the original spacing is twenty feet or less the new slabs keep the same length, and if it exceeds twenty feet the joint spacing shall be adjusted to a minimum of ten feet and a maximum of seventeen feet with intermediate joints as needed [1]. Any deviation from these criteria requires an engineering‑judgment exemption supported by documentation [1]. The material specification in the manual is tied to compressive‑strength‑based properties; Class AA concrete in Pennsylvania has an average compressive strength of 4,925 psi with a standard deviation of 432 psi, which generates a 28‑day mean modulus of rupture of 631 psi that shall be used for all rigid pavement designs unless district experience indicates otherwise [1]. The design procedure also recommends using a mean concrete elastic modulus of 4,000,000 psi, corresponding to the mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi; this value should be applied unless district historical data show mean compressive strengths significantly different from 4,925 psi, in which case the elastic modulus must be adjusted accordingly [1]. The Department of Transportation specifications state that a Class AA mix design shall be prepared in accordance with ACI 211 and shall employ an aggregate gradation that follows PTM 528 or PTM 529 as required for the pavement type (section 704.1(c) Design Basis) [2]. The overdesign compressive strength shall be at least 1,000 psi, except for AAAP, AAAP LW, AA Paving and ASC where the minimum is 500 psi (section 704.1(c)) [2]. For accelerated acceptance the mix shall be capable of attaining a target compressive strength of 1,500 psi at seven hours when tested according to PTM No. 604 (sections 704.1 General and 525.2 Material) [2]. The 28‑day strength must exceed the required value by 500 psi for all Class AA paving mixtures; for AAAP mixes the average 14‑day compressive strength shall be greater than or equal to 3,500 psi and the mix must meet the 28‑day minimum strength shown in Table A before acceptance (section 704.1(d) Testing and Acceptance) [2]. When used on long‑life pavements the slab core depth shall be greater than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch and the compressive‑strength quality index must achieve at least a 55 % pass‑weight‑limit based on 28‑day test results; additionally, for a lot of Class AA concrete the average core depth must be more than the design thickness (D) minus 0.5 inch and no individual sublot may have a core depth less than D − 0.5 inch, each sublot must produce a 28‑day compressive strength whose lower quality index meets the required limit, and the percentage of plastic air content is accepted only when the percent within limits (PWL) is 55 % or greater after evaluating both a lower quality index ((average – 5.0 %) / s) and an upper quality index ((8.5 % – average) / s) (section 530.3 Construction) [2]. The mix shall contain Portland cement, Type A fine aggregate, and a Type A coarse aggregate not exceeding the AASHTO sizes listed; gravel absorption may not exceed 3.0 % and other aggregates may not exceed 2.0 % unless higher‑absorption material passes a freeze‑thaw durability test of 300 cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation or yields a durability factor greater than 80 (section 704.1 General) [2]. Only durable stone or gravel aggregates that are washed free of slate texture or cleavage planes may be used; blast‑furnace‑slag aggregate is prohibited, and reactive aggregates showing 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % require an approved cement‑supplementary combination to reduce expansion below 0.1 % (section 545.2 Material) [2]. The cement factor shall not be less than the minimum of 530 lb/yd³ for AA slip‑form paving, paving and accelerated patching, and shall not exceed the maximum of 600 lb/yd³ for slip‑form paving and paving (section 545.2 Material); adjustments to the cement factor up to 690 lb/yd³ are permitted only with DME/DMM approval when test data demonstrate adequate performance (section 704.1 General) [2]. When supplementary cementitious material is used, at least 400 lb/yd³ of Portland cement must be retained; slag cement may constitute no more than 30 % of the total by weight, and the total cementitious content may not exceed the limits in Table A (section 545.2 Material) [2]. The water‑cement ratio for all Class AA concrete shall be capped at 0.42 lb/lb, with a target of 0.40 lb/lb and never dropping below 0.38 lb/lb (section 545.2 Material); for long‑life pavements the acceptable water‑to‑cementitious material ratio is between 0.37 and 0.42, and the calculated ratio must agree with laboratory verification within ±0.03 (section 530.3 Construction) [2]. The plastic‑state air content shall be designed for 6 % for most mixes and 7 % for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving (section 704.1(c)); for slip‑form paving and accelerated patching the air content at placement must be 7.0 % ± 1.5 % (section 545.2 Material) [2]. The hardened concrete shall meet a chloride‑ion penetration target of Low, Very Low or Negligible after 56 days as defined by AASHTO T277/T358; a Moderate rating may be accepted only if the measured charge does not exceed 2,800 coulombs or the resistivity limits specified in T 358 (section 541.2 Material) [2]. Shrinkage shall not exceed 500 microstrain unless a higher limit is approved (section 704.1(c)) [2]. For thin bonded overlays with a thickness less than three inches, No. 67 coarse aggregate must replace No. 57, and macrofibers shall be added in an amount that achieves the design residual strength f150 as determined by ASTM C1609‑12 testing at 14 days (section 541.2 Material) [2]. A comprehensive quality‑control program is required; a Department‑reviewed QC plan and field‑operation QC plan must be submitted and approved, a trained concrete technician and certified flatwork finishers assigned, and calibrated testing equipment provided (section 704.1(d)) [2]. The mix slump shall be maintained within 1½ inches of the target range and may not exceed the specified upper limit; air‑content, temperature and density for AA mixes shall be tested on the first three trucks and thereafter at least one test per 50 cubic yards or every 30 minutes, whichever is greater (section 704.1(d)); combined lot sizes shall not exceed 100 cubic yards (section 704.1(d)) [2]. For partial‑depth repair work concrete may be placed only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F and rising; placement must stop if they fall below 40 °F, the fresh concrete must be placed before grout dries or epoxy loses tackiness, and the repaired area must be protected from freezing for at least 24 hours (section 525.3 Construction) [2]. The return‑to‑service time influences whether a Modified or Accelerated mix is selected for the final repair before traffic reopens (section 525.3 Construction) [2]. Class AA concrete may be used only where the plans indicate or direct its use and is prohibited for long‑term lane closures except when the closure serves local traffic (section 506.3 Acceptance) [2]. Performance evaluation and implications

The laboratory evaluation for Class AA concrete in Pennsylvania relies on the 28‑day mean PCC modulus of rupture, which is derived from an average compressive strength of 4,925 psi (standard deviation 432 psi) and calculated as S′c = 631 psi; this modulus of rupture value is prescribed for use in all rigid pavement designs unless district experience and data indicate otherwise. For Class AA Cement Concrete, Section 704 specifies a laboratory program that includes slump measurement in accordance with AASHTO T 119, compressive‑strength determination by PTM No. 604 or by the maturity‑meter method PTM No. 640, early‑strength testing at three days for high‑early mixes or at seven days for standard mixes, a fourteen‑day test for AAAP mixes, the standard twenty‑eight‑day mix‑design strength, and, where required, fifty‑six‑day cylinder testing for AAAP and Prevention Level Z mixes; acceptance of a lot is based on the average twenty‑eight‑day cylinder strength meeting the specified minimum. The Representative forwards three QA cylinders to an independent laboratory for twenty‑eight‑day compressive‑strength testing according to PTM No. 604 and hardened‑air‑content analysis per PTM No. 623, providing additional long‑term performance evidence. If the hardened concrete exhibits deficiencies or the Representative suspects deficiencies, air‑void quality may be evaluated by ASTM C457. Permeability is assessed after a curing period of fifty‑six days or less using AASHTO T 277 with a limit not to exceed two thousand eight hundred coulombs, or AASHTO T 358 with limits not to exceed fifteen point six kilohm‑centimetre for four‑by‑eight‑inch cylinders and twelve point three kilohm‑centimetre for six‑by‑twelve‑inch cylinders. Shrinkage is measured according to ASTM C157 and the twenty‑eight‑day microstrain shall not exceed five hundred unless approved up to five hundred fifty. Freeze‑thaw durability is verified by subjecting mix‑design specimens to three hundred cycles per ASTM C666 with less than zero point zero four percent dilation and a durability factor greater than eighty. Initial set time may not be less than fifteen minutes at seventy‑five degrees Fahrenheit plus or minus two degrees as measured by AASHTO T 131. Water‑cement ratio is verified in the field using batch tickets for cementitious content determination and AASHTO T 318‑02 for moisture, with a W/C between zero point three seven and zero point four two within plus or minus zero point zero three of the ticket calculation. Potentially reactive aggregate must have a fourteen‑day expansion less than zero point one percent after mitigation; aggregates with expansion greater than zero point three percent are rejected, and any blended hydraulic cement used may not exceed mortar expansions of zero point zero two percent at fourteen days and zero point zero six percent at eight weeks. Field measurements also include slump, air content, and density taken on the first trucks and at least once per fifty cubic yards or every thirty minutes for slip‑form or form paving using calibrated meters and cones, and water‑cement ratio verification as described above. In Section 506 acceptance is based on field core checks and laboratory testing; for each lot the Representative verifies that the average core depth exceeds the design thickness minus zero point five inch and that no individual sublot core is less than that limit, and plastic concrete samples from each sublot are molded into acceptance cylinders and tested in two cylinders per PTM No. 604 to obtain a twenty‑eight‑day compressive strength while a separate sample is tested for percentage of air content according to AASHTO T 152 or T 196. The mean values are entered into quality‑index equations that compare the mean strength with three thousand three hundred psi and the mean air content with lower and upper limits of five point zero percent and eight point five percent, respectively; acceptance requires a PWL of at least fifty‑five percent for air content. Long‑term performance evidence includes repeat twenty‑eight‑day compressive‑strength testing on independent laboratory cylinders, a twenty‑eight‑day to seven‑day strength ratio of at least one point two zero, overdesign strengths of one thousand psi except for AAAP, AAAP LW, AA Paving and ASC which are five hundred psi, durability factor requirements after three hundred freeze‑thaw cycles greater than eighty percent, permeability limits as noted, shrinkage microstrain limits as noted, reactive‑aggregate expansion mitigation as noted, and blended cement mortar expansion limits as noted. For unbonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 548) the mix must achieve a residual strength ratio (R150,3) of twenty point zero percent measured by the Illinois Modified ASTM C1609, and synthetic fiber dosage may not exceed five pounds per cubic yard unless a field demonstration shows that the mixture remains workable and fiber clumping does not occur. For bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavement (Section 540) evaluation may rely on the maturity method, correlating degree‑hours of cure to compressive strength in accordance with PTM No. 640 at eight hours, one day, seven days, twenty‑eight days and fifty‑six days, thereby supplying laboratory or field evidence of early and longer‑term performance. Class AA cement concrete is defined as a high‑strength, durable mixture that exceeds the requirements of Publication 408 Section 704, allowing precast concrete paving systems built with the material to be designed to carry the same traffic loads as conventional cast‑in‑place PCC while offering superior durability[1]. The elevated strength permits thinner or longer slabs and reduces the risk of excessive shrinkage cracking associated with high early‑strength mixes, thereby improving long‑term pavement performance and lowering maintenance needs[1]. Because the slabs are fabricated off‑site, use of Class AA concrete shortens construction time, enables rapid opening to traffic, creates a safer work zone, and lessens environmental and economic impacts on travelers and adjacent communities[1]. The 28‑day mean modulus of rupture for Class AA concrete is taken as 631 psi and is prescribed for use in all rigid pavement designs unless district experience indicates otherwise; this tensile strength parameter directly enters the structural design calculations that determine slab thickness, reinforcement and joint spacing, influencing the ability of the pavement to resist cracking under service loads[1]. The elastic modulus (Ec) quantifies stiffness; a mean Ec of 4,000,000 psi corresponding with an average compressive strength of 4,925 psi is used in all rigid pavement designs unless district data show significantly different strengths, and it governs how a slab will respond to traffic loads, affecting overall performance and durability[1]. If a lower 28‑day design compressive strength such as 3,500 psi is applied, the calculated Ec becomes 3.4 × 10⁶ psi, indicating a less stiff slab that would affect load distribution and potentially reduce service life[1]. Under Section 704—Cement Concrete, the design of Class AA concrete is governed by strength, air‑entrainment, permeability and shrinkage limits that shape pavement performance; overdesign strengths shall be at least 1,000 psi except for AAAP, AAAP LW, AA paving and ASC where the minimum is 500 psi, and the average 14‑day QC compressive‑strength test result must be greater than or equal to 3,500 psi for AAAP[2]. Mixes for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving shall contain about 7 % air in the plastic state and shall achieve a hardened‑concrete total air content between 4.5 % and 8.5 %, while Section 506 requires assessment of air‑content values with lower and upper limits of 5.0 % and 8.5 % respectively[2]. The 28‑day to 7‑day compressive‑strength ratio shall be greater than or equal to 1.20, and accelerated cement concrete shall have a target compressive strength of 1,500 psi at 7 hours when tested according to PTM No. 604[2]. The maximum water‑cement ratio for all Class AA concrete shall not exceed 0.42 lb/lb; the target ratio is 0.40 lb/lb and shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb, with a minimum water‑cement ratio of 0.40 lb/lb set for AAAP[2]. Cement factor shall be at least 530 lb/yd³ for AA Slip Form Paving, paving and accelerated patching and shall not exceed 600 lb/yd³ for slip‑form and regular paving[2]. Aggregate absorption shall not exceed 3.0 % for gravels and 2.0 % for all other aggregates; higher‑absorption coarse aggregate may be permitted only when mix‑design specimens satisfy 300 freeze‑thaw cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80[2]. The material shall attain a target chloride‑ion penetration classified as Low, Very Low or Negligible after ≤56 days and shall not exceed 2,800 coulombs (AASHTO T 277) or the resistivity limits of 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4×8 in cylinders and 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6×12 in cylinders (AASHTO T 358)[2]. When synthetic or steel macrofibers are used, they shall be added at a rate that achieves the design residual strength value f150 as determined by ASTM C1609‑12 testing at 14 days; fiber dosage shall not exceed 5.0 lb/yd³ unless a field demonstration shows the mixture remains workable without clumping[2]. Section 506 requires evaluation of Class AA concrete for 28‑day compressive strength using a quality‑index expression whose lower limit is 3,300 psi, and acceptance is based on adequacy and uniformity of strength together with an air‑content quality index having lower and upper limits of 5.0 % and 8.5 %; the pavement‑wide limit (PWL) shall be 55 % or greater, and any lot whose PWL for compressive strength is less than 55 % is considered defective[2]. Section 548 specifies that overlays thinner than 7 inches shall use Class AA cement concrete reinforced with 100 % virgin polypropylene fibers and shall achieve a residual strength ratio of 20.0 percent; the fiber dosage shall not exceed 5.0 lb/yd³ unless a manufacturer demonstration proves acceptable workability[2]. Section 525 governs partial‑depth repair with Class AA cement concrete: the existing surface must be cleaned and coated with grout or epoxy bonding compound before placement, and placement may occur only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F and rising; if temperature falls below 40 °F the work must be discontinued and the repair protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours[2]. A white membrane‑forming curing compound shall be applied with an atomizing spray tank whose nozzle is adjustable and whose solution is agitated before each application; when accelerated mixes are used and ambient temperature drops below 80 °F, insulation or heating shall be provided, and the curing temperature shall be monitored at least hourly so that any change does not exceed 40 °F within a one‑hour period, with exceeding this limit rendering the work defective[2]. The return‑to‑service time must be considered before opening the repaired section to traffic[2]. Section 530—Long‑Life Concrete Pavement imposes dimensional and material quality controls: a lot is defective if the average core depth is less than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch, if the compressive‑strength PWL is less than 55 %, or if the water‑to‑cementitious ratio falls outside the range of 0.37 to 0.42; these provisions affect construction practices, long‑term serviceability and maintenance cycles[2].
