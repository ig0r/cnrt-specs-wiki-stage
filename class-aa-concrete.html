Class AA Concrete

Part of: Portland Cement Concrete is-subtopic-of

Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

Class AA Concrete is a high‑strength, durable normal‑weight Portland‑cement concrete mix that satisfies the performance criteria of Publication 408, Specifications Section 704 and is identified as the standard paving material for rigid‑pavement structures[1]. Designed to ACI 211 using PTM 528/529 aggregate gradations, it must achieve an overdesign compressive strength of at least 500 psi (with higher values for most mixes), a plastic‑state air content of 7 % ± 1.5 %, and limits on permeability, shrinkage and chloride‑ion penetration to ensure freeze‑thaw resistance and long‑term durability[2]. The resulting mean 28‑day compressive strength of about 4,925 psi yields an elastic modulus of roughly 4 × 10⁶ psi, providing the stiffness required for slab behavior and allowing the material to carry traffic loads comparable to conventional jointed plain or reinforced concrete pavements[1]. Because it meets these mechanical and durability thresholds, Class AA Concrete is specified for new pavement layers, lane‑addition projects, bonded and unbonded overlays, and partial‑depth repairs where rapid opening to traffic and structural integrity are required[2].

Material purpose and applicability

Class AA Concrete is a high‑strength, durable Portland cement concrete mix that meets or exceeds the requirements set out in Publication 408, Specifications, Section 704 for Class AA Cement Concrete and is identified as the normal‑weight Portland cement concrete specified for paving. [1] The mix is also defined by the specifications for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving, and AA Fixed Form to be designed according to ACI 211 with aggregate gradations from PTM 528 or PTM 529, a minimum overdesign compressive strength of 500 lb/in², and a plastic‑state air content of 7.0 % (±1.5 %) together with limits on permeability, shrinkage and chloride ion penetration to provide the high strength, durability and freeze‑thaw resistance required for the structural pavement layer. [2] In rigid‑pavement design the material provides a structural capacity comparable to conventional jointed plain or reinforced concrete pavements, supporting the full range of traffic loading conditions while supplying the stiffness or rigidity needed for the slab, with its elastic‑modulus value used as an input in design calculations. [1] The average 28‑day compressive strength of Class AA Concrete is reported as 4,925 psi (standard deviation 432 psi) and its 28‑day mean modulus of rupture is 631 psi; these values are to be used in all rigid‑pavement designs unless district experience indicates otherwise. [1] The corresponding 28‑day mean elastic modulus is taken as 4,000,000 psi, which aligns with the mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi and provides the stiffness that governs slab behavior in the pavement system. [1] For widening or lane‑addition projects on rigid pavements the specification requires the use of Class AA cement concrete; full lane additions of at least 10 feet shall be designed to the same thickness as the original pavement, while partial widenings of less than 10 feet shall match the existing pavement type and depth. [1] Uniform‑depth rigid pavements constructed since 1945 must employ Class AA concrete unless an exemption is granted. [1] In bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 540) Class AA Cement Concrete replaces unsound asphalt pavement and functions as the new structural base that bonds with the overlay, forming a durable rigid layer capable of bearing traffic loads. [2] For bonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545) the same mix is placed to form the overlay layer, providing a protective, high‑strength surface that contributes structural capacity and may be compensated up to an additional 2 % of the computed overlay quantity for material used to replace unsound pavement removed during milling. [2] Thin bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 541) use Class AA Paving Cement Concrete reinforced with synthetic or steel macrofibers to achieve required residual strength and a target chloride ion penetration of Low, Very Low, or Negligible after 56 days, thereby enhancing structural capacity and durability. [2] In partial‑depth repairs of rigid pavements (Section 525) Class AA Cement Concrete—provided in Modified or Accelerated variants—is placed into saw cuts or other repair zones after grout or epoxy bonding compound is applied; placement must occur when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F and rising, the concrete is consolidated with vibration before it hardens, and it is cured with a white membrane‑forming curing compound. [2] The Modified variant follows the base specification but limits the water‑cement ratio to 0.42 and requires Type A No. 8 coarse aggregate, whereas the Accelerated variant also uses that aggregate and must achieve a minimum target compressive strength of 1,500 psi at 7 hours; in both cases the material provides structural reinforcement and durable surface restoration for repaired pavement sections. [2] Within Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (Section 530) Class AA concrete must satisfy the quality‑index calculation based on the average of sublot test results and the specified standard deviation, with adequacy and uniformity of the 28‑day strength required for acceptance when the percent passing weight loss (PWL) is 55 % or greater, ensuring that the LLCP layer meets design performance. [2] For rigid pavement sections covered by Section 506.3, Class AA Concrete is the mix whose 28‑day compressive strength and air‑content must meet the acceptance limits set out in that section; it is evaluated lot‑by‑lot with one compressive‑strength test and one air‑content test per sublot, and it is accepted only when its 28‑day strength is adequate and uniform and its plastic air content meets the specified percentage. [2] In full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529) Class AA accelerated cement concrete is used as a patch material to fill or repair utility casting blockouts, restoring continuity of the slab after utilities have been installed. [2] For unbonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 548) Class AA Cement Concrete provides the protective overlay layer that restores surface smoothness and contributes structural capacity; when the overlay thickness is less than 7 inches the mix must be reinforced with polypropylene fibers not exceeding 5.0 lb/yd³ and meeting detailed fiber type, length, aspect ratio and residual strength requirements. [2] In slip‑form or accelerated paving operations (Section 501.2) Class AA Concrete supplies the structural concrete layer for the pavement base, with the accelerated type limited to situations indicated or directed and prohibited for use in long‑term lane closures except to accommodate local traffic. [2] The plain‑cement‑concrete gutter (Section 640.2) is formed with Class AA Cement Concrete, which creates the foundation for the gutter bottom at the required depth below the finished surface, providing the structural base that establishes the gutter channel within the pavement system. [2]

Composition and characteristics

The material identified as Class AA cement concrete is a high‑strength, durable normal‑weight Portland‑cement mix that must satisfy the performance criteria of Publication 408, Specifications, Section 704 and is typically produced with the optimized aggregate gradation required by that specification, making it suitable for projects where traffic can be reopened within 24–48 hours. According to the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, Class AA cement concrete shall contain Portland cement, a Type A fine aggregate, and a Type A coarse aggregate selected from stone, gravel or slag. The coarse aggregate may be sized No. 57, No. 67 or No. 8 as required for the particular paving application, and while lightweight aggregates are permitted, their water absorption shall not exceed 3.0 % for gravels or 2.0 % for all other aggregates unless higher‑absorption material meets the freeze‑thaw durability requirement of 300 cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80. The cement factor is limited to a minimum of 530 lb/yd³ for AA Slip Form Paving, paving and accelerated patching and to a maximum of 600 lb/yd³ for AA Slip Form Paving and paving (but not accelerated patching). The mix design must incorporate Portland cement or an equivalent cement factor supplemented with at least one supplementary cementitious material—slag cement in a minimum proportion of 25 %, fly ash in a minimum proportion of 15 % or silica fume in the range of 5‑10 %. When Portland cement is partially replaced, at least 400 lb/yd³ of Portland cement must remain, total cementitious material must stay within Table A limits, and slag cement may not exceed 30 % of the total cementitious weight. The water‑cement ratio shall not exceed 0.42 lb/lb for any Class AA concrete; a target value of 0.40 lb/lb is prescribed and shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb, and the same 0.42 limit applies when the maturity method is used. The fresh mix must contain 7.0 % air in the plastic state with a tolerance of ±1.5 %, and after hardening the total air content shall be between 4.5 % and 8.5 %. Overdesign compressive strengths are required to be at least 500 psi for AAAP, AAAP LW, AA Paving and ASC, and a minimum of 1,000 psi for all other Class AA concrete. The mix must also be capable of achieving at least 1,500 psi compressive strength measured after seven hours in accordance with PTM No. 604. When the design compressive strength is 3,500 psi, the calculated 28‑day elastic modulus is about 3.4 × 10⁶ psi, whereas AASHTO recommends using a mean modulus of 4 × 10⁶ psi that corresponds with a mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi unless local historical data indicate significantly different strengths. For slip‑form and fixed‑form paving the maximum allowable 28‑day shrinkage is specified as 0.05 %. When lightweight aggregates are employed, an equilibrium density between 110 and 117 lb/ft³ (target 115 lb/ft³) is required. The concrete must be designed to attain a target chloride‑ion penetration classified as Low, Very Low or Negligible after a curing period of 56 days or less; a Moderate classification may be permitted provided it does not exceed 2,800 coulombs per AASHTO T277 Table 1, or does not exceed 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑by‑8 in cylinders and 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑by‑12 in cylinders. Class AA paving cement concrete must be reinforced with synthetic or steel macrofibers, and when the overlay depth is less than three inches No. 67 coarse aggregate shall be used instead of No. 57, with the macrofiber dosage set to achieve the design residual strength value (f150) as determined by ASTM C1609‑12 testing at 14 days. For overlays whose thickness is less than seven inches the mix must be reinforced with virgin polypropylene synthetic fibers that are monofilament or bundled monofilament, having a length between one and two‑and‑a‑half inches, an aspect ratio not greater than 150, added in sufficient quantity to achieve a residual strength ratio (R150,3) of 20 percent while keeping the fiber dosage at or below five pounds per cubic yard unless field testing shows the mix remains workable without clumping. If a maturity method is employed, degree‑hours of cure must be correlated to compressive strengths at eight hours, one day, seven days, twenty‑eight days and fifty‑six days in accordance with PTM No. 640. In pavement work the choice of concrete type influences early‑age behavior; high early strength (HES) concrete should be avoided because it may cause excessive shrinkage cracking [1]. Accelerated concrete, however, can achieve opening‑to‑traffic strength in 4 to 7 hours, providing a rapid service entry compared with standard pavement concrete that reaches the required strength in 24 to 48 hours when an optimized aggregate gradation is used [1]. Precast pavement units are produced from a high‑strength, durable mix that typically exceeds the Class AA Cement Concrete requirements and therefore deliver superior long‑term performance [1]. The performance of Class AA concrete is highly dependent on strict control of its constituents, and any variation in composition, source, processing, or proportioning must remain within the limits set out in the applicable sections [2]. In Section 704.1(c) a change in aggregate source or gradation may be permitted only if it stays within PTM limits, and a deviation of less than 5 % on one sieve may be allowed by the DME/DMM on a case‑by‑case basis because such changes directly alter coarse‑to‑fine volume ratios that influence strength and durability [2]. The cement factor may include supplementary cementitious material, with slag cement required at a minimum of 25 %, fly ash at a minimum of 15 % and silica fume between 5 % and 10 %, provided that maximum replacement percentages are not exceeded; these substitutions affect compressive strength and durability characteristics [2]. Air‑entraining admixture dosage is adjusted to obtain a plastic‑state air content of 6.0 % for general mixes or 7.0 % for AAAP, AA Slip Form, and AA Fixed Form mixes, and the hardened concrete must then fall within 3.5 %–7.5 % (or 4.5 %–8.5 % for those specific mixes), governing workability and freeze‑thaw performance [2]. For AAAP concrete the component proportions are tuned to achieve a 28‑day to 7‑day compressive strength ratio of at least 1.20 while also meeting low chloride‑ion permeability targets and a maximum 28‑day shrinkage of 500 µε; any proportioning variation therefore directly impacts these key performance thresholds [2]. Fresh‑concrete test results reflect mix variations; the slump must be maintained within ±1 ½ inches of the selected target slump value, and the upper slump limit may not be exceeded—if it is, the concrete must be rejected [2]. Air‑content or temperature values that depart from the specified limits trigger corrective action and increased testing frequency, while hardened properties are similarly controlled: when the 3‑day, 7‑day, 14‑day (AAAP) or 28‑day compressive strengths fall below the minimum mix‑design requirements listed in Table A, field curing must be continued and additional investigations are required [2]. Section 545.2 permits variations in cementitious composition provided that at least 400 lb/yd³ of Portland cement remains and the total cementitious content stays within the limits set by Table A; slag cement may not exceed 30 % of the combined cementitious material [2]. The water‑cement ratio must not exceed 0.42, the target is 0.40 and it shall never fall below 0.38, which directly influences workability and long‑term performance [2]. Air content at placement is required to be 7 % ±1.5 %, providing freeze‑thaw resistance [2]. Reactive aggregates showing a 14‑day expansion of 0.10–0.30 % may be used only with an approved cement‑supplementary blend that reduces the expansion to less than 0.1 %; any aggregate exceeding 0.30 % expansion is rejected, and blended hydraulic cements are limited to 0.02 % expansion at 14 days and 0.06 % at eight weeks to maintain dimensional stability [2]. Aggregate absorption may not exceed 3.0 % for gravels and 2.0 % for all other aggregates; higher‑absorption aggregates may be allowed only if mix‑design specimens meet at least 300 freeze‑thaw cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80 [2]. Proportioning is performed by weight for plant‑ and truck‑mixed concrete and by volume for volumetric mixing, and the batch weight of aggregate must be adjusted to compensate for free water on the aggregate, thereby controlling the effective water‑cement ratio and strength [2]. The chosen processing method—plant mixed, truck mixed (dry batched with water added in the truck but not mixed en route), or volumetric mixed—determines where mixing occurs, yet all methods must follow the same constituent limits to achieve the required density and durability [2]. For thin bonded overlays whose depth is less than 3 inches the specification calls for No. 67 coarse aggregate instead of No. 57, altering gradation; macrofiber is added at a rate that achieves the design residual strength value (f150) as evaluated at 14 days according to ASTM C1609‑12 [2]. The mixture is designed to meet a target chloride‑ion penetration of Low, Very Low or Negligible after a curing period of 56 days or less, and a Moderate rating is acceptable only if it does not exceed 2,800 coulombs [2]. Synthetic polypropylene fibers may be used in overlays less than 7 inches; fiber length must be at least 1.0 inch and may not exceed 2½ inches, the aspect ratio may not exceed 150, and dosage shall achieve a residual strength ratio R150,3 of 20.0 percent, with a maximum dosage of 5.0 lb/yd³ unless a field demonstration shows that the concrete mixture remains workable and free of fiber clumping [2]. When the maturity method is employed, degree‑hours of cure are correlated to compressive strengths at 8 hours, 1 day, 7 days, 28 days and 56 days, linking the curing process to strength development [2].

Key material properties

In the Pavement Policy Manual, Class AA concrete is characterized mechanically by an average 28‑day compressive strength of 4,925 psi with a standard deviation of 432 psi, and this strength yields a calculated modulus of rupture of 631 psi that should be used in all rigid pavement designs unless district experience indicates otherwise [1]. The same manual recommends adopting a mean concrete elastic modulus of 4,000,000 psi for Class AA concrete, which corresponds with the mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi [1]. When a design compressive strength of 3,500 psi is employed, the ACI correlation Ec = 57,000 (f’c)^0.5 produces an elastic modulus of approximately 3.4 × 10^6 psi, illustrating the direct effect of strength variations on stiffness [1].

Under the Long‑Life Concrete Pavement specification, physical performance is judged by depth and moisture loss: acceptance requires that the average pavement depth of a lot be more than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch and that not more than one individual sublot fall below that same limit, and the concrete must achieve a percent‑water‑loss (PWL) of at least 55 percent [2]. The mix design for all Class AA concrete is limited to a maximum water‑cement ratio by weight of 0.42, with a target ratio of 0.40 that shall not fall below 0.38, and the cement factor shall be at least 530 lb/yd³ and shall not exceed 600 lb/yd³ [2]. Plastic‑state air content is prescribed at 6.0 percent, increasing to 7.0 percent for AAAP, AA Slip Form and AA Fixed Form mixes; this translates to a total hardened‑concrete air content between 3.5 percent and 7.5 percent inclusive (4.5 percent to 8.5 percent inclusive for those latter mixes), while placement air content shall be 7.0 percent ± 1.5 percent for AA Slip‑Form Paving and Accelerated Patching mixes [2]. Chemical durability is addressed by requiring a target chloride‑ion penetration classified as Low, Very Low or Negligible after a curing period of no more than 56 days, with any moderate level not exceeding 2,800 coulombs or being at least 15.6 kΩ·cm as defined by AASHTO tests [2]. Aggregate water absorption shall not exceed 3.0 percent for gravels and 2.0 percent for all other aggregates; higher‑absorption aggregates are permissible provided the mix still satisfies a durability requirement of at least 300 freeze‑thaw cycles with less than 0.04 percent dilation and a durability factor greater than 80 [2].

Mechanical adequacy is further verified by compressive‑strength tests at multiple ages: overdesign compressive strength shall be a minimum of 1,000 psi except for AAAP, AAAP LW, AA Paving and ASC where the minimum is 500 psi; for partial‑depth repair the mix must achieve a minimum target compressive strength of 1,500 psi at seven hours, an initial set time of at least 15 minutes at 75°F ± 2°F, and durability of at least an 80 percent factor after 300 freeze‑thaw cycles with dilation under 0.04 percent [2]. The fourteen‑day AAAP result is required to be greater than or equal to 3,500 psi before field cure may be discontinued, and the pavement may be opened to traffic only after the concrete reaches a minimum compressive strength of 3,000 psi as verified by cylinder testing or the maturity method [2]. Residual tensile strength (f150) is obtained by dosing macrofiber according to Table B and evaluating it with ASTM C1609‑12 at fourteen days; the mixture must provide a residual strength ratio (R150,3) of 20.0 percent, with fiber geometry limited to a minimum length of 1.0 inch, a maximum length of 2½ inches and an aspect ratio not exceeding 150, and total fiber dosage shall not exceed 5.0 lb/yd³ unless workability is demonstrated [2].

Behavior and mechanisms

Under loading, the 28‑day mean tensile stress capacity for Class AA concrete is 631 psi as derived from its average compressive strength and should be used in all rigid pavement designs unless district experience indicates otherwise [1]. The same specification characterizes the rigidity of Class AA paving concrete by a 28‑day mean elastic modulus of 4,000,000 psi, which corresponds to a mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi and serves as the basis for structural analysis [1]. In addition, the design criteria require an overdesign compressive strength of at least 1,000 psi, except that AAAP, AAAP LW, AA Paving and ASC mixes are limited to 500 psi, providing the load‑bearing capacity expected for pavement structures [2]. When placed as a bonded overlay, Class AA concrete must develop a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi before it may be opened to traffic, establishing an additional loading threshold specific to that construction method [2]. For partial‑depth repair applications the mix is limited to a maximum water‑cement ratio of 0.42 and must achieve a minimum target compressive strength of 1,500 psi at seven hours, indicating strong early resistance to applied loads [2]. The plastic‑state air content is targeted at 6.0 % (7.0 % for AAAP, AA Slip Form and AA Fixed Form mixes) and the hardened total air must fall between 3.5 % and 7.5 % (4.5 %–8.5 % for those mixes), which helps maintain durability under moisture variations and freeze‑thaw temperature cycles [2]. Shrinkage is limited to 500 µstrain, with an approved increase to 550 µstrain, and for slip‑form or fixed‑form paving the dimensional change after air storage must not exceed 0.05 % [2]. Durability and deterioration

Class AA cement concrete used in pavement is susceptible to excessive shrinkage cracking when high‑early‑strength mixes are employed [1]. It also may experience surface spalling that occurs only in the top half of the slab depth if severe joint loss is not present [1]. Joint deterioration—especially at transverse‑longitudinal intersections that extend beyond the mid‑slab depth or produce crushed joints—causes loss of load transfer and tends to be most pronounced near joints and cracks at the slab bottom [1]. Movements of longitudinal tie bars or bolts in partially set patch concrete, induced by traffic on adjacent lanes, can be more detrimental than their intended benefit for patches longer than 65 feet, so proper dowel anchoring and full‑depth removal of deteriorated material are required to preserve long‑term performance [1]. Durability of Class AA cement concrete is governed primarily by its resistance to chloride ingress, shrinkage, freeze‑thaw damage, sulfate attack, moisture, contamination, temperature extremes, inadequate curing, and improper joint or panel design [2]. The mix must be designed to achieve a target chloride ion penetration classified as Low, Very Low, or Negligible after no more than 56 days of curing; if a Moderate rating is accepted the penetration may not exceed 2 800 coulombs (AASHTO T 277) or must meet the specified resistivity values for the test cylinders [2]. Shrinkage is limited to a maximum of 500 microstrain at 28 days unless a higher value is specifically approved, and for slip‑form and fixed‑form paving the 28‑day shrinkage after air storage may not exceed 0.05 % [2]. The hardened concrete’s air‑void system must meet a spacing factor of about 0.008 inch and a specific surface of about 630 in²/in³ to protect against freeze‑thaw damage [2]. Sulfate‑resistant mixes are required to follow ACI 201.2 [2]. Standing water must be eliminated after milling and the milled surface must be less than 120 °F to avoid bond loss; oil or grease drippings must be removed before placement [2]. Panels larger than 18 times the overlay thickness or with an aspect ratio greater than 1.5 should be avoided because they are prone to premature cracking, and joints must be sawed at a depth of one‑third the slab thickness with a minimum width of 1/8 inch to control random cracking [2]. The concrete is considered defective if insulation removal causes the surface temperature to change more than 40 °F in any one‑hour period, and traffic may not be permitted until a compressive strength of at least 3 000 psi is achieved [2]. Temperature‑related damage during partial‑depth repairs requires that placement be discontinued if ambient air or surface temperature falls below 40 °F and the repair be protected from freezing for at least 24 hours; during curing a temperature swing greater than 40 °F within any one‑hour period is considered defective because it can generate internal stresses that lead to delamination, and any delaminated repair area must be replaced [2]. Durability is further protected by limiting the water‑cement ratio to a maximum of 0.42 lb/lb, targeting 0.40 lb/lb and not falling below 0.38 lb/lb; an air content of 7.0 % ±1.5 % is required for slip‑form paving and accelerated patching [2]. Material interactions and compatibility

Class AA Concrete mixes are calibrated to the specific cement, supplementary cementitious material (SCM), and aggregate properties supplied for a project; the minimum cement factor is used unless Portland cement is partially replaced by SCMs within the maximum mass‑percentage limits. For AAAP and AAAP LW mixes the Type I or II Portland cement may be substituted with slag cement, fly ash, or silica fume in amounts equal to or greater than the cement removed, and at least one of those replacements must meet the minimum levels of 25 % slag, 15 % fly ash, or 5‑10 % silica fume. The aggregate gradation must follow PTM 528/529 limits and lightweight aggregates must be stored saturated (greater than SSD) to control absorption and maintain consistent paste demand. Absorption is limited to not exceed 3.0 % for gravels and 2.0 % for all other aggregates, and higher‑absorbing aggregate may be used only if mix‑design specimens meet the freeze‑thaw durability criteria; the batch weight of each aggregate is adjusted to compensate for free water on the aggregate surface so that the intended water‑to‑cement ratio is maintained, and all batching for plant‑ or truck‑mixed concrete is performed by weight using the prescribed bulk specific gravity values. Air content is controlled by adding an air‑entraining admixture so that the plastic concrete contains 7.0 % air for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving, and AA Fixed Form Paving mixes (6.0 % for other classes), which in turn governs the hardened‑state total air between 4.5 % and 8.5 % for those mixes; the required air content is 7.0 % ±1.5 %. In bonded concrete overlays the water‑cement ratio may not exceed 0.42 lb/lb, the target is 0.40 lb/lb and it shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb; cement may be partially replaced by fly ash or slag cement only when at least 400 lb/yd³ Portland cement remains and the total cementitious content stays within Table A limits, with slag cement limited to no more than 30 % of that total. Reactive aggregates showing a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % may be used only with an approved cement‑supplementary material blend that reduces the expansion to less than 0.1 %, while any aggregate exceeding 0.30 % expansion is rejected. Blended hydraulic cements (Type IS or IP) are allowed provided their mortar expansion does not exceed 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at 8 weeks.

When a partial‑depth repair is performed, Class AA Cement Concrete is first bonded to the existing pavement by applying grout or epoxy bonding compound to all exposed surfaces, and the fresh concrete must be placed before the grout dries or while the epoxy remains tacky, ensuring a direct bond between the new mix and the preparatory material. Placement of the concrete is limited to times when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F and rising, and the repaired area must be protected from freezing for at least 24 hours; during curing a white membrane‑forming curing compound is applied as specified, with temperature changes monitored so they do not exceed 40 °F within any one‑hour period. Expansion joint material and preformed cellular polystyrene forms are used to keep the concrete out of existing joints, further controlling its interaction with surrounding pavement elements.

In unbonded overlays whose thickness is less than 7 inches the concrete is reinforced with polypropylene fibers that meet Performance Level 1; the fibers are supplied as monofilament or bundled monofilament with a minimum length of 1.0 inch and a maximum length of 2½ inches, an aspect ratio not exceeding 150, and enough is added so that the mixture attains a residual strength ratio (R150,3) of 20.0 percent, while the fiber dosage must stay at or below 5.0 lb/yd³ unless a field demonstration shows the mix remains workable and free of clumping. Reinforcement such as dowel bars may receive a shop‑applied bondbreaker; these bondbreakers must be kept indoors or otherwise shielded from the environment, and if left exposed for more than six months they require retesting before use. Influence of production and placement

Mix design for Class AA Concrete must consider durability; the Pavement Policy Manual advises that high‑early‑strength concrete should be avoided because it may cause excessive shrinkage cracking, whereas a standard pavement mix satisfies strength requirements over a longer period [1]. When early opening of traffic is required, accelerated mixes capable of reaching opening strength in four to seven hours are used, while conventional mixes typically need twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours before traffic can be permitted [1]. The same manual stresses that poor placement of patches fails to provide the uniform support needed and leads to performance problems, underscoring the importance of careful placement practices [1].

The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania specifications require trial batching for each Class AA mix and mandate that the planned mixer type be used so that component proportions can be adjusted to meet target slump, air‑content and strength values; a full‑size batch using the same procedure must be produced at the start of construction for final design adjustment [2]. The water‑cement ratio shall not exceed 0.42 as revised in Table A of Section 704, and the fresh concrete consistency must remain within one‑and‑a‑half inches of the selected target slump; if any slump test exceeds the upper limit the batch must be rejected [2]. The first three trucks delivered are tested for slump, air content and temperature, and material control is considered established only when those results stay within the prescribed action points [2]. Plastic concrete must retain an air content within ±1.5 % of the target—six percent for general cement concrete or seven percent for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving, and AA Fixed Form Paving—and this controls the hardened‑state total air range of three point five to seven point five percent (or four point five to eight point five percent for those mixes) [2].

Placement of a Class AA overlay is permitted only after the milled asphalt surface has cooled to less than 120 °F and within thirty minutes of final cleaning, with no standing water remaining; for partial‑depth repairs placement may occur only when ambient air and surface temperatures are at least forty degrees Fahrenheit and rising, and placement must be discontinued if they fall below that level, while the fresh mix must be protected from freezing for a minimum of twenty‑four hours [2]. Compaction is achieved by consolidating the concrete with a spud vibrator or other acceptable method to remove entrapped air [2].

Finishing shall be performed by at least sixty percent certified flatwork finishers, and after finishing the curing material—either a white‑pigmented liquid membrane or another approved cure—must be applied within thirty minutes after bleed water has dissipated, with a uniform coverage rate of at least one gallon per 125 ft²; striking‑off and shaping of patches to follow existing wheel ruts is required for repair work [2].

Curing practices directly influence early‑age strength development, permeability, shrinkage, durability and the final compressive strength required before traffic opening. The specifications require maintaining a temperature of sixty to eighty degrees Fahrenheit for the first twenty‑four ± two hours using curing boxes or tanks, and insulating blankets shall be provided when ambient air temperature is forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or lower; blankets may be removed only when the air temperature exceeds forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or when average compressive strength exceeds three thousand psi, provided that surface temperature does not change more than forty degrees Fahrenheit in any one‑hour period [2]. For repair patches the curing temperature must be kept above eighty degrees Fahrenheit or insulated/heated if it drops lower, and temperature may not change more than forty degrees Fahrenheit within any hour; specimens are wet cured for at least fourteen days before shrinkage testing and must achieve a low, very low, or negligible chloride ion penetration after fifty‑six days or less, as required by the permeability target [2].

In the pavement‑patching guidance of Section 4.2 (CONCRETE PAVEMENT PATCHING) of the Pavement Policy Manual, long‑term performance of Class AA cement concrete is most sensitive to construction variations that impair load transfer at transverse joints; if dowels are not bonded throughout their entire embedded length, transverse joint load transfer is lost and premature distress can develop [1]. Cracked, spalled or delaminated repairs are identified as defective work and must be removed and replaced in accordance with Section 501.3(t) [2].

Selection, specification, and improvement

For widening and lane‑addition projects on rigid pavements, Class AA cement concrete is the default material, but its application is limited to uniform‑depth rigid pavements constructed since 1945, whether or not they have been overlaid. Full lane additions that are ≥ 10 feet wide shall retain the original pavement thickness unless heavier traffic loads require a different design, in which case the design shall reflect the actual traffic; partial widenings that are < 10 feet must match the existing pavement type and depth. Jointed plain cement pavement (JPCP) widenings must be plain cement concrete, whereas jointed reinforced cement pavement (JRCP) may use either reinforced or plain cement concrete. Transverse joints shall follow the original spacing rules: if the original spacing is 20 feet or less the new slabs keep the same length; if it exceeds 20 feet the joint spacing is adjusted to a minimum of 10 feet and a maximum of 17 feet with intermediate joints as needed, and any deviation requires an engineering‑judgment exemption supported by documentation. The material specification also relies on compressive‑strength‑based properties: Class AA concrete in Pennsylvania has an average compressive strength of 4,925 pounds per square inch with a standard deviation of 432 pounds per square inch; these values generate a 28‑day mean modulus of rupture of 6 3 1pounds per square inch, which the manual directs to use for all rigid pavement designs. The design procedure recommends using a mean concrete elastic modulus of 4,000,000 pounds per square inch, corresponding to the mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi, and this value should be applied unless district historical data show mean compressive strengths significantly different from 4,925 psi, in which case the elastic modulus must be adjusted accordingly.\nSelection of Class AA concrete for general pavement design is governed by a set of criteria tied to the contract documents. For the general design basis (Section 704.1(c)) the compressive strength must be at least 1,000 psi, although mixes identified as AAAP, AAAP LW, AA Paving and ASC are limited to 500 psi. The plastic‑state air content is prescribed as 6.0 % for general mixes and 7.0 % for AAAP, slip‑form and fixed‑form paving, with a tolerance of ±1.5 % at placement. The cement factor must include at least one supplementary cementitious material – slag cement (Grade 100 or higher) ≥25 %, fly ash (Type C or Type F) ≥15 % or silica fume 5‑10 % – and the minimum cement content is 530 lb/yd³ for AA Slip Form Paving, Paving and Accelerated Patching, while it shall not exceed 600 lb/yd³ for slip‑form paving and regular paving (accelerated patching is exempt from the upper limit). The water‑cement ratio may not exceed 0.42 lb/lb; a target of 0.40 lb/lb is typical and values below 0.38 lb/lb are not permitted. Aggregate gradations must conform to PTM 528 or PTM 529 limits, the coarse aggregate shall be Type A stone, gravel or slag that meets durability requirements, blast‑furnace‑slag aggregate is prohibited, and absorption may not exceed 3.0 % for gravels or 2.0 % for all other aggregates unless higher‑absorption material can demonstrate compliance with 300 freeze‑thaw cycles (ASTM C666) with less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80. Lightweight mixes are required to achieve an equilibrium density between 110 and 117 pcf, targeting 115 pcf, and sulfate‑resistant concrete shall be designed in accordance with ACI 201.2. Performance evaluation and implications

The laboratory evaluation for Class AA concrete in Pennsylvania relies on the 28‑day mean PCC modulus of rupture, which is derived from an average compressive strength of 4,925 psi with a standard deviation of 432 psi and calculated as S′c = 631 psi; this modulus of rupture value is prescribed for use in all rigid pavement designs but may be modified if district experience and data indicate otherwise based on field or long‑term performance evidence [1]. During placement operations the contractor must perform plastic‑concrete slump, air, density (for AAAP LW and AA LW) and temperature tests on the first three consecutive trucks; thereafter at least one truck per 50 cubic yards or one set every 50 cubic yards or 30 minutes whichever is greater for AA Slip Form Paving and AA Fixed Form Paving must be tested, and any slump result that exceeds the specified upper limit triggers rejection [2]. Field crews also lift plastic‑concrete samples at locations prescribed by PTM No. 1, drill cores in accordance with PTM No. 606 while the Inspector is present, and provide a measuring apparatus conforming to PTM No. 614; plastic‑air content of each sample is determined by AASHTO T 152 for stone‑and‑gravel aggregate or AASHTO T 196 for slag coarse aggregate, and the water‑cement ratio is verified in the field using batch tickets together with AASHTO T 318; acceptance is based on adequacy and uniformity when the plastic‑air‑content workability limit (PWL) is 55 % or greater [2]. The primary laboratory strength evidence consists of the average of two 28‑day compressive‑strength cylinders molded from each sublot sample and tested according to PTM No. 604; a sufficient number of QC cylinders are also molded from the same sample and tested for 3‑day or 7‑day compressive strength, 14‑day compressive strength (for AAAP mixes), 28‑day compressive strength, form‑removal strength and loading strengths as specified, with two additional cylinders cured under QC conditions for 56 days for AAAP and Prevention Level Z mixes; trial mixtures are evaluated for slump and plastic‑state air content of 6 % (or 7 % for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Fixed Form paving) using AASHTO T152/T196, for hardened‑state air‑void parameters by ASTM C457, and must meet a minimum 28‑day‑to‑7‑day compressive‑strength ratio of 1.20 for AAAP mixes [2]. Permeability is assessed after up to 56 days using AASHTO T277 or T358; when Moderate chloride ion penetration levels are accepted the results must not exceed 2,800 coulombs and must meet resistivity values greater than or equal to 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑inch × 8‑inch cylinders and greater than or equal to 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑inch × 12‑inch cylinders; shrinkage measured per ASTM C157 may not exceed 500 microstrain at 28 days (or up to 550 if approved) and must not exceed 0.05 % after the 28‑day air‑storage method for AA Slip Form and AA Fixed Form paving [2]. The concrete must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 3,000 psi as measured either by cylinder testing in accordance with PTM No. 604 or by the maturity method specified in AASHTO T 413; durability requirements call for mix‑design specimens to undergo 300 freeze‑thaw cycles according to ASTM C666 and to exhibit less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80; for partial‑depth repair applications the mix must attain at least 1,500 psi compressive strength at 7 hours (PTM No. 604), a durability factor of at least 80 % after 300 freeze‑thaw cycles (ASTM C666 procedure B) with and without aggregate extenders, and an initial set time not less than 15 minutes at 75°F ± 2°F (AASHTO T 131) [2]. When the maturity method is employed, degree‑hours of cure are correlated to compressive strengths at 8 hours, 1 day, 7 days, 28 days and 56 days in accordance with PTM No. 640, providing additional laboratory or field evidence of strength development over time; residual flexural strength is obtained by running ASTM C1609‑12 on a 14‑day specimen and relating the result to fiber volume fraction, and Section 548 requires a residual strength ratio (R150,3) of 20.0 % as measured by the Illinois Modified ASTM C1609 procedure, with synthetic‑fiber dosage not to exceed 5.0 lb per cubic yard unless the manufacturer provides a field demonstration that the mixture remains workable and free of fiber clumping [2]. Aggregates are accepted only if 14‑day expansion lies between 0.10 % and 0.30 % with an approved cement‑supplementary blend; aggregates expanding greater than 0.30 % are rejected, and mortar expansion is limited to a maximum of 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at eight weeks; long‑term performance for AAAP and Prevention Level Z mixes is demonstrated by curing the mixes for 56 days and requiring an average 14‑day QC compressive strength of at least 3,500 psi before field cure may be discontinued [2]. Additional acceptance criteria specify that shop‑applied bondbreakers left unprotected from environmental elements for more than six months after coating application must be retested in accordance with AASHTO M 254, and anchor material must demonstrate an average percentage of unbonded area of less than or equal to 10 % for n = 3 test unit assemblies when tested according to PTM No. 634 [2].

Class AA cement concrete is defined as a high‑strength, durable mixture that exceeds the requirements of Publication 408 Section 704, establishing it as a material capable of meeting demanding pavement needs [1]. Because it meets this definition, precast concrete paving systems built with the material can be designed to carry the same traffic loads as conventional cast‑in‑place PCC while offering superior durability [1]. The elevated strength permits thinner or longer slabs and reduces the risk of excessive shrinkage cracking that is associated with high early‑strength mixes, thereby improving long‑term pavement performance and lowering maintenance needs [1]. Using Class AA concrete in PCPS also shortens construction time since the slabs are fabricated off‑site and can be opened to traffic quickly, which creates a safer work zone and lessens environmental and economic impacts [1]. In Section 704 the mix shall be placed so that the slump remains within 1½ inches of the selected target range; if any slump test result falls outside the target range and has not exceeded the upper limit, air‑content and temperature tests must be performed and the batch may be rejected [2]. Early‑age strength testing at three or seven days and a fourteen‑day AAAP compressive strength of at least 3,500 psi are used to decide whether field curing can be discontinued, while the average twenty‑eight‑day compressive strength must be greater than or equal to the minimum mix‑design value in Table A before the lot is accepted [2]. The design basis requires a minimum overdesign compressive strength of 1,000 psi (or 500 psi for AAAP, AAAP LW, AA Paving and ASC) to provide sufficient load‑carrying capacity [2]. Air content shall be 6.0 % in the plastic state for general concrete and 7.0 % for AAAP, AA Slip Form and AA Fixed Form paving, establishing an entrained‑air void system that resists freeze–thaw damage; at placement the air content must be 7.0 % ± 1.5 % [2]. Chloride ion penetration shall meet a target of Low, Very Low or Negligible after fifty‑six days, limiting permeability and helping control reinforcement corrosion [2]. Twenty‑eight‑day shrinkage shall not exceed 500 microstrain (or up to 550 microstrain when approved), reducing cracking potential [2]. For lightweight AAAP LW mixes the equilibrium density must be between 110 and 117 lb/ft³ [2]. In bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements a minimum compressive strength of 3,000 psi must be achieved before opening to traffic; when air temperature is 45°F or less insulating blankets shall be applied and shall not be removed until the average compressive strength exceeds 3,000 psi and surface‑temperature change does not exceed 40°F within any one‑hour period [2]. Joint layout is limited so that maximum panel dimensions should not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio shall not exceed 1.5, helping prevent premature cracking [2]. Placement of Class AA concrete for partial‑depth repairs shall occur only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40°F and rising, and placement must be discontinued if those temperatures fall below 40°F; repairs shall be protected from freezing for a minimum of twenty‑four hours and after placement a white membrane‑forming curing compound shall be applied while the repair area is monitored at least hourly to ensure that curing temperature change does not exceed 40°F within any one‑hour period, with insulation or heating provided if ambient air drops below 80°F [2]. The water‑cement ratio target is 0.40 lb/lb, shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb, and shall never exceed an absolute maximum of 0.42 lb/lb; for partial‑depth repair material the maximum water‑cement ratio shall also be 0.42 [2]. Cementitious content shall include a minimum of 400 lb/yd³ Portland cement and a total cement factor not exceeding the Table A limits of 530–600 lb/yd³, while supplementary materials may replace up to thirty percent of the cement [2]. Reactive aggregates are limited to 0.10‑0.30 % fourteen‑day expansion and must be paired with an approved cement‑supplementary blend that reduces expansion to less than 0.1 % [2]. Type A fine and coarse aggregates shall have absorption limited to 3.0 % for gravels and 2.0 % for all other aggregates; higher‑absorption aggregate may be used only when mix‑design specimens meet three hundred freeze‑thaw cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation or achieve a durability factor greater than 80, directly influencing long‑term pavement performance [2]. Concrete shall be proportioned and batched by weight (or by volume for volumetric mixing) in plant‑mixed, truck‑mixed, or volumetric‑mixed production methods before delivery, ensuring consistent quality during construction and reducing variability that could affect maintenance needs [2]. The accelerated repair version must achieve a minimum target compressive strength of 1,500 psi at seven hours, providing rapid early load capacity for pavement restoration [2]. A minimum durability factor of 80 % at three hundred cycles in ASTM C666 testing is required, indicating resistance to freeze‑thaw damage [2]. When the maturity method is used, degree‑hours of cure shall be correlated to compressive strengths at eight hours, one day, seven days, twenty‑eight days and fifty‑six days in accordance with PTM No. 640, providing a systematic way to verify that sufficient strength has been attained during construction [2]. For overlays thinner than three inches the mix shall use No. 67 coarse aggregate instead of No. 57, and macro‑fibers shall be added at a rate that achieves the design residual strength value (f150) as calculated from ASTM C1609‑12 testing at fourteen days, ensuring the required post‑cure strength [2]. In Section 530—Long‑Life Concrete Pavement the average installed thickness must be more than the design thickness (D) minus 0.5 inch and no individual sublot may be less than that limit; acceptance is based on adequacy and uniformity of twenty‑eight‑day compressive strength when the percent‑within‑limits is 55 % or greater [2]. Trucks whose plastic air content falls outside specification limits are discarded before placement, preventing low‑air concrete that could compromise long‑life performance [2]. The 28‑day mean modulus of rupture for Class AA concrete is taken as 631 psi and is prescribed for use in all rigid pavement designs unless district experience and data indicates otherwise; this tensile strength parameter directly enters the structural design calculations that determine slab thickness, reinforcement and joint spacing, thereby influencing the ability of the pavement to resist cracking under service loads [1]. Because the modulus is derived from an average compressive strength of 4,925 psi for Class AA concrete in Pennsylvania, designers rely on this consistent value to achieve predictable performance [1]. The elastic modulus (Ec) quantifies the stiffness of Class AA concrete and directly governs how a rigid pavement slab will respond to traffic loads, influencing overall performance and durability; for design work the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures use a mean Ec of 4,000,000 psi for Class AA concrete, which corresponds with a mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi and is used in all rigid pavement designs unless District historical data show mean compressive strengths significantly different than 4,925 psi [1]. If a lower 28‑day design compressive strength such as 3,500 psi is applied, the calculated Ec becomes 3.4 × 10^6 psi, indicating a less stiff slab that would affect load distribution and potentially reduce service life [1].
