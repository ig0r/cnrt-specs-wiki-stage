Spalling

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Spalling is a topic under Distress.
spallingspallspallsjoint-spallingsurface-spalling
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Spalling is a concrete distress in which fragments break away from the pavement slab, appearing as break‑off of chips or fragments within the upper half of the slab depth and manifesting as surface breakouts on joints, edges, corners, or interior slab surfaces[1][2]. It is reported only on Portland‑cement concrete pavements—including slabs that form part of a concrete overlay or shoulder—and can be caused by moisture intrusion, improper joint construction, inadequate curing, unsatisfactory final finish, cracking, honeycombing, compressive stresses and point‑loading at joints[1][2]. The resulting loss of material undermines uniform support, degrades ride quality and structural capacity, and may progress to deeper deterioration if left unaddressed[1][2].

Description and identification

Spalling is a concrete distress in which fragments break away from the pavement slab[1][2]. In the Pavement Policy Manual, spalling typically appears as break‑off of concrete chips or fragments within the upper half of the slab depth, manifesting as surface breakouts on a joint or slab and may also be observed as interior voids deeper within the slab[1]. It can present as interior slab surface spalling, minor spalling at transverse joints, or corner spalls where longitudinal and transverse joints intersect; the latter may extend beyond mid‑slab depth[1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications describe spalling as a loss of material at the edge of a saw cut, appearing as break back or detachment of concrete fragments along that edge[2]. For rigid and long‑life concrete pavements it is identified as a surface defect occurring along longitudinal edges, between adjacent lanes, or at the outer pavement edge, where concrete separates from the slab[2]. When spalling occurs on existing slabs during slab fracturing, it appears as extensive surface spalling along a crack, with pieces detaching and exposing underlying aggregate[2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–91); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 379–441)

Spalling on concrete pavements is recognized by visible chips or fragments that have broken away from the surface, joints, or slabs and is referred to in specifications as “break back or spalling,” which distinguishes it from a simple crack [1][2]. The visual assessment first confirms that the loss is limited to the top half of the slab depth; when spalls are confined to this upper portion they are treated as surface‑level damage, whereas spalls extending beyond the mid‑slab depth are classified as deeper deterioration [1]. In addition, extensive surface spalling that follows a fracture line is identified as distinct from punched holes or shattered pavement edges, and equipment that would cause such punching or shattering must be avoided [2]. To differentiate spalling from joint‑related distress such as faulting or loss of load transfer, the inspector verifies that the observed loss is not caused by severe joint problems; if loss of load transfer is present a full‑depth patch is required rather than a partial‑depth repair [1][2]. For interior spalls within badly spalled joints or slabs, the procedure calls for cleaning and filling with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material before any overlay preparation [1]. When spalling is observed during saw‑cutting, a new full‑depth transverse saw‑cut must be placed beyond the area of break back or spalling [2]. The depth category then determines the repair method: top‑half spalls are addressed with partial‑depth spall repairs, while spalls that extend beyond mid‑slab depth require full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs [1]. If break back or spalling occurs in an adjacent lane, repair must be made with at least a full‑depth Type A concrete patch at no additional cost to the Department, and any crack or spall appearing before final inspection mandates removal and replacement of the pavement at no additional cost [2]. When surface spalling is extensive, replacement of the pavement or construction of a thin‑bonded concrete inlay may be justified as an alternative remedial action [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–415)

Where it occurs

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, spalling is reported only on Portland‑cement concrete pavements, including slabs that form part of a concrete overlay or a concrete shoulder. The manual further describes interior‑slab surface spalling, transverse or longitudinal joint spalling, corner spalls where longitudinal and transverse joints intersect, and spalling on jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP). In the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification, spalling is reported on concrete rigid pavements in several sections. Section 516 notes that spalling may appear on the existing concrete surface or in an adjacent concrete lane during patching operations, and when break back or spalling is observed while making full‑depth saw cuts a new cut must be placed beyond the damaged area with repair using at least a full‑depth Type A concrete patch. Section 525 states that spalling is reported on interior slab surfaces of rigid pavements and permits a partial‑depth repair provided the repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness; the work is classified as Type 1 when the spalled area length is between 15 inches and 6 feet and as Type 2 for lengths greater than 6 feet. Section 519 indicates that spalling is reported on concrete rigid pavements when full‑depth saw cutting is performed for longitudinal joint repairs, and if spalling occurs the specification requires a new full‑depth transverse saw‑cut to be placed beyond the damaged area. Section 506 lists spalling as a defect that can affect plain cement concrete and reinforced cement concrete surfaces, describing edge slumping or longitudinal edge spall between adjacent lanes or outside pavement edges and requiring removal of at least ten feet of pavement between transverse joints or replacement of an entire panel. Section 528 requires the contractor to drill shallow vertical holes at each tie‑bar location if spalling of the concrete pavement surface occurs during cross‑stitching of longitudinal joints or cracks. Section 530 identifies spalling as a surface distress on long‑life concrete rigid pavements along longitudinal edges and between adjacent lanes or outside the pavement edge, declaring any lot exhibiting such spalling defective and stating that longitudinal edge spall repairs are not considered satisfactory. Section 522 notes that spalling is identified as a surface distress on rigid (concrete) pavements during slab‑fracturing operations and requires avoidance of equipment that would cause extensive surface spalling. For asphalt pavement, Section 492 addresses spalling only for the milled surface of an asphalt pavement; any area where spalling occurs must be patched in accordance with that section. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 342–343), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 379–441)

In the Pavement Policy Manual, Section 4.3 JOINTS reports that spalling in concrete pavements occurs at concrete pavement joints—both transverse and longitudinal—and also as interior spalls within the slab body [1]. The same manual’s Section 12.3 DESIGN GUIDELINES further characterizes spalling as transverse or longitudinal spalls that appear in slab interiors along faulted joints and cracks, explicitly stating that the distress is located within the concrete slab along joint lines rather than at edges, corners, wheel paths, layer interfaces, or the base/subgrade [1]. Section 4.4 CONCRETE PAVEMENT PARTIAL‑DEPTH REPAIR notes that spalling appears as interior‑slab surface spalls, at transverse or longitudinal joints, and at corners where those joints intersect; it adds that minor spalling at a transverse joint may be linked to poor joint performance and that corner spalls extending beyond mid‑slab depth are treated as deeper distress [1]. For spalls whose depth does not exceed half the slab thickness, Publication 408 Specifications Section 525 prescribes partial‑depth repair with Type 1 spot repairs for lengths between 15 inches and 6 feet and Type 2 extended repairs for lengths greater than 6 feet; spalls deeper than half the slab thickness require full‑depth patches per Section 516 [1]. In Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification Section 525.1, which governs concrete pavement partial‑depth repair, spalling is described as occurring on interior slab surfaces and may be associated with transverse or longitudinal joints and cracks, and the description emphasizes that this distress occurs within the slab body rather than at corners, edges, wheel paths, layer interfaces, or the base/subgrade [2]. Section 516.3 of the concrete pavement patching specifications observes spalling at the edges of full‑depth transverse saw cuts—including in the adjacent lane—and at corners where patches are installed, requiring a new cut or a concrete patch when such spalling is encountered [2]. Section 519.3, which addresses full‑depth longitudinal concrete joint repair, limits spalling to the perimeter of repair zones when full‑depth saw cuts are made along existing longitudinal joints or around patches, and mandates a new transverse cut beyond any spalled area [2]. The specifications identify spalling as a defect that occurs at the longitudinal edges of concrete slabs, including between adjacent lanes or outside pavement edges, in Section 506.3(t); this clause also requires removal and replacement of at least ten feet of pavement between transverse joints when edge slumping or spalling is found [2]. The Long‑Life Concrete Pavement provisions repeat that location, stating in Section 530.3(t) that spalling occurs along the longitudinal edges of the slab, including between adjacent lanes or outside the pavement edges [2]. In the cross‑stitching provision, Section 528.3 describes spalling as occurring on the concrete surface at longitudinal joints or cracks when the cross‑stitching operation creates damage [2]. The profile‑milling specification, Section 492.3(c), treats spalling as a defect of the milled asphalt surface itself, locating the distress at the top layer of the pavement where material has been removed during milling [2]. Finally, Section 522.3, which covers slab fracturing of existing concrete pavements, references spalling only as surface spalling that can develop along a crack face, indicating that the distress occurs on the top surface of the concrete slab at the location of the fracture [2].

Causes and mechanisms

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), spalling is treated as a surface distress that may be eligible for a partial‑depth repair only when it is confined to the top half of the slab thickness and is not caused by severe joint problems such as loss of load transfer; extensive spalling may instead require full‑depth replacement or placement of a thin‑bonded concrete inlay. The manual further notes that minor spalling occurring at a transverse joint can be attributed to poor joint performance, which it classifies as a design or construction deficiency, and it does not attribute spalling to traffic loading, climate, moisture‑drainage, or material factors. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications (Publication 408/2020, Section 506.3(t)) list spalling among surface defects that render a concrete lot defective and therefore mandate removal and replacement; the specification identifies moisture from rain and several construction deficiencies—including improper joint construction, improper curing procedures, improper final finish, cracking, honeycombing, and edge slumping that can generate longitudinal edge spall—as causes of spalling. Neither document provides a tabular classification of spalling by cause, so no row‑by‑row breakdown is presented. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–91); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 379–380)

In the Pavement Policy Manual’s concrete pavement patching guidance (section 4.2), spalling is described as developing when severe joint problems such as loss of load transfer cause the concrete to break away in the upper half of the slab depth, and it progresses as a symptom of joint deterioration rather than an isolated surface defect [1]. The same manual’s partial‑depth repair guidance (section 4.4) attributes spalling further to compressive stresses and point‑loading forces that cause slab material to separate, particularly at joints where joint performance is poor [1]. Those mechanisms are aggravated by repeated expansion–contraction cycles, with cool nights combined with high daytime temperatures creating critical conditions for additional breakaway, and progression can be limited only when the new patch is placed with sufficient clearance from adjacent slabs so that contact does not occur during these temperature swings [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications for plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (SECTION 506) treat spalling as a surface defect that originates when the pavement experiences rain‑induced damage, poorly constructed joints, inadequate curing, an unsatisfactory final finish, cracking or honeycombing; these conditions create weak zones that allow chips or fragments to break away and to progress if not repaired [2]. The Long‑Life Concrete Pavement specifications (SECTION 530) repeat the same causative factors—rain exposure, improper joint construction, insufficient curing, poor final finish, existing cracking or honeycombing—and state that they accelerate spalling because they prevent proper consolidation and strength development [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–91); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 379–441)

Severity levels and rating

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) Section 4.3 “Joints”, spalling severity is defined by depth, with extensive spalls that are confined to the top half of a concrete slab classified as partial‑depth spalls and required to be repaired in accordance with Publication 408 Specification Section 525. The same manual states that spalls which extend beyond the mid‑slab depth are classified as full‑depth spalls and must be repaired according to Publication 408 Specification Section 519. Also in Section 4.3, joints or slabs that are described as badly spalled, including those with interior spalls, shall be cleaned and filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material (or equivalent) before an overlay as directed in Publication 408 Specification Section 469. In Section 4.4 “Concrete Pavement Partial‑Depth Repair”, the PennDOT classification groups spalling by repair type based on the length of the damaged area, assigning a Type 1 spot repair to interior slab surface spalls whose length lies between 15 inches and 6 feet. The same section specifies that when the length of interior slab surface spalling exceeds 6 feet, a Type 2 extended‑length repair is required. Publication 408/2020 Specifications Section 525—Concrete Pavement Partial‑Depth Repair likewise defines two severity types for spalling based on length: Type 1 applies to interior slab surface spalling measuring between 15 inches and 6 feet, and Type 2 applies when the spalling exceeds 6 feet; both repair types are limited to a depth no greater than one‑half of the slab thickness. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 416)

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) the assessment of spalling is linked to Publication 408 Specifications and treatment decisions are governed by the depth of the spall [1]. For a longitudinal joint that exhibits extensive spalls confined to the top half of the slab, Section 525 of Publication 408 specifies that a partial‑depth spall repair shall be performed, indicating a severity limited to the upper portion of the slab [1]. When spalling extends beyond the mid‑slab depth in a longitudinal joint, Section 519 of Publication 408 requires a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair, reflecting a more severe condition that penetrates past the midpoint of the slab [1]. The Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (LLCP) specification provides an explicit severity rating for spalling in Section 530.3(t) titled “Defective Work,” where any surface defect such as spalling that cannot be satisfactorily repaired, as determined by the Representative, is classified as defective [2]. The same LLCP provision further states that longitudinal edge spall repairs are not considered satisfactory for edge slumping or spalling between adjacent lanes or outside pavement edges, thereby reinforcing the designation of a defective condition when such spalls cannot be adequately remedied [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 439–441)

Measurement, survey and extent

In the Pavement Policy Manual, spalling on concrete pavements is quantified by measuring both its depth and its surface length; the depth is obtained by extracting representative cores from the spalled area to determine how far the deterioration extends, while the surface length of the spall is recorded for repair classification purposes [1]. The manual further specifies that a repair may be classified as a Type 1 spot repair when the measured surface length falls between 15 inches and 6 feet, and as a Type 2 extended‑length repair when it exceeds 6 feet [1]. Additionally, the depth measurement must confirm that the required repair does not exceed half the slab thickness before a partial‑depth patch is applied, as stipulated in Chapter 4, Section 4.4 guidelines [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications evaluate spalling on rigid concrete pavements by measuring pavement core depth; a representative core or set of cores is extracted and the average core depth is compared to the design thickness [2]. If the average core depth is less than the design thickness minus ½ inch, or if any individual sub‑lot core falls below that limit, the spalling is deemed defective, and the result is reported as a depth deviation from the specified thickness [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 90–91); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 379–380)

Effects on performance

Partial‑depth repairs should only be made where spalling occurs in the top half of the slab depth and is not caused by severe joint problems, such as loss of load transfer; otherwise extensive surface spalling may also justify pavement replacement or the construction of a thin‑bonded concrete inlay. Because spalled areas undermine uniform support, they degrade ride quality and indicate reduced structural capacity, safety, durability and service life if left unaddressed. Spalling that appears as interior‑slab surface loss reduces the pavement’s rideability and shortens its remaining service life. A partial‑depth repair limited to no more than half the slab thickness for spalls extends the service life of concrete pavements and restores rideability, provided the work follows Publication 408 Section 525 procedures and uses appropriate joint material to avoid compression or point‑loading failures that could affect durability. [1]

Repairs and treatments

Partial‑depth repair is prescribed for interior slab surface spalling and for joint or crack defects whose depth does not exceed half the slab thickness; it may be applied as a Type 1 spot patch for spalls from 15 inches to 6 feet long or as a Type 2 extended patch for spalls longer than 6 feet (section 4.4) [1]. The same partial‑depth repair is required when a longitudinal joint exhibits extensive spalling confined to the top half of the slab, in which case the joint shall receive partial‑depth spall repairs (section 4.3) [1]. Partial‑depth repair may be used only where spalling occurs in the upper half of the slab depth and is not caused by severe joint problems such as loss of load transfer (section 4.2) [1]. Full‑depth concrete pavement patching is required when spalling extends beyond the top half of the slab or is associated with severe joint deterioration, because those problems can only be corrected by full‑depth patching (section 4.2) [1]. Full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs are prescribed for spalls that penetrate beyond mid‑slab depth (section 4.3) [1]. Full‑depth corner repairs are indicated for corner spalls extending beyond mid‑slab depth (section 4.4) [1]. Concrete full‑depth patching and joint rehabilitation may be performed up to, but not exceeding, 10 % of the pavement area unless cost‑effectiveness for a larger share can be demonstrated (section 12.3), and concrete patching greater than 10 % of the pavement area is generally not cost‑effective or permitted for preservation projects (section 13.7) [1]. When a spalled concrete pavement will receive an overlay, the surface may simply be cleaned and filled with asphalt materials rather than applying concrete repair operations (section 4.2) [1]. Badly spalled joints and slabs that have interior spalls should be cleaned and filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material before placing an unbonded PCC or asphalt overlay (section 4.3) [1]. Bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays can be used to correct extensive surface spalling, provided the existing pavement is in relatively good condition, the subgrade is stable, and drainage is functional; the overlay adds structural capacity while eliminating the surface distress (section 4.9) [1]. Routine‑maintenance activities address spalling by performing spall repair, mechanized patch, or spray patch whenever spalling appears as an existing distress on the pavement (section 13.7) [1]. In preservation projects, concrete patching is employed to retard further deterioration and maintain functional condition before extensive damage occurs, subject again to the 10 % area limitation (section 13.7) [1]. If minor spalling at a transverse joint is caused by poor joint performance, slab stabilization together with dowel‑bar retrofit is recommended instead of a partial‑depth patch (section 4.4) [1]. When break back or spalling is observed in the cut zone during saw‑cutting, the specification requires making a new full‑depth transverse cut placed beyond the spalled area (Section 516.3(b)) [2]. If spalling appears in an adjacent lane, the source mandates repairing that damage with at least a full‑depth Type A concrete patch at no additional cost to the Department (Section 516.3(b)) [2]. Should any crack or spall occur anywhere on the pavement before final inspection and resolution of all issues, the affected portion must be removed and replaced at no extra cost (Section 516.3(e)) [2]. Cracked or spalled concrete patches or corner repairs identified as deficient under the defective‑work provision must be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department (Section 516.3(r)) [2]. For interior slab surface spalling whose repair depth does not exceed one half of the slab thickness, a partial‑depth repair may be used; Type 1 is applied to spot spalls that are between 15 inches and 6 feet in length, while Type 2 is applied to extended spalls longer than 6 feet, and both types also cover transverse or longitudinal joints and cracks (Section 525.1) [2]. In the full‑depth longitudinal concrete joint repair procedure, if break back or spalling occurs during removal of the existing pavement, a new full‑depth transverse saw‑cut must be made beyond the affected area and the repair length is to be extended at no additional cost (Section 519.3) [2]. Longitudinal edge spall repairs are not considered satisfactory for edge slumping or spalling between adjacent lanes or outside pavement edges; consequently the required remedy is to remove and replace a minimum of ten feet of pavement between transverse joints, or an entire panel, as determined by the Representative (Section 506.3(t)) [2]. When spalling of the concrete pavement surface occurs, cross‑stitching requires drilling shallow vertical holes at each tie‑bar location, and the overall cross‑stitching work may not be performed if either the ambient air temperature or the pavement surface temperature is below 40 °F (Section 528.3) [2]. If the Representative designates an area of a milled asphalt surface where spalling or other unsound pavement has been dislodged during the profile‑milling operation, the prescribed remedy is to excavate to the existing depth, clean the loose material, apply an emulsified asphalt tack coat and fill with an asphalt mixture of the same type, then level and compact it to conform to the adjacent pavement (Section 492.3(c)) [2]. When spalling or other surface defects render a long‑life concrete pavement panel defective because it cannot be satisfactorily repaired, the specification requires removal and replacement of an entire panel as shown on the Standard Drawings; longitudinal edge spall repairs are not considered satisfactory for edge slumping or spalling between adjacent lanes or outside pavement edges, limiting such repairs to isolated longitudinal edge spalls (Section 530.3(t)) [2].

Prevention in design and construction

The manual advises that partial‑depth repair shall be used only where spalling is confined to the upper half of the slab and is not caused by severe joint loss of load transfer, thereby targeting shallow, joint‑independent surface loss. When spalling extends beyond the mid‑slab depth, full‑depth patching or replacement—including thin‑bonded concrete inlay—is recommended to address deeper or widespread deterioration. For concrete pavements slated for an unbonded PCC or asphalt overlay, badly spalled joints and slabs with interior spalls shall be cleaned and filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material (or equivalent) before the overlay to eliminate loose fragments. Longitudinal joints that exhibit extensive spalling in the top half of the slab shall receive partial‑depth spall repairs as specified in Specification Section 525, whereas spalls extending beyond the mid‑slab depth shall be repaired with full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs as specified in Specification Section 519. Partial‑depth repair is suitable for transverse or longitudinal joints, cracks, and interior slab spalls when the repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness. If minor spalling at a transverse joint is caused by poor joint performance, slab stabilization together with dowel‑bar retrofit may be considered. Corner spalls that extend beyond mid‑slab depth shall be addressed by full‑depth corner repairs as specified in Specification Section 516. To prevent compression or point‑loading failure of partial‑depth patches—which could otherwise lead to further spalling—rigid polystyrene board for longitudinal joints or premolded expansion joint material for transverse joints must be installed between the patch and the adjacent slab, especially when the pavement is in a contracted state during cool‑night/high‑day temperature cycles. A bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlay may be used to correct extensive surface spalling provided that the existing pavement is in relatively good condition, the subgrade is stable, and drainage is functional. Full‑depth concrete patching combined with joint rehabilitation shall be applied to repair failed joints as well as transverse and longitudinal spalls and cracks, and the total patched area may not exceed ten percent of the pavement unless cost effectiveness for exceeding that limit can be demonstrated. Routine‑maintenance actions that mitigate spalling include cyclic crack sealing of asphalt‑surfaced pavements every three to five years, joint sealing of concrete pavements every five years, and direct spall repair as part of the routine‑maintenance package. Section 516.3 requires that all perimeter cuts be full‑depth and not exceed one‑half inch in width, a measure applied whenever saw cutting for a concrete patch to avoid break‑back or spalling of the surrounding slab. The same section also mandates that drop hammers and hydro‑hammers shall not be used when removing existing pavement, requiring jackhammering at an angle of forty‑five degrees or less to minimize disturbance that could cause spalling during removal operations. If break back or spalling occurs in a cut, Section 516.3 calls for a new full‑depth transverse saw‑cut placed beyond the damaged area and an additional patch length; this remedial step is applied only when spalling is observed in the cut zone. In the cross‑stitching guidance of Section 528.3, when surface spalling of the concrete pavement occurs during tie‑bar installation, shallow vertical holes shall be drilled at each tie‑bar location; this measure targets surface spalling and is applied only after spalling has been observed. The slab‑fracturing provisions of Section 522.3 state that fracturing equipment must not cause extensive surface spalling, punch holes, or shatter the slab, a restriction intended to prevent impact‑generated spalling during the fracture step. Use of sonic‑vibratory equipment is permitted only when the fracturing will not cause soil liquefaction of the subgrade, thereby avoiding vibration‑induced spalling of the concrete surface. Section 522.3 also requires care during fracturing operations to prevent shattering pavement edges adjacent to full‑depth asphalt patches, addressing edge spalling when cracks intersect patched areas. Finally, Section 522.3 specifies that the roller for seating operations shall not be operated in wet conditions and that fractured and seated pavement shall not be left exposed to freezing conditions; these measures reduce freeze‑thaw or moisture‑related spalling after fracturing. Full‑depth concrete pavement patching, which removes all deteriorated slab and requires dowelled load transfer as specified in Publication 408 Section 516, should be considered when spalling extends beyond the top half of the slab depth or when joint loss of load transfer is present (source [1] section 4.2). Partial‑depth repair may be used only where spalling occurs in the top half of the slab depth and is not caused by severe joint problems, thereby addressing minor surface loss (source [1] section 4.2). When a PCC pavement is to be patched but not overlaid, cleaning and sealing all joints and cracks with rubberized joint‑sealing material helps prevent spalling by protecting the joint from moisture intrusion (source [1] section 4.3). For pavements that will be overlaid, badly spalled joints and slabs that have interior spalls should be cleaned and filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material (or equivalent) before placing the overlay, thereby preventing further spalling of those areas (source [1] section 4.3). Joint rehabilitation, which creates new sealant reservoirs and reseals joints, is applicable only when restoring existing concrete pavements (not overlaying) and is most cost‑effective on pavements just beginning to exhibit minor joint distress, addressing spalling that can develop from unsealed joints (source [1] section 4.3). Longitudinal joints with extensive spalls in the top half of the slab shall be repaired with partial‑depth spall repairs as specified in Publication 408 Section 525, while spalls extending beyond mid‑slab depth require full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs per Section 519, directly treating surface and deeper spalling respectively (source [1] section 4.3). If minor spalling at a transverse joint is caused by poor joint performance, slab stabilization and dowel‑bar retrofit should be considered (source [1] section 4.4). Installing a rigid polystyrene board for longitudinal‑joint patches or a premolded expansion‑joint material of equal width to the existing joint between the patch and the adjacent slab eliminates compression and point‑loading failures that could otherwise cause spalling of the repair (source [1] section 4.4). When patches are placed while pavement slabs are contracted—typically during cool nights with high daytime temperatures—the repair must provide adequate space between the patch and the adjacent slab to prevent compression failure by point loading, thereby preventing spalling of the repair area (source [1] section 4.4). A bonded jointed plain‑concrete pavement overlay may be used to correct extensive surface spalling, but only when the existing concrete pavement is in relatively good condition, the subgrade is stable, and drainage functions properly (source [1] section 4.9). All saw cuts must be full depth and shall not exceed a width of ½ inch before traffic is permitted, which prevents spalling caused by insufficient cutting (source [2] Section 516.3(b)). If break back or spalling occurs during cutting, the contractor must make a new full‑depth transverse cut beyond the damaged area, and when the occurrence is in an adjacent lane the damaged area must be repaired with at least a Type A concrete patch; any cracked or spalled patches identified as defective must be removed and replaced at no cost to the Department (source [2] Sections 516.3(b) and 516.3(r)). For interior slab surface spalling, partial‑depth repair is prescribed but may be used only when the required repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness (source [2] Section 525.1). Spot repairs are applied to spalls between 15 inches and 6 feet in length, while extended‑length repairs are applied to spalls longer than 6 feet, each under the same depth limitation (source [2] Sections 525.1). Under the long‑life concrete pavement defective‑work provisions, spalling is prevented by ensuring that the pavement exhibits no surface defects resulting from rain, improper joint construction, improper curing procedures, improper final finish, cracking, or honeycombing, and by meeting the edge‑slumping criteria because longitudinal edge spall repairs are not considered satisfactory for correcting edge slumping or spalling between adjacent lanes or outside pavement edges (source [2] Section 530.3(t)). For slab fracturing, the contractor must select equipment capable of delivering a controllable, variable impact force and is expressly prohibited from using any machine that would cause extensive surface spalling along the crack; if spalling‑inducing results appear, operations must be ceased and an alternate equipment plan submitted for Representative approval (source [2] Section 522.3(a)).

Specification limits and acceptance

Under the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242) Section 4.2 Concrete Pavement Patching, partial‑depth repairs are limited to spalling that occurs in the top half of the slab depth and is not caused by severe joint problems such as loss of load transfer; more extensive surface spalling may also justify pavement replacement or a thin‑bonded concrete inlay. In Section 4.3 Joints of the same manual, when a longitudinal joint exhibits extensive spalls confined to the top half of the slab, the joint shall be repaired with partial‑depth spall repairs as prescribed in Publication 408 Specifications Section 525, whereas spalls that extend beyond mid‑slab depth shall be repaired with full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs as prescribed in Publication 408 Specifications Section 519; badly spalled joints and interior spalls slated for overlay must be cleaned and filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material (or equivalent) as directed in Publication 408 Specifications Section 469. Section 4.4 Concrete Pavement Partial‑Depth Repair states that partial‑depth repair is suitable when the depth of the repair does not exceed one half of the slab thickness, and the length of the spalling determines whether a Type 1 spot repair (spalling between 15 inches and 6 feet) or a Type 2 extended‑length repair (spalling greater than 6 feet) is applied; these criteria are specified in Publication 408 Specifications Section 525. Under the Design Guidelines of Section 12.3, projects that require concrete patching exceeding 10 % of the total pavement area will not be eligible for Pavement Preservation unless it can be shown that exceeding the 10 % threshold is cost effective, and patching quantities must include areas such as faulted joints, cracks, existing asphalt patches, failed concrete patches, and transverse/longitudinal spalls and cracks. The Department of Transportation Publication 408 Specification Section 516.3(e)‑1 and Section 516.3(r) impose a zero‑tolerance acceptance limit for spalling; any spall observed at any location before final inspection must be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department, with judgment made by visual inspection during construction and at final inspection. In Publication 408 Specification Section 525.1, interior slab surface spalling whose measured length is between 15 inches and 6 feet requires a Type 1 repair, while spalling whose length exceeds 6 feet requires a Type 2 repair, and both repair types are limited to a depth that does not exceed one half of the slab thickness.
