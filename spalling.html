Spalling

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Spalling is a topic under Distress.
spallingspallspallsjoint-spallingsurface-spalling
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Spalling is the loss of concrete chips or fragments that break away from a pavement, typically appearing as break‑off in the top half of the slab depth[1][2]. It occurs on Portland‑cement concrete pavements—including slabs forming part of overlays or shoulders, long‑life concrete, jointed plain concrete, and precast/prestressed units—and is observed as interior‑slab surface spalling, transverse or longitudinal joint spalling, and corner spalls where joints intersect[1][2]. The distress can arise from improper joint construction, curing procedures, final finish, rain exposure, or severe joint problems such as loss of load transfer, and when extensive it may progress to deeper damage that necessitates full‑depth replacement or a thin‑bonded concrete inlay[1][2].

Description and identification

In section 4.2 of the Pavement Policy Manual spalling is described as the loss of concrete chips or fragments from a pavement, typically appearing as break‑off in the top half of the slab depth. It may be limited to shallow surface areas or become extensive enough to require full replacement; when it is confined to the upper portion and not caused by severe joint problems a partial‑depth repair can be used, while extensive surface spalling may also justify pavement replacement or the construction of a thin‑bonded concrete inlay, and spalled sections can be cleaned and filled with asphalt material. In section 4.3 the manual defines spalling as loss of concrete chips or fragments that may appear as surface breakouts on a joint or slab or exist as interior voids deeper within the slab; extensive spalls in the top half of a longitudinal joint require partial‑depth spall repairs, whereas spalls extending beyond mid‑slab depth require full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs, and badly spalled joints and slabs with interior spalls should be cleaned and filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material prior to overlay placement. Section 4.4 identifies spalling as a concrete‑pavement distress that shows up as interior slab surface spalling, minor spalling at transverse joints, or corner spalls where longitudinal and transverse joints intersect, the latter sometimes extending beyond mid‑slab depth; when the spall depth does not exceed half the slab thickness partial‑depth repair may be used, while spalls exceeding that limit require full‑depth corner repairs. Section 516.3 of the 2026 Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications describes spalling as a loss of concrete that breaks away from the slab, showing up as a break‑back or missing piece during construction activities. In long‑life concrete pavements (Section 530.3(t)) spalling is identified as a surface defect where concrete fragments break away, typically along longitudinal edges, between adjacent lanes, or beyond the pavement edge. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–91); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–452)

Spalling on concrete pavements is recognized by the presence of visible chips or fragments that have broken away from the surface, joints, or slabs [1]. During construction activities such as saw‑cutting, spalling may also be identified when concrete fragments detach at the cut line, triggering a requirement for additional full‑depth cuts beyond the affected area [2]. The visual assessment in the policy manual first confirms that the loss is limited to the top half of the slab depth; losses confined to this upper portion are treated as surface‑level damage, whereas losses extending beyond the mid‑slab depth are classified as deeper deterioration [1]. To distinguish spalling from joint‑related distress such as faulting or loss of load transfer, the inspector verifies that the observed loss is not caused by severe joint problems; if loss of load transfer is present a full‑depth patch is required rather than a partial‑depth repair [1]. The specifications treat spalling as a distinct condition from break back, listing both together but prescribing separate corrective actions; break back is addressed with a new transverse cut, while spalling mandates removal and replacement of the pavement before final inspection [2]. When spalling occurs in an adjacent lane, the specification requires repair of the damaged area with at least a full‑depth Type A concrete patch at no additional cost to the Department, further separating it from other distresses that may not require such full‑depth treatment [2].

Where it occurs

Spalling is reported on Portland‑cement concrete pavements, including the slabs that form part of a concrete overlay or a concrete shoulder [1]. The distress appears as interior‑slab surface spalling, transverse or longitudinal joint spalling, corner spalls where longitudinal and transverse joints intersect, and on jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) [1]. In the Pennsylvania specifications spalling is reported on rigid concrete pavements, encompassing plain or reinforced cement concrete, long‑life concrete, and precast/prestressed concrete slabs [2]. Section 516 describes spalling as break‑back or spalled areas on existing concrete surfaces or newly placed patches of rigid pavement, requiring full‑depth transverse saw cuts beyond the damaged zone [2]. Section 525 limits spalling to interior slab surface spalling on rigid pavements and distinguishes Type 1 spot repairs for lengths between 15 inches and 6 feet and Type 2 extended repairs for lengths greater than 6 feet [2]. Section 519 notes that spalling may be encountered during full‑depth transverse saw cutting adjacent to longitudinal joints on rigid pavements, mandating placement of a new cut beyond the damaged area if break back or spalling occurs [2]. Section 530 identifies spalling especially along longitudinal edges and in zones between adjacent lanes or outside the pavement edge, treating such edge spalling as a defect that requires replacement of the affected long‑life concrete panel [2]. Section 506 describes spalling as a surface defect appearing along longitudinal edges, between adjacent lanes, or at pavement shoulders on plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements, and specifies that longitudinal edge spall repairs are not considered satisfactory, requiring removal and replacement of the defective portion [2]. In cross‑stitching of longitudinal joints (Section 528), when spalling of the concrete pavement surface occurs, the procedure calls for drilling shallow vertical holes at each tie‑bar location on the rigid slab [2]. Profile milling of asphalt pavement surfaces (Section 492) records that spalling can appear on the milled surface of an asphalt pavement, and when observed the affected area must be patched with an asphalt mixture of the same type [2]. Slab fracturing rehabilitation (Section 522) identifies spalling as an undesirable surface condition on rigid pavements during slab fracture work, requiring equipment not to cause extensive surface spalling along the crack [2]. Full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529) consider spalling only for these rigid concrete slabs, with any spalls to be repaired according to Section 501.3(t) [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 349–350), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 389–452)

In concrete pavements spalling is reported primarily at joints—both transverse and longitudinal—including the intersections of those joints, and also within the interior of slabs [1]. Section 4.3 of the Pavement Policy Manual notes that when a spall is limited to the top half of the slab it is treated with a partial‑depth joint repair, whereas spalls extending beyond mid‑slab depth require full‑depth joint repairs; interior spalls are addressed by cleaning and filling with wearing‑course material before overlay [1]. The manual further describes minor spalling at a transverse joint as potentially linked to poor joint performance and indicates that corner spalls that extend beyond mid‑slab depth are considered deeper distress [1]. Section 12.3 confirms that the distress appears in slab interiors along faulted joints and cracks, emphasizing that it is located within the concrete slab along joint lines rather than at edges, corners, wheel paths, layer interfaces, base or subgrade [1]. The 2026 Pennsylvania specifications state that spalling is found on the interior of concrete slabs and may be associated with transverse or longitudinal joints and cracks, describing the condition as interior slab surface spalling and explicitly indicating it occurs within the slab surface rather than at corners, edges, wheel paths, layer interfaces, or the base/subgrade [2]. In the full‑depth longitudinal joint repair requirements, spalling is reported to occur at the edges of the joint where saw cuts are made; if break back or spalling is observed along a cut, a new transverse saw‑cut beyond that area is required, showing the distress is localized at the joint perimeter [2]. The Long‑Life Concrete Pavement criteria treat spalling as a defect when it appears on the longitudinal edges of a concrete slab or in the zone between adjacent lanes or outside the pavement edge, indicating that such edge spalls are not satisfactory repairs and associating the distress with edge locations rather than joints, cracks, corners, wheel paths, slab interiors, layer interfaces, or the base/subgrade [2]. Likewise, the plain or reinforced cement‑concrete specifications observe spalling at the longitudinal edges of slabs—between adjacent lanes or beyond the pavement edge—and state that longitudinal edge‑spall repairs are not acceptable, reinforcing the confinement of the distress to slab periphery [2]. The cross‑stitching procedure notes that spalling can appear on the concrete surface where longitudinal joints or cracks are being treated, and shallow vertical holes are drilled at each tie‑bar location, indicating the spall is observed at the surface along the joint or crack rather than in corners, edges, wheel paths, interior slab areas, layer interfaces, or the base/subgrade [2]. During profile milling of asphalt surfaces, any area of the milled surface where spalling or dislodgement of unsound pavement occurs must be patched, showing that spalling is observed at the surface layer of the asphalt rather than specifically at joints, cracks, corners, edges, wheel paths, slab interiors, layer interfaces, or the base/subgrade [2]. Finally, slab fracturing specifications note that spalling can appear as surface loss along a crack when equipment causes excessive impact, identifying the distress at the exposed faces of cracks and again not attributing it to joints, corners, edges, wheel paths, interior slab areas, layer interfaces, or the base/subgrade [2].

Causes and mechanisms

In section 4.2 of the Pavement Policy Manual, spalling is treated as a surface distress that may receive a partial‑depth repair only when it is limited to the top half of the slab thickness and is not caused by severe joint problems such as loss of load transfer [1]. If the spalling is extensive, the guidance allows full‑depth replacement or placement of a thin‑bonded concrete inlay [1]. Section 4.4 notes that minor spalling at a transverse joint can be caused by poor joint performance, which is classified as a design or construction deficiency; traffic loading, climate, moisture‑drainage, and material causes are not discussed [1]. The manual does not provide a table classifying spalling by cause, so no row‑by‑row breakdown is given [1]. In SECTION 530—LONG‑LIFE CONCRETE PAVEMENT §530.3(t) of the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, spalling is identified as a defect that can arise from surface problems caused by rain and from construction shortcomings such as improper joint construction, improper curing procedures or improper final finish; edge‑slumping related spalling is not remedied by longitudinal edge spall repairs [2]. Similarly, SECTION 506—PLAIN OR REINFORCED CEMENT CONCRETE PAVEMENTS §506.3(t) lists surface defects caused by rain together with design‑ and construction‑related deficiencies including improper joint construction, improper curing procedures, improper final finish, cracking, or honeycombing as causes of spalling in rigid pavements [2]. Both sections omit traffic loading and material quality as causes, and neither provides a table classifying distresses by cause [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–91); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 389–452)

In the guidance for concrete pavement patching (section 4.2), spalling is described as developing when severe joint problems such as loss of load transfer cause the concrete to break away in the upper half of the slab depth; consequently it progresses as a symptom of joint deterioration rather than an isolated surface defect, and extensive surface spalling may justify full pavement replacement or a thin‑bonded concrete inlay. In the partial‑depth repair context (section 4.4), spalling is further attributed to compressive stresses and point‑loading forces that cause slab material to separate, particularly at joints where joint performance is poor; the mechanism is aggravated by repeated expansion–contraction cycles, with cool nights combined with high daytime temperatures creating critical conditions for additional breakaway, and progression can be limited only when the new patch is placed with sufficient clearance from adjacent slabs so that contact does not occur during these temperature swings. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–91)

Severity levels and rating

In the Pavement Policy Manual, Section 4.3 “Joints” defines spalling severity by depth: spalls confined to the top half of a slab are classified as partial‑depth spalls and are to be repaired in accordance with Publication 408 Specification Section 525, whereas spalls that extend beyond the mid‑slab depth are classified as full‑depth spalls and must be repaired according to Publication 408 Specification Section 519. The same section also requires that joints or slabs that are badly spalled, including those with interior spalls, shall be cleaned and filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material (or equivalent) before an overlay as directed in Publication 408 Specification Section 469. Section 4.4 “Concrete Pavement Partial‑Depth Repair” of the manual groups spalling by repair type based on length: interior slab surface spalls whose length lies between 15 inches and 6 feet are assigned a Type 1 spot repair, while spalling that exceeds 6 feet requires a Type 2 extended‑length repair. Publication 408/2026 Specification Section 525.1 repeats these length thresholds, specifying Type 1 spot repairs for interior slab surface spalling between 15 inches and 6 feet and Type 2 extended‑length repairs when spalling exceeds 6 feet. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 426)

The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) ties the assessment of spalling to Publication 408 Specifications and uses depth‑based criteria to dictate repair actions. For a longitudinal joint that has extensive spalls confined to the top half of the slab, the manual directs a partial‑depth spall repair as specified in Publication 408 Section 525. When spalls extend beyond mid‑slab depth on a longitudinal joint, the same manual requires a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair according to Publication 408 Section 519. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification 408 (2026) assigns a condition rating for spalling through the Defective Work procedure in Rigid Pavement Specification Section 506.3(t); under this procedure any pavement exhibiting spalling that cannot be satisfactorily repaired, as determined by the Representative, is classified as defective and must be removed and replaced, treating such spalling as a severe defect. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 389)

Measurement, survey and extent

Spalling on concrete pavements is quantified by measuring both its depth and its surface length; the depth is obtained by extracting representative cores from the spalled area to see how far the deterioration extends, while the surface length is recorded to classify the repair as a Type 1 spot repair when it falls between 15 inches and 6 feet or as a Type 2 extended‑length repair when it exceeds 6 feet. The depth measurement must confirm that the required repair does not exceed half the slab thickness before a partial‑depth patch, as stipulated in the Chapter 4, Section 4.4 guidelines (Publication 408, Specifications Section 525). [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 90–91)

Effects on performance

Partial‑depth repairs should only be made where spalling occurs in the top half of the slab depth and is not caused by severe joint problems, such as loss of load transfer; otherwise extensive surface spalling may also justify pavement replacement or the construction of a thin‑bonded concrete inlay. Because spalled areas undermine uniform support, they degrade ride quality and indicate reduced structural capacity, safety, durability and service life if left unaddressed. Spalling that appears as interior‑slab surface loss reduces the pavement’s rideability and shortens its remaining service life. A partial‑depth repair limited to no more than half the slab thickness for spalls extends the service life of concrete pavements and restores rideability, provided the work follows Publication 408 Section 525 procedures and uses appropriate joint material to avoid compression or point‑loading failures that could affect durability. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–91)

Repairs and treatments

Partial‑depth repair is prescribed for interior slab surface spalling and for joint or crack defects whose depth does not exceed half the slab thickness, and it may be applied as a Type 1 spot patch for spalls from 15 inches to 6 feet long or as a Type 2 extended patch for spalls longer than 6 feet (section 4.4) [1]. The same treatment is required when a longitudinal joint exhibits extensive spalling confined to the top half of the slab, in which case the joint shall receive partial‑depth spall repairs (section 4.3) [1]. Partial‑depth repair may be used only where spalling occurs in the upper half of the slab and is not caused by severe joint problems such as loss of load transfer (section 4.2) [1]. Full‑depth concrete pavement patching is required when spalling extends beyond the top half of the slab or is associated with severe joint deterioration, because those problems can only be corrected by full‑depth patching (section 4.2) [1]. Full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs are prescribed for spalls that penetrate beyond mid‑slab depth (section 4.3) and full‑depth corner repairs are indicated for corner spalls extending beyond mid‑slab depth (section 4.4) [1]. Concrete full‑depth patching and joint rehabilitation may be performed up to, but not exceeding, 10 % of the pavement area unless cost‑effectiveness for a larger share can be demonstrated (section 12.3), and concrete patching greater than 10 % of the pavement area is generally not cost‑effective or permitted for preservation projects (section 13.7) [1]. When a spalled concrete pavement will receive an overlay, the surface may simply be cleaned and filled with asphalt materials rather than applying concrete repair operations (section 4.2) [1]. Badly spalled joints and slabs that have interior spalls should be cleaned and filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material before placing an unbonded PCC or asphalt overlay (section 4.3) [1]. Bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays can be used to correct extensive surface spalling, provided the existing pavement is in relatively good condition, the subgrade is stable, and drainage is functional; the overlay adds structural capacity while eliminating the surface distress (section 4.9) [1]. Routine‑maintenance activities address spalling by performing spall repair, mechanized patch, or spray patch whenever spalling appears as an existing distress on the pavement (section 13.7) [1]. In preservation projects, concrete patching is employed to retard further deterioration and maintain functional condition before extensive damage occurs, subject again to the 10 % area limitation (section 13.7) [1]. If minor spalling at a transverse joint is caused by poor joint performance, slab stabilization together with dowel‑bar retrofit is recommended instead of a partial‑depth patch (section 4.4) [1]. Section 516 requires that a new full‑depth transverse saw‑cut be placed beyond the area of break back or spalling, and when spalling occurs in an adjacent lane the damaged portion must be repaired with at least a full‑depth Type A concrete patch (section 516) [2]. Any crack or spall that appears before final inspection triggers removal and replacement of the pavement at no extra cost, and cracked or spalled concrete patches or corner repairs are mandated to be removed and replaced when deemed defective (section 516) [2]. Section 525 states that partial‑depth repair may be used for interior slab surface spalling provided the required repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness; spot repairs of transverse or longitudinal joints, cracks, and interior slab surface spalling between 15 inches and 6 feet in length are prescribed as Type 1, while extended‑length repairs for spalls longer than 6 feet are prescribed as Type 2 (section 525) [2]. Section 519.3(b) requires making a new full‑depth transverse saw‑cut beyond the damaged area and placing the additional length of repair at no additional cost to the Department when spalling or break‑back occurs in a longitudinal joint (section 519) [2]. Section 530 classifies a lot as defective when surface spalling cannot be satisfactorily repaired, and requires removal and replacement of at least an entire LLCP panel; longitudinal edge spall repairs are explicitly excluded as satisfactory for edge‑slumping or spalling between adjacent lanes or outside pavement edges (section 530) [2]. Section 506.3(t) requires removal and replacement of a minimum of 10 ft of pavement between transverse joints—or an entire panel—when spalling is present and cannot be satisfactorily repaired as determined by the Representative; longitudinal edge‑spall repairs are expressly stated not to be satisfactory for edge slumping or spalling occurring between adjacent lanes or outside pavement edges (section 506) [2]. The cross‑stitching repair described in Section 528.3 addresses spalling of the concrete surface by drilling shallow vertical holes at each tie‑bar location and angled holes to intersect the crack, a step that is required only when spalling of the pavement surface occurs (section 528) [2]. During profile milling of asphalt pavement, Section 492.3 calls for surface patching as the repair that addresses spalling or dislodged unsound pavement; this patching is applied only to those milled‑surface locations designated by the Representative as needing repair (section 492) [2]. In the installation of precast/prestressed concrete slabs, Section 529.3 requires that all spalls identified as manufacturing, handling or construction damage be repaired in accordance with Section 501.3(t); this repair is mandated whenever such spalls are observed during installation (section 529) [2].

Partial‑depth concrete pavement repairs are limited to spalls that are confined to the upper half of the slab thickness and are not associated with severe joint deficiencies such as loss of load transfer (section 4.2). The same half‑thickness limitation applies to partial‑depth repairs on transverse or longitudinal joints, cracks, and interior slab surface spalling; these repairs may be used when the required repair depth does not exceed one‑half of the slab thickness (section 4.4) and are classified as Type 1 for spot repairs whose length is between 15 inches and 6 feet, while Type 2 is prescribed for longer sections exceeding 6 feet (section 4.4) [1]. Section 525.1 of the specifications reinforces this depth rule for interior slab surface spalling, allowing a partial‑depth repair only when the spall depth is no greater than half the slab thickness and directing that any required depth beyond that limit be treated with full‑depth patches per Section 516 (section 525) [2]. When spalling extends beyond the mid‑slab depth, full‑depth concrete pavement patching is mandated; such patches may not exceed ten percent of the total pavement area unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided, otherwise the treatment is ruled out for Pavement Preservation projects (section 12.3) [1]. Full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs are required when spalling observed during removal of existing pavement necessitates a new full‑depth transverse saw‑cut beyond the spalled zone; the additional length is incorporated into the repair at no extra cost to the Department (section 519.3(c)) [2]. For corner spalls located at the intersection of longitudinal and transverse joints that extend past the midpoint of the slab, full‑depth corner repairs as specified in Sections 516 and 519 are prescribed (section 4.4) [1]. Longitudinal edge spall repairs are expressly excluded as satisfactory when the spalling occurs between adjacent lanes or outside pavement edges; instead a minimum removal and replacement of 10 feet of pavement between transverse joints for reinforced cement concrete, or an entire panel for plain cement concrete, is required (sections 530.3(t) and 506.3(t)) [2]. Cross‑stitching remains applicable in the presence of surface spalling, requiring only shallow vertical holes to be drilled at each tie‑bar location without any defined severity, depth, width or extent limits (section 528.3) [2]. During profile‑milling operations, if spalling or dislodgement of unsound pavement is identified by the Representative, a surface‑patch repair must be performed that involves excavation to match existing depth, cleaning, tack coating, and filling with an asphalt mixture of the same type (section 492.3(c)) [2]. All spalls observed on full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slabs must be repaired according to Section 501.3(t), which does not impose specific size limits (section 529.3) [2]. Extensive surface spalling may also justify complete pavement replacement or the construction of a thin‑bonded concrete inlay, and for pavements slated for an unbonded PCC or asphalt overlay badly spalled joints and interior slabs should be cleaned and filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material (or equivalent) prior to overlay placement (section 4.2 and 4.3) [1]. Routine Maintenance activities include a spall‑repair component that can address minor spalling without any stated severity limit (section 13.7) [1].

Prevention in design and construction

The manual states that a partial‑depth repair should be used only where spalling is confined to the upper half of the slab and is not caused by severe joint loss of load transfer, thereby targeting shallow, joint‑independent surface loss (Section 4.2) [1]. When spalls extend beyond mid‑slab depth, full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs (Specification Section 519) or full‑depth corner repairs (Specification Section 516) are prescribed to address deeper or more extensive deterioration [1]. Badly spalled joints and slabs with interior spalls should be cleaned and filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material (or equivalent) prior to placing an unbonded PCC or asphalt overlay, eliminating loose fragments before the overlay (Publication 408, Specification Section 469) [1]. For longitudinal joints that exhibit extensive spalling in the top half of the slab, partial‑depth spall repairs per Specification Section 525 are required, while transverse joints with minor spalling caused by poor joint performance may be treated with slab stabilization together with dowel‑bar retrofit [1]. To prevent compression or point‑loading failure of partial‑depth patches that could otherwise lead to further spalling, a rigid polystyrene board for longitudinal joints or premolded expansion joint material for transverse joints must be installed between the patch and the adjacent slab, equal in width to the existing joint (Publication 408) [1]. A bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlay can be used to correct extensive surface spalling when the existing concrete pavement is in relatively good condition, the subgrade is stable, and drainage is functional [1]. Routine‑maintenance actions that mitigate spalling include cyclic crack sealing of asphalt‑surfaced pavements, which should be done every three to five years to address surface cracking that could evolve into spall, and joint sealing of concrete pavements, which should be performed every five years to treat joint deterioration that leads to joint spalling; both activities are part of the routine‑maintenance package that also includes direct spall repair [1]. When surface spalling is observed, the specification prescribes drilling shallow vertical holes at each tie‑bar location as a remedial action addressing existing surface spalling (Section 528.3) [2]. Impact equipment used for slab fracturing shall not cause extensive surface spalling, punch holes, or shatter the concrete, thereby preventing spalling during the fracture operation (Section 522.3) [2]. Sonic vibratory equipment may be used only when the fracturing will not cause soil liquefaction of the subgrade, a condition intended to avoid subgrade‑related damage that could manifest as spalling (Section 522.3) [2]. The pneumatic roller for seating operations shall not be operated under wet conditions, as determined by the Representative, which reduces moisture‑induced shattering and subsequent spalling of pavement edges (Section 522.3) [2]. Finally, the fractured and seated surface must not be left exposed to freezing conditions before the asphalt overlay, a restriction aimed at preventing freeze‑thaw induced spalling (Section 522.3) [2].

For spalling that extends beyond the top half of the concrete slab depth or when joint loss of load transfer is present, full‑depth concrete pavement patching that removes all deteriorated slab and includes dowelled load transfer as specified in Publication 408 Section 516 should be considered (section 4.2) [1]. Where spalling is confined to the top half of the slab depth and is not caused by severe joint problems, partial‑depth repair may be used, addressing minor surface loss (section 4.2) [1]. When a PCC pavement is to be patched but not overlaid, cleaning and sealing all joints and cracks with rubberized joint‑sealing material is required to protect the joint from moisture intrusion and thereby help prevent spalling (section 4.3) [1]. For pavements that will receive an overlay, badly spalled joints and slabs with interior spalls shall be cleaned and filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material (or equivalent) before placing the overlay, preventing further spalling of those areas (section 4.3) [1]. Joint rehabilitation that creates new sealant reservoirs and reseals joints is applicable only when restoring existing concrete pavements (not overlaying) and is most cost‑effective on pavements just beginning to exhibit minor joint distress, addressing spalling that can develop from unsealed joints (section 4.3) [1]. Longitudinal joints with extensive spalls in the top half of the slab shall be repaired with partial‑depth spall repairs as specified in Publication 408 Section 525, while spalls extending beyond mid‑slab depth require full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs per Section 519, directly treating surface and deeper spalling respectively (section 4.3) [1]. If minor spalling at a transverse joint is caused by poor joint performance, slab stabilization and dowel‑bar retrofit should be considered (section 4.4) [1]. Installing a rigid polystyrene board for longitudinal‑joint patches or a premolded expansion‑joint material of equal width to the existing joint between the patch and the adjacent slab eliminates compression and point‑loading failures that could otherwise cause spalling of the repair (section 4.4) [1]. When patches are placed while pavement slabs are contracted—typically during cool nights with high daytime temperatures—the repair must provide adequate space between the patch and the adjacent slab to prevent compression failure by point loading, thereby preventing spalling of the repair area (section 4.4) [1]. A bonded jointed plain‑concrete pavement overlay may be used to correct extensive surface spalling only when the existing concrete pavement is in relatively good condition, the subgrade is stable, and drainage functions properly (section 4.9) [1]. Proper joint construction and proper curing procedures are required so that surface defects such as rain‑induced cracking, honeycombing or spalling are avoided when a lot is evaluated for “Other Conditions” (Section 530.3(t)) [2]. Compliance with the edge‑slumping criteria in Section 501.3(g)5 is also required because failure to meet those limits makes the pavement defective, and longitudinal edge spall repairs are not accepted as a cure for edge slumping or spalling between lanes (Section 530.3(t)) [2]. Only impact equipment able to apply a controllable variable force may be used during slab fracturing, and any machine that would cause extensive surface spalling, punch holes, or shatter the concrete is explicitly forbidden (Section 522.3(a)) [2]. After fractured slabs are seated, crews must not pulverize the pavement and must take care to avoid shattering edges of full‑depth asphalt patches, thereby preventing surface spalling of the concrete (Section 522.3(g)) [2].

Specification limits and acceptance

A partial‑depth repair may be performed only when the spalling is confined to the upper half of the slab thickness, i.e., the repair depth must not exceed 50 % of the slab thickness [1][2]. When the surface spall measures between 15 inches and 6 feet in length it is classified as a Type 1 spot repair, whereas any spall longer than 6 feet requires a Type 2 extended‑length repair; these classifications are set out in Publication 408 Specification Section 525 [1][2]. For longitudinal joints, if the extensive spalling is limited to the top half of the slab the joint shall be repaired with a partial‑depth spall repair in accordance with Publication 408 Specification Section 525, while spalls that extend below the mid‑slab depth must be addressed with a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair as prescribed in Publication 408 Specification Section 519 [1]. Badly spalled joints and interior spalls slated for overlay are required to be cleaned and filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material or an equivalent, per Publication 408 Specification Section 469 [1]. Longitudinal edge spall repairs are expressly excluded from being considered satisfactory repairs for edge slumping or spalling between adjacent lanes or outside pavement edges; this acceptance condition is qualitative and appears in the Long‑Life Concrete Pavement Defective Work clause (Section 530, subsection t) [2]. A concrete pavement is deemed defective when a surface defect cannot be satisfactorily repaired as determined by the Representative, and the corrective action prescribed is to remove and replace at least 10 feet of pavement between transverse joints or an entire panel, as stated in the Defective Work clause of Section 506.3(t) [2]. Under the Design Guidelines (Section 12.3), projects that would require concrete patching covering more than 10 % of the total pavement area are ineligible for Pavement Preservation unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided, and the patching quantity must include faulted joints, cracks, existing asphalt patches, failed concrete patches, and transverse or longitudinal spalls and cracks [1].

When spalling is observed under Section 516—Concrete Pavement Patching, the specification requires that the Contractor remove and replace the affected concrete patch—either by cutting a new full‑depth transverse saw‑cut and adding patch length or by fully removing and replacing the pavement—at no additional cost to the Department; this remedial work must be performed as soon as the spall is detected, i.e., before final inspection or when identified as defective work, with no separate time limit beyond immediate correction. When spalling is encountered during a full‑depth longitudinal concrete joint repair under Section 519—Full Depth Longitudinal Concrete Joint Repair, Section 519.3(b) requires the contractor to cut a new full depth transverse saw‑cut beyond the spalled area and to extend the repair length at no additional cost to the Department; this remedial action is performed as part of the construction work and the specification does not prescribe a separate time limit. When spalling reaches the limit set by the Long‑Life Concrete Pavement specification in Section 530, the required remedy is to remove and replace at least an entire panel of long‑life concrete pavement as shown on the Standard Drawings; this removal and replacement is directed by the District Executive under Section 110.10(d)1 and must be performed by the responsible contractor, and the source does not provide a specific time period for completing the work. When spalling is identified as a defect under Section 506—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements (clause t Defective Work), the specification requires removal and replacement of the affected concrete, with at least ten feet of pavement between transverse joints to be removed (or an entire panel for plain cement concrete) because longitudinal edge spall repairs are not considered satisfactory; the removal and replacement is to be carried out under direction of the District Executive as referenced in Section 110.10(d)1, with the contractor performing the work, and the excerpt does not specify a time limit for completing this remedial action. When spalling is identified during profile milling under Section 492—Profile Milling of Asphalt Pavement Surfaces, Section 492.3(c) requires that the affected area be patched; the Representative designates the locations and the contractor must excavate to the existing depth, clean loose material, apply emulsified asphalt tack coat, fill with an asphalt mix of the same type, then level and compact so it matches adjacent pavement, and the specification does not prescribe a specific calendar time for completing this patching.
