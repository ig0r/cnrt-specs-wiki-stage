Asphalt Stabilization

Part of: Stabilizing Agent is-subtopic-of
Asphalt Stabilization is a topic under Stabilizing Agent.
asphalt-stabilizationemulsified-asphalt-stabilizationasphalt-stabilized-layersasphalt-stabilized-fdrasphalt-material-stabilization
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Asphalt stabilization is a full‑depth reclamation pavement treatment that blends the existing flexible pavement and a portion of the underlying material with an asphalt binder or other stabilizing agents to produce a stabilized base course [1]. By pulverizing, mixing, and compacting the reclaimed material—often with emulsified asphalt, cement, lime, calcium‑ or magnesium‑chloride, or additional aggregate—and then curing it to at least 95 % of laboratory maximum density and a minimum seven‑day indirect tensile strength of roughly 50 psi, the process raises structural capacity and enhances resistance to water‑related damage, fatigue cracking, and freeze‑thaw cycling [1][2]. Variants such as hot‑asphalt grout injection beneath slabs or pre‑coated aggregate seal coats follow the same principle, requiring controlled temperatures (380 °F–450 °F for grout, ≤350 °F for coating), specified injection pressures (25–45 psi), and coverage criteria (≥90 % of visible aggregate surface) to ensure a durable, load‑bearing layer [2].

Material purpose and applicability

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), asphalt stabilization is a form of pavement treatment within the full‑depth reclamation (FDR) process in which the existing flexible pavement and a portion of the underlying material are mixed with an asphalt binder or other stabilizing agents to produce a stabilized base course; this treatment may be combined with mechanical or chemical actions and additional stabilizing material may be added to further improve the integrity of the recycled product, thereby enhancing the structural capacity of the rebuilt layer [1]. In the same manual, the section on full‑depth reclamation techniques (section 5.15 B. Selection of Projects) states that adding an asphalt binder—such as emulsified‑asphalt stabilization—during reclamation stabilizes and compacts the reclaimed layer, which increases its strength and contributes to the structural integrity of the rebuilt pavement [1]. The manual further describes, in section 5.15 INTENTIONALLY BLANK, a treatment that incorporates emulsified asphalt—often combined with granular material, cement or lime—to bind reclaimed pavement particles, enhancing the pavement’s ability to resist water‑related damage and increasing its fatigue resistance under traffic loading, thereby improving overall durability [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications define several distinct uses of asphalt stabilization. Section 679 Slab Stabilization specifies Asphalt Grout Stabilization in which asphalt is heated to a temperature of 380 °F to 450 °F before pumping begins and the hot grout is injected under the slab through drilled holes at a pressure of 25 psi to 45 psi; the grout fills voids beneath the pavement to provide support and control slab movement, and upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed [2]. The same section requires that the grout employed meet ASTM D3141 with a minimum flash point of 500 °F and be placed together with approved cylindrical plugs and rapid‑set concrete patching materials; the grout binds the material and thereby stabilizes the slab, improving its structural integrity for pavement construction and maintenance [2]. Section 471 Asphalt Seal Coat Using Precoated Aggregate defines asphalt stabilization as the preparation of a pre‑coated aggregate by applying a thin film of asphalt cement—or other asphalt material—so that at least 90 % of the visible surface area is covered; the coating contains between 0.6 % and 1.2 % residual binder when asphalt cement is used (or 0.4 % to 1.0 % with other materials) and is mixed at temperatures not exceeding 350 °F (150 °F for cut‑back asphalts), and the resulting pre‑coated aggregate is spread as an asphalt seal coat on flexible pavements, fulfilling the seal‑coat role specified in the pavement system [2]. Finally, Section 344 Full Depth Reclamation describes asphalt stabilization as the reclamation step where pulverized existing roadway material is combined with a stabilizing additive such as cement, calcium chloride, emulsified asphalt or foamed asphalt and water according to an approved mix design; the mixture is compacted to a minimum density of at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum density and cured so that it develops the required strength, for example a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi, thereby providing a durable base capable of supporting traffic loads and receiving the final pavement overlay [2].

Composition and characteristics

Asphalt stabilization is identified as one of the possible full‑depth reclamation methods and may be performed by mechanical, chemical, asphalt or other processes that blend reclaimed pavement material with additional stabilizing constituents to form a stabilized base course [1]. The resulting asphalt‑stabilized layer exhibits a structural capacity expressed through its structure‑layer coefficient, which is generally comparable to existing layer coefficients for cold recycling and falls between typical subbase values and higher‑performance stabilized materials [1]. In the simplest mechanical approach, only the in‑place reclaimed material and moisture are used to aid compaction; more elaborate mechanical stabilization involves pulverizing the existing pavement, reshaping it, recompacting it, and adding imported aggregate or reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) to improve gradation and structural integrity [1]. Chemical stabilization incorporates wet or dry chemical additives—most commonly Portland cement, lime, fly ash, and occasionally limekiln dust—which may be applied either dry or as a slurry ahead of the reclaimer or introduced through a spray bar within the mixing chamber; increasing stabilizer content generally raises strength, although excessive amounts can cause brittleness [1]. Calcium chloride or magnesium chloride are cited as particle‑cementing additives that increase strength and lower the freezing temperature of the mix, thereby enhancing freeze‑thaw resistance; when calcium chloride is used, a minimum 35 % solution is applied at a rate of 0.1–0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of reclaimed depth [1]. Emulsified asphalt typically serves as the primary binder in asphalt stabilization and can be combined with granular aggregates, cement or lime to improve water resistance and fatigue performance [1].

In slab stabilization that employs hot asphalt grout, the grout is heated to a temperature range of 380 °F to 450 °F, and any asphalt exceeding 500 °F is rejected; it is pumped at pressures between 25 psi and 45 psi through a reversible underseal pump into vertically drilled round holes not larger than 1½ inches in diameter, with water applied around each hole to prevent seepage and slab lift monitored by equipment capable of detecting movement as small as 0.001 inch, the allowable upward movement not exceeding 0.05 inch [2]. The grout composition consists of three defined constituents: asphalt cement that must conform to ASTM D3141 and possess a minimum flash point of 500 °F, cylindrical plugs with tapered ends approved by the Representative, and rapid‑set concrete patching materials supplied by a Bulletin 15 listed manufacturer, used within their shelf life and temperature limits [2].

The pre‑coated aggregate seal coat specification achieves asphalt stabilization by coating coarse aggregate with a thin film of asphalt binder; when asphalt cement is used the residual binder must be between 0.6 % and 1.2 % by weight of the mix, while other asphalt materials require 0.4 % to 1.0 %; the film should appear brownish and translucent, cover at least 90 % of the aggregate’s visible surface, and any uncoated particles passing a No. 200 (75 µm) sieve must not exceed 0.5 %; production temperatures are limited to a mixing temperature not exceeding 350 °F for cement‑based pre‑coat and an aggregate temperature not exceeding 150 °F when cut‑back asphalts are used [2].

For full‑depth reclamation, asphalt stabilization consists of a pulverized existing roadway mixed with any required aggregates, RAP, stabilizing additives such as cement or chemical agents (including calcium chloride, emulsified or foamed asphalt) and water at the mix‑design moisture level; the blend is compacted to a minimum density of at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum density at optimum moisture content and cured for seven days while kept damp with the moisture content not exceeding optimum by more than 3 % [2]. When cement‑stabilized, the stabilized layer must achieve a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi; chemically stabilized mixes must attain an unconfined compressive strength of 300 to 500 psi (or 280 to 500 psi depending on overlay thickness) after seven days [2].

In section 2.1 GENERAL of the Pavement Policy Manual, variations in the type of stabilizing material used during full‑depth reclamation may lead to differences in the resulting structural capacity, expressed as the structure layer coefficient; basic pulverization produces a product similar in support characteristics to a standard 2A subbase material, stabilization with calcium chloride or similar additives is slightly improved, asphalt‑stabilized layers can generally be considered similar to existing layer coefficients for cold recycling, and chemical stabilization using cement, lime, and similar additives provides support stiffness equivalent to or slightly better than that achieved from asphalt material stabilization, while adding extra stabilizing material can further improve the integrity of the recycled product [1]. In section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES > B. Selection of Projects, variations in composition—such as the type and amount of chemical stabilizer or the addition of aggregate or RAP—affect the reclaimed layer’s performance; generally increasing the amount of chemical stabilizer increases strength, but excessive amounts may result in brittleness, so the type and content of stabilizer should be determined by laboratory testing [1]. In the same section, mechanical stabilization is accomplished by pulverizing the existing pavement, reshaping, and re‑compacting the reclaimed material as with pulverization stabilization, but also adding aggregate material or RAP to improve gradation, using only the reclaimed in‑place materials with moisture added solely to aid compaction [1]. Section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES > INTENTIONALLY BLANK states that the type and amount of stabilizing material—whether chlorides such as calcium chloride or magnesium chloride, or emulsified asphalt—directly influence strength and durability outcomes; calcium chloride and magnesium chloride additives result in some gain in strength due to particle cementing and also lower the freezing temperature of the reclaimed material, improving resistance to freeze‑thaw cycles, and their effectiveness generally requires applying a minimum 35 % solution at a rate of 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each 1 inch of depth reclaimed [1]. Emulsified asphalt offers improved resistance to water‑related damage and greater fatigue resistance under loading, and its performance can be further modified by combining it with granular materials, cement, or lime as part of the mix design [1]. In SECTION 471—ASPHALT SEAL COAT USING PRECOATED AGGREGATE > 471.2 MATERIAL— of Publication 408/2026, variations in the type of asphalt material and its binder content directly change the residual asphalt binder left on the aggregate; using asphalt cement calls for a pre‑coating of 0.6 % to 1.2 % binder, while other asphalt materials require only 0.4 % to 1.0 %, which influences the thickness and durability of the brownish translucent film [2]. The same specification imposes processing limits such that mixing must be performed at a temperature not exceeding 350°F for cement‑based coats and aggregate temperatures may not exceed 150°F when cut‑back asphalts are used, conditions that affect how well the coating adheres and cures [2]. Proportioning of the seal coat must achieve coverage of at least 90 % of the visible aggregate surface; if coverage falls short, acceptance hinges on a sieve test where the percent passing the No. 200 (75 µm) sieve may not exceed 0.5 %, linking fine‑particle content to final performance [2]. In SECTION 344—FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION > 344.3 CONSTRUCTION— (e) Reclamation, because the mix design dictates the proportions of reclaimed material, aggregates, RAP, stabilizing additive(s), and water, any variation in composition or source must be compensated by field adjustments to keep the FDR material within the required density of at least 95 % of the laboratory‑compacted maximum at optimum moisture content and to achieve the specified strength ranges; moisture content may not exceed the optimum by more than 3 %, and mixing and compaction must be completed within four hours of water/additive addition [2]. The curing period is also sensitive to material type, amount, and climate, so that strength gain can vary from a few days to several weeks, and overly high compressive strengths above 900 psi may induce shrinkage cracking requiring rework [2]. In SECTION 679—SLAB STABILIZATION > 679.2 MATERIAL—, the basic composition determines which performance criteria apply; an asphalt grout must contain asphalt cement that complies with ASTM D3141 and has a flash point of at least 500°F, and each shipment must be accompanied by a supplier certification from a Bulletin 15 listed manufacturer, implying that deviations from these specified components could compromise the intended stabilization performance [2]. When the material source is altered in a cement‑based mix, the specification requires submission of a new mix design because changes in source can affect compressive strength, setting time, expansion, bleed and other properties [2].

Key material properties

Physical moisture conditions must be controlled; adequate moisture is required for compaction during pulverization, while excess water in the subgrade can cause loss of material integrity, and in full‑depth reclamation the mix must be placed at its optimum moisture content and the moisture may not exceed the optimum by more than 3 % [1][2]. Mechanically, the strength potential of the reclaimed mix governs stiffness and may be increased by adding aggregate to improve gradation or by applying chemical additives such as cement, lime, or fly ash; however, excessive stabilizer content generally leads to brittleness [1]. The stabilized material is required to achieve a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi after seven days and an unconfined compressive strength between 300 psi and 500 psi (or 280 psi to 500 psi for thicker overlays) as measured in accordance with the specified test methods [2]. Chemically, the addition of calcium chloride and magnesium chloride provides a gain in strength through particle cementing, while cement used as a binder shall conform to ASTM D3141 and possess a minimum flash point of 500 °F [1][2]. Thermally, the chloride additives lower the freezing temperature of the reclaimed material, improving resistance to freeze‑thaw cycles [1]. During slab stabilization the asphalt grout must be heated to a temperature between 380 °F and 450 °F before pumping; any material heated to 500 °F or more will be rejected, and the overall mixing temperature for seal‑coat applications shall not exceed 350 °F, with aggregate heated with cut‑back asphalts limited to 150 °F [2]. Physical appearance requirements for pre‑coated aggregate include a residual binder content of 0.6 % to 1.2 % by weight of the mix (or 0.4 % to 1.0 % when other asphalt materials are used), at least 90 % of the visible surface must be covered by a thin translucent film, and fines passing a No. 200 sieve must remain below 0.5 % after dry sieving [2]. The grout is injected at a pressure ranging from 25 psi to 45 psi, and the upward movement of the pavement may not exceed 0.05 inch so that the slab remains level [2].

Classification may be based on differences in structural capacity and the associated structure layer coefficient for various stabilization materials, while an alternative approach groups treatments by mechanism—pulverization, mechanical, chemical, calcium chloride, or emulsified‑asphalt stabilization—as recorded in the material design and quality‑control section [1]. The appropriate type and amount of stabilizer should be determined through laboratory testing, which also establishes the mix composition required for the reclaimed layer [1]. For quality assessment, Percent Within Limits (PWL) captures both the mean and the standard deviation in a single measure, and its standard‑deviation component influences the graduated pay factors for asphalt content, percent passing the No. 200 sieve, percent passing the primary control sieve, and pavement in‑place density; density acceptance is normally verified by pavement core testing [1]. Field performance of the treated pavement is further evaluated with in‑situ stiffness tests such as Falling Weight Deflectometer, Dynamic Cone Penetrometer, or Light Weight Deflectometer conducted before and after construction to assess strength [1]. In slab‑stabilization projects that employ asphalt grout stabilization, the amount of asphalt used must be measured with a certified method and temperature readout equipment on the transport tanker, and certification for the accuracy of that method is required [2]. Vertical movement testing equipment shall be capable of detecting slab lift to measurements of 0.001 inch, and any upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed [2]. During pumping, the injection pressure shall be maintained between 25 pounds per square inch and 45 pounds per square inch [2]. The asphalt cement used in the grout must conform to ASTM D3141 and have a minimum flash point of 500°F; each shipment must be accompanied by a supplier certification as required in Section 106.03(b)3 [2]. For an asphalt seal‑coat that uses precoated aggregate, adequacy is first judged by a visual inspection that must show at least 90 % of the aggregate’s visible surface covered with a thin brownish film; if coverage falls short, the uncoated fraction may be quantified by dry sieving the material for ten minutes on a No. 200 (75 µm) sieve, and the pre‑coated aggregate is accepted only when the percent passing does not exceed 0.5 % [2]. The residual binder applied to the aggregate must be within the specified ranges—0.6 % to 1.2 % by weight for asphalt cement or 0.4 % to 1.0 % by weight for other asphalt materials [2]. In full‑depth reclamation, the stabilized base is characterized by its in‑place density, moisture condition, and strength; density is verified on a 300‑ft control strip using calibrated nuclear gauges operated in direct transmission mode per PTM No. 402, with ten random readings, and the reclaimed material must reach at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum density at optimum moisture content [2]. Moisture content is determined before mixing by ASTM D2216, ASTM D44643 or AASHTO T 265 and may not exceed the design optimum by more than 3 % [2]. Strength is classified according to the stabilization type: asphalt‑stabilized full‑depth reclamation must achieve a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi when tested in accordance with Bulletin 27 on specimens compacted in the Superpave Gyratory Compactor, whereas chemically stabilized material must attain an unconfined compressive strength between 300 and 500 psi after seven days as measured by ASTM D1633 [2].

Behavior and mechanisms

When calcium chloride or magnesium chloride is incorporated as stabilizing agents in reclaimed asphalt mixtures, the additives cement particles, providing a modest increase in strength and lowering the freezing point of the mix, which enhances resistance to freeze‑thaw cycling [1]. The use of emulsified asphalt as a stabilizer further improves performance by increasing resistance to water‑related damage and delivering better fatigue resistance under repeated traffic loading [1]. In full‑depth reclamation projects that employ asphalt stabilization, the mixture must be compacted at the optimum moisture content determined from the project mix design and may not exceed that optimum by more than 3 % [2]. After placement, the cured layer is kept damp while its cure rate accelerates under favorable weather conditions—no rain, sunshine, low humidity, and high temperature—and the curing period can range from a few days to two weeks depending on those conditions [2]. The stabilized base is required to develop a compressive strength of 300–500 psi for chemically stabilized material or an indirect tensile strength of 50 psi for asphalt‑stabilized material within seven days before unlimited traffic may be permitted [2]. Proof rolling with the heaviest vehicle expected is used to verify that no damage occurs at speeds under 10 mph, and light vehicles may be allowed once a stable condition is reached while heavy trucks must wait until the seven‑day strength criterion is satisfied [2]. If the compressive strength of the chemically stabilized layer exceeds about 900 psi, shrinkage cracking may develop, indicating that excessive stiffness can be detrimental to service performance [2]. The combined use of chloride additives and emulsified asphalt therefore helps the pavement maintain durability under moisture ingress, temperature fluctuations, and cyclic traffic loads [1].

In asphalt stabilization the performance is controlled by a combination of physical, chemical and mechanical mechanisms that vary with the specific treatment being applied. Physical mechanisms include pulverizing the existing pavement to a specified size and depth, which re‑compacts the material using only moisture, followed by compaction to at least 95 % of laboratory maximum density while maintaining moisture within three percent of optimum; these actions establish the required particle arrangement and void reduction [1][2]. Chemical mechanisms involve the reaction of wet or dry additives such as calcium chloride, cement, lime and fly ash with the reclaimed mix; a minimum 35 % calcium‑chloride solution is applied at 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of reclaimed depth, and during the prescribed curing period these additives develop a seven‑day unconfined compressive strength of 300 to 500 psi (280 psi for foamed asphalt) and an indirect tensile strength of 50 psi, while excessive stabilizer may induce brittleness [1][2]. Mechanical mechanisms are achieved by adding aggregate or reclaimed asphalt pavement during pulverization to improve gradation and structural integrity, thorough mixing at the design moisture, a defined roller pattern on a compaction control strip, and proof rolling with the heaviest expected vehicle to verify that density no longer increases with additional passes; in slab‑stabilization operations the behavior of the asphalt grout is governed primarily by thermal control—heating the asphalt to a temperature between 380 °F and 450 °F so that its viscosity permits flow—and hydraulic control—injecting the material at a pressure limited to 25 psi to 45 psi to fill voids without over‑lifting the slab, while mechanical actions such as drilling vertical holes, blowing compressed air to clear them, applying water around the hole, and continuously monitoring slab lift with equipment capable of detecting movement to 0.001 inch (upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited) further influence placement [2]. The seal‑coat that uses precoated aggregate relies on the physical presence of a thin brownish translucent film covering at least 90 % of the visible surface area, which is achieved by using an asphalt cement that leaves 0.6 % to 1.2 % residual binder (or other asphalt materials leaving 0.4 % to 1.0 %) and mixing at a temperature not exceeding 350 °F (cut‑back asphalts require aggregate temperatures not exceeding 150 °F); mechanical control is provided by sieving the coated material after production so that any uncoated fraction passing a No. 200 (75 µm) sieve does not exceed 0.5 %, ensuring continuity of the film and intended physical integrity [2]. Emulsified asphalt contributes both physical and mechanical benefits by coating aggregate particles, thereby reducing water ingress, limiting moisture‑related damage, and enhancing fatigue resistance under repeated traffic loading [1].

Durability and deterioration

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES > B. Selection of Projects, excess moisture may undermine the durability of asphalt stabilization in full‑depth reclamation by causing loss of pavement material integrity and weakening the pavement’s structural capacity [1]. The same source notes that applying too much chemical stabilizer may make the reclaimed mix brittle [1]. It further requires that any pre‑existing distresses such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes or drainage problems be identified and corrected through a distress survey so that those damage mechanisms are not repeated in the stabilized layer [1]. In section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES > INTENTIONALLY BLANK, durability of asphalt stabilization is challenged primarily by moisture infiltration and cyclic freezing‑thawing, which can degrade the reclaimed material if not properly treated [1]. The use of calcium or magnesium chloride lowers the freezing temperature of the reclaimed mix, thereby improving its resistance to freeze‑thaw effects [1]. Emulsified asphalt provides a protective film that enhances resistance to water‑related damage and increases fatigue resistance under traffic loading [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification (Publication 408/2026) section SECTION 344—FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION > 344.3 CONSTRUCTION > (e) Reclamation and section SECTION 679—SLAB STABILIZATION > 679.3 CONSTRUCTION > (c) Asphalt Grout Stabilization state that durability of asphalt grout stabilization is governed by strict temperature, pressure and slab‑lift limits together with a minimum curing time before traffic [2]. Asphalt must be heated only within 380 °F to 450 °F [2]. Injection pressure is limited to 25–45 psi to prevent over‑pressurizing that could force asphalt into joints and cause uplift [2]. Upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed as an indication of loss of support [2]. The treated area must remain closed for a minimum of 30 minutes after grouting so the material can congeal before traffic loads are applied [2]. Overworking the stabilized layer because excessive handling may detrimentally affect the ultimate strength of the stabilized layer [2]. Strength development must reach the specified 7‑day unconfined compressive strength—300 to 500 psi for thin overlays or 280 to 500 psi for thicker overlays—or an indirect tensile strength of 50 psi for asphalt‑stabilized mixes [2]. Chemically stabilized material tested to strengths greater than 900 psi may result in shrinkage cracking and require rework [2]. Maintaining moisture within design limits is critical because the moisture content cannot exceed optimum by more than 3% [2]. Achieving at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum density is required to avoid rejected lots and additional compaction [2]. Opening the pavement to traffic before the 7‑day strength is achieved or allowing heavy loads during curing may cause damage that must be repaired [2].

Moisture infiltration increases susceptibility because excess water in any pavement layer or subgrade can degrade material integrity and weaken structural capacity, so a well‑drained pavement system must be identified and corrected before reclamation. Using only the amount of cement, lime, fly ash or other additives prescribed by laboratory mix‑design testing reduces susceptibility, since while increasing stabilizer generally raises strength, excessive amounts may cause brittleness that heightens cracking risk. Applying calcium chloride or magnesium chloride at a minimum 35 % solution and a dosage of 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each 1 inch of depth reclaimed lowers the freezing temperature of the reclaimed material, improving resistance to freeze‑thaw cycling and thus reducing deterioration. Employing emulsified asphalt as the stabilizing binder enhances resistance to water‑related damage and provides better fatigue performance under loading, further decreasing susceptibility to degradation. Compaction below the required density—specifically less than 95 % of the laboratory maximum density—increases the likelihood of deterioration in an asphalt‑stabilized full‑depth reclamation layer. When the moisture content for compaction exceeds the optimum by more than 3 percent, susceptibility to deterioration rises. Over‑working the stabilized material during finishing and allowing traffic before the mandated 7‑day strength has been achieved also increase the risk of degradation. Excessive compressive strength above 900 psi may induce shrinkage cracking, thereby raising susceptibility. Maintaining the reclaimed base at a minimum of 95 % of the laboratory maximum density and keeping moisture within 3 percent of optimum reduces the likelihood of deterioration. Protecting the surface with daily water, an emulsified asphalt primer, or fog seal to keep it damp, curing under favorable weather (no rain, sunshine, low humidity, high temperature), and restricting traffic until strength criteria are met all serve to lower susceptibility to deterioration. Material interactions and compatibility

Asphalt stabilization, typically applied with emulsified asphalt, enhances the pavement’s resistance to water‑related damage and fatigue, indicating improved binder‑aggregate adhesion that reduces stripping. [1] In full‑depth reclamation systems the stabilized mix can incorporate granular materials, cement, and lime, which are reported to work well together within the FDR system. [1] When chlorides are used for stabilization, calcium chloride is applied as a minimum 35 % solution at a rate of 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of reclaimed depth, providing particle cementing that adds strength and lowers the freezing temperature of the reclaimed material. [1] For seal‑coat applications the stabilization is achieved by pre‑coating coarse aggregate with a thin film of binder that must cover at least 90 % of the visible aggregate surface. [2] If an asphalt cement is used, the residual binder in the film is limited to 0.6 %–1.2 % of the mix weight and the mixture is blended at a temperature not exceeding 350 °F to ensure proper adhesion without overheating. [2] When damp aggregate and cut‑back asphalts are employed, the pre‑coating must contain 0.4 %–1.0 % residual binder and the aggregate temperature may not exceed 150 °F, with sufficient curing required before use to prevent pickup. [2] The Representative inspects a sample and any uncoated material passing the No. 200 sieve after ten minutes of dry sieving must be ≤0.5 % of the total, ensuring that virtually all aggregate particles receive the binder film. Influence of production and placement

During slab‑stabilization work the grout asphalt is required to be heated to a temperature between 380 °F and 450 °F before pumping; any batch that reaches 500 °F or more shall be rejected because temperatures above this limit risk degrading the binder and impairing injection performance. After the grout has been placed, the pavement must remain closed to traffic for at least 30 minutes, providing a brief curing period that allows the asphalt to congeal and prevents premature lift or loss of support. For pre‑coated aggregate seal‑coat applications the mixing operation must be kept cool enough that the mix temperature does not exceed 350 °F, which preserves the binder film and enables uniform coverage. When cut‑back asphalts are used, the aggregate temperature is limited to 150 °F and the coated material must be sufficiently cured before placement to avoid pickup of the binder. The mixed pre‑coated aggregate should be stored on a suitable base no higher than four feet; these practices together help achieve at least 90 % surface coverage and maintain the intended durability of the stabilized pavement. [2]

Selection, specification, and improvement

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), selection of an asphalt‑stabilized layer is primarily guided by the structural capacity required for the reclaimed base, which is expressed through the appropriate structure‑layer coefficient; because asphalt‑stabilized layers generally have coefficients comparable to those used for cold recycling while mechanical or chemical stabilizations can provide higher coefficients, the desired support stiffness and corresponding coefficient values direct the choice of stabilization method. The manual further requires that, before any stabilization mechanism is chosen for a Full‑Depth Reclamation project, the engineer conduct a comprehensive evaluation of the existing pavement that must include a reliable estimate of future traffic loading, a recent distress survey identifying types and causes of damage, and in‑situ testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements to assess current layer stiffness; sampling plans, determination of appropriate layer thicknesses, and verification of adequate drainage conditions also shape the design envelope. Once these baseline criteria are satisfied, selection among pulverization, mechanical, chemical, calcium‑chloride, or emulsified‑asphalt stabilization is based on the strength potential of the reclaimed material, the need for added aggregate or additives, and laboratory‑determined type and content of stabilizer required to meet performance goals without inducing brittleness. For chloride‑based asphalt stabilization the manual specifies a minimum 35 % calcium‑chloride solution applied at 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of depth reclaimed in order to provide particle cementing and lower the material’s freezing temperature, thereby improving freeze‑thaw resistance. For emulsified‑asphalt stabilization the appropriate type and amount of emulsion must be chosen to satisfy water‑damage mitigation and fatigue resistance criteria listed in the design method for PennDOT Full‑Depth Reclamation projects, and all selections must follow the mix‑design process and quality‑control requirements set forth in Bulletin 27. In the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 Specifications, slab stabilization using an asphalt‑based grout is limited to the three grout types permitted in Section 679.2; when asphalt cement is selected it shall conform to ASTM D3141 with a minimum flash point of 500°F and be supplied by a manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15 together with the supplier certification required by Section 106.03(b)3, and only cylindrical plugs with tapered ends approved by the Representative may be used. For asphalt grout stabilization under Section 679.3(c), the asphalt must be heated to a temperature between 380°F and 450°F before pumping operations begin, any material heated to 500°F or more shall be rejected, the injection pressure shall be limited to 25 psi–45 psi, drilled holes may not exceed 1½ in. in diameter and must be vertical and smooth, upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 in. is not allowed, and a minimum 30‑minute cure is required before traffic reopens. The seal‑coat requirements in Section 471 specify that when asphalt cement is used as the pre‑coating material it shall provide a residual asphalt binder content of 0.6 % to 1.2 % by weight of the mix (or 0.4 % to 1.0 % when other asphalt materials are applied), must produce a thin brownish translucent film covering at least 90 % of the aggregate’s visible surface, and if coverage falls short the percent passing a No. 200 (75 µm) sieve after ten minutes of dry sieving may be measured with acceptance only if it does not exceed 0.5 %; mixing temperatures shall not exceed 350°F for asphalt‑cement pre‑coating and aggregate temperatures shall not exceed 150°F when cut‑back asphalts are used, with stockpiled material stored on a suitable base no higher than 4 feet. For Full‑Depth Reclamation under Section 344.3(e), the compacted mix must achieve at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum dry density at optimum moisture content, the moisture content may not exceed the optimum by more than 3 %, a compaction control strip is required to verify consistent achievement of this density, and the stabilized material shall attain a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi after the prescribed seven‑day cure before traffic can be opened. [1][2]

To improve the performance of asphalt‑stabilized layers, the guidance permits adding chemical stabilizers such as calcium chloride or similar additives for a slight improvement and using cement, lime, or comparable additives to raise stiffness to levels equal to or better than conventional asphalt stabilization. Reclaimed asphalt pavement may also be blended into hot‑mix, warm‑mix, or cold‑recycled base courses to conserve binder and aggregates. Full depth reclamation can further enhance integrity by adding additional stabilizing material during the process. For full‑depth reclamation projects, engineers may modify the reclaimed base by mechanically stabilizing it—adding aggregate or RAP to improve gradation—or chemically stabilizing it with wet or dry additives such as Portland cement, lime, fly ash, limekiln dust, or fluidized‑bed combustion fly ash; laboratory testing should determine the appropriate type and content because higher stabilizer levels raise strength but can cause brittleness if excessive. Stabilizing agents may be applied dry or as a slurry ahead of the reclaimer, or may be introduced as a slurry in the mixing chamber of the reclaimer through a spray bar. Incorporating calcium chloride or magnesium chloride as a chloride‑based additive can increase particle cementation strength and lower the reclaimed material’s freezing temperature, thereby enhancing freeze‑thaw resistance; the recommended application is a minimum 35 % solution delivered at 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of depth reclaimed. Emulsified asphalt stabilization further improves water‑related damage resistance and fatigue performance and can be combined with granular aggregates, cement or lime to boost durability, provided that the mix design and quality‑control procedures prescribed in Publication 27 (Bulletin 27) are followed for PennDOT full‑depth reclamation projects. In slab‑stabilization grout operations, the asphalt must be heated only within the range of 380 °F to 450 °F and any batch exceeding 500 °F shall be rejected. Pumping pressure shall be limited to between 25 psi and 45 psi, and lift monitoring equipment capable of detecting movement as small as 0.001 inch must continuously monitor slab uplift, stopping pumping if upward movement exceeds 0.05 inch. Drilling holes no larger than 1½ inches and blowing compressed air for 5 to 15 seconds before injection further reduces void contamination and promotes uniform fill. Asphalt cement shall comply with ASTM D3141 and have a minimum flash point of 500 °F, and each shipment must be accompanied by a supplier certification as specified in Section 106.03(b)3. Alternative stabilizers such as cement grout or high‑density polyurethane grout may also be used. For seal‑coat applications, a pre‑coating that leaves a residual binder of 0.6 % to 1.2 % by weight of the mix (or a damp‑aggregate approach with cut‑back asphalts providing 0.4 % to 1.0 %) is permitted; the pre‑coat must form a thin brownish translucent film covering at least 90 % of the visible aggregate surface, and if coverage is less than 90 % the contractor must dry‑sieve the uncoated material for ten minutes, with the percent passing the No. 200 (75 µm) sieve not exceeding 0.5 %. Mixing temperature shall not exceed 350 °F and aggregate temperature shall be kept below 150 °F when cut‑back asphalts are used; the mixed material must be stored on a suitable base no higher than four feet. In full‑depth reclamation, calcium chloride may be incorporated during pulverization, and where cement is used accelerating or retarding admixtures may be added to the slurry with written approval of the DME/DMM. The designed moisture level must be maintained as verified by ASTM D2216, D44643, or AASHTO T 265, and compaction shall achieve a minimum density of at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum. Keeping the stabilized layer damp through daily water application, an emulsified‑asphalt primer with a residual asphalt rate between 0.02 and 0.10 gallons per square yard, or a tack coat supports curing to meet the required seven‑day strength; the mix must attain a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi when tested according to Bulletin 27. Nuclear density control strips and proof rolling are used before traffic opening to verify compliance with the density requirement. [1][2]

Performance evaluation and implications

In the Pavement Policy Manual, the structural capacity of asphalt‑stabilized layers is quantified by structure layer coefficient values that are drawn from industry literature and existing full‑depth reclamation practices in other states and then verified through laboratory testing conducted in Pennsylvania. The manual further requires that the type and content of the stabilizer be selected on the basis of laboratory testing, recognizing that different stabilization materials may produce differing structure layer coefficients. Field performance of such stabilized layers is monitored with Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements taken before rehabilitation to establish existing pavement stiffness and repeated after construction to confirm the stiffness of the new pavement. Additional subgrade strength information in the field is obtained by dynamic cone penetrometer testing and light weight deflectometer testing. For asphalt grout stabilization, the specifications mandate vertical‑movement testing using equipment capable of detecting slab lift to a resolution of 0.001 inch, and any upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited. In the case of an asphalt‑stabilized pre‑coated aggregate seal coat, the Representative inspects a representative sample to verify that at least 90 % of the visible aggregate surface is covered by a thin brownish film, and if this visual criterion is not met, the fraction of uncoated particles passing the No. 200 (75 µm) sieve after ten minutes of dry sieving must not exceed 0.5 %. For asphalt‑stabilized full‑depth reclamation, laboratory evaluation requires preparing a minimum of three standard Proctor specimens in accordance with ASTM D1633, sealing and curing them for seven days, and then demonstrating either an indirect tensile strength of at least 50 psi as measured by Bulletin 27 or an unconfined compressive strength between 300 and 500 psi (or 280 to 500 psi when the road will receive an asphalt overlay of three inches or greater) after the same curing period, with moisture content verified by ASTM D2216, D44643, or AASHTO T 265. The corresponding field evaluation for full‑depth reclamation involves constructing a 300‑ft compaction control strip and measuring density with calibrated nuclear gauges in direct transmission mode after each pass until no appreciable increase occurs, followed by at least ten random PTM No. 1 density tests to demonstrate that the reclaimed material attains at least 95 % of the laboratory‑compacted maximum density; surface tolerance on each 3,000‑sq‑yd lot is checked and proof rolling with the heaviest expected vehicle confirms strength before traffic opening. Neither source provides a description of specific long‑term monitoring or durability studies for asphalt‑stabilized materials. [1][2]
