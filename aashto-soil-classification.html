AASHTO Soil Classification

Part of: Subgrade is-subtopic-of
AASHTO Soil Classification is a topic under Subgrade.
aashto-soil-classificationaashto-soils-classification-systemaashto-soil-classification-systemaashto-classification-systemaashto-soil-classification-group-index
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

The AASHTO Soil Classification is a systematic procedure for characterizing the subgrade soils that will support flexible or rigid pavements, especially on interstate and expressway projects[1]. By integrating field sampling from cut, fill, or at‑grade locations with laboratory tests—including moisture‑density, unconfined compressive strength, gradation, percent passing the #200 sieve, Atterberg limits, Group Index, and either resilient modulus or soaked CBR—the classification quantifies soil stiffness, bearing capacity, moisture sensitivity, and frost‑heave potential, providing the basis for estimating resilient modulus (Mr = 1500 × CBR) used in design[1]. The resulting group‑index map reveals the distribution of coarse‑ versus fine‑grained materials and flags soils that fail minimum subgrade specifications (e.g., OL, MH, OH or marginal ML‑OL), thereby guiding material selection, improvement strategies, and construction timing to accommodate seasonal variations in modulus[1].

Purpose and investigation objectives

The AASHTO Soil Classification is used to evaluate the condition of the subgrade soil that will support a pavement, especially for reconstruction or rubblized projects. The investigation must determine the resilient modulus of representative soils under seasonal moisture conditions, obtain CBR values and verify whether they exceed 10 or fall below 5, classify each sample according to the AASHTO group index, assess gradation, percent passing the #200 sieve, Atterberg limits, maximum dry density and optimum moisture content, and document where each sample was taken (cut, fill, or at‑grade) so that variability across the alignment can be quantified. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143)

The AASHTO Soil Classification results are used to map the distribution of soil groups across a project, allowing designers to quantify how much of the alignment is composed of coarse‑grained versus fine‑grained material and to identify whether low CBR values are confined to isolated spots or spread throughout the site. Because the classification feeds directly into the resilient modulus estimate (Mr = 1500 × CBR) and the calculation of the Group Index, it influences subgrade improvement decisions; for example, CBR values less than 5 ensure that recommendations for significant undercutting and/or other methods of subgrade improvement are necessary and justified, while CBR values greater than 10 shall be recommended by the District Geotechnical Engineer and approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer to avoid unrealistically high Mr. Seasonal testing also shows that spring modulus values are 10% to 20% lower than normal/summer values and frozen subgrade modulus values are typically two times greater, so the classification helps planners schedule construction when the subgrade will provide adequate stiffness. In addition, the classification directly guides pavement planning by flagging soils that fail the required subgrade criteria; OL, MH, and OH classifications are identified as not meeting minimum specifications for subgrade material, indicating they must be avoided or improved before use. Soils classified as ML‑OL are described as marginal and may or may not satisfy the same specifications, prompting designers to conduct additional evaluation or consider stabilization measures, thereby shaping decisions on material selection, design thickness, and potential rehabilitation strategies for roadway foundations. [1]

Scope and applicability

AASHTO Soil Classification is applied to the subgrade layer of flexible or rigid pavements, especially on interstate and expressway projects where resilient modulus or CBR values are required for design. The classification of samples taken from cut, fill, or at‑grade sections is used to map soil type distribution and to evaluate properties such as gradation, fines content, Atterberg limits, maximum dry density, optimum moisture content and Group Index, which together indicate subgrade strength, moisture sensitivity and potential frost‑heave susceptibility. Seasonal testing is also considered because spring modulus values are typically 10% to 20% lower than normal/summer values and frozen subgrade modulus values are about twice the normal/summer values. [1]

Existing information and records

In section 6.2 Subgrade Soils the existing records often lack seasonal resilient‑modulus data and spatially representative subgrade properties, so field investigation must capture spring modulus values that are 10 % to 20 % lower than normal/summer values and frozen values that are typically two times greater, and must verify CBR results against the full suite of laboratory tests across cut, fill and at‑grade locations. Because any CBR value greater than 10 or less than 5 triggers mandatory review by the District Geotechnical Engineer and Chief Geotechnical Engineer, investigators must resolve whether high or low CBR readings are isolated or widespread and confirm that the accompanying classification, gradation, fines content, Atterberg limits and Group Index support the estimated Mr, ensuring that the resilient‑modulus conversion to k for rigid pavements and the selection of subgrade improvement measures are based on a complete site‑wide data set rather than incomplete or inconsistent laboratory records. In section 9.6 Effective Roadbed Soil Resilient Modulus the intentionally blank subsection highlights an additional uncertainty for ML‑OL soils, which are described as marginal and may or may not satisfy the minimum subgrade specifications; field investigation must verify whether any particular ML‑OL material actually meets those requirements and provide the in‑situ data needed to fill this gap. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143), Ch. 9 (pp. 169–170)

Field measurements and testing

To support the AASHTO Soil Classification in pavement design, a Falling Weight Deflectometer may be employed before laboratory resilient‑modulus testing to locate representative subgrade spots, providing an indication of in‑situ stiffness. Laboratory analysis of the collected samples includes a Moisture Density Test (PTM No. 106) that quantifies the field dry density and optimum moisture content, an Unconfined Compressive Strength Test (AASHTO T 208) that gauges soil strength at the tested moisture condition, a Soil Classification test (AASHTO M 145) that determines the group index, gradation and fines content, and a Resilient Modulus Test (AASHTO T 307) that measures the elastic modulus of the soil acknowledging its nonlinear behavior. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143)

Sampling and laboratory investigation

For AASHTO Soil Classification, subgrade soils—including material from cut, fill, or at‑grade areas—are sampled, with three 50‑pound bags of each representative material submitted for laboratory testing. Sampling locations are chosen to reflect every soil condition observed along the roadway so that the collected samples account for all conditions present and allow a thorough evaluation of CBR variability across the entire alignment. The resulting classification results are then used to quantify the distribution of soil types throughout the project area. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143)

To support AASHTO Soil Classification the subgrade laboratory program requires a Moisture‑Density test (PTM No. 106), an Unconfined Compressive Strength test (AASHTO T 208), a soil classification per AASHTO M 145, and a Resilient Modulus test (AASHTO T 307); alternatively, a soaked California Bearing Ratio test (AASHTO T 193) may be used in place of the modulus test. The moisture‑density test provides maximum dry density and optimum moisture content, the unconfined compressive strength gives a direct measure of soil strength, and the classification determines gradation, percent passing #200 sieve, Atterberg limits and Group Index, all of which affect CBR and resilient modulus values. The resilient modulus quantifies the elastic response of the subgrade under simulated seasonal stress‑moisture conditions—spring values are typically 10 % to 20 % lower than normal/summer values and frozen values are two times greater—and the CBR test supplies a bearing capacity index that, when greater than 10, requires additional review and, when less than 5, calls for subgrade improvement. Collectively these tests evaluate moisture sensitivity, compressibility, frost‑heave potential, and overall suitability of the soil for pavement foundations. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143)

Structural and functional performance

The AASHTO Soil Classification requires a suite of laboratory and field measurements to assess the subgrade’s structural capacity, beginning with determination of the resilient modulus (Mr) for elastic behavior and, when Mr is unavailable, a California Bearing Ratio (CBR) test on soaked samples. Supporting tests include a moisture‑density test, an unconfined compressive strength test, and a soil classification per AASHTO M 145 together with gradation analysis (coefficients of uniformity and curvature), the percent passing the #200 sieve, Atterberg limits (liquid limit, plastic limit, plasticity index) to compute the Group Index, as well as determination of maximum dry density and optimum moisture content. These measurements collectively provide the data needed to verify that subgrade soils can achieve the required resilient modulus or CBR values for pavement design. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143)

Measured resilient modulus values from laboratory or field testing are used directly in flexible pavement design and, after conversion, in rigid designs; the source advises that seasonal testing should capture spring conditions which are 10% to 20% lower than normal/summer values and frozen conditions which can be typically two times greater than the normal/summer values, ensuring the design assumptions reflect expected traffic loading throughout the year. CBR test results, which may substitute for resilient modulus, must be checked against the thresholds that CBR values greater than 10 require recommendation by the District Geotechnical Engineer and approval by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer to avoid unrealistically high modulus inputs, while CBR values less than 5 trigger required subgrade improvement measures; these checks align measured soil conditions with design assumptions and service‑life expectations. Additionally, the AASHTO Group Index and other index properties are evaluated to confirm that the soils meet typical ranges for the identified classification, providing confidence that the subgrade will support the projected traffic loads over the intended service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143)

Data interpretation and diagnosis

The observed poor subgrade performance is most often linked to moisture‑related issues and low stiffness of the soil. Soils with a high percent passing the #200 sieve are more moisture sensitive, tend to have lower CBR values and greater susceptibility to frost heave, which points to inadequate drainage as a primary cause. In addition, fine‑grained soils and those with high Group Index values normally yield lower resilient modulus values, providing weak support for the pavement structure. Seasonal effects also play a role because spring modulus values are 10% to 20% lower than normal/summer values, indicating that environmental exposure can further reduce subgrade strength. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143)

Findings, confidence, and next actions

The AASHTO Soil Classification can be used to map the distribution of soil types across a project, allowing designers to estimate where low CBR values may be isolated or widespread and to infer that coarse‑grained soils generally yield higher CBR values than fine‑grained soils. Together with the classification, laboratory CBR results are converted by Mr = 1500 × CBR to provide a resilient modulus for pavement design. The reliability of these conclusions is limited by seasonal variations in modulus; spring values are 10% to 20% lower than normal/summer and frozen values are typically two times greater. In addition, the need to verify CBR against other laboratory parameters such as gradation, fines content, Atterberg limits and Group Index introduces further uncertainty, and mandatory engineer review is required when CBR exceeds 10 or falls below 5 to prevent unrealistic Mr estimates. Consequently, while classification supports preliminary suitability assessments, uncertainty remains until a full suite of site‑specific tests confirms the assumed properties. Based on the AASHTO Soil Classification, soils identified as OL, MH, and OH are concluded to fail the minimum specifications for subgrade material, indicating they are unsuitable for roadbed use. Soils in the ML‑OL range are described as marginal and may or may not meet those same minimum subgrade specifications, leaving uncertainty about their acceptability without additional evaluation. [1]

Following an AASHTO Soil Classification, the designer shall obtain resilient modulus data either from direct laboratory testing (AASHTO T307) on three 50‑lb bags together with moisture‑density, unconfined compressive strength and classification tests, or estimate it from soaked CBR results (AASHTO T193) while accounting for seasonal variations such as spring values that are 10% to 20% lower than normal/summer values; the resulting CBR must then be cross‑checked against the full suite of laboratory properties—including gradation, percent passing #200, Atterberg limits, maximum dry density, optimum moisture content and Group Index—to confirm it is realistic for the identified soil groups. If a project CBR exceeds 10, the value shall be recommended by the District Geotechnical Engineer and approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, and if it falls below 5 a justification for subgrade improvement such as undercutting must be documented. Additionally, field FWD testing may be used to target sampling locations and the variability of soil conditions (cut, fill or at‑grade) across the alignment should be captured in the sample plan. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143)
