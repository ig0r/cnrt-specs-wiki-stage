Concrete Patching

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Concrete Patching is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
concrete-patching
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-28

Concrete patching is a remedial preservation treatment applied to rigid concrete pavements to repair localized loss of structural continuity that threatens bearing capacity, load transfer, ride quality and joint functionality[1][2]. The method works by delineating the distressed zone, removing unsound concrete, cleaning and saturating the substrate, then completely filling the voids with an approved rapid‑set, non‑shrink mortar or grout that attains at least 1,000 psi compressive strength, and finally finishing the repair flush with the surrounding surface to restore uniform support[1][2]. It is limited to defects larger than about four square inches and deeper than one inch, unstable slab fragments, or joints whose loaded slab‑corner deflection reaches 0.02 in with joint efficiency below roughly 65 %, and the total patched area must not exceed roughly ten percent of the pavement unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided[2][1].

PennDOT publications covering “Concrete Patching” also include the following subtopics: Full-Depth Patching and Partial-Depth Repair.

Full‑Depth Patching serves as a special case of Concrete Patching when distress penetrates the entire slab thickness, requiring removal of all compromised material and replacement with new Portland cement concrete featuring fully bonded dowels. [1][2] Partial‑Depth Repair functions as another specialized application for localized surface spalling or delamination limited to the upper half of the slab, utilizing a Class AA cement‑concrete patch placed over a bonding coat while preserving intersected joints. [1][2]

Purpose and treatment objectives

Concrete patching is employed to remediate localized loss of structural continuity in rigid concrete pavements that jeopardizes bearing capacity, load transfer, ride quality, and joint functionality [1][2]. The treatment targets a range of distress types including failed or faulted joints, joint spalling, cracks, transverse or longitudinal spalls, holes, voids, surface blemishes larger than one‑half inch, break‑back, delamination, edge or corner damage, and unstable slab fragments that rock under traffic [1][2]. It also addresses manufacturing, handling or construction‑induced imperfections, as well as subbase irregularities exceeding one inch in depth that produce non‑uniform support beneath the concrete [2]. In addition, joints or transverse cracks whose loaded slab‑corner deflection reaches 0.02 in or more and whose joint efficiency falls to roughly 65 % or less are considered deficient and require patching [2]. The scope of a preservation project is generally limited to about ten percent of the total pavement area unless a cost‑effective justification demonstrates the need for a larger extent [1]. Full‑depth removal of the distressed concrete, often delineated by saw cuts at least three‑quarters of an inch deep and extending beyond the affected zone, is followed by replacement with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink mortar or grout that meets a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi (or up to about 2,500 psi for certain applications) [2]. The repaired surface is compacted and finished flush so that vertical differentials across joints do not exceed one‑quarter inch and overall surface deviations are limited to three‑sixteenths of an inch, thereby restoring uniform bearing support and load transfer [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–462), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 526–589), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 694), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (pp. 965–1066)

Concrete patching on rigid pavements is fundamentally a remedial treatment that repairs localized distress such as failed joints, cracks, spalls and other defects so the pavement regains its structural integrity and smooth ride performance [1][2]. The process involves delineating the damaged area, removing unsound concrete, cleaning and saturating the substrate, and completely filling the voids with an approved rapid‑set, non‑shrinking mortar or comparable cementitious material, followed by curing to achieve strength [2]. By restoring these zones to their original condition and striking patches flush with surrounding pavement, the treatment reestablishes functional performance and ride quality [1][2]. In addition to repairing damage, sealing joints and cracks during patching limits moisture and contaminant ingress, thereby retarding further deterioration of the concrete surface and slowing the progression of distress [1][2]. Consequently, the primary objectives of concrete patching are to repair damage, restore functionality, and inhibit additional degradation, which together contribute to extending the service life of the pavement [1][2].

Distress characterization and causes

The distress that triggers concrete patching is observed on the traffic‑lane surface where entire slabs or fragments become unstable, moving or rocking under normal loads, and also at individual transverse joints and cracks that have lost support. The affected areas are defined by measurable criteria: any surface defect larger than four square inches in plan area and deeper than one inch must be treated, and any joint or crack that exhibits a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 inches or more, together with a joint efficiency falling to about 65 percent, is also included. The extent of the problem is therefore limited to the number of such defects identified during field inspection and testing. These conditions represent severe deterioration because they produce non‑uniform support for overlays, can cause premature overlay failure, generate radial or transverse cracking around grout injection points, and create unsafe driving conditions. Although the specification does not assign a numerical progression rate for surface defects, the requirement to repair them before overlay placement implies that they are expected to worsen if left untreated. For joints and cracks, continued vertical movement is treated as an indicator of ongoing progression; if after two grout injections the deflection remains at or above 0.02 inches, full‑depth patching or slab replacement is mandated. Operationally, any observed vertical movement triggers a stop‑work order for pumping activities, reflecting immediate safety concerns for equipment operators and exposure to hot asphalt, while the underlying distress also threatens the structural integrity of the pavement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Concrete patching is typically required when localized distress originates from deficiencies that undermine the slab’s ability to carry traffic loads, reflecting weak support and material degradation that accelerate pavement deterioration [1][2]. Inadequate subbase material can produce premature mid‑slab cracking by providing insufficient bearing, a form of weak support that hastens failure [1]. Aggregate‑related problems such as the presence of Vanport Limestone or susceptibility to Alkali Silica Reaction further degrade the concrete matrix, representing material aging and degradation not readily detected in routine condition surveys [1]. When slab fragments become unstable—moving or rocking under normal traffic—the condition signals inadequate structural capacity or insufficient underlying support that would otherwise lead to non‑uniform overlay support if left unrepaired [2]. Surface defects extending deeper than one inch also compromise the integrity of the pavement surface, creating pathways for water infiltration and reducing load‑bearing performance prior to any overlay placement [2]. Impaired drainage, such as when a geotextile interlayer is not terminated in a drainable location, allows moisture to accumulate behind the slab, promoting freeze‑thaw damage and further weakening both the concrete and its supporting layers [2]. Heavy traffic loads exacerbate these weaknesses by imposing repeated stresses on already compromised slabs, accelerating crack propagation and slab movement [2].

Applicability and treatment selection

Concrete patching is limited to rigid (concrete) pavements that are structurally sound, as verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing.[1] The treatment may be applied when localized distress such as failed joints, cracks, spalls, or slab fragments occupies no more than roughly ten percent of the total pavement area unless a cost‑effectiveness justification for exceeding that limit can be demonstrated.[1] Additional triggers include surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch, unstable slabs that move or rock under normal traffic loads, or joints that show a loaded slab‑corner deflection of at least 0.02 in and a joint efficiency below about 65 percent; all such conditions are considered appropriate for patching.[2] All identified failed joints within the affected zone must be repaired as part of the patching operation.[1][2] Work should be scheduled so that the final partial‑depth repair can be placed before reopening to traffic, allowing for the required return‑to‑service time.[2] Patching is permitted only when ambient air and surface temperatures are at least 40 °F, preferably not above about 70 °F for daytime work, and rising enough to keep the subgrade or base course unfrozen; placement must be avoided during spring‑thaw periods or whenever temperatures fall below the manufacturer’s recommended range.[2] After placement, the repaired area must be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours before traffic is allowed to resume.[2] An approved rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete repair mix should be used together with expansion‑joint material and temporary forms that keep new concrete out of existing joints; the patch is finished flush with the surrounding surface and any drill holes or wooden plugs are removed and filled full depth.[2]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 418–462), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Concrete patching is intended solely for localized distress that occupies a small fraction of the slab, and when inspection or testing shows that the area to be patched would exceed roughly ten percent of the total pavement surface, the treatment is no longer considered cost‑effective as a preservation measure. In such cases the project must be reclassified as a more extensive rehabilitation or reconstruction unless a clear cost‑effectiveness justification for exceeding the ten‑percent limit can be demonstrated. Similarly, scratch overlays applied to existing concrete that would require a thickness greater than four and one‑half inches when using a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing‑course mix, or greater than five inches with a 12.5 mm mix, fall outside the preservation scope and must be addressed through alternative treatments such as mill‑and‑overlay, diamond grinding, micro‑surfacing, or ultra‑thin bonded courses. When the pavement exhibits widespread structural deficiencies—extensive cracking, joint faulting, or has surpassed its functional service life—the appropriate response shifts from spot patching to larger‑scale interventions such as base repair, full‑depth overlays, or complete reconstruction. At the defect level, concrete patching becomes unsuitable when an individual surface defect exceeds four square inches in area or is deeper than one inch, because such volumes cannot be adequately compacted with an interlayer material and require other techniques like slabjacking or flexible‑base replacement. Any portion of a slab that is unstable—showing visible movement or rocking under normal traffic—or contains moving fragments must first be stabilized before any patch can be placed; otherwise stabilization or alternative repair methods are required. The method is also confined to manufacturing defects and minor damage as defined in Publication 145, and distress that extends beyond these small‑scale issues must be submitted to the CSME for evaluation, with the expectation that a different maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair approach will be prescribed. Patching is not warranted when the measured loaded slab corner deflection does not reach the minimum 0.02 inch threshold or when joint efficiency equals or exceeds sixty‑five percent, because in those situations stabilization alone suffices. If vertical movement occurs during grout injection, if radial cracks emanate from injection holes, or if transverse cracks appear between adjacent holes, work must be stopped and a full‑depth slab replacement or another suitable repair method employed. Rapid‑set concrete patching material is limited to corner repairs; its use elsewhere in a rigid pavement is inappropriate, and it may only be applied within the manufacturer’s specified shelf life and temperature limits. [1][2]

Condition assessment and timing

Determining the need for concrete patching begins with a systematic condition assessment that integrates both remote sensing and on‑site observations [1][2]. Each year the network is surveyed using an automated videologging system under the STAMPP program, which records surface distress such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling or weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting, and these data are entered into treatment matrices that flag potential patch locations [1]. In parallel, a comprehensive visual walk‑over is performed to identify any surface blemishes larger than one‑half inch, depressions exceeding four square inches and deeper than one inch, or slab fragments that move or rock under normal traffic, with each observation recorded for further evaluation [2]. Flagged areas are then inspected in the field to verify the presence and severity of the observed defects, using a ten‑foot straightedge placed along barriers and across decks; any deviation greater than one‑quarter inch from the edge of the straightedge signals that patching or replacement may be required [2]. Joint faulting is also measured during this walk‑over, with offsets exceeding one‑quarter inch over geotextile interlayers—or more than one‑half inch where asphalt interlayers are present—prompting milling, grinding, or a scratch course before any overlay can be applied [2]. Cross‑slope uniformity is checked at 100‑foot intervals and longitudinal surface roughness is measured in accordance with Section 507.3, providing ride‑quality indices such as IRI that later confirm whether rehabilitation has restored acceptable smoothness [2]. To delineate the exact unsound areas, a full‑deck sounding is conducted using a three‑foot‑wide chain drag with drops spaced every six inches in the presence of an authorized representative who marks the repair zones [2]. Core samples are extracted from the pavement—at least one per lane for sections under 100 ft and at minimum 100‑foot intervals for longer stretches—to verify existing concrete thickness, overall integrity, and to support slab design decisions [1][2]. In addition to core analysis, a Falling Weight Deflectometer test is performed on the same sections to quantify joint efficiency and to locate the precise quantities of concrete that have lost structural capacity, thereby confirming whether patching is necessary [1]. When ambient temperatures permit (typically between 40 °F and 70 °F), ASTM D4695 deflection testing is carried out on each joint and crack; a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 in or more combined with a joint efficiency below 65 % mandates both patching and stabilization, whereas the same deflection with efficiency at or above 65 % calls for stabilization alone [2]. If temperature conditions preclude deflection testing, visual verification of pumping problems or poor joint performance is used as an alternative basis for specifying repair actions [2]. The combination of automated condition data, verified visual observations, geometric measurements, core‑sample analysis, chain‑drag sounding, FWD results, and, where applicable, deflection testing together defines both whether concrete patching is required and the extent of the repair needed [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 395–462), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 526–589), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (pp. 1065–1066)

Concrete patching is prescribed when a rigid concrete pavement exhibits localized failures—such as faulted joints, spalling, isolated cracking or other deteriorated zones—that have been identified in condition surveys and have not yet expanded to affect more than roughly ten percent of the total surface area. [1] The guides also state that the treatment must be applied as soon as any hole, crack, spall, break‑back or other unsound concrete is observed, particularly before the pavement’s final inspection, so that the defective portion can be removed and replaced without delay. [2] Across the service life of a typical pavement, the timing for patching aligns with distinct intervals: around ten years when about five percent of the area shows distress; near fifteen years at roughly two percent; at twenty years the affected portion may rise to eight percent; at twenty‑five years it is typically four percent; by thirty years the distressed area returns to about two percent; and a further application around thirty‑five years addresses roughly six percent. [1] These percentages represent stages where localized damage is sufficient to warrant full‑depth patching yet remains below the ten‑percent threshold that would render preservation uneconomical. [1] In addition, any slab or slab fragment that moves or rocks under normal traffic—creating non‑uniform support for an overlay—must be stabilized through patching before interlayer placement. [2] Joints or cracks that exhibit a loaded slab‑corner deflection of at least 0.02 inch and a joint efficiency below sixty‑five percent are also required to be stabilized and patched with rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete after removal of temporary plugs. [2] For minor surface distress, the guides allow a light repair using non‑staining, non‑shrinking mortar or a paint‑coat mixture of neat cement and water, provided that excavated areas are not left unpatched for more than forty‑eight hours and a full‑depth transverse saw cut is made when break‑backs or spalling are present. [2]

Materials and repair details

Concrete patching on rigid pavements is limited to a maximum of ten percent of the total pavement area unless a cost‑effectiveness analysis demonstrates justification, and the repair scope must include every failed joint, crack, spall or other localized distress identified before final design. The work begins with thorough cleaning of the damaged zone, saturation of any through‑wall holes with water, and immediate filling of those holes with a non‑staining, non‑shrinking mortar or grout; through‑wall openings are forced from the back face using a plunger‑type caulking gun while a burlap sheet is held over the front opening, and blind holes are rammed in place. Surface blemishes larger than one‑half inch are first filled with mortar, whereas minor patches may be made with a paint‑coat mix of neat cement and water tamped to at least a half‑inch depth and allowed five minutes for excess mortar to set before finishing. For larger repairs the specified rapid‑set concrete patching material of Section 525.2(c) is placed, or an approved asphalt‑based, cementitious or other pavement repair product listed in Bulletin 15 may be used after representative approval. The non‑shrinking grout mix consists of one part cement—acceptable types include IP, IS, IL, II, Class AA, Class AAAP, latex‑modified or accelerated structural concrete—and two parts fine aggregate (Type A or C), with water limited to four and a half gallons per bag and a water‑cement ratio not exceeding 0.42 for Class AA mixes, a target of 0.40 preferred but never below 0.38. The rapid‑set material must meet ASTM C928, achieve at least an eighty percent durability factor after three hundred freeze‑thaw cycles, set in no less than fifteen minutes at seventy‑five degrees Fahrenheit, and when the repair depth exceeds two inches it must incorporate the manufacturer’s aggregate extenders; the mix is to be placed within one hour of preparation, with grout mixes mixed for sixty seconds, rested forty‑five minutes, remixed for another sixty seconds, and placed within thirty minutes of the final mixing. All repair mixes conform to modified Class AA requirements that specify a minimum cement factor of five hundred thirty pounds per cubic yard, maximum water‑cement ratio of 0.42, air content about seven percent plus or minus one and a half percent, and coarse aggregate limited to Type A stone, gravel or No. 57, 67 or 8 particles, while blast‑furnace slag aggregate is prohibited and any partial replacement of Portland cement by fly ash or slag cement must retain at least four hundred pounds per cubic yard of Portland cement and limit slag cement to thirty percent of the total cementitious blend meeting ASTM C595 Type IS or IP. When bridge decks or barriers are involved, modified Class AAAP cement concrete, low‑modulus concrete, air‑set concrete or Class AA cement concrete may be employed as appropriate. Patches up to four inches deep may be placed concurrently with a latex overlay, but deeper repairs require scarifying or hydro‑demolition before the new material is placed. Full‑depth concrete replacement combined with joint rehabilitation may be used, optionally followed by diamond grinding when no asphalt overlay is planned, and dowel‑bar retrofits or slab stabilization/jacking can be incorporated as required. Overlay options for structurally sound sections include a one‑and‑a‑half inch Superpave mix with nine‑point‑five millimeter nominal maximum aggregate size placed over a two‑and‑a‑half inch Superpave base of nineteen‑millimeter nominal aggregate, an optional half‑inch scratch course, a two‑inch Superpave twelve‑point‑five millimeter mix over the same base, a one‑inch thin asphalt overlay of six‑point‑three millimeter thickness, or polymer‑modified emulsified micro‑surfacing. Saw cutting defines the patch geometry; a diamond‑tipped blade saw equipped with a guard, alignment guides, water cooling and cut‑depth controls is used to make perimeter cuts that may not exceed one‑half inch in width when opened to traffic, full‑depth transverse cuts at repair limits may extend into adjacent pavement up to the full depth plus two inches provided dowel bars are not damaged, longitudinal joint cuts are limited to a quarter inch wide and one inch deep, and expansion joints that are rebuilt receive three‑quarters of an inch material reduced to one‑half inch for corner repairs. In Type A and B patches and corner repairs a full‑depth quarter‑inch polystyrene board serves as a bond breaker in longitudinal joints, while removable formwork of sufficient strength with side forms including drip strips and chamfers is placed on all sides not abutting existing pavement to prevent lateral or vertical deflection. All exposed surfaces within the repair area receive a thin coat of grout or epoxy bonding compound brushed on while still wet or tacky, fresh concrete is placed before the coating dries, and any dried coating is removed and reapplied; exposed reinforcement is abrasive‑blasted, air‑blown clean, epoxy‑coated and then treated with the same bonding compound prior to concreting. Placement is limited to ambient or surface temperatures of forty degrees Fahrenheit or higher, with protection from freezing for at least twenty‑four hours, and consolidation is achieved with a spud vibrator, pencil vibrator or acceptable tamping method; expansion‑joint material and preformed cellular‑polystyrene forms keep the new concrete out of existing joints. After placement the patch is cured for a minimum of three days using a moisture‑retaining method or an approved membrane compound such as a Type 2 curing compound, and once the concrete reaches a compressive strength of three thousand psi the laitance is removed by water blasting and a penetrating sealer is applied unless a latex overlay follows. The operation requires a three‑foot‑wide chain drag with drops spaced every six inches for sounding, hand tools such as hammers and chisels (chisels no narrower than two inches), pneumatic hammers limited to thirty foot‑pounds (fifteen foot‑pounds for final cleaning) operated at no more than forty‑five degrees from the surface, water‑blasting equipment rated at a minimum of five thousand psi, and epoxy bonding compounds meeting ASTM C881 Type II or V Grade 2 applied according to manufacturer limits. Reinforcement consists of continuously reinforced cement concrete with load‑transfer dowels or units installed on the transverse faces; dowel bars are Type B coated, lubricated with a bond‑breaker over at least half their length, and supported by plastic or non‑metallic chairs providing at least one‑half inch clearance, while expansion joints use one‑and‑a‑half inch non‑metallic caps positioned with a quarter‑inch gap to the cap bottom, cellular polystyrene (¼ in thick) cut to slot dimensions, appropriate filler, sealant reservoir and self‑adhesive tape bond breaker sized to the reservoir. Joint preparation requires full‑depth or partial‑depth saw cuts made with a power‑driven, self‑propelled saw capable of two parallel cuts per dowel slot, air blasting at a minimum of one hundred psi to clean the slots, and jackhammers not exceeding thirty pounds (fifteen pounds when pavement damage is present) for slot opening. The completed patch must extend across at least one lane width, be constructed as a single‑course full‑depth section, and may be classified as Type A (approximately six to twenty feet long, four to twenty feet when welded splices are used), Type B (just over twenty to sixty‑five feet), or Type C (roughly sixty‑five up to five hundred feet). A subbase conforming to the applicable specifications must be provided beneath the repair area, and the geometry of the patch is established by measuring pavement thickness at each of the four corners, averaging those measurements, subtracting the indicated pavement thickness and adding one inch; this calculated depth governs both the dimensional configuration of the repair and the basis for payment. All of these materials, products, dimensions, configurations and repair details together satisfy the requirements for concrete patching on rigid pavements. [1][2]

Performance of a concrete patch depends on the condition and structural capacity of the existing slab and its supporting layers, requiring verification that the slab is sound through Falling‑Weight Deflectometer testing and rehabilitation of all failed joints. An inadequate subbase that permits premature mid‑slab cracking can compromise bond between new and old concrete, making integrity of the underlying material a prerequisite for successful repair. Problematic aggregates such as Vanport Limestone or those prone to alkali‑silica reaction must be identified because they affect durability and skid resistance, often requiring special treatment before patching. The repair mix itself must be non‑staining, non‑shrinking, typically a one‑part cement to two‑parts fine aggregate blend limited to no more than four‑and‑a‑half gallons of water per bag to obtain a stiff yet placeable consistency. For Class AA concrete the water‑cement ratio must not exceed 0.42, with a target of 0.40 and never dropping below 0.38, and the air content at placement should be about seven percent plus or minus one and a half percent. Acceptable aggregates are thoroughly washed stone or gravel whose 14‑day expansion does not exceed 0.30 %; if measured between 0.10 % and 0.30 %, an approved cement‑supplementary blend must be used to limit effective expansion to less than 0.1 %, while any aggregate exceeding 0.30 % is rejected. The mix may incorporate Type IP, IS, IL or II cement but must exclude admixtures containing chloride salts or high‑early‑strength cement, and air‑entraining material is limited to fifteen percent of the total cementitious mass. When rapid‑set concrete is specified, the manufacturer’s initial‑set time—typically five minutes for very fast sets up to fifteen minutes at approximately 75 °F—must be met, and the material must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 1 000 psi before traffic (3 000 psi for bridge decks prior to laitance removal). Early‑age durability requirements include a durability factor of at least eighty percent after three hundred freeze–thaw cycles, a seven‑day compressive strength no less than 700 psi, bleed loss not greater than 2.5 %, and overall expansion limited to ten percent. For repairs deeper than two inches, aggregate extenders supplied with the rapid‑set product or, if unavailable, AASHTO No. 8 coarse aggregate with an expansion index below 0.10 must be incorporated. Compatibility demands that the repair material match the existing concrete type—typically Class AA or modified Class AAAP for bridge decks—and that any overlay use the same cementitious system when a latex overlay is planned, while selecting mix designs and overlay thicknesses that conform to prescribed limits ensures harmonious work with surrounding pavement. Bonding agents such as epoxy (ASTM C881 Type II Grade 2) or approved grout must be applied to clean, tacky surfaces within the manufacturer‑specified time window, and contact must occur before the bonding compound dries. Dowel bars coated with type B require a bond‑breaker lubricant applied to at least half of their length using a non‑graphite product having a flash point of not less than 149 °F, whereas type A dowels need no lubricant. Expansion joints are to be filled with preformed cellular polystyrene or other joint filler cut to the exact cross‑sectional dimensions and extended to the pavement surface so that fresh concrete is kept out of the joint. All materials, including polymer‑modified rapid‑hardening concrete, bag mixes, accelerated structural concrete, or accelerated slip‑form paving, must conform to the approved mix design; any change requires a new submission and approval. Existing pavement conditions that jeopardize patch performance include unstable slabs or fragments that rock under traffic, which must be stabilized before placement. Surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be fully filled and compacted to a flat, tight surface; when a geotextile interlayer is present every such defect must be repaired, while with an asphalt interlayer shallow defects may be incorporated only if the patch material can be properly compacted together with the overlay. Full‑depth saw cuts, undamaged adjacent slabs, and subbase irregularities exceeding one inch must be corrected, and a clearance of at least three‑quarters of an inch around all reinforcement bars is required. Ambient temperature during placement must be forty degrees Fahrenheit or higher and rising; repairs must be protected from freezing for a minimum of twenty‑four hours, and if curing temperatures fall below eighty degrees Fahrenheit insulation or heating must be provided. The fresh patch should be manually tamped to at least a half‑inch depth, finished flush with the existing surface, and kept moist for a minimum of three days using an acceptable method that prevents evaporation. Curing is completed with a white membrane‑forming compound, and temperature fluctuations during curing must not exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit in any one‑hour period; exceeding this limit renders the repair defective. The patch must be placed within forty‑eight hours of excavation, saw cuts opened to traffic may not exceed half an inch in width nor remain open longer than seventy‑two hours, and joints or cracks exhibiting a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more must be stabilized before repair. For preservation projects the patched area may not exceed ten percent of the total pavement surface unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided; exceeding this threshold generally disqualifies the work from preservation eligibility. When no asphalt overlay is planned after the concrete repair, diamond grinding may be required to achieve the intended smooth ride. [1][2]

Treatment procedures

To perform a concrete patch the crew first submits a quality‑control plan at least two weeks before work begins, assigning only personnel who have a minimum of five years’ experience and confirming that daytime air temperatures are above 40 °F and the subgrade is not frozen. Traffic protection and site safety measures are installed, then the pavement surface is swept or vacuumed to remove loose debris and any unstable slab fragments are stabilized or removed; when indicated scarification or hydro‑demolition is carried out before delineating the repair zone with diamond‑tipped saw cuts. The perimeter of each patch is cut with a water‑cooled, guided blade that provides full‑depth longitudinal cuts followed by transverse cuts, typically a six‑inch wide strip beside a longitudinal joint or a three‑quarter‑inch deep outline around each unsound area; the cuts are made to create a sealant reservoir at the pavement/patch interface. Load‑transfer dowels, expansion caps and non‑metallic chairs are placed in the transverse faces of the existing slab, the dowels being lubricated with a bond‑breaker at least one hour before concrete placement. The damaged concrete is then removed in one or more pieces using hand tools, pneumatic hammers or jackhammers not exceeding 30 lb (15 lb when damage is severe), taking care to avoid disturbance of the subbase and adjacent pavement; all loose material is cleared within 24 hours and a final air‑blast cleaning with at least 100 psi compressed air is performed no later than thirty minutes before placement. Exposed reinforcement is abrasive‑blasted to a clean white metal finish and coated with epoxy, while any through‑wall openings are filled by forcing a non‑staining, non‑shrinking mortar through the opening with a plunger‑type caulking gun, holding burlap or canvas over the front face; blind holes are rammed in the same manner. All contact surfaces are wetted and a grout or epoxy bonding compound is brushed onto them while still tacky. The patching material—selected from rapid‑set concrete, modified Class AAAP cement concrete, LMC, ASC, or a minor‑patch grout of one part cement to two parts fine aggregate with water limited to 4½ gallons per bag—is mixed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (mix for 60 seconds, cover to prevent moisture loss, stand 45 minutes, remix for another 60 seconds and place within 30 minutes). The fresh concrete is placed directly into its final position while the bond coat remains tacky, consolidated promptly with a spud or pencil vibrator or internal/external vibration, and worked into the saw cuts beyond the patch corners; forms are set on all sides not touching existing pavement and temporary polystyrene forms keep new concrete out of existing joints, with expansion‑joint material used as required. The surface is finished true and even, free of roughness, honeycombing, depressions or projections, textured to match the surrounding cross‑section and struck flush with adjacent pavement without adding water during finishing. Within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates a curing compound or a white membrane‑forming curing agent is applied by atomizing spray, the repair is protected from freezing for at least 24 hours and curing is continued for a minimum of three days, insulating or heating the area if ambient temperature falls below 80 °F and monitoring that temperature changes do not exceed 40 °F in any one‑hour period. After the patch attains a minimum compressive strength of 3,000 psi the laitance is removed by water blasting, normal curing continues until sufficient strength for traffic is reached, joints and extended saw cuts are resealed, grout is placed around the patch and, after the prescribed curing period, traffic is reopened. Implementation of concrete patching begins with locating the distressed area and marking its limits, typically by laying a chain‑drag three feet wide with drops spaced six inches apart. Full‑depth longitudinal saw cuts are made first, followed by transverse cuts; the saw must be power‑driven, self‑propelled and fitted with a diamond‑tipped blade, blade guard, alignment guides, water‑cooling system and depth‑control features, capable of producing two parallel cuts per dowel slot. After cutting, concrete is removed using hand tools or pneumatic hammers limited to 30 ft‑lb (15 ft‑lb for final cleanup) and a jackhammer not exceeding thirty pounds (or fifteen pounds when damage is present). The voids are cleared with water‑blasting equipment rated at a minimum of 5,000 psi (or clean dry oil‑free air at least 100 psi) and the exposed surfaces are cleaned within thirty minutes before placement.

The repair material is a rapid‑set concrete patching mix that complies with ASTM C928; it must be used within the manufacturer’s shelf life and temperature limits and is required to achieve an initial set in no more than fifteen minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F. When the repair depth exceeds two inches, aggregate extenders prescribed by the supplier are added. Mixing consists of a sixty‑second blend, covering the mix to prevent moisture loss, a forty‑five minute rest, a second sixty‑second remix and placement within thirty minutes; each batch must be placed in form no more than half an hour after the preceding one. Holes are saturated with water and filled immediately with non‑staining, non‑shrinking mortar using a plunger‑type caulking gun or similar device; for minor patches a neat cement‑and‑water paint coat is applied, tamped at least half an inch deep, overfilled when possible and then struck off excess. Placement proceeds while the epoxy bonding compound remains tacky or before grout begins to dry. Consolidation of the fresh mix is achieved with manual or mechanical tampers and vibrators—pencil, spud, internal or external—or small vibratory compactors for sub‑base work.

Quality and workmanship

Concrete patching projects begin with a thorough materials inspection that confirms every item slated for use—structural concrete, reinforcing steel, dowel or tie bars, rapid‑set patching mixes, bonding agents, sealants and any admixtures—carries the required certifications, is within its manufacturer’s shelf life and temperature limits, and originates from manufacturers approved on Bulletin 15. The cement types permitted are limited to those authorized for the project such as Types IP, IS, IL or II, while high‑early‑strength cement and any admixture containing chloride salts are expressly prohibited, and water consumption may not exceed four and a half gallons per bag of cement. Mix designs must demonstrate a cement factor between 530 lb/yd³ and 600 lb/yd³, a water‑cement ratio that does not exceed 0.42 with a target of 0.40 and never falling below 0.38, an air content at placement of 7 % ± 1.5 %, and when supplementary cementitious material is used, at least 400 lb/yd³ of Portland cement must be present with the SCM limited to thirty percent of the total blend. The aggregate shall be durable washed stone or gravel meeting specified expansion limits, and any air‑entraining admixture may not exceed fifteen percent by mass of the cementitious portion; all constituents must satisfy a durability requirement of at least eighty percent retention after three hundred freeze‑thaw cycles (ASTM C666). Independent laboratory testing is required to document one‑, three‑ and seven‑day compressive strengths, flowability, shrinkage or expansion limited to zero‑to‑ten percent, an initial set time of fifteen minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F, and bleed loss not greater than two point five percent of the volume. For conventional patches a minimum average seven‑day compressive strength of seven hundred psi, based on five cylinders, is required; accelerated or bridge‑deck repairs must achieve at least one thousand five hundred psi after seven hours (PTM 604) and ultimately one thousand psi in cylinder tests before traffic may be allowed, with three thousand psi specified for certain bridge decks. Prior to any concrete placement a qualified quality‑control plan, submitted at least two weeks in advance, must specify testing frequencies and corrective actions, and the inspector verifies that this plan is on file. Saw cuts defining the repair perimeter are made with a diamond‑tipped blade equipped with water cooling, depth controls and guards; the cut depth shall be no less than three‑quarters of an inch and follow the prescribed mid‑depth location. All unsound concrete within the saw cut is removed, the exposed reinforcement is abrasive‑blasted to a clean white metal surface, and any oil or loose material is eliminated before placement. Holes are saturated with water immediately prior to being completely filled with non‑staining, non‑shrinking mortar, and defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be swept clean of debris using an approved method such as a power broom or vacuum truck. When a geotextile interlayer is employed no more than three layers may be placed, the material must lie flat, and its temperature may not exceed one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit. Sub‑base grading must be within plus‑or‑minus half an inch and the bedding surface flattened to within one‑eighth inch over the slab length before the patch is laid. Dowel inserts and expansion caps are inspected for correct size, clearance and positioning, with allowable tilt of a quarter inch and longitudinal translation not exceeding one inch. During placement the inspector confirms that forms, side forms, drip strips and chamfers are correctly set and that concrete is placed while still wet or while epoxy bonding compound remains tacky to ensure bond continuity. The concrete must be placed without segregation; each batch is deposited within thirty minutes of the preceding one and consolidated by approved vibration (pencil vibrator) or manual tamping to at least a half‑inch depth, ensuring all voids are filled and fresh concrete extends into saw cuts beyond repair corners. Joint gaps are required to be between one‑quarter and one‑half inch transversely, longitudinal joints not exceeding three‑quarters inch, and vertical differentials no greater than one‑quarter inch. Dowel‑bar and tie‑bar grout must reach a minimum compressive strength of two thousand five hundred psi before traffic is permitted. The patch is cured with an acceptable moisture‑retention method for at least three days; ambient temperature must be at least forty degrees Fahrenheit and rising, the surface temperature must be increasing, and the repair must be protected from freezing for a minimum of twenty‑four hours. Curing temperature changes are monitored hourly and may not exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit in any one‑hour period; exceeding this limit constitutes a defect. Upon completion, cores are taken from the repaired area to verify depth and integrity of the existing slab as well as to confirm that peripheral voids around dowel anchors do not exceed half an inch. The surface flatness is checked with a twelve‑foot straightedge, and any high or low spot greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch is ground out to match the surrounding pavement. Deflection testing of each joint or crack using Falling Weight Deflectometer (or ASTM D4695) confirms that loaded slab‑corner deflection does not exceed zero point zero two inch and that joint efficiency meets specifications. The inspector also verifies that all material certifications have been satisfied, that no prohibited lubricants or chloride‑containing admixtures remain, and that the patch has achieved the required compressive strength before opening to traffic. If the repair area exceeds ten percent of the total pavement area a cost‑effective justification must be shown; otherwise the project is ineligible for pavement preservation treatment. All failed joints must be repaired and this repair verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, and when no asphalt overlay follows the patch, diamond grinding may be required to achieve the intended smooth ride. Final acceptance is documented by resounding the completed repair area in the presence of the representative; any delamination, excessive voids, surface deficiencies or failure to meet strength, durability or flatness requirements results in a defective rating and the patch must be repaired at no cost to the department. A satisfactory concrete patching repair must address every identified failed joint, crack, spall or other faulted area, with the extent of the work defined before final design and verified through appropriate testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements. The total patched surface is limited to ten percent of the overall pavement unless a cost‑effectiveness analysis demonstrates that exceeding this proportion provides net benefit. A documented quality‑control plan must be submitted at least two weeks prior to commencement, and all repair steps are required to conform to defined dimensional, material, placement and performance tolerances. The repaired segment must extend across a minimum of one lane width, with the allowable length governed by splice type—six to twenty feet for tied Type A splices (four to twenty feet when welded), twenty‑point‑one to sixty‑five feet for Type B, and sixty‑five‑point‑one to five hundred feet for Type C. Prior to cutting, the distressed zone is sound‑checked and clearly delineated; full‑depth saw cuts at least three‑quarters of an inch deep and no wider than one‑half inch are made, providing a minimum clearance of three‑quarters of an inch around any reinforcement. Saw cuts exceeding a half‑inch in width must not be opened to traffic. All cut surfaces and adjacent concrete are air‑blasted with oil‑free compressed air, cleared of debris, and the exposed steel is epoxy‑coated, with final cleaning completed within thirty minutes before placement. The patching material must be an approved non‑staining, non‑shrinking mortar or rapid‑set cementitious repair product that meets Class AA requirements, including a minimum cement factor of 530 lb/yd³, a water‑cement ratio not exceeding 0.42 (target 0.38–0.40), air content of 7 % ± 1.5 %, expansion limited to 0.1 % (aggregate extenders up to ten percent), an initial set time of at least fifteen minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F, and a durability factor of eighty percent after three hundred freeze‑thaw cycles per ASTM C666 B; the product must be listed in Bulletin 15 and conform to ASTM C928 and used within its shelf life and temperature limits. Placement is permitted only when ambient air and surface temperatures are forty degrees Fahrenheit or higher and rising, and the fresh mix is consolidated by vibration or manual tamping to a minimum depth of one‑half inch, overfilled where possible, then struck off after five minutes without excessive trowelling. The patch must be cured for at least three days using a method that prevents moisture loss, protected from freezing for a minimum twenty‑four hours, and the curing temperature may not change more than forty degrees Fahrenheit in any one‑hour period; any greater fluctuation is considered defective. Before traffic is reopened the repaired concrete must achieve a compressive strength of at least one thousand psi as measured on cylinders, with barrier patches required to reach three thousand psi before laitance removal by water blasting, and an early benchmark of seven‑day strength of seven hundred psi based on the average of five test cylinders. The finished surface must be flush with the existing deck, matching elevation, profile and wheel ruts; any high points or depressions greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch (0.05 in) are corrected by grinding, and a twelve‑foot straightedge is used to verify smoothness. Visible defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be completely filled and compacted to a flat, tight surface, providing uniform support for subsequent layers. Void dimensions around dowel bars may not exceed half an inch on any core; dowels may not tilt more than a quarter inch vertically or horizontally at either end, nor translate more than one inch longitudinally or transversely, and plastic or non‑metallic chairs must provide at least a half‑inch clearance between the dowel cap and slot surfaces. Joint preparation requires a partial‑depth cut, formation of a sealant reservoir and sealing before concrete placement; expansion‑joint material and temporary polystyrene forms extend to the pavement surface to keep fresh concrete out of existing joints. Repair areas may not remain unpatched for more than forty‑eight hours, and any subbase irregularities deeper than one inch must be corrected prior to placement. Acceptance is indicated by the absence of delamination during inspection, no radial cracks emanating from grout injection holes, no transverse cracks between adjacent holes, and joint deflection less than 0.02 in after two groutings; any occurrence of these indicators requires correction or full‑slab replacement at no cost to the owner. When an asphalt overlay is not planned, additional diamond grinding may be required to achieve the intended smooth ride. [1][2]

Performance and service life

Concrete patching is scheduled at key points in a pavement’s service life—around year 15, again near year 25, and once more by year 35—to treat small, localized defects that affect only a modest portion of the surface (approximately 2 % at the first interval, rising to about 4 % and then 6 %) [1]. The treatment targets cracked joints, spalls, unstable slabs, or moving slab fragments, repairing them with an approved cementitious or asphalt‑based patching material compacted to a flat surface, thereby restoring structural condition and preventing non‑uniform support of any subsequent overlay [1][2]. When the repaired area remains below roughly ten percent of the total pavement, the activity is classified as a non‑structural preservation measure; it may be combined with diamond grinding when no asphalt overlay is planned to regain smooth ride quality and skid resistance, enhancing functional performance [1]. All failed joints must be identified and verified—typically through Falling Weight Deflectometer testing—to ensure that the repair achieves the intended durability and that the repaired sections can sustain traffic loads without premature cracking [1][2]. By stabilizing moving slab fragments and filling surface defects larger than four square inches and one inch deep, the load‑bearing functionality of the pavement is maintained and the likelihood of further distress is reduced, supporting the durability of the repaired portion and contributing to the overall system’s service life [2]. Within a fifty‑year analysis horizon, these periodic modest‑scale interventions collectively sustain pavement condition, preserve functional capacity, and extend the expected service life of the concrete pavement system [1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (p. 73), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462)

Concrete patching is employed to remediate localized failures such as failed or faulted joints, various crack types, and transverse or longitudinal spalling, including deterioration of earlier asphalt or concrete patches [1]. The presence and severity of these distresses are typically confirmed through Falling Weight Deflectometer testing of joint performance, which guides the decision to undertake patching work [1]. Guidelines restrict the total patched area to less than ten percent of the pavement surface unless a cost‑effectiveness justification can be demonstrated; exceeding this limit may disqualify the project from preservation eligibility [1]. When a concrete patch is not followed by an asphalt overlay, additional diamond grinding is often required to achieve the desired ride smoothness, underscoring the role of structural integrity and surface finish in treatment success [1]. Recurring failures frequently arise from workmanship shortcomings or adverse site conditions, manifesting as break‑back or spalling at saw cuts, premature cracks or spalls before final inspection, and surface irregularities that surpass the allowable tolerance of three‑sixteenths of an inch [2]. Panels that are raised more than five hundredths of an inch must be ground back to grade, reflecting the importance of maintaining tight vertical tolerances after placement [2]. Radial cracks emanating from grout injection holes and transverse cracks between adjacent injections indicate poor drilling or sequencing practices and often necessitate full‑depth slab replacement [2]. Edge damage incurred during handling or installation, as well as excessive vertical differentials across joints greater than one‑quarter inch, can lead to settlement of ungrouted slabs and require removal, regrading, and reseating before grout application [2]. Unstable slab fragments that rock under traffic create non‑uniform support for any subsequent overlay, making stabilization or repair a prerequisite before interlayer placement [2]. Surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch, if left unfilled or insufficiently compacted, tend to delaminate or settle, compromising the patch’s performance [2]. Improper curing conditions—such as ambient temperatures dropping below 80 °F, hourly temperature swings exceeding 40 °F, surface temperatures above 120 °F for geotextile interlayers, work on frozen subgrade, rain exposure, or prolonged open‑patch periods—promote cracking and loss of bond [2]. Inadequate application of curing compounds or lack of insulation further aggravates these temperature‑related failures, emphasizing the need for controlled curing environments [2]. Substrate preparation errors, including insufficient compaction of disturbed material deeper than one inch, leaving excavated areas unpatched for more than forty‑eight hours, and permitting traffic on fresh saw cuts wider than one‑half inch, accelerate premature distress development [2]. Dowel anchoring must avoid peripheral voids larger than half an inch, and traffic should not be reopened until grout cylinders achieve at least 1,000 psi compressive strength, highlighting the influence of material strength criteria on durability [2]. Collectively, these failures are driven by inadequate saw‑cut dimensions, damaging removal equipment, poor subbase compaction, use of unapproved materials, insufficient compaction quality, improper temperature management, and failure to meet specified material strengths and tolerances [2].

Monitoring and follow-up

After the concrete patch has been placed, its cure must be observed at least hourly, confirming that the temperature does not change more than forty degrees Fahrenheit in any one‑hour period; a larger swing renders the work defective. Once curing is complete, all wooden plugs are removed and each drill hole is filled with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink patching material struck flush with the surrounding pavement. The finished surface is then examined with a twelve‑foot straightedge both longitudinally and across the joints; any high spots or depressions of three‑sixteenths inch or greater are ground down and the area rechecked with the straightedge. Cylindrical specimens taken from the patch are tested for compressive strength, and the pavement may be opened to traffic only after the Representative confirms that a minimum of 1,000 psi has been achieved in accordance with PTM 604. A resound test is performed on each repaired section in the presence of the Representative, and any delaminated patches must be replaced at no cost to the Department. Ride quality is subsequently measured using the pavement surface‑profile equipment and procedures of Sections 507.3(a) and (b), establishing a baseline before any diamond‑grinding work begins. If directed, a deflection test is repeated about twenty‑four hours after grouting; slabs that move more than 0.02 inch are re‑grouted and retested, with persistent movement after two attempts permitting the Representative to require full‑depth replacement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 395–419), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

When a patched concrete area continues to develop distress such as new or repeated cracking, joint spalling, faulting, delamination, or radial and transverse cracks associated with grout injection holes, the trend signals that routine patching is insufficient and further corrective action is required [1][2]. If the cumulative extent of patching approaches or exceeds roughly ten percent of the pavement surface, the cost‑effectiveness of additional patches diminishes and a transition to more extensive repair or rehabilitation becomes advisable [1]. Persistent deficiencies in skid resistance observed after a patch are also an indicator that additional maintenance is needed [1]. Any occurrence of cracking, spalling, delamination, break‑back, or similar surface damage at any time before the final acceptance inspection mandates immediate removal and replacement of the affected section at no cost to the owner [2]. Surface profiling that reveals high points or depressions equal to or greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch after grinding is considered a defect and triggers repair actions [2]. The patched slab must attain a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi before it can be opened to traffic; failure to meet this threshold requires remedial measures [2]. Dowel bar anchorage voids larger than one‑half inch around the perimeter of any core are unacceptable and signal the need for additional repair [2]. Vertical differentials between the patched area and the adjoining permanent pavement greater than one‑quarter inch constitute a permanent fault, requiring removal of the patch, regrading of the bedding surface, and resetting before grout placement can resume [2]. Joint gaps that exceed specified limits or grout that does not achieve its required compressive strength are further signs that additional repair is necessary [2]. Recurring distress after installation, such as repeated cracking, obliges cessation of work until the underlying cause is identified and mitigated [1][2]. A temperature swing exceeding forty degrees Fahrenheit within any one‑hour period during curing is deemed defective and must be corrected [2]. Loaded slab‑corner deflections of at least 0.02 in, whether the joint efficiency is above or below sixty‑five percent, demand stabilization or patching of the affected joints [2]. Panel displacement greater than five hundredths of an inch or observable vertical movement during pumping also triggers maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation actions [2].
