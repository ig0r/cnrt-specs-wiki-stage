Concrete Patching

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Concrete Patching is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
concrete-patching
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-23

Concrete patching is a preservation treatment applied to rigid concrete pavements to repair localized distress such as failed joints, cracks, spalls, or deteriorated patches, thereby restoring structural integrity and ride quality[1]. The method involves full‑depth removal of the damaged zone, verification that the surrounding slab remains sound through Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, replacement with new concrete (often followed by joint rehabilitation and optionally diamond grinding when no asphalt overlay is used), and limits the total patched area to roughly ten percent of the pavement unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided[1]. By addressing these isolated defects at strategic points in the pavement’s service life—typically around years 15, 25, and 35—the treatment halts further deterioration, preserves functional performance, and can extend the overall service life of the concrete system while remaining classified as a non‑structural preservation activity[1].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Concrete patching is used to treat localized distress in concrete pavements that threatens structural integrity, ride quality, and joint functionality. The treatment targets conditions such as failed or faulted joints, joint spalling, cracks, transverse or longitudinal spalls, and any isolated areas where the surface has broken away or existing patches have deteriorated. Full‑depth patching is applied to these zones, with the condition of the joints confirmed by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing. The scope of a preservation project is generally limited to ten percent of the total pavement area unless a cost‑effective justification can be demonstrated; within that limit the locations and quantities of required patches are identified and repaired to restore joint performance and halt further deterioration. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Concrete patching on rigid pavements is primarily a repair treatment aimed at fixing localized damage such as failed joints, cracks, spalls and other distress so that the pavement regains a smooth ride and functional performance. The guidelines require that every failed joint be repaired—verification being performed with Falling Weight Deflectometer testing—and that the patching volume encompass faulted joints, cracks, existing asphalt patches, failed concrete patches and transverse or longitudinal spalls. By restoring the damaged areas, the treatment reestablishes the pavement’s functional condition, while the act of sealing and rebuilding those zones also serves to retard further deterioration of the concrete surface. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Distress characterization and causes

Concrete patching is often required when localized distress arises from underlying deficiencies such as inadequate subbase material that can lead to premature mid‑slab cracking, and aggregate‑related problems like Vanport Limestone concerns or Alkali Silica Reaction which weaken the concrete but may not be captured by routine condition surveys. These mechanisms reflect weak support and material degradation that accelerate pavement deterioration and necessitate targeted patch repairs. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Applicability and treatment selection

Concrete patching is appropriate on rigid (concrete) pavements that are structurally sound, as confirmed by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, and where the localized distress—such as failed joints, cracks or spalls—does not exceed about ten percent of the total pavement area unless a cost‑effectiveness justification can be demonstrated. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Concrete patching is appropriate only for localized distress affecting a small portion of the slab. When testing or inspection indicates that the area to be patched would exceed roughly ten percent of the total pavement surface, the activity ceases to be cost‑effective as a preservation measure; in such cases the project must be reclassified as a more extensive rehabilitation or reconstruction unless a clear cost‑effectiveness justification for exceeding the ten‑percent limit can be demonstrated. Similarly, scratch overlays applied to existing concrete that would require a thickness greater than 4½ inches when using a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing‑course mix, or greater than 5 inches with a 12.5 mm mix, fall outside the preservation scope and must be addressed through the 3R criteria or alternative treatments such as mill‑and‑overlay, diamond grinding, micro‑surfacing, or ultra‑thin bonded courses. When the pavement exhibits widespread structural deficiencies—extensive cracking, joint faulting, or has surpassed its functional service life—the appropriate response shifts from spot patching to larger‑scale interventions, including base repair, full‑depth overlays, or complete reconstruction, which provide the necessary structural renewal. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Condition assessment and timing

Determining the need for concrete patching begins with a systematic condition assessment that combines both remote and on‑site investigations. Each year the roadway network is surveyed using an automated videologging system under the STAMPP program, which records surface distress such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling or weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting. The survey results are entered into treatment matrices that flag locations where patching may be required. For those flagged areas, engineers conduct field visual inspections to verify the presence and severity of the observed defects. Where verification is needed, core samples are extracted from the pavement; the cores are examined to confirm concrete thickness and overall integrity. In parallel, a Falling Weight Deflectometer test is performed on the same sections to quantify joint efficiency and to pinpoint the exact locations and quantities of concrete that have lost structural capacity. The combination of the automated condition data, confirmed visual observations, core‑sample analysis, and FWD measurements defines both whether patching is necessary and the extent of the repair. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Concrete patching is employed when a rigid concrete pavement exhibits localized failures—such as faulted joints, spalling, isolated cracking or other deteriorated zones—that are identified in condition surveys and have not yet expanded to affect more than roughly ten percent of the total surface. In practice the treatment is scheduled at distinct points in the pavement’s service life: around the ten‑year mark when about five percent of the area shows distress; again near fifteen years when approximately two percent requires repair; at twenty years the affected portion may rise to eight percent; at twenty‑five years it is typically four percent; by thirty years the distressed area is again close to two percent; and a further application around thirty‑five years addresses roughly six percent. These intervals reflect stages where the localized damage is sufficient to warrant full‑depth patching but remains below the ten‑percent threshold that would render preservation uneconomical. The work, classified as routine maintenance and pavement preservation, must include repair of all failed joints (verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing) and may also cover cracks, transverse or longitudinal spalls, existing asphalt patches, and other isolated defects. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 72–75), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Materials and repair details

Concrete patching on rigid pavements is limited to a maximum of ten percent of the total pavement area unless cost‑effectiveness can be demonstrated, and it must address all failed joints, cracks, spalls and other localized distresses identified before final design. The repair may consist of full‑depth concrete replacement combined with joint rehabilitation, optionally followed by diamond grinding when no asphalt overlay is used, and can incorporate dowel‑bar retrofits or slab stabilization/jacking as needed. Overlay options include a 1 ½‑inch Superpave 9.5 mm mix or a 2‑inch Superpave 12.5 mm mix placed over a 2 ½‑inch Superpave 19.0 mm base, with an optional half‑inch scratch course, as well as a 1‑inch (6.3 mm) thin asphalt overlay or polymer‑modified emulsified micro‑surfacing for structurally sound sections. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

The performance of a concrete patch is fundamentally tied to the condition and structural capacity of the existing slab and its supporting layers. Before any repair is placed, the slab must be shown to be sound—typically through Falling‑Weight Deflectometer testing—and every failed joint must be rehabilitated. An inadequate subbase that leads to premature mid‑slab cracking can undermine the bond between new and old concrete, so the integrity of the underlying material is a critical prerequisite. Likewise, the presence of problematic aggregates such as Vanport Limestone or susceptibility to alkali‑silica reaction influences durability and skid resistance, often necessitating earlier overlay or special treatment prior to patching. Compatibility requirements further restrict the extent of repair: unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided, the patched area may not exceed ten percent of the total pavement surface for preservation projects. When an asphalt overlay is not planned after the concrete repair, diamond grinding may be required to achieve the intended smooth ride. Finally, selecting mix designs and overlay thicknesses that conform to prescribed limits ensures that the new material works harmoniously with the surrounding pavement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Quality and workmanship

Concrete patching must begin with coring of the existing slab so that depth and integrity can be inspected, and the planned patch area should be limited to ten percent of the pavement unless a cost‑effective justification is shown; all failed joints are required to be repaired and this repair verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, and if no asphalt overlay follows the patch, diamond grinding may be needed to achieve the intended smooth ride. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

A satisfactory concrete patching must repair every failed joint, with verification by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, and the total patched area may not exceed ten percent of the pavement unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided. The quantity of patches must be defined before final design and should encompass all faulted joints, cracks, spalls and any existing failed sections. If no asphalt overlay follows, additional diamond grinding may be required to achieve the intended smooth ride. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Performance and service life

Concrete patching is employed at strategic points in a pavement’s lifecycle—typically around year 15, again near year 25, and once more by year 35—to treat small, localized defects that affect only a modest portion of the surface (approximately 2 % at the first interval, rising to about 4 % and then 6 %). By repairing cracked joints, spalls, or other failed patches, the treatment restores the structural condition of the rigid pavement and halts the progression of distress, thereby preserving overall pavement condition. When confined to less than roughly ten percent of the total area, patching is classified as a non‑structural preservation activity; it can be paired with diamond grinding when no asphalt overlay is planned to regain smooth ride quality and skid resistance, enhancing functional performance. The requirement that all failed joints be identified and repaired—verified through Falling Weight Deflectometer testing—ensures the durability of the repair and supports the intended service life extension. Within a 50‑year analysis horizon, these periodic, modest‑scale interventions contribute to sustained durability and an extended expected service life for the concrete pavement system. [1]

Concrete patching commonly addresses failed joints, faulted joints, cracks and spalling (including transverse and longitudinal spalls) as well as areas where previous asphalt or concrete patches have deteriorated. Monitoring and follow-up

When a concrete‑patched section continues to exhibit distress such as recurring cracking, joint spalling or faulting, or when the amount of patching required begins to approach or exceed about ten percent of the pavement’s surface, those trends signal that routine patching alone is no longer sufficient and that additional maintenance, more extensive repair, or even rehabilitation may be needed. Persistent skid‑resistance problems identified after patching also trigger further corrective action. [1]
