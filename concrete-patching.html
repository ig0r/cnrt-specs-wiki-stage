Concrete Patching

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Concrete Patching is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
concrete-patching
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-29

Concrete patching is a preservation‑level repair treatment applied to rigid Class AA concrete pavements that removes and replaces localized failures such as faulted joints, cracks wider than 0.007 in, spalls larger than four square inches or unstable slab fragments, thereby restoring structural continuity, load transfer and ride quality [1][2]. The procedure consists of full‑depth saw cutting around the distressed area, extraction of the unsound concrete, cleaning of the substrate, placement of a rapid‑set, non‑shrink cementitious patch (often with dowel‑bar reinforcement) followed by controlled curing and verification of joint efficiency and slab deflection with Falling Weight Deflectometer testing before traffic is reopened [1][2]. It is limited to patches that affect no more than roughly ten percent of the total pavement surface, requires ambient temperatures of at least 40 °F and a minimum compressive strength of about 1,000 psi for the new material, and is triggered when loaded slab‑corner deflection reaches 0.02 in or joint efficiency falls below about 65 % [1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Concrete Patching” also include the following subtopics: Full-Depth Patching, Partial-Depth Repair, Patch Joint, Patching Material, Percent Patching, and Slab Replacement.

Full‑Depth Patching and Slab Replacement address the general procedure when distress compromises the entire slab thickness or requires complete unit removal, while Partial‑Depth Repair applies it to surface-level defects confined to the upper half of the pavement. [1][2] The method utilizes specific Patching Material formulations that meet Class AA specifications to ensure rapid strength gain and durability during placement. [2] A Patch Joint serves as a specialized application for restoring structural continuity at deteriorated joints using dowel bars and sealed interfaces, with Percent Patching providing the quantitative metric that determines whether this treatment remains viable or if alternative strategies are required. [1]

Purpose and treatment objectives

Concrete patching is employed to remediate localized distress on rigid concrete pavements that jeopardizes slab integrity, load transfer, ride quality, or joint performance. The treatment specifically targets failed or faulted joints and joint spalling, as well as transverse or longitudinal spalls where the surface has broken away or previous patches have deteriorated. Cracks that exceed a width of 0.007 inches are also addressed; those extending deeper than one‑third of the slab thickness are removed and replaced, while shallower cracks are repaired in accordance with ACI 224R recommendations. Additional distress conditions include break‑back at slab edges, delamination, and manufacturing or handling imperfections such as rough spots, honeycombed zones, depressions, or projections that compromise surface uniformity. Joint or crack movement that produces a loaded corner deflection of 0.02 inches or more together with a joint efficiency below roughly 65 percent is another criterion for patching. Unstable slabs or slab fragments that rock visibly under normal traffic loads are stabilized through full‑depth patching to restore uniform support. Sub‑base surface irregularities exceeding one inch in depth are also corrected as part of the concrete patching scope. The condition of joints slated for repair is typically verified with Falling Weight Deflectometer testing before full‑depth patching is applied. When dowel bar reinforcement is required, slots are cut around the affected cracks or transverse joints and filled with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete patching material; the same material may be used for corner repairs where localized distress exists. Preservation projects that incorporate concrete patching generally limit the total patched area to ten percent of the overall pavement surface unless a cost‑effective justification demonstrates the need for a larger extent. [1][2]

Concrete patching on rigid pavements is defined as a repair treatment that addresses localized distress such as failed joints, cracks, spalls and other damage so the pavement regains a smooth ride and functional performance. The guidelines require immediate full‑depth replacement of any excavated, spalled or cracked area with an approved cementitious patch, removal of damaged sections, and matching of the repaired cross‑section to allow traffic to resume safely. By restoring the damaged zones the treatment reestablishes structural integrity, serviceability and uniform support, thereby repairing damage and returning the slab to its intended functional condition. In addition, sealing and rebuilding these areas also serves to retard further deterioration of the concrete surface, slowing the progression of distress. For cracks exceeding specified dimensions—wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third of the slab thickness—the treatment mandates replacement, reflecting the aim of preventing worsening of damage. Overall, the primary objective of concrete patching is to repair localized damage, restore functional performance, and concurrently slow future deterioration, thereby extending the effective service life of the pavement. [1][2]

Distress characterization and causes

The distress that triggers concrete patching is found on rigid concrete pavement surfaces, primarily within the active traffic lanes and at joints or cracks where slab movement is evident. It manifests as localized surface defects—cracks, spalls, break‑back, or unstable slab fragments—that exceed four square inches in area and more than one inch in depth, or as slabs that rock visibly under normal loads. Additional criteria include loaded slab‑corner deflections of at least 0.02 inch and joint efficiencies falling below about 65 percent, as well as any upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 inch. Although each individual defect is limited in size, the presence of spalling or break‑back can extend into adjacent lanes and compromise load transfer. The severity of these conditions is judged by the visible loss of concrete material, the measured deflection values, and the degree of joint inefficiency; when any of these thresholds are met a full‑depth transverse saw cut and replacement with rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete is required. Because the defects can deteriorate rapidly, the specification mandates that excavated or opened patch areas be repaired within 48 hours—excluding weekends or holidays—and that traffic be protected from drop‑off hazards. Traffic may remain on a patched section for no more than 72 hours until the new material attains the required strength, and any upward movement exceeding 0.05 inch must halt pumping operations. If left unaddressed, the distress creates safety and operational impacts: open pits present drop‑off dangers to vehicles, lane capacity is reduced during repair, ride quality degrades, and unstable support can jeopardize the performance of an overlying pavement layer. Consequently, prompt stabilization or replacement of the affected concrete is essential before any interlayer material is placed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Concrete patching becomes necessary when localized pavement distress stems from underlying weaknesses that compromise structural continuity [1][2]. Inadequate subbase material is identified as a primary factor that can precipitate premature mid‑slab cracking in jointed concrete pavements [1]. Aggregate‑related problems such as the presence of Vanport Limestone or the development of Alkali Silica Reaction further degrade the concrete matrix, accelerating deterioration even though they may escape detection by routine condition surveys [1]. Slab instability, manifested as visible movement or rocking of slabs or slab fragments under normal traffic loads, creates non‑uniform support that undermines the integrity of any subsequent overlay [2]. Surface defects exceeding four square inches in area and deeper than one inch also break the structural continuity of the pavement, reflecting weak underlying support that must be addressed before interlayer placement [2]. These mechanisms—weak support from deficient subbase, material aging and chemical reactions within the concrete, and physical instability of slabs—collectively accelerate distress and necessitate targeted cementitious or asphalt patch repairs as pre‑overlay stabilization measures [1][2].

Applicability and treatment selection

Concrete patching is appropriate on rigid, Class AA cement‑concrete pavements that are structurally sound as verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, and where the distress is confined to localized areas such as failed joints, cracks, spalls, or unstable slab fragments that move or rock under normal traffic loads [1][2]. The treatment is limited to situations in which the total area requiring patching does not exceed roughly ten percent of the overall pavement surface unless a cost‑effectiveness justification can be demonstrated, and it may be applied even when joint efficiencies are either above or below the 65 percent threshold provided the Representative approves the work [1][2]. Patching must be completed before any interlayer material is placed so that an overlay will rest on a uniformly supported surface, and traffic may be reopened only after the prescribed return‑to‑service time and minimum curing period have elapsed [2]. Ambient air and surface temperatures of at least 40 °F and rising are required for placement; daytime temperatures should remain between 40 °F and 70 °F during testing, and work must cease if conditions fall outside the manufacturer’s recommended range or drop below 40 °F. When accelerated mixes are used, insulation or heating is needed when temperatures dip below 80 °F to limit curing‑temperature changes to no more than 40 °F per hour, and all repairs must be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours; the subgrade or base may not be frozen at the time of placement [2]. Site preparation includes extending saw cuts beyond the corners of the distressed area, installing expansion‑joint material and temporary forms to keep the patch out of existing joints, and filling drilled full‑depth holes with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete patching material approved by the Representative; qualified personnel with documented experience must be present on site before work begins [2].

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Concrete patching is intended solely for the repair of localized distress that affects a relatively small portion of a concrete slab [1][2]. When inspection or testing shows that the area to be patched would exceed roughly ten percent of the total pavement surface, the treatment ceases to be cost‑effective as a preservation measure and the project must be reclassified as a more extensive rehabilitation or reconstruction unless a clear cost‑effectiveness justification can be demonstrated [1]. Likewise, scratch overlays on existing concrete that would require a thickness greater than 4½ inches with a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing‑course mix, or greater than 5 inches with a 12.5 mm mix, fall outside the preservation scope and must be addressed through alternative treatments such as mill‑and‑overlay, diamond grinding, micro‑surfacing, or ultra‑thin bonded courses [1]. If the pavement exhibits widespread structural deficiencies—extensive cracking, joint faulting, or has surpassed its functional service life—the appropriate response shifts from spot patching to larger‑scale interventions such as base repair, full‑depth overlay, or complete reconstruction [1][2]. The specifications also require that any crack or spall that appears anywhere on the pavement before the final inspection be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department, and the same requirement applies when a previously placed patch later cracks or spalls after curing [2]. Patching is confined to correcting manufacturing defects and other minor damage as defined in Publication 145; distress that exceeds those limits—such as larger cracks, extensive spalling, or other widespread deterioration—must be submitted to the CSME for evaluation and disposition, with only the approved patching material from Bulletin 15 used within its intended scope [2]. Areas where slabs or slab fragments are unstable, moving, or rocking under normal traffic create non‑uniform support for an overlay; such conditions require slab stabilization, slab jacking, or base replacement before any interlayer can be placed because patching cannot address the underlying instability [2]. The size of a defect that may be patched is limited to surface areas generally smaller than four square inches and less than one inch deep; larger or deeper failures fall outside the acceptable range and must be treated by an alternative repair technique [2]. Finally, rapid‑set concrete patching material is authorized only for corner repairs, and if the damage occurs elsewhere or the material’s shelf life or temperature limits are exceeded, the patching procedure is unsuitable and another maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair method must be employed [2].

Condition assessment and timing

Determining the need for concrete patching begins with a systematic condition assessment that integrates remote surveys and on‑site investigations [1]. Each year the roadway network is captured by an automated videologging system under the STAMPP program, which records surface distress such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling or weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting, and these observations are entered into treatment matrices that flag locations where patching may be required [1]. Flagged sections are then inspected in the field to verify the presence and severity of the recorded defects through a thorough visual walk‑over that seeks spalls, cracks, moving slabs, and joint or surface anomalies [2][1]. During this visual inspection each crack is measured for width and depth relative to slab thickness D; cracks wider than 0.007 in and deeper than one‑third D are identified as candidates for full‑depth replacement, while those of the same width but shallower than one‑third D are earmarked for repair in accordance with ACI 224R recommendations, and cracks at or below the 0.007 in threshold are slated for sealing with a high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate sealant [2]. All spalls are noted and will be repaired according to the applicable specification, and any slab or slab fragment that rocks under normal traffic loads is recorded as unstable and must be stabilized before any overlay can be placed [2]. Joint faulting is measured and joints exhibiting more than a quarter‑inch of vertical offset are marked for milling and patching, while surface depressions larger than four square inches in area and deeper than one inch trigger the use of a patching material [2]. When temperature conditions permit, a full deflection survey performed per ASTM D4695 is conducted on each transverse joint and crack; a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 in or greater signals that treatment is needed [2]. Joint efficiency is then calculated from the deflection data, with joints showing an efficiency of 65 % or higher considered for stabilization and those below 65 % requiring patching and stabilization subject to representative approval [2]. In parallel, core samples are extracted from the pavement at each flagged location; the cores are examined to confirm concrete thickness, overall integrity, and to provide a physical basis for repair design, with at least one core per lane taken for repair lengths under 100 ft and additional cores spaced at minimum 100‑foot intervals for longer sections, and further cores may be requested as directed [1][2]. A Falling Weight Deflectometer test is also performed on the same sections to quantify joint efficiency and to pinpoint the exact locations and quantities of concrete that have lost structural capacity, thereby corroborating the visual and core findings [1]. Geometric assessments include measuring cross‑slope uniformity at roughly 100‑foot stations; any segment deviating more than a quarter‑inch over a twelve‑foot length is recorded for correction before patching proceeds [2]. Ride‑quality evaluation is carried out by testing longitudinal surface roughness and calculating the International Roughness Index, with IRI values exceeding 70 in/mi on roads posted above 45 mph or 90 in/mi on slower routes mandating patching prior to subsequent diamond grinding operations [2]. The combination of automated condition data, confirmed visual observations, measured crack and joint dimensions, deflection and FWD results, core‑sample analysis, and geometric and roughness assessments together defines both whether concrete patching is necessary and the extent of repair required [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 404–471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Concrete patching is indicated whenever a rigid concrete pavement shows localized failures—faulted joints, isolated spalling, corner damage or cracks—that have not yet expanded to affect more than about ten percent of the total surface area, because beyond that level preservation becomes uneconomical. [1] The guides also agree that the treatment is appropriate when the distress is limited in extent such that it does not require full‑depth slab replacement but threatens structural continuity or overlay performance. [2][1] In practice, agencies schedule patching at distinct points in the pavement’s service life, for example around ten years when roughly five percent of the area is distressed, again near fifteen years with about two percent, twenty years with eight percent, twenty‑five years with four percent, thirty years with two percent and thirty‑five years with six percent, each representing a stage where localized damage is sufficient to warrant full‑depth repair yet remains below the ten‑percent ceiling. [1] When cracks are detected before final inspection and exceed a width of approximately 0.007 in, they are candidates for patching provided their depth is less than one‑third of the slab thickness; wider or deeper cracks trigger slab replacement instead. [2] Spalling, delamination, break‑back, corner damage and other manufacturing or construction imperfections that are confined to the surface or a small area are also repaired with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink full‑depth Type A patch as prescribed in the relevant sections. [2] Joints or cracks that exhibit a loaded slab‑corner deflection of at least 0.02 in together with a joint efficiency below 65 % are similarly patched and later filled full depth after removal of temporary plugs. [2] If the observed distress exceeds these minor‑damage criteria—such as extensive cracking, large spalls or other conditions not covered by the specified thresholds—the repair is referred to the CSME for further evaluation rather than being treated as a routine patch. [2] All patches must include repair of any failed joints verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing and may also encompass existing asphalt patches, transverse or longitudinal spalls, and other isolated defects identified in condition surveys. [1] Projects where the area requiring concrete patching surpasses ten percent of the total pavement are generally excluded from preservation programs unless a cost‑effectiveness justification can be demonstrated. Materials and repair details

Concrete patching on rigid pavements is limited to a maximum of ten percent of the total pavement area unless cost‑effectiveness can be demonstrated, and it must address all failed joints, cracks, spalls and other localized distresses identified before final design [1]. The repair may consist of full‑depth concrete replacement combined with joint rehabilitation, optionally followed by diamond grinding when no asphalt overlay is used, and can incorporate dowel‑bar retrofits or slab stabilization/jacking as needed [1]. Overlay options include a 1½‑inch Superpave 9.5 mm mix or a 2‑inch Superpave 12.5 mm mix placed over a 2½‑inch Superpave 19.0 mm base, with an optional half‑inch scratch course, as well as a 1‑inch (6.3 mm) thin asphalt overlay or polymer‑modified emulsified micro‑surfacing for structurally sound sections [1].

Removal of the distressed slab begins with a diamond‑tipped, water‑cooled saw equipped with a blade guard, alignment guides and cut‑depth controls; perimeter cuts are limited to one‑half inch in width and corner repairs do not exceed two inches in depth [2]. Extraction of unsound concrete is performed with a wheel saw or jackhammer whose weight does not exceed thirty‑five pounds (or fifteen pounds when damage is present) and operated at an angle of forty‑five degrees or less [2]. Exposed surfaces are cleaned within twenty‑four hours and again within thirty minutes after fresh concrete placement using compressed air of at least one hundred psi, then primed with grout or an epoxy bonding compound before any new material is placed [2].

The final patch thickness is established by measuring the pavement thickness at each of the four corners, averaging those values and adding one inch; the new concrete is placed to match this calculated depth [2]. The repair zone is filled with Class AA cement concrete that may be modified, accelerated or rapid‑set, conforming to a minimum cement factor of 530 lb/yd³ (maximum six hundred for slip‑form and regular paving), a water‑cement ratio not exceeding 0.42 (targeted at 0.40 and never below 0.38), and an air content of about seven percent plus or minus one point five percent [2]. The aggregate must be durable Type A coarse material—No. 57, No. 67 or No. 8 stone or gravel—that is washed clean; supplementary cementitious material may be used provided at least four hundred pounds per cubic yard of Portland cement remain, slag cement is limited to thirty percent of the total cementitious content and blended hydraulic cements such as Type IS or IP are permissible [2]. All admixtures must be admissible and any accelerating admixture must be chloride‑free [2]. When a rapid‑set concrete patching material is specified, it must be listed in Bulletin 15, meet ASTM C928, achieve an initial set of fifteen minutes or less at seventy‑five degrees Fahrenheit (±2 °F) as verified by AASHTO T 131, and retain at least eighty percent durability after three hundred freeze–thaw cycles tested to ASTM C666 [2]. If the repair depth exceeds two inches, only the aggregate extenders supplied with the pre‑packaged material may be used unless written approval is obtained for an AASHTO No. 8 coarse aggregate with an expansion index below 0.10 [2].

Load‑transfer units are installed in accordance with Section 705.3; coated Type B dowels are positioned with their exposed half lubricated as a bond breaker, the drilled holes being no more than one‑eighth inch larger than the dowel diameter, and a fast‑setting anchoring material that sets within five minutes secures them while keeping the exposed portion bondless [2]. Expansion joint filler of three‑quarters inch is placed in standard joints and half‑inch filler for corner repairs [2]. Preformed cellular polystyrene forms—quarter‑inch thick for slot dimensions and quarter‑inch full‑depth boards for longitudinal joints of Type A/B patches—are cut to fit around each dowel, providing at least a half‑inch clearance on all slot surfaces; non‑metallic expansion caps one and a half inches high are set with a quarter‑inch clearance from the dowel end [2]. Dowel alignment tolerances limit vertical tilt or horizontal skew to a maximum of 0.25 inch, longitudinal translation to one inch and horizontal translation also to one inch [2].

Joint preparation includes partial‑depth saw cuts at the patch interface to create a sealant reservoir; all joints are sealed before concrete placement, with longitudinal joints receiving a quarter‑inch full‑depth polystyrene bond breaker (none for Type C) cut one quarter inch wide and one inch deep [2]. The prepared zone is filled with the selected rapid‑set or Class AA mix, consolidated with a spud vibrator, worked into the saw cuts beyond the defect corners, textured to match the surrounding pavement and finished to the existing surface profile [2]. Curing is achieved with an approved liquid membrane‑forming compound or a white membrane‑forming curing compound for Class AA mixes; concrete curing and intermediate curing compounds specified in Section 711 are applied and the patch is protected until it reaches at least one thousand pounds per square inch compressive strength (or seven hundred psi after seven days when cement‑based grout is used) [2]. After curing, rapid‑setting grout is placed through a minimum of four ports per slab around dowel or tie‑bar slots, and surrounding areas are grouted as required [2]. Hairline cracks up to 0.007 inch wide are sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate sealant and covered with dry‑blast sand after roughly twenty minutes; spalls are repaired in accordance with Section 501.3(t) [2]. Cracks wider than 0.007 inch that extend deeper than one‑third of the slab thickness require full‑depth replacement, while shallower cracks are repaired following the latest ACI 224R recommendations [2].

The patch itself is a single‑course, full‑depth, continuously reinforced cement‑concrete slab extending at least one lane width; lengths depend on splice type: tied splices may be six to twenty feet, welded splices four to twenty feet, longer repairs are classified as Type B (twenty‑point‑one to sixty‑five feet) or Type C (sixty‑five‑point‑one to five hundred feet) [2]. Ambient air and surface temperatures must be at least forty degrees Fahrenheit and rising, with protection from freezing for a minimum of twenty‑four hours [2]. All work is performed using a power‑driven self‑propelled saw capable of making two parallel cuts per dowel slot when required [2].

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 377–471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 600–603), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 707)

The performance of a concrete patch depends first on the structural condition of the existing slab and its supporting layers, which must be demonstrated to be sound—typically verified by Falling‑Weight Deflectometer testing—and all failed joints must be rehabilitated before any repair material is placed. The integrity of the underlying material is a critical prerequisite because an inadequate subbase that can cause premature mid‑slab cracking undermines bond between new and old concrete. Problematic aggregates such as Vanport limestone or those susceptible to alkali‑silica reaction must be identified because they affect durability and skid resistance and may require special treatment prior to patch placement. The repaired area is generally limited to ten percent of the total pavement surface for preservation projects unless a cost‑effectiveness justification can be shown, and when no asphalt overlay follows the repair, diamond grinding may be required to obtain the intended smooth ride. The repair mix must achieve minimum compressive strengths that correspond to its intended use—at least 1,000 psi for rapid‑set concrete and 2,500 psi for grout placed in dowel slots, with higher performance specifications calling for a 4,925 psi compressive strength together with a flexural strength of 650 psi. A seven‑day average compressive strength of 700 psi, based on five test cylinders, is also required as a baseline durability check. Air content must be controlled to 7 percent ± 1.5 percent, the water‑cement ratio may not exceed 0.42, and the cement factor for accelerated patching must be at least 530 lb/yd³. Up to fifteen percent of the total cementitious material may consist of air‑entraining admixture, while supplementary cementitious blends must retain a minimum of 400 lb/yd³ Portland cement and limit slag content to no more than thirty percent of the binder. Reactive aggregates are acceptable only when their fourteen‑day expansion lies between 0.10 percent and 0.30 percent, and an approved cement‑supplementary blend must reduce effective expansion to less than 0.10 percent; any aggregate expanding beyond 0.30 percent is rejected. Overall volumetric expansion of the mix is limited to ten percent, bleed water may not exceed 2.5 percent of total volume, and initial set time must fall between one and six hours, with accelerated mixes required to set within fifteen minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F. The repaired patch must retain at least eighty percent durability after three hundred freeze–thaw cycles, and all materials must be used within the manufacturer’s shelf life, temperature limits, and listed in Bulletin 15 or otherwise approved by the CSME; accelerating admixtures must be free of chlorides. Compatibility with the existing pavement is achieved by matching the thermal‑expansion coefficient of the surrounding concrete as prescribed in the AASHTO mechanistic‑empirical guide and by using approved joint fillers such as cellular polystyrene or expansion‑joint material sized to the crack width. All dowel bars must be coated, lubricated on at least half their length with a bond‑breaker lubricant having a flash point of not less than 149 °F and not graphite‑based, and anchored with a fast‑setting material free of voids; joint geometry tolerances should stay within one‑quarter to one‑half inch. Full‑depth saw cuts are made at the mid‑depth of the thinnest slab with slot bottoms level with the roadway surface and widths not exceeding one‑half inch when opened to traffic, and non‑metallic chairs and caps (1½ inch caps providing a quarter‑inch clearance) are used to maintain proper dowel clearances. When an overlay is planned, the patch material must be compatible with the intended interlayer; if an asphalt‑based interlayer is used, the repair mix must be capable of being compacted together with it, otherwise a separate repair mix is required. Any geotextile fabric employed may not be exposed to temperatures above 120 °F. Prior to placement all spalling, break‑back, soft, wet or unsuitable subbase material must be removed, and surface irregularities deeper than one inch must be corrected and recompacted with small vibratory compactors. Debris, loose material and slurry must be cleared from slots within twenty‑four hours, with final cleaning completed no more than thirty minutes before concrete placement, after which a grout or epoxy bonding agent is applied while still tacky and the fresh mix placed before the bond agent dries. Unstable slabs, visible slab movement, or excessive joint faulting must be stabilized; joints or cracks exhibiting a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more, or whose joint efficiency falls below 65 percent, also require stabilization before repair. Work may not commence when daytime temperatures are below 40 °F or when the subgrade or base is frozen, and placement must occur only when ambient and surface temperatures are at least 40 °F and rising; placement must be stopped if temperature drops below this threshold. After placement the patch must be protected from rain and freezing for a minimum of twenty‑four hours (or forty‑eight hours of rain exposure), cured according to manufacturer recommendations while monitoring temperature swings, and traffic may be reopened only after the grout or repair mix attains the required compressive strength—1,000 psi for rapid‑set concrete or 2,500 psi for dowel grout—or after a minimum closure period of twelve hours as dictated by the field quality‑control plan. Treatment procedures

Concrete patching begins with the organization of a qualified crew under an experienced supervisor and the submission of a field quality‑control plan at least two weeks before work, as required by Section 106.03(a)2.a. Once approved, traffic protection measures are installed, the repair zone is clearly marked, and all equipment, trained personnel, and haulage capacity are readied for continuous placement. Ambient air and surface temperatures must be 40 °F or higher and rising; the area must also be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours. The damaged pavement is first saw‑cut around its perimeter with a power‑driven, self‑propelled saw fitted with a diamond‑tipped blade, blade guard, alignment guides, water cooling and cut‑depth controls. Full‑depth longitudinal cuts are made before transverse cuts, each cut limited to ½ inch in width to limit slurry intrusion, and for dowel‑bar slots two parallel cuts are centered over the joint. The concrete between these narrow cuts is then removed in one or more pieces using wheel saws or a jackhammer not exceeding thirty pounds (fifteen pounds when damage is present), taking care not to disturb adjacent pavement, subbase or subgrade; any disturbed subbase is re‑compacted or replaced to proper grade. After removal, the area is thoroughly cleaned and final cleaning must be completed within thirty minutes before concrete placement. Immediately thereafter a thin coat of grout or epoxy bonding compound is brushed onto all exposed surfaces and left tacky so that fresh concrete can be placed before the coating dries.

Placement proceeds only when temperature criteria are satisfied and the bonding agent remains tacky. The specified rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete mix referenced in Publication 145 (or a rapid‑set mix for precast slab repairs) is placed directly into its final position without segregation; each batch must be deposited within half an hour of adjacent batches to maintain continuity. Load‑transfer dowels or tie bars are installed at joints: polystyrene filler is cut, bond‑breaker lubricant applied, the bars are seated on non‑metallic chairs with required clearances, epoxy bonding compound is applied, and the slot is then filled with the patching material. The fresh mix is consolidated by internal vibration, external vibration, or a spud vibrator as appropriate, ensuring that concrete fully penetrates the saw cuts and surrounds dowels. After placement any temporary wooden plugs used during drilling are removed and the resulting cavities are filled full‑depth with the rapid‑set, non‑shrink material.

Finishing is performed to match the existing pavement cross‑section and texture. The surface is struck off true and even, free of honeycomb, rough areas, depressions or projections; any high spots exceeding 3/16 inch are ground down. All patches are textured to correspond with the surrounding pavement, original joints and extended saw cuts are resealed with grout and joint sealant, and a sealant reservoir is constructed at the interface before placement where required. The finished patch is level with the surrounding surface and struck flush.

Curing follows the manufacturer’s instructions or the approved white membrane‑forming curing compound specified in Section 516.3(j). The compound is applied with an atomizing spray tank, the solution being agitated before each application, while hourly temperature monitoring ensures changes do not exceed 40 °F within any one hour. Curing performance is verified by test cylinders from the same lot; traffic may be reopened only after cylinder compressive strength reaches at least 1,000 psi and after a minimum protection period of twelve hours (or as specified in the approved QC plan and manufacturer’s guidance). Additional considerations include patch length and width limits—Type A patches between 6 ft and 20 ft for tied splices or 4 ft to 20 ft for welded splices, Type B from 20.1 ft to 65 ft, Type C from 65.1 ft to 500 ft—and no patch may be narrower than a single lane. When precast slabs are used, the damaged pavement is saw‑cut and removed, the subbase prepared, an approved bedding layer installed, load‑transfer units placed, and the slab erected with appropriate equipment; after placement the slab is undersealed, dowel‑bar slots patched, a temporary asphalt wedge may be added for transition, and the surface finished by diamond grinding and joint sawing with sealing as required. Implementation of concrete patching begins with the submission of a quality‑control plan and an approved equipment list at least ten days – commonly two weeks – before work starts, confirming that a qualified crew is available. The required tool set includes a power‑driven, self‑propelled saw fitted with a diamond‑tipped blade, blade guard, alignment guides, water‑cooling and depth‑control features for making full‑depth perimeter cuts, as well as a jackhammer not exceeding thirty pounds (or a fifteen‑pound unit when the existing concrete is fragile). Concrete consolidation calls for small vibratory compactors or spud vibrators, and an air‑blower capable of delivering at least one hundred psi is used to clean saw slots. Load‑transfer dowels, dowel lubricants, temporary polystyrene forms, sealant material, and curing and sealing equipment specified in Section 516.3(q) are also required. For hole‑type repairs the crew must have a positive‑displacement cement injection pump coupled with a mixing machine, a water tanker, a core drill equipped with diamond‑tip barrels, hoses, valves, pressure gauges, expanding packers, and vertical‑movement testing devices capable of detecting movement to 0.001 inch. The mixer must not be overloaded beyond its rated dry‑concrete capacity, and blade wear is inspected regularly to prevent inadequate mixing.

Sequencing follows a strict order. After the QC plan is approved, full‑depth longitudinal cuts are placed before any transverse cuts; when patches abut longitudinal joint repairs, the patch work is completed first. Saw cutting is followed by removal of broken concrete, installation of dowels in the transverse faces, construction of a sealant reservoir and sealing of the joint. The slots must be air‑blasted and cleaned within twenty‑four hours, with a final cleaning no later than thirty minutes before fresh concrete placement. Grout or epoxy bonding compound is brushed onto exposed surfaces while still tacky, and concrete batches are placed in forms within half an hour of each other to avoid cold joints. The mix is then vibrated, finished, cured and finally sealed before traffic is restored. For hole‑type repairs the sequence begins with drilling the repair holes, mixing grout (which must be used within one hour), and injecting it at a maximum pressure of two hundred psi while continuously monitoring lift; pumping stops immediately if any vertical movement is detected. After grouting, the patched area remains closed to traffic for at least twelve hours. All concrete rehabilitation—including patching, spall repair, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization and slab jacking—must be completed before any diamond‑grinding operation can commence.

Environmental constraints are governed primarily by temperature. Placement may occur only when ambient air and surface temperatures are at least forty degrees Fahrenheit and rising; work is prohibited if the subgrade or base is frozen, and deflection testing must be performed between forty and seventy degrees without spring‑thaw conditions. Rapid‑set materials require an initial set time of fifteen minutes at seventy‑five degrees ± two degrees, and accelerated mixes need insulation or heating whenever air temperature falls below eighty degrees, with temperature changes not exceeding forty degrees per hour. All materials must be used within the manufacturer’s specified shelf life, temperature ranges and pot life; epoxy bonding compounds are applied within their prescribed setting times. When repair depth exceeds two inches, approved aggregate extenders are added as directed. Bond‑breaking lubricants must have a flash point of at least one hundred forty‑nine degrees Fahrenheit and may not be graphite‑based, while any accelerating admixture must be chloride‑free. The repaired area must be protected from freezing for a minimum of twenty‑four hours after placement.

Traffic restrictions limit exposure of saw‑cut surfaces to seventy‑two hours, require cuts to be full depth and no wider than one‑half inch, and mandate drop‑off protection. Excavated areas may not remain unpatched for more than forty‑eight hours, including weekends and holidays. When adjacent lanes are involved, only one lane may be patched at a time, and the lane cannot be reopened until curing and sealing steps required by Section 516.3(q) are complete. After grouting operations traffic must remain closed for at least twelve hours, and slurry generated during any subsequent diamond grinding must be prevented from flowing across occupied lanes or into drainage structures. Quality and workmanship

Concrete patching projects start with coring the existing slab so that the depth and integrity of the pavement can be inspected, and the thickness of each lane is verified by these cores. The area to be patched is limited to ten percent of the total pavement unless a cost‑effective justification is provided, ensuring that preservation projects remain within scope. All failed joints in the repair zone must be repaired and the effectiveness of those repairs confirmed by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing. The mix design submitted at least two weeks before work begins must be approved, and all material certifications and independent laboratory results showing 1‑, 3‑ and 7‑day compressive strengths, flowability, shrinkage/expansion limits, bleed loss, initial set time, and durability factor are required to be on file. The concrete used for patching must conform to Class AA specifications, with a cement factor of not less than 530 lb/yd³, a water‑cement ratio not exceeding 0.42, an air content of 7 % ±1.5 %, air‑entraining admixture limited to fifteen percent by mass, and supplementary cementitious materials restricted to approved blends containing at least 400 lb/yd³ Portland cement with slag cement no more than thirty percent of the total binder. Strength requirements are enforced throughout the project; a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and flexural strength of 650 psi must be achieved before placement, cylinders must reach at least 1,000 psi before traffic is allowed, an average of 700 psi from five cylinders after seven days is required, and dowel‑slot or bedding grout must attain at least 2,500 psi prior to opening the slab. Prior to concrete placement the repair area is swept clean, loose debris removed, and the perimeter is correctly marked; any unsuitable subbase material deeper than one inch is excavated and replaced, while shallower damage is re‑compacted with small vibratory compactors to produce a firm surface within ±½ inch of the design grade and straight‑edge variation not greater than 1⁄8 inch. Full‑depth saw cuts are made with a power‑driven self‑propelled saw or diamond‑tipped blade equipped with water cooling, alignment guides and cut‑depth controls; cut width may not exceed one‑half inch, the cuts are cleared of loose material within twenty‑four hours, and a final air‑blast cleaning at a pressure of at least 100 psi is performed no later than thirty minutes before concrete placement. Bond‑breaker lubricant is applied to any polystyrene joint filler at least one hour before placement, expansion caps are installed, and dowel bars are positioned with tilt or skew not exceeding 0.25 inch, translation limited to one inch, and a minimum half‑inch chair clearance. When a geotextile interlayer is used, joints faulting more than a quarter‑inch are milled, the fabric overlaps edges by eight inches ±2 (no more than three layers), extends at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, remains clean and dampened, and its temperature does not exceed 120 °F. Drilled injection holes must be vertical, smooth, no larger than 1½ inch in diameter, and free of breakouts. Concrete is placed continuously by qualified equipment and trained crews while ambient and surface temperatures remain at or above forty degrees Fahrenheit; placement is discontinued if temperature falls below this limit, and the material must be placed without segregation, consolidated by vibration or tamping, and worked fully into the saw cuts while epoxy bonding compound or grout remains tacky. Injection pressure is kept below 200 psi and upward slab movement is limited to 0.05 inch; load‑transfer units such as dowel bars are installed at transverse joints, undersealing and joint sealing are performed per design, and all original longitudinal and transverse joints are resealed after placement. Wooden plugs used during drilling are removed and the holes are filled flush with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink patching material. After curing, surface accuracy is checked with a twelve‑foot straightedge and any high points or depressions greater than three‑sixteenths inch are ground down; the finished texture must match the surrounding pavement and all joints must be sealed and grout‑stabilized. Curing is performed with a white membrane‑forming compound while hourly temperature checks confirm that curing temperature changes do not exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit in any one‑hour period. The patch is resounded for delamination, joint deflection is retested twenty‑four hours after curing, and traffic may not be permitted until at least twelve hours have elapsed and all strength criteria—including the minimum cylinder strength of 1,000 psi and dowel‑slot grout strength of 2,500 psi—are satisfied. Test cylinders, a minimum of four per shift, are recorded to verify that compressive‑strength thresholds have been met, and daily plant production records are reviewed to confirm material quantities, calibration of weighing equipment, and adherence to the approved mix design throughout the project. If no asphalt overlay is planned, diamond grinding may be required after patching to achieve the intended smooth ride. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 377–471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 600–603), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 707)

A satisfactory concrete patching must address every failed joint, faulted joint, crack, spall or other distressed area that has been identified in the pre‑design quantity estimate, and verification of the repair’s effectiveness is confirmed through Falling Weight Deflectometer testing[1]. The total area subjected to patching may not exceed ten percent of the overall pavement surface unless a cost‑effectiveness analysis demonstrates justification for exceeding that limit[1]. All saw cuts required to isolate the repair zone are to be cut to full depth and limited to a maximum width of one‑half inch before the repaired section is opened to traffic[2]. Core inspections of dowel‑bar anchors must show voids around each dowel perimeter of less than half an inch, while any tilt or skew at either end of a dowel may not exceed a quarter‑inch and longitudinal as well as horizontal translation from the intended location must remain within one inch[2]. The finished surface of the slot is to be ground so that no high points or depressions greater than three‑sixteenths inch remain, and vertical differentials across any joint are limited to a quarter‑inch; transverse joint widths must stay between one‑quarter and one‑half inch along their entire length and longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters inch[2]. The repaired surface is textured and profiled to match the existing pavement cross‑section, eliminating any high points or depressions that could affect ride quality[2][1]. The concrete mix used for the patch must be an approved design containing at least 530 lb of cement per cubic yard, a water‑cement ratio not exceeding 0.42 (targeted between 0.38 and 0.40), and air entrainment of 7 percent ± 1.5 percent; additional material properties such as efflux time (10–15 seconds), expansion (0‑10 percent), bleed loss (≤2.5 percent) and initial set time (1‑6 hours, with rapid‑set minimum 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F) are also required[2]. Target compressive strengths include a minimum of 1,500 psi at seven hours for rapid‑set material, at least 1,000 psi for standard concrete before traffic is permitted, and a minimum of 2,500 psi for grout or dowel‑bar bedding; a seven‑day strength of 700 psi and a durability factor of no less than eighty percent after three hundred freeze‑thaw cycles are also mandated[2]. Placement may proceed only when ambient air and surface temperatures are forty degrees Fahrenheit or higher and rising, and placement must be halted if the temperature falls below that threshold; during curing the temperature change across the repair area must not exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit in any one‑hour period, with hourly monitoring required[2]. All subbase irregularities deeper than one inch must be corrected before placement, and excavated areas may not remain unpatched for more than forty‑eight hours, including weekends and holidays[2]. Grout or epoxy bonding compounds must not run or puddle in low spots, and upward movement of the pavement during grout injection is limited to five hundredths of an inch; if after grouting a joint or crack still deflects two thousandths of an inch or more, it must be re‑grouted and retested after twenty‑four hours, with a second failure requiring full‑depth slab replacement[2]. Joints are considered stabilized when loaded slab‑corner deflection meets the 0.02‑inch criterion and joint efficiency is at least sixty‑five percent; lower efficiencies demand additional patching and stabilization actions[2]. Any occurrence of cracking, spalling or delamination after placement renders the repair defective and necessitates removal and replacement, and bond‑breaker lubricants applied to steel interfaces must have a flash point of at least one hundred forty‑nine degrees Fahrenheit and may not be graphite‑based[2]. After curing, the repaired area is resounded in the presence of the Representative; traffic may resume only after the Representative confirms that all strength, dimensional and surface‑profile criteria have been satisfied and any delaminated portion has been replaced at no cost to the agency[1][2].

Performance and service life

Concrete patching is applied at strategic points in a pavement’s lifecycle, typically around year 15, again near year 25, and once more by year 35, targeting small localized defects that affect only a modest portion of the surface (approximately 2 % at the first interval, rising to about 4 % and then 6 %) [1]. By repairing cracked joints, spalls, or other failed patches, the treatment restores the structural condition of the rigid pavement and halts distress progression, thereby preserving overall pavement condition [1][2]. The repair process requires removal of all spalled areas as specified, and cracks wider than 0.007 inches or deeper than one‑third the slab thickness are replaced in accordance with ACI 224R recommendations [2]. Repaired joints are grouted to achieve at least a 2,500 psi compressive strength before traffic is permitted, and the finished joint vertical differential is limited to a quarter‑inch, ensuring proper geometry and load transfer [2]. After grout stabilization, drilled holes are filled with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete patching material (ASTM C928) that can set in as little as 15 minutes at 75 °F (±2 °F), allowing prompt reopening to traffic and contributing to functional performance [2]. For repairs deeper than two inches, aggregate extenders are incorporated to maintain the prescribed mix design and shelf life, preserving durability of the patch [2]. The material must retain a durability factor of at least 80 % after 300 freeze‑thaw cycles, which ensures that the patched area maintains structural integrity under climatic stress [2]. Functional performance is further protected by limiting upward pavement movement to no more than 0.05 inch and by re‑grouting any joint or crack that exhibits a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or greater or a joint efficiency below 65 % [2]. When the patching area remains less than roughly ten percent of the total pavement, it is classified as a non‑structural preservation activity and may be paired with diamond grinding if no asphalt overlay is planned to regain smooth ride quality and skid resistance, enhancing functional performance [1]. All failed joints must be identified and verified through Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, which confirms joint efficiency and guides the quantity of required patching, supporting durability of the repair [1]. Within a fifty‑year analysis horizon, these periodic modest‑scale interventions contribute to sustained durability and an extended expected service life for the concrete pavement system [1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (p. 73), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 426–447), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Concrete patching is typically employed to remediate failed or faulted joints, various crack types, transverse and longitudinal spalls, and deteriorated legacy patches. The presence of these distresses is confirmed through Falling Weight Deflectometer testing of joint performance, which guides the decision to undertake a repair. Across the guidance, the repaired area is generally limited to less than ten percent of the total pavement surface unless a cost‑effectiveness analysis demonstrates justification; exceeding this threshold can disqualify the work from preservation eligibility. When a patch is not followed by an asphalt overlay, additional diamond grinding is often required to restore ride quality, underscoring the role of structural integrity and surface finish in treatment success. Edge break‑back or spalling represents one of the most frequent failures and arises when saw cuts are made deeper than recommended, when cutting tools damage the slab edge, or when the excavated area remains unpatched for more than forty‑eight hours; allowing traffic on openings wider than a half‑inch further aggravates the condition. Dowel‑bar anchorage problems, such as voids greater than 0.5 in., dowel tilt, translation or excess clearance, and failure to apply bond‑breaker lubricant, also produce defective retrofits that must be removed. Surface irregularities exceeding three‑sixteenths of an inch, cracks or spalls that appear before final inspection, and any cracked, spalled or delaminated sections are classified as defective work requiring replacement. These surface defects commonly stem from improper cutting or removal methods, inadequate sub‑base preparation or recompaction after disturbance, and insufficient curing that prevents the concrete from reaching its specified compressive strength—1,000 psi for standard patches and 2,500 psi when grout is used to transfer load. When grout fails to achieve the required strength or joint geometry falls outside tolerance, spalling, cracking, edge‑differential settlement greater than one‑quarter inch, and loss of load transfer are observed. Cracks wider than 0.007 in. and deeper than one‑third of slab thickness typically mandate full‑depth slab replacement, whereas narrower cracks are sealed with methacrylate. Temperature control during curing is critical; a temperature swing exceeding forty degrees Fahrenheit within any one‑hour period renders the repair defective, ambient or surface temperatures below forty degrees Fahrenheit require cessation of placement and protection from freezing for at least twenty‑four hours, and substrate temperatures above one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit must be avoided. Rapid cooling or freezing before the concrete has attained strength leads to delamination and loss of bond. Improper handling of grout or epoxy—allowing it to run or puddle in low spots, or placing fresh concrete before the bonding agent becomes tacky—also compromises adhesion. Equipment selection influences outcomes; using excessively heavy jackhammers (e.g., thirty‑pound) can fracture aggregates and cause spalling, so lighter tools are prescribed. Failure to clean exposed slot surfaces within twenty‑four hours prior to placement permits loose material or slurry to become embedded in the fresh patch, further weakening the repair. Radial cracks radiating from grout injection holes, transverse cracks between adjacent holes, upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 in., panel displacement beyond a 0.05‑in. tolerance, and joint deflection exceeding 0.02 in. are indicative of poor drilling practices, excessive injection pressure, low temperatures or frozen subgrade, delayed grout placement, and insufficient monitoring of pavement movement. Unstable slabs or slab fragments that rock under normal traffic, joint faulting beyond one‑quarter inch (or half an inch when an asphalt interlayer is present), and surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch left unfilled all point to inadequate stabilization before overlay and insufficient repair thickness or length. Across the sources, the dominant influencing factors for these failures are improper cutting techniques, delayed or out‑of‑window placement, inadequate sub‑base handling, insufficient curing time or strength, temperature extremes, poor material handling, and non‑compliance with dimensional tolerances. Monitoring and follow-up

After the patch has cured, the area is inspected before traffic is allowed. First, cylinder specimens are tested and the concrete must have reached at least 1,000 psi compressive strength. The finished surface is then checked with a 12‑foot straightedge both longitudinally and across joints; any high or low spots greater than 3/16 inch are ground smooth and the area retested. During curing the temperature of the repair zone is monitored at least hourly to be sure that it does not change more than 40 °F in any one‑hour period. Once curing is complete a resounding test is performed in the presence of the Department Representative, and any delaminated sections are replaced at no cost to the Department. Ride quality of the repaired zone is measured with the pavement‑surface profile equipment specified in Sections 507.3(a) and (b); the results must meet the acceptance criteria before any diamond‑grinding work proceeds, confirming that cross‑slope uniformity and longitudinal roughness are within tolerance. The Representative also verifies dowel‑bar anchoring at three randomly selected locations and will reject a patch showing excessive voids. After final inspection the crew removes any wooden plugs and fills the drill holes with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink patching material, striking the repair flush with the surrounding pavement. Traffic is prohibited for a minimum of twelve hours, or longer if required by the field QC plan or manufacturer recommendations; if directed, a deflection retest is performed twenty‑four hours after grouting to ensure that movement does not exceed the allowable limit, with additional grouting or replacement ordered if necessary.
