Structural Overlay

Part of: Overlay is-subtopic-of
Structural Overlay is a topic under Overlay.
structural-overlaystructural-overlays
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-29

Structural overlay is a pavement‑rehabilitation technique applied when an existing asphalt or Portland‑cement concrete surface lacks sufficient structural capacity to safely support current or projected traffic loads, so an additional load‑bearing layer—such as asphalt, bonded concrete, or unbonded PCC—is placed at a thickness calculated for future traffic [1][2]. Before placement the substrate must be cleaned, dried and repaired; surface defects larger than four square inches by one inch are patched, joint faulting is milled or scratch‑coursed, and the pavement is level‑milled or scarified to provide a uniform bearing plane [1][2]. The overlay’s design controls panel dimensions (no side longer than eighteen times the thickness and aspect ratio ≤1.5), mandates concrete temperatures between 65 °F and 90 °F, requires compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi (3,600 psi for traffic opening) and bond‑strength averages ≥175 psi, and incorporates fibers and durability mixes to achieve low chloride penetration [2]. By providing this calibrated structural layer and correcting the identified deficiencies, a structural overlay restores load distribution, extends service life, and improves functional performance without requiring full reconstruction [1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Structural overlay is applied when the existing pavement lacks sufficient structural capacity to safely support current or anticipated traffic loads, requiring an additional load‑bearing layer such as asphalt, bonded concrete, or unbonded Portland cement concrete with a thickness that meets the calculated requirement for future traffic. The technique also addresses situations where the underlying pavement cannot provide uniform support because concrete slabs or fragments move or rock under normal traffic, creating non‑uniform bearing conditions that would otherwise compromise the overlay. To achieve a uniform load distribution, unstable slab areas must be stabilized or repaired before placement of the interlayer material. Surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch are filled with patching material and compacted to a flat surface to prevent localized stress concentrations. Joint faulting greater than a quarter‑inch is milled, and if fault exceeds half an inch a scratch course or grinding of the joint is required to provide a smooth bearing plane for the overlay. By adding the structural layer and correcting these deficiencies, the overlay restores and enhances the pavement’s ability to carry loads, extending service life while improving both structural strength and functional performance without full reconstruction. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 10 (p. 186); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

The design of a structural overlay must first meet functional objectives that ensure proper drainage by improving removal of surface and subsurface water, and provide a level, stable platform achieved through scratch‑and‑leveling or milling before the new layer is placed. [1] It also requires strict temperature control during placement: concrete is to be placed when its temperature lies between 65 °F and 90 °F, the existing pavement surface must be cleaned, rough‑blasted and cooled below 120 °F after removal of oil, moisture or debris, insulating blankets are required when ambient temperature falls to 45 °F or lower, and the rate of temperature change at the concrete surface may not exceed 40 °F per hour. [2] Structural objectives are satisfied by providing sufficient load‑bearing capacity; binder‑course thickness is increased in accordance with non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures, and for an unbonded Portland cement concrete overlay on asphaltic concrete pavement the total overlay thickness equals the calculated Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic using rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors. Minimum compressive strength of 3,000 psi (or 3,600 psi where higher performance is required) must be achieved before traffic opening, and bond‑strength testing shall show an average of at least 175 psi with no individual result below 160 psi. Panel dimensions are limited to a maximum of eighteen times the overlay thickness and an aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5; joints are sawed either full depth plus one‑half inch or to one‑third of slab thickness as appropriate and then sealed. [1][2] Durability criteria call for the concrete mix to incorporate synthetic or steel macrofibers at a dosage determined from fourteen‑day ASTM C1609‑12 testing to achieve the design residual strength (f150); for overlays thinner than three inches No. 67 coarse aggregate replaces No. 57, and the mixture must attain a chloride‑ion penetration rating of Low, Very Low, or Negligible within 56 days of curing, with an optional Moderate limit not exceeding 2,800 coulombs by AASHTO T277 and resistivity limits of 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4×8‑in cylinders and 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6×12‑in cylinders. Core sampling must verify that measured depths are within a quarter inch of the design thickness, and any deficiency beyond this tolerance triggers additional coring to ensure serviceability. [2] Safety objectives include enhancing surface friction to maintain safe skid resistance and improving shoulder condition to supply lateral support and reduce edge stresses; surface slump is controlled near shoulders and lane edges, and any unstable concrete slabs or moving fragments must be stabilized or repaired before interlayer placement. When a geotextile separation layer is used it shall be laid flat, overlapped eight inches ± 2 inches on the edges, extended at least four inches beyond the edge, kept free of debris and maintained below 120 °F; an asphalt interlayer, if specified, is placed to a nominal depth of one inch with joints faulted more than half an inch treated by scratch coating or milling. [1][2] Serviceability objectives require that surface defects larger than three‑quarters of an inch in asphalt or greater than four square inches and one inch deep in concrete be filled with approved patching material, thereby providing a smooth riding surface while maintaining adequate drainage, levelness, and friction throughout the overlay’s service life. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 10 (p. 186); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 458–471)

Scope and applicability

Structural overlays are indicated for both asphalt‑concrete and Portland‑cement‑concrete (PCC) pavements when a condition assessment shows that the existing structure lacks sufficient load‑bearing capacity [1]. Typical triggers include poor non‑destructive testing results, an inadequate binder‑course thickness, or deficiencies in drainage and base support that can be remedied by surface repair, leveling and sealing before overlay placement [1]. In such cases the overlay provides the additional structural layer required to meet future traffic demands and is classified as a Pavement Rehabilitation treatment that extends service life and improves functional performance [1]. The design may employ a conventional asphalt overlay, a bonded concrete overlay, an unbonded concrete overlay, or a crack‑and‑seat treatment combined with a structural asphalt layer for flexible pavements, depending on whether the existing surface can contribute structurally or must be bypassed [1]. When the existing pavement is asphalt and the intent is to add strength without relying on its direct contribution, an unbonded PCC overlay is appropriate; the required concrete thickness is set equal to the calculated Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic based on ESAL calculations for rigid pavements [1]. Structural overlays may be applied alone, with partial recycling of the existing pavement, or in conjunction with complementary treatments such as rubblization, providing a versatile alternative to full reconstruction [1]. The overlay design is also appropriate for both existing rigid (concrete) and flexible (asphalt‑surfaced) pavements when project objectives include correcting minor surface defects, replacing full‑depth asphalt patches, lowering pavement elevation, or providing a bonded concrete skin that increases load‑bearing capacity and extends service life [2]. For rigid pavements the overlay is placed directly over a scarified surface that has been cleaned to the depth shown on the plans, and concrete placement is limited to fresh temperatures between 65 °F and 90 °F [2]. For asphalt pavements the existing surface is typically profile‑milled to the plan‑specified depth, cleaned, and any longitudinal cracks wider than three‑quarters of an inch are filled with approved non‑shrink grout to ensure bonding; the milled surface must be dry, free of oil or grease, and its temperature kept below 120 °F at the time of concrete placement [2]. When a thin bonded skin is required, a Portland cement concrete overlay ranging from about 2.5 inches to 4 inches in thickness may be used; for moderate‑thickness applications the layer can be 4 to 8 inches thick, and it is bonded to the prepared asphalt surface with construction of transition zones, joint sawing and sealing as required [2]. The material specified for most structural overlays is Class AA cement concrete, and when the overlay thickness is less than seven inches the concrete must be reinforced with 100 % virgin polypropylene fibers that meet the length, aspect‑ratio and dosage limits set by ASTM C1116—generally not exceeding five pounds per cubic yard unless field testing demonstrates workability [2]. The design also permits the use of a geotextile debonding layer at overlay edges when the underlying base is treated or when transitioning to existing concrete, ensuring proper load transfer and preventing reflective cracking [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 10 (p. 186); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 458–470)

When the existing pavement exhibits severe base failure, poor drainage, or cannot be provided a stable and properly leveled surface, it is considered unstable for an overlay and designers must turn to full reconstruction rather than a structural overlay [1]. Likewise, if the pavement has failed outright or become functionally obsolete such that the underlying base and subgrade cannot be remedied by adding a layer, total rebuilding of the structure—potentially after rubblization—is required [1]. In addition, overlays are impractical when the preparation criteria cannot be satisfied, for example when the existing surface is wet, contaminated with dirt, gravel, vegetation, oil, or otherwise not dry and clean [2]. Weather conditions that fall outside the manufacturer’s recommended installation range also preclude placement of an asphalt interlayer system, making alternative rehabilitation methods preferable [2]. Finally, if the contractor cannot achieve the required minimum compacted thickness of one‑and‑a‑half inches for the first lift of the overlay, the design must shift away from a structural overlay to another solution [2].

Design inputs and requirements

The design of a structural overlay is governed by a hierarchy of federal, state and agency specifications that define allowable materials, analysis methods and procedural constraints, requiring conformity to the Federal‑aid Highway Program policy on pavement management and design promulgated by FHWA as well as to state guidance such as Pennsylvania’s Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242) and the Superpave Restricted Performance Specification in Publication 408 Section 413 for binder courses [1]. For projects receiving federal aid, FHWA approval is required after a quality‑control review by the Board of Directors’ Design Division, while PennDOT‑oversight designs must be generated with the DARWin software and submitted on Form D‑4332 for district engineer approval; any deviation from the manual must be presented as a waiver to the Board of Directors [1]. Additional project constraints include a 30,000‑square‑yard threshold that determines whether the type‑determination procedure applies and the requirement for soil reports when reconstruction is involved [1].

The department’s rigid‑pavement specifications, principally Section 500 together with related provisions in Sections 501.2(a), 545.3, 540.3, 466.3, 548.3, 516, 679, 681, 316 and the Superpave mix criteria of Section 410.3, provide the detailed technical framework for overlay design and construction [2]. Before any work commences a comprehensive quality‑control plan must be prepared, submitted for approval and accepted by the Representative; no construction activity may start until that plan is approved [2].

Panel geometry is limited so that no panel dimension exceeds eighteen times the overlay thickness and the aspect ratio (longer side divided by shorter side) shall not exceed 1.5, while saw‑cut depths must be at least one‑third of the slab thickness within a foot of the edge and at least one‑quarter elsewhere [2].

Surface preparation requires milling, thorough cleaning, removal of standing water and maintaining milled asphalt below 120 °F; all unstable slabs, slab fragments and surface defects larger than four square inches by one inch must be repaired with an approved patching material before any interlayer is placed, and the pavement surface must be swept clean [2].

When a geotextile interlayer is used, joints faulted more than a quarter‑inch are milled, the fabric is laid flat with edges overlapped eight ± 2 inches (limited to three layers) and extended at least four inches beyond the edge, and pavement temperature must remain below 120 °F; an asphalt interlayer requires a nominal one‑inch depth of Superpave mix, reduction of joint faulting over half an inch by scratch coursing or milling, and the same temperature limitation [2].

For overlays that incorporate a Portland‑cement concrete layer bonded to an existing asphalt surface, Section 500 governs the work with overlay thickness constrained between four and eight inches for standard applications and between 2.5 and 4 inches for thin overlays; if the depth is less than three inches the coarse aggregate shall be No. 67 rather than No. 57 [2]. The contractor must submit a concrete paving job‑mix formula for approval at least fourteen days before placement, and mixes are required to contain polypropylene macro‑fibers that meet ASTM C1116 Type III 4.13 and ASTM C1018 performance level 1, with fiber dosage not exceeding five pounds per cubic yard unless a field demonstration proves adequate workability [2]. Mix design follows Table A of Section 501 for Class AA cement concrete and must achieve a residual‑strength ratio (R150,3) of at least 20 percent as measured by the Illinois‑modified ASTM C1609 test, with durability requirements calling for chloride‑ion penetration classified as Low, Very Low or Negligible after a curing period of fifty‑six days or less according to AASHTO T 277 or T 358; a Moderate rating is permissible only if total charge does not exceed 2 800 C or electrical resistance remains below the specified limits [2]. Concrete temperature at placement must be between 65 °F and 90 °F, and the prepared surface must be cleaned within thirty minutes of paving; curing materials must comply with Sections 711.1 and 711.2(b) [2].

Strength criteria require bond‑strength testing in accordance with ASTM C1583 at seven days, with an average of at least 175 psi and no individual test below 160 psi, while compressive strength of cylinders must reach a minimum of 3 600 psi (or 3 000 psi when the maturity method per AASHTO T 413 is used) before traffic may be opened; core depth verification is performed in lots up to 3 000 sq yd using PTM No. 606 and PTM No. 614, with a tolerance of only one‑quarter inch deficiency before remedial coring is required [2]. All joints are sealed with an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant, and joint overlaps for the asphalt interlayer must be at least one inch [2].

Material handling requirements dictate that asphalt interlayer material be stored dry, wrapped and labeled per ASTM D4873, and placed only when binder temperature is between 290 °F and 320 °F; tack‑coat application rates range from roughly 0.02 to 0.28 gallons per square yard depending on reinforcement type [2]. Payment for the overlay follows the quantity‑based provisions of Section 500: cement‑concrete volume is calculated from the indicated depth and placement area, with an additional allowance not exceeding two percent of that computed quantity for material used to replace unsound pavement removed during milling (excluding transition zones), and contractors must submit a written request with justification citing delivery tickets and showing deductions for both overlay volume and waste [2].

Design methods and criteria

Design of a structural overlay is carried out with the DARWin pavement‑design software in accordance with the requirements of Publication 242 of the Pavement Policy Manual, and the design package must include at least one structural asphalt or concrete overlay together with full‑depth designs for asphalt, concrete, and reclamation [1]. When an unbonded Portland cement concrete (PCC) overlay is placed over existing asphalt, DARWin treats the underlying asphalt solely as a support layer and derives the required overlay thickness directly from the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic value given in Table 10.8, while future traffic loading is evaluated using the rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors listed in Table 7.1 to compute equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) [1]. Upon completion the design package is submitted on Form D‑4332 for Federal or PennDOT oversight review to demonstrate compliance with sound engineering practice and Publication 242 stipulations [1].

The material selection for a structural overlay may follow either the Superpave mixture‑design procedure for plant‑mixed fine‑graded asphalt courses or a Class AA cement‑concrete mix that incorporates polypropylene fibers when the required thickness is less than seven inches; the fibers must meet ASTM C1116 specifications, be 1.0 to 2½ inches long with an aspect ratio not exceeding 150, and be dosed at no more than five pounds per cubic yard unless a field demonstration shows higher rates remain workable without clumping [2]. The concrete mix is proportioned so that, after curing, the Illinois‑modified ASTM C1609 residual strength ratio R150,3 reaches at least twenty percent, providing the target residual strength f150 for durability and performance [2].

Prior to overlay placement any unstable slabs or surface defects are repaired through concrete patching, slab stabilization, slab‑jacking, flexible‑base replacement, or shallow repairs using approved asphalt, cementitious, or similar repair materials; the pavement is then swept clean and dried, free of debris, oil, vegetation, and loose material [2]. A pre‑placement meeting with the manufacturer’s technical representative confirms the tack‑coat type and application rates, and when an asphalt interlayer is used it consists of a one‑inch nominal depth Superpave mix applied at 0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard, while a geotextile interlayer is overlapped eight inches (± 2 in) beyond the edges [2]. Reinforcement such as geotextile, fiberglass mesh, or composite grid is placed on the tack coat before it cools, with overlaps trimmed to at least one inch, and the first lift of asphalt overlay must be compacted to a minimum thickness of 1½ inches [2].

Concrete placement is limited to slab temperatures between 65 °F and 90 °F and must be kept below 120 °F using fogging or blankets; insulating blankets are required when ambient temperature falls below 45 °F, and after placement a curing compound is applied uniformly at a rate of one gallon (± 0.1 gallon) per 125 ft² [2]. Bond‑strength testing is performed at seven days in accordance with ASTM C1583, requiring an average of at least 175 psi and no individual result below 160 psi; strength development is monitored either by cylinder testing or by the AASHTO T 413 maturity method, which correlates degree‑hours of cure to compressive strength using PTM No. 640 at intervals of eight hours, one day, seven days, twenty‑eight days and fifty‑six days [2]. The overlay must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 3,000 psi (or 3,600 psi for traffic opening) before it can be opened to traffic [2].

Joint layout limits panel dimensions to eighteen times the overlay thickness and requires an aspect ratio not greater than 1.5; transverse joints are saw‑cut to a depth no less than one‑third of the slab thickness within twelve inches of the edge and at least one‑fourth elsewhere, with the sawing window determined using FHWA’s HIPERPAV software or an equivalent department‑approved tool that predicts strength gain [2]. Macro‑fibers are added to meet the target residual strength f150, with fiber volume derived from a fourteen‑day ASTM C1609‑12 test relating residual strength to fiber content, and the concrete mixture must also satisfy durability criteria by achieving a chloride ion penetration classification of Low, Very Low or Negligible after fifty‑six days as measured by AASHTO T 277 or T 358 [2].

Overlay quantity is calculated from the specified thickness and placement area, with an allowance of up to two percent for cement concrete needed to replace unsound pavement removed during profile milling, provided supporting documentation is submitted [2].

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 10 (p. 186); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 317–319), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 458–474)

In the design of a structural overlay the existing asphalt pavement is treated solely as a support layer, with no direct contribution to structural capacity, so the required overlay thickness is taken directly from the pavement‑thickness values needed for anticipated future traffic loads [1]. The calculation of those loads uses the average initial truck factors for rigid pavements as presented in the standard tables, providing the ESAL input for the design [1]. Placement of the concrete overlay is permitted only when the concrete temperature lies between 65 °F and 90 °F and the underlying asphalt or geotextile surface has cooled to below 120 °F, ensuring proper bonding conditions [2]. Prior to placement the surface must be dry, clean, free of debris, and all cracks sealed; any defect larger than four square inches in area and deeper than one inch must be repaired, establishing a reliable substrate [2]. Panel dimensions are limited to a maximum length of eighteen times the overlay thickness in any direction, with an aspect‑ratio cap of 1.5 and a preference for near‑unity ratios, thereby controlling slab stability and joint performance [2]. Saw cuts defining transverse and longitudinal joints must penetrate at least one‑third of the slab depth within a foot of the edge and at least one‑quarter elsewhere, providing adequate load transfer across joints [2]. The overlay may not be opened to traffic until cylinder tests demonstrate a compressive strength of at least 3 600 psi and bond‑strength testing after seven days yields an average of no less than 175 psi with each individual test not below 160 psi, establishing the required reliability thresholds for serviceability [2]. For overlays thinner than three inches the coarse aggregate shall be No. 67 rather than No. 57, and macrofibers are dosed to achieve the design residual strength f150 as verified by a fourteen‑day ASTM C1609‑12 test, ensuring sufficient tensile performance in thin sections [2]. When the overlay thickness is less than seven inches polypropylene fibers must be incorporated; they are limited to monofilament or bundled monofilament forms 1.0–2.5 inches long with an aspect ratio not exceeding 150 and a dosage not greater than five pounds per cubic yard unless a field demonstration proves workability, providing additional crack‑control capacity [2]. The concrete mixture must attain a residual strength ratio of at least twenty percent (R150,3) and meet a chloride‑ion penetration classification of Low, Very Low or Negligible after no more than fifty‑six days of curing, satisfying durability reliability requirements [2]. If an asphalt paving interlayer is specified, the tack coat temperature must be maintained between 290 °F and 320 °F, applied at a rate of 0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard with a minimum compacted first lift thickness of one‑and‑a‑half inches, and no traffic other than construction vehicles may use the overlay until it has cured, thereby preserving bond integrity and early‑age strength development [2]. Curing compounds are applied within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation, insulation blankets are removed only when the concrete surface temperature change rate is less than forty degrees Fahrenheit per hour, and core testing must confirm that measured thickness is within one quarter inch of the specified depth; failure to meet any of these criteria renders the overlay defective, reinforcing the overall factor‑of‑safety framework for the rehabilitation work [2].

Structural section and dimensions

The structural overlay design specifies a concrete slab thickness ranging from four to eight inches. When the slab is divided into panels, each panel’s longest side may not exceed eighteen times the slab thickness and the ratio of the longer side to the shorter side must be limited to 1.5. If an asphalt interlayer is employed, the first lift must be compacted to at least one‑and‑a‑half inches. Reinforcement fabrics or grids placed within that interlayer require a minimum one‑inch overlap, and the tack coat applied over them must extend six inches beyond each edge of the reinforcement width. When a fiberglass mesh grid rests on a leveling course, that course must be at least twice the nominal maximum aggregate size. A geotextile separation layer, if used, is to be overlapped by approximately eight inches (allowable variation plus or minus two inches) and its free edge must project at least four inches beyond the pavement edge; no more than three such layers may be stacked. An asphalt interlayer, when specified, is placed to a nominal thickness of one inch. For overlays thinner than three inches, a coarser No. 67 aggregate replaces the standard No. 57 aggregate. Overlays thinner than seven inches must incorporate polypropylene fibers whose length ranges from one to two‑and‑a‑half inches, with an aspect ratio not exceeding 150 and a dosage limited to five pounds per cubic yard. The specification does not prescribe any dimensional requirements for the existing pavement surface, base, subbase, treated‑soil, shoulders, edge supports, or additional reinforcement beyond the items already described. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 317–319), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 458–471)

Overlay thickness governs the allowable panel size; the longest dimension of any unbonded concrete overlay panel may not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and should keep an aspect ratio near one to one‑and‑a‑half. Joint spacing follows the project plans or, if none are provided, a department‑approved layout is prepared while avoiding longitudinal joints in wheel paths. Panels are sized so that the longer side divided by the shorter does not exceed 1.5, and transitions are constructed at the overlay limits, employing a geotextile debonding layer where the underlying surface is treated base or existing concrete. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 472–474)

Materials and mixture design

The structural overlay specification must first identify the material system to be used. A rigid overlay shall be placed with Class AA cement concrete, which may incorporate Portland‑cement concrete with fibers, while an asphalt overlay requires a hot‑mix binder that conforms to a Superpave mix design and is placed at a temperature not below 290 °F and not above 320 °F. When concrete is selected the mix must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 3,600 psi, although placement may proceed once the slab reaches at least 3,000 psi. The bond between the new concrete and the existing pavement shall average no less than 175 psi, with each individual ASTM C1583 test not falling below 160 psi. Fresh concrete is to be placed when its temperature lies between 65 °F and 90 °F and the prepared surface is cooler than 120 °F. The overlay thickness depends on the existing surface: a conventional rigid overlay over any pavement shall be four to eight inches thick, whereas a thin Portland‑cement concrete layer applied directly over an asphalt surface is limited to two‑and‑a‑half through four inches; in all cases the concrete must be Class AA because the depth is less than seven inches. Panel dimensions may not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the ratio of the longer side to the shorter side shall not exceed 1.5. For concrete depths under three inches the coarse aggregate shall be No. 67, while greater depths may use a standard gradation. Reinforcement shall be provided by synthetic or steel macrofibers, or by virgin polypropylene fibers that are one to 2½ inches long with an aspect ratio not exceeding 150; the fiber dosage must be calibrated so that a fourteen‑day ASTM C1609 test yields a residual strength ratio of approximately twenty percent. The concrete mixture must also satisfy a chloride‑ion penetration rating of Low, Very Low, or Negligible after no more than fifty‑six days of curing in accordance with AASHTO T277 or T358. After the slab attains the required compressive strength, a white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene liquid membrane‑forming curing compound shall be applied uniformly at a minimum of one gallon per 125 ft² (or one gallon per 150 ft² for sections thicker than six inches) with an allowable tolerance of ±0.1 gallon. If an interlayer is required, the specification must name the material—geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grid, composite reinforcing grid, or a one‑inch nominal thickness Superpave asphalt—and prescribe its installation. Geotextile fabric shall be laid flat over repaired pavement, overlapped eight inches on each side (±2 in), extended at least four inches beyond the edge, limited to three layers, kept clean and damp but not saturated, and maintained below 120 °F during placement; application rates range from about 0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard. Fiberglass mesh and composite grid are applied at rates of roughly 0.02–0.05 and 0.19–0.28 gallons per square yard respectively, each covering the reinforcement width plus six inches on either side. An asphalt interlayer shall be placed as a one‑inch Superpave mix; any joint faulting exceeding half an inch must be corrected with a scratch course before placement. All cracks wider than three‑quarters of an inch must be repaired beforehand with department‑approved non‑shrink grout, and after the concrete reaches at least 3,000 psi compressive strength all transverse and longitudinal joints shall be sealed with an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant. The quantity of concrete may include up to a two‑percent excess over the calculated volume to accommodate replacement of unsound milled material, provided delivery tickets substantiate the additional amount. When a structural overlay is planned, engineers first assess the existing pavement condition, including drainage, base integrity, surface friction and shoulder support, to assure durability and proper load transfer. The required thickness of the additional binder course is then determined by non‑destructive testing or other accepted design procedures, which establishes the structural capacity that the overlay must provide. Based on this evaluation the choice between an asphalt overlay and a bonded‑concrete overlay is made, each with its own mix‑design criteria aimed at meeting structural, durability, constructability and performance goals. For an asphalt overlay the existing surface must be stable, free of movement under rolling, properly leveled, sealed and patched before any new material is placed to satisfy constructability requirements. The mix is then proportioned using the Superpave method so that aggregate, binder and additive contents comply with the relevant ASTM performance specifications. Prior to placement a calibrated distributor applies a uniform tack coat whose temperature is maintained between 290 °F and 320 °F, and the volume of tack coat is set according to the type of reinforcement that will be embedded—approximately 0.22–0.28 gal per square yard for geotextile fabric, 0.02–0.05 gal per square yard for fiberglass mesh, or 0.19–0.28 gal per square yard for composite grid—with a six‑inch lateral margin to ensure complete saturation on a clean, dry surface. The overlay is placed in lifts, the first lift being compacted to at least one‑and‑a‑half inches thick, which establishes the baseline structural capacity and constructability required by design. When a concrete overlay is specified, the mix design is driven primarily by the intended thickness and durability objectives; overlays thinner than three inches require No. 67 coarse aggregate instead of No. 57 to achieve the proper gradation. For overlays less than seven inches thick a Class AA cement concrete is mandated, reinforced with virgin polypropylene fibers of 1–2½ in length and aspect ratio not exceeding 150, dosed so that the residual strength ratio R150,3 reaches twenty percent, with a maximum fiber dosage of five pounds per cubic yard unless field demonstration proves acceptable workability. Additional synthetic or steel macrofibers are incorporated at rates that satisfy the target residual flexural strength (f150) indicated in Table B, and their effectiveness is verified by ASTM C1609‑12 testing after fourteen days. Durability of the concrete overlay is addressed by designing for low, very low, or negligible chloride ion penetration within fifty‑six days of curing, with limits of no more than 2 800 coulombs on AASHTO T 277 or resistivity values of 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4×8 in cylinders and 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6×12 in cylinders as measured by AASHTO T 358. All proportions, whether asphalt or concrete, are calibrated to satisfy the structural capacity, durability, constructability, and performance criteria established in the applicable ASTM and AASHTO standards. Structural details and interfaces

A structural overlay must be placed on a clean, dry pavement that has been prepared by removing all loose reinforcement, exposed steel plates and deteriorated material, and by installing joint backing or an approved filling within about one‑quarter inch of the surface of every existing transverse joint. Existing transverse joints are identified, marked and then saw‑cut directly over them to a depth equal to the full overlay thickness plus one‑half inch; the cut must be at least as wide as the underlying joint and must not damage any dowel load‑transfer devices that remain in the original pavement. Intermediate transverse cuts are required so that no panel exceeds fifteen feet, and where the cut is within twelve inches of an edge its depth shall be no less than one‑third of the overlay thickness, elsewhere no less than one‑quarter of the thickness. Longitudinal joints are located and a sealant reservoir one‑quarter inch wide is sawed to the full overlay depth plus one‑half inch; all longitudinal cuts must be at least one‑eighth inch wide and are avoided in wheel paths. Joint layout follows the project plans or an approved plan, with panel dimensions limited to eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and a length‑to‑width ratio not greater than 1.5, keeping aspect ratios close to 1 : 1. After cutting, joints are cleaned and sealed with an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant. Any joint fault exceeding one‑quarter inch must be milled, and if the fault exceeds one‑half inch when an asphalt interlayer is used a scratch course or additional milling is required before overlay placement.

Reinforcement for the overlay may be provided by macro‑fibers—synthetic or steel—added at a dosage sufficient to achieve the specified residual strength (f150), and, for overlays thinner than three inches, the concrete mix shall use No. 67 coarse aggregate instead of No. 57. When the overall thickness is less than seven inches, polypropylene fibers meeting ASTM C1116 requirements are used; these fibers must be 1.0 to 2½ inches long, have an aspect ratio not exceeding 150, and be added so that the residual strength ratio reaches about twenty percent without exceeding five pounds per cubic yard unless field testing shows the mix remains workable. In addition, a geotextile or other paving fabric interlayer may be placed over the prepared surface; the material is saturated with asphalt at the manufacturer‑specified rate, extended at least six inches beyond its edges, overlapped by approximately eight inches (±2 in) on each side, and its free edge must extend a minimum of four inches beyond the pavement edge. No more than three overlapping geotextile layers are permitted, and the interlayer is kept clean, damp but not saturated, secured as directed by the manufacturer, and protected from construction traffic until concrete placement.

Load‑transfer devices such as dowels are retained in place; transverse saw cuts must be performed so that these devices are not damaged, and no additional load‑transfer devices are required for the overlay. Connections between the new concrete and the existing pavement are achieved by overlapping the asphalt interlayer paving system a minimum of one inch (or per manufacturer recommendation) and by orienting end joints in the direction of overlay placement. Traffic is restricted to construction vehicles until the first lift reaches at least one‑and‑a‑half inches compacted thickness.

Transitions are constructed at the limits of the concrete overlay as shown on the plans; where a transition rides over existing treated base or concrete, a geotextile debonding layer is installed beneath the new concrete. Edge faces must be neat and free of honeycomb, with edge slump limited to values specified for shoulders and adjacent lanes. The overlay itself consists of a Portland‑cement concrete layer four to eight inches thick that is bonded to the existing asphalt surface, and the concrete mix must satisfy chloride ion penetration limits defined by AASHTO test methods. The design of a structural overlay begins by defining how the new concrete layer will interact with the underlying pavement, either as an unbonded bearing surface that provides only support or as a bonded interface that participates in composite action [1][2]. When the overlay is specified as unbonded, such as a Portland cement concrete (PCC) slab placed over an asphalt concrete (AC) base, the existing AC layer is treated solely as a support and no direct structural contribution across the interface is assumed; consequently the required overlay thickness is set equal to the pavement thickness calculated for future traffic using rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors [1]. For bonded designs, thorough preparation of the existing surface is required to achieve reliable shear transfer, beginning with scarifying or shot‑blasting concrete decks or milling asphalt decks to the design depth, followed by removal of all loose material, oil, grease, standing water and debris [2]. Joint backing material must be installed so that its face lies within ±¼ in of the existing pavement surface, and any transverse cracks larger than ¾ in are filled with an approved non‑shrink grout before the surface is dried and cleaned [2]. Temperature control is essential; fresh concrete for the overlay is placed only when its temperature is between 65 °F and 90 °F and the underlying pavement does not exceed 120 °F, with fog‑misting or protective covers used as needed to maintain these limits [2]. Bond strength at the interface is verified by ASTM C1583 testing, requiring an average of at least 175 psi and a minimum individual result of 160 psi; failure to meet this criterion renders the area defective [2]. When an interlayer is incorporated, either a geotextile fabric or a thin asphalt course may be used, each with prescribed detailing to control slip and moisture movement [2]. A geotextile sheet is limited to three layers, laid flat with edges overlapped by 8 in ± 2 in and extended at least 4 in beyond the pavement margin where it can be drained or daylighted, and the fabric is saturated with a tack coat of asphalt applied at 0.22–0.28 gal/yd² covering the width of the fabric plus an additional 6 in on each side; overlaps at joints must be a minimum of 1 in and any wrinkles larger than that are trimmed and re‑overlapped [2]. An asphalt interlayer, when specified, is placed to a nominal depth of 1 in, kept clean and damp but not saturated, and its surface temperature is also limited to 120 °F [2]. Joint layout follows geometric limits that control panel dimensions and stress concentrations; individual overlay panels may not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the ratio of the longer side to the shorter side shall not exceed 1.5, with a preference for an aspect ratio close to unity [2]. Full‑depth saw cuts are made at existing transverse expansion joints to a depth equal to the full overlay thickness plus ½ in (or at least one‑third of slab depth within 12 in of edges and at least one‑quarter elsewhere), with a minimum width of ⅛ in, and the cuts are cleaned and sealed with an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant [2]. Longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths, and after sawing all cuts are resealed with the same material to maintain continuity across the interface [2]. Edge conditions are controlled to limit slump; at shoulders the overlay edge may not sag more than ¼ in and adjacent lane edges are limited to ⅛ in, while transition zones where the overlay meets treated base course or existing concrete incorporate a geotextile debonding layer to provide proper shear transfer [2]. Where joint faulting exceeds ½ in, a scratch course or additional milling is required before overlay placement to reduce faulting and ensure adequate contact [2]. Overlay thickness may range from 2.5 in to 4 in for thin designs up to 4–8 in for more robust sections, and in unbonded cases the thickness is directly tied to the calculated pavement thickness needed for future traffic loads [1][2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Before a structural overlay is placed, the existing pavement must be converted into a dry, clean, and drainable substrate. All moisture, oil, grease, debris, vegetation, and depressions are removed so that water can flow away, and any standing water that appears after milling is eliminated immediately with Department‑approved drainage measures. The asphalt paving interlayer system is stored wrapped, elevated, and protected from precipitation, UV light, chemicals, and temperature extremes; it may only be installed when the weather meets the manufacturer’s recommendations and never on a wet surface or during rain. When a geotextile interlayer is used, it is terminated in a drainable layer or at a daylighted location to preserve its free‑drainage capability, kept clean, and lightly dampened—never saturated—prior to overlay placement.

Temperature and moisture control are integral to the overlay design. Concrete placement is limited to ambient conditions that keep the concrete temperature between 65 °F and 90 °F, and the surface temperature of both concrete and asphalt must be below 120 °F immediately before placement. Fog‑misting, protective covers, misting, white‑pigmented curing compounds, lime‑water whitewashing, or other approved cooling methods are employed to achieve these limits while preventing any standing water on the surface. When forecast air temperatures are 45 °F or lower, insulating blankets are applied to prevent rapid heat loss; they remain until the slab’s surface temperature change does not exceed 40 °F per hour, the air temperature rises above 45 °F, or the concrete reaches the specified compressive strength (3,000 psi). Curing compounds are placed within 30 minutes after bleed water dissipates and stay in place until the pavement attains the required strength. The quality‑control plan schedules activities to accommodate anticipated air‑temperature variations, indicating timing adjustments as needed. [2]

Verification and expected performance

The completed structural overlay is verified through an integrated quality‑control process that begins with submission of the concrete paving job‑mix formulas at least two weeks before placement for approval by the District Materials Engineer/Manager and the preparation of a comprehensive QC plan that must be accepted prior to any construction activity. Compliance is first confirmed by ensuring that concrete is placed when its temperature lies between 65 °F and 90 °F, that asphalt material temperatures meet the manufacturer’s minimum as recorded in the field QC plan, and that all surface‑preparation steps—scarification, removal of loose material, shot blasting, joint backing within a quarter‑inch, milling to the specified depth, filling cracks larger than three‑quarters of an inch with non‑shrink grout, and final cleaning performed within thirty minutes using oil‑free compressed air at not less than one hundred psi—are completed before placement. The overlay thickness must match the contract drawings, with the first lift of asphalt interlayer compacted to at least 1½ in., and any interlayer sections must be paved on the same day; overlaps are required to be a minimum of one inch and fully saturated.

Panel dimensions are measured against the design limits—no panel longer than eighteen times the overlay thickness and an aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5—and joint spacing is checked to ensure that longitudinal joints do not fall in wheel paths and that transitions at overlay limits incorporate the specified geotextile debonding layer where required. Saw cuts made with a green‑cut saw are inspected for depth of at least one‑third of slab thickness within twelve inches of the edge (or one‑quarter elsewhere) and a minimum width of one‑quarter inch, and all deleterious material is removed before sealing.

Constructability requirements are satisfied by using No. 67 coarse aggregate when the overlay depth is under three inches, dosing macrofibers to achieve the design residual strength as verified by ASTM C1609‑12 testing at fourteen days, and applying a uniform white PAMS curing compound within thirty minutes after bleed water has dissipated; insulating blankets are employed when air temperature is forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or lower and are removed only when the surface temperature rise rate remains below forty degrees per hour. Edge slump limits of no more than one‑quarter inch near shoulders and one‑eighth inch adjacent to lanes are also confirmed.

Performance is evaluated through a series of strength and durability tests. Bond strength is measured at seven days in accordance with ASTM C1583 on five random locations, requiring an average of at least 175 psi and no individual result below 160 psi; compressive‑strength cylinders are tested and traffic may be opened only when the concrete reaches the specified minimum—typically three‑thousand psi as measured by cylinders or maturity method, with a higher threshold of three‑six hundred psi cited for certain sections—and the bond‑strength criteria are satisfied. Core samples taken from each lot (not exceeding three thousand square yards) must not deviate more than one‑quarter inch from the required thickness, and permeability testing is performed to achieve a Low, Very Low or Negligible chloride ion penetration after no more than fifty‑six days of curing, with moderate values limited to 2,800 coulombs. Any deficiency in these checks renders the area defective and requires remedial action before final acceptance. [2]

The structural overlay is expected to provide a durable, high‑strength pavement surface that will extend service life by resisting fatigue cracking, rutting, corrosion and other distress mechanisms while maintaining acceptable ride quality. Its performance is verified through a series of laboratory and field measurements. The concrete must achieve a compressive strength of at least 3,600 psi on cured cylinder specimens; this value is used to confirm that the overlay can sustain anticipated traffic loads. Bond integrity between the new layer and the existing pavement is assessed with ASTM C1583 after seven days, requiring an average bond strength of no less than 175 psi and a minimum of 160 psi for each individual test. Surface finish quality is controlled by limiting edge slump to a maximum of one‑quarter inch adjacent to the shoulder and one‑eighth inch next to an adjoining lane, as determined by visual measurement. In addition, the mix design incorporates macrofibers that must deliver the prescribed residual tensile strength (f150). This requirement is demonstrated by conducting ASTM C1609‑12 testing at fourteen days and relating the measured residual strength to the fiber volume fraction. Durability against chloride ingress is confirmed by performing AASHTO T277 or T358 on specimens cured up to fifty‑six days, with the target classification of Low, Very Low, or Negligible penetration. When a maturity approach is employed, degree‑hours of cure are correlated to compressive strength at defined ages—eight hours, one day, seven days, twenty‑eight days and fifty‑six days—in accordance with PTM No. 640, ensuring that the overlay develops the required load‑bearing capacity within the anticipated construction schedule.
