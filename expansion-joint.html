Expansion Joint

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Expansion Joint is a topic under Rigid Pavement.
expansion-joints
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-29

Expansion joints are built‑in sealed gaps in Portland‑cement concrete pavements that allow adjacent slabs to expand and contract with temperature changes, thereby preventing faulting, spalling, water infiltration and uncontrolled cracking that would otherwise compromise structural integrity [1][2]. The joint geometry is limited to a transverse opening of one‑quarter to one‑half inch (and up to three‑quarters inch longitudinally) with vertical differentials no greater than one‑quarter inch, dimensions that provide sufficient freedom for slab edges to open and close without inducing excessive tensile stress while still supporting load transfer [2]. By maintaining a continuous seal—typically a rubberized material placed in a 0.75 in × 0.75 in reservoir—and using premolded filler of prescribed thickness, the joint preserves ride quality, drainage and long‑term pavement performance, extending service life [1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Expansion joints are incorporated into concrete pavements to accommodate movements caused by thermal expansion and contraction that would otherwise generate faulting, loss of load transfer, and cracking [1][2]. When these movements are not provided for, the pavement can develop spalls, water infiltration, reduced serviceability, and uncontrolled cracking that compromise structural integrity [1][2]. The design therefore requires joints of sufficient width to allow the slab edges to open and close without inducing excessive tensile stress, with transverse gaps limited to a minimum of one‑quarter inch and not exceeding one‑half inch, while longitudinal joints may be as wide as three‑quarters inch [2]. Proper joint placement is determined by accounting for ambient average daily temperatures and site exposure conditions in the mix design, ensuring that panel size and joint locations provide adequate freedom for expansion and contraction [2]. In overlay work, existing transverse joints are identified and marked, precut while the concrete remains plastic, and then full‑depth saw cuts are made on each side before hardening to create controlled unbonded gaps that prevent random cracking [2]. Effective sealing of these joints is essential; when joint performance deteriorates through faulting or poor load transfer, restoration of support below the pavement or reconstruction of sealant reservoirs and resealing is needed to revitalize transverse contraction capacity and maintain load‑transfer capability [1]. Providing effective joint seals markedly improves overall pavement performance and extends service life [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–462)

Expansion joints must provide a sealed, continuous gap that accommodates anticipated thermal movements of concrete slabs without restraint, thereby preserving ride quality and preventing uncontrolled cracking. [2] To achieve this functional requirement the joint geometry is limited to a transverse width between one‑quarter and one‑half inch and a longitudinal width not exceeding three‑quarters inch, with vertical differential across any joint kept at or below one‑quarter inch. [2] These dimensional limits support proper load transfer and maintain slab integrity, which is further enhanced by the use of dowel‑bar retrofits or cross‑stitching where hairline cracks appear to preserve structural continuity. [1][2] The layout of joints follows site‑specific temperature data so that panel dimensions do not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the ratio of longer to shorter side remains below 1.5, thereby controlling stresses within each slab. [2] Existing transverse joints must be identified, marked, and prepared before cutting, with saw cuts extending at least one‑third the slab depth within twelve inches of edges and at least one‑quarter depth elsewhere; longitudinal joints are prohibited in wheel paths to protect safety. [2] Durability is achieved by timely sawing and sealing according to a quality‑control plan that prevents edge slump or damage before traffic opens, and by resealing the joint as soon as the first signs of distress appear. [1][2] Sealing employs rubberized sealants placed in reservoirs sized at 0.75 in × 0.75 in for pavement‑shoulder joints, which together with proper drainage prevent water, chlorides and incompressible materials from infiltrating the slab or undermining the subgrade. [1] The joint filler must be a premolded material installed to a total thickness of at least one‑half inch through the full pavement depth, increasing to not less than three‑quarters inch adjacent to rigid structures, and must provide a smooth riding surface. [2] For transverse joints the filler extends across the entire lane width with a minimum one‑inch thickness, while for longitudinal joints in base courses the filler pieces are at least eight feet long and securely joined; all fillers include punched holes to accommodate dowel bars without loss of material thickness. [2] Acceptable filler materials are those approved by AASHTO or ASTM, such as cork, preformed rubber, fiber, closed‑cell polypropylene foam, or a polyurethane‑bonded recycled‑rubber composite, ensuring long‑term performance. [2] The design assumes a stable subgrade and a functional drainage system; maintaining these conditions together with the sealed joint prevents faulting, spalling, slab migration, and other distress that could compromise safety and overall pavement performance. [1][2] By meeting these functional, structural, durability, safety, and serviceability objectives, expansion joints enable concrete pavements to accommodate thermal movements while delivering a safe, comfortable driving surface throughout their design life. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–462), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 670)

Scope and applicability

Expansion joints are employed on all rigid concrete pavements, including plain cement‑concrete sections such as paved divider areas, reinforced slabs, and continuous prestressed precast slab systems. They are required wherever the design calls for them, when an existing concrete pavement is widened—so that new transverse joints are aligned with any pre‑existing joints—and when a pavement meets a structure or other rigid element such as manholes, inlets, valve boxes, bridges, culverts, or railway tracks. In overlay work, unbonded concrete overlays use newly cut transverse and longitudinal joints to control slab movement; joint locations must follow the project plans or an approved layout, with panel dimensions limited so that the longer side does not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the aspect ratio is kept near one and never above 1.5. Joints are placed without load‑transfer units in paved divider areas and must conform to the dimensions of adjacent curbs. For continuous prestressed precast systems, the concrete mix design must account for the site’s ambient average daily temperature and exposure conditions; standard aggregate expansion coefficients from the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide are used to size panels and locate joints, with joint widths limited by prescribed tolerances. Saw cutting is the only acceptable technique for creating overlay joints, with cuts at least one‑third of slab depth within twelve inches of edges and a minimum cut width of one‑quarter inch. When repairing or patching concrete pavements, specific filler thicknesses are required. Standard patches that replace an existing joint use three‑quarter‑inch expansion‑joint material; corner repairs use half‑inch material to reestablish the original transverse joint location. Partial‑depth repairs abutting an existing transverse joint receive a half‑inch thick expansion‑joint filler extending at least a half inch below the repair, while those abutting an existing longitudinal joint are filled with one‑quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene. For joints adjacent to structures, premolded expansion‑joint filler is placed for the full pavement depth with a minimum thickness of one‑half inch for general structures and three‑quarters inch where the pavement adjoins another rigid structure; railway crossings require a three‑quarter‑inch filler against mortar‑filled rails and a quarter‑inch filler along each rail when running alongside tracks. Transverse joints in reinforced or plain cement concrete pavements must span the full lane, ramp, or taper width with a minimum thickness of one inch, while longitudinal joints in concrete base courses or other structural joints require pieces at least eight feet long that are securely joined. Acceptable joint filler materials include cork, sponge rubber, fiber‑based fillers, or closed‑cell polypropylene foam that meet the applicable AASHTO or ASTM standards and must be supplied by a manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–462), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 670)

When an existing concrete pavement exhibits joint faulting, poor load transfer, or distress that cannot be corrected by resealing, designers should look beyond expansion joints and use other treatments; slab stabilization or jacking is chosen when support beneath the slab is inadequate, a dowel‑bar retrofit is used to restore load transfer if drainage remains functional and the subgrade is stable, and full‑depth joint replacement may replace the joint when rehabilitation is not feasible. Joint rehabilitation and sealing are limited to restoration projects and are not appropriate when an overlay will be placed, making alternative design approaches preferable in those situations. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90)

Design inputs and requirements

The design, installation, and maintenance of expansion joints in Portland‑cement concrete pavements are governed by a suite of federal specifications that include Publication 408, the FHWA Roadway Construction Standards (Publication 72M), and the rigid‑pavement specifications embodied in Section 501.3 together with its related subsections such as Sections 516.3(e), 525.3(e) and 548.3(i) as well as the Standard Drawings and the quality‑control plan required by Section 106.03[1][2]. Joint sealing must be accomplished with the rubberized joint‑sealing material prescribed in Publication 408 Sections 512 and 521, and this requirement is reiterated in Section 501.3(n) of the rigid‑pavement specifications, with sealant reservoirs sized to the shape factor defined in Section 521 and illustrated in FHWA RC‑20M; pavement‑shoulder joints specifically require a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch reservoir that may be sealed with either rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material[1][2]. For plain cement‑concrete divisor areas the transverse joints are installed without load‑transfer units and must match the dimensions of adjacent concrete curbs, while continuous prestressed precast pavements derive joint spacing and size from thermal‑expansion coefficients found in the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide, with transverse gaps limited to a minimum of ¼ inch and a maximum of ½ inch and longitudinal gaps not exceeding ¾ inch[2]. Transverse joints are placed where indicated on the construction drawings; when a pavement is widened they should be aligned with existing joints whenever practicable, and any deviation from the plan requires approval from the responsible department[2]. Existing transverse joints that are to be cut for an overlay are identified and pre‑cut, each side receiving two full‑depth saw cuts whose depth is at least one‑third of the pavement thickness within 12 inches of the edge and at least one‑fourth elsewhere, with a minimum width of ¼ inch; the resulting panels must have an aspect ratio no greater than 1.5 (ideally near 1.0) and a maximum dimension limited to eighteen times the overlay thickness[2]. When a patch replaces an existing joint, a ¾‑inch expansion‑joint filler is placed adjacent to the remaining joint and bond‑breaker lubricant must be applied to at least half of each Type B coated dowel bar before concrete placement, while corner repairs use a ½‑inch filler to reestablish the original transverse joint location[2]. All fillers used in expansion joints must conform to material specifications cited in the contract documents and may include cork, sponge rubber, fiber or polypropylene foam supplied by manufacturers listed in Bulletin 15; these materials are tested according to AASHTO M 153 for cork/rubber, AASHTO M 213 for fiber, or ASTM D8139 for closed‑cell foam, with specimens at least 4 inches square and a minimum area of 16 square inches, and must be certified per Section 106.03(b)3[2]. Minimum filler thicknesses are prescribed according to the type of structural interface: at least ½ inch around appurtenances, ¾ inch where the pavement meets other rigid structures, and between ¼ inch and ¾ inch when interfacing with track ballast; premolded expansion‑joint filler is also required in gutters, cut to a thickness of ¾ inch and shaped to match the gutter cross‑section as shown on the Standard Drawing and Section 501.3(i)[2]. Fillers must be installed to the required thicknesses—minimum one inch for transverse concrete joints, with lengths equal to lane width or at least eight feet for longitudinal joints—and must provide a smooth top surface and dowel‑bar holes that do not reduce material thickness[2]. Rehabilitation of saw‑cut or metal‑plate joints follows Publication 408 Section 513 and the procedures outlined in FHWA RC‑26M, and all joint work is a prerequisite for concrete pavement repair projects, to be performed before patching but not when an overlay is planned[1]

Design methods and criteria

The design and construction of expansion joints in rigid pavements follows a procedural sequence that incorporates empirical inputs from the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide and, when required, software such as FHWA’s HIPERPAV to define appropriate sawing windows. [2] Mix design for continuous prestressed precast systems must first consider the site’s average daily temperature and exposure conditions, using the standard coefficient of thermal expansion supplied by the guide to determine panel size and joint locations. [2] Joint geometry is governed by empirical width limits, with transverse joints required to be no narrower than one‑quarter inch and no wider than one‑half inch, while longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters inch in width. [2] Panel dimensions are constrained so that the aspect ratio of length to width approaches unity and does not exceed 1.5, and the overall panel length is limited to eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness. [2] During construction transverse expansion joints are placed at locations shown on the plans; when existing pavements are widened the new joints are aligned with any pre‑existing joints whenever feasible, and in plain‑cement divisor areas they are installed without load‑transfer units. [2] Existing joints are identified and marked, then saw‑cut to a depth of at least one‑third of slab thickness within twelve inches of an edge and at least one‑quarter thickness elsewhere, with the appropriate sawing window confirmed by HIPERPAV or an equivalent approved program. [2] After cutting each joint is cleaned and sealed in accordance with manufacturer recommendations. [1][2] Joint filler material is installed according to prescribed minimum thicknesses, using a premolded filler not less than one‑half inch thick around structures, increasing to three‑quarters inch adjacent to bridges, culverts, or other critical features, and a three‑quarter‑inch piece cut to match the gutter cross‑section at each structure and at daily work stops. [2] When repairing or replacing joints a half‑inch filler is placed in the joint and extended at least one‑half inch below the repaired slab bottom; if the repair permits, the joint is re‑established by full‑depth sawing of the new concrete. [2] Dowel bars used in joint reconstruction receive bond‑breaker lubricant on at least half their length, are anchored with a fast‑setting material that reaches initial set within five minutes, and longitudinal joints in Type A and B patches incorporate a quarter‑inch full‑depth polystyrene board as an additional bond breaker. [2] Maintenance of Portland cement concrete pavements relies on early resealing of joints followed by joint rehabilitation that creates new sealant reservoirs—type 1 for saw‑cut joints and type 2 for metal‑plate joints—and routine cleaning and sealing of longitudinal, transverse, and shoulder joints using rubberized or asphalt sealants placed in a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch pocket. [1] Crack sealing is performed according to the relevant specification sections, dowel‑bar retrofits are installed to restore load transfer across cracks or joints, and cross‑stitching with epoxy‑coated tie bars is employed to achieve near‑100 % load transfer; all procedures are detailed in Publication 408 specifications and Roadway Construction Standards (RC‑20M, RC‑26M). [1] All constructed, repaired, or rehabilitated joints are sealed or caulked per manufacturer recommendations to ensure durability and accommodate thermal movement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–462), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (p. 549)

Expansion joints in Portland cement concrete pavements must be cleaned and sealed with the rubberized joint‑sealing material prescribed in Publication 408, and the sealant reservoir is required to have the shape factor and typical dimensions of 0.75 in by 0.75 in; resealing is mandated as soon as any distress appears even if overall serviceability remains acceptable, based on the assumption of a stable subgrade and functional drainage that prevents water, chlorides, or incompressible materials from infiltrating the structure [1]. The joints are to be placed only at locations shown on the construction drawings, and when an existing concrete pavement is widened the new transverse joints should be aligned with any pre‑existing joints whenever feasible; in plain‑cement concrete divisor areas the joint is installed without load‑transfer units and must conform in size and position to the dimensions of the adjacent concrete curb, using the specified joint filler [2]. Design of the joint for continuous prestressed precast pavements relies on the site’s average daily temperature and exposure conditions, employing the standard aggregate thermal‑expansion coefficient from the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide to size panels and locate joints [2]. The transverse gap between slabs shall not exceed one‑half inch and shall be no less than one‑quarter inch along its entire length, while longitudinal gaps shall not exceed three‑quarters inch; any finished vertical differential across a joint must be limited to one‑quarter inch [2]. Joint material thickness is prescribed as three‑quarters of an inch when replacing an existing joint and one‑half inch for corner repairs or partial‑depth repairs that abut an existing transverse joint, with the half‑inch material extending at least one‑half inch below the bottom of the repair whenever possible, and longitudinal joints require a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene board as a bond breaker with the same embed depth [2]. Premolded expansion‑joint filler thickness minima are dictated by the surrounding structure: at least one‑half inch for the full depth of the slab around manholes, inlets and similar appurtenances; at least three‑quarters inch where concrete meets other rigid elements such as bridges, culverts or railway tracks; and a minimum of one‑quarter inch along each rail when paving alongside tracks [2]. All filler materials must meet the applicable material standards—cork or rubber fillers to AASHTO M 153, fiber fillers to AASHTO M 213, polypropylene foam fillers to ASTM D8139—and be certified under Section 106.03(b)3 [2]. For transverse concrete joints the filler shall be supplied in lengths equal to the lane width with a minimum thickness of one inch, provide a smooth top surface and include punched holes for dowel bars without loss of thickness; longitudinal joint pieces must be at least eight feet long and securely joined [2]. Fiber‑type fillers are required to be tested on specimens at least four inches on a side with a minimum area of sixteen square inches, and the compression test may use an applied force lower than one hundred pounds per square inch [2]. Existing joints shall be identified and marked before any cutting; precut plastic concrete is required prior to full‑depth saw cuts on each side of the underlying joint, and saw cut depth must be no less than one‑third of slab thickness within twelve inches of the edge and at least one‑quarter of the thickness elsewhere, with a minimum width of one‑quarter inch, while full‑depth cuts defining the joint may not exceed one‑half inch in width [2]. Panel dimensions are limited so that the longer side does not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness, and the aspect ratio of any panel shall be kept as close to unity as feasible but never greater than 1.5; joint spacing follows the project plans or an approved department layout, and cracking outside the sawed joints renders the panel defective [2]. Dowel bars used in Type B patches must receive bond‑breaker lubricant on at least half their length by spray or gloved hand at least one hour before concrete placement; anchorage material shall achieve initial set within five minutes, and acceptance is granted only when the total voids around the dowel perimeter on a core sample are less than 0.50 inch [2]. All of these dimensional tolerances, material performance limits, procedural requirements, and assumed conditions together constitute the design criteria, assumptions, thresholds, factors of safety and reliability requirements that an expansion joint must satisfy.

Structural section and dimensions

The expansion‑joint provisions dictate specific geometric limits for the pavement slab, the underlying subbase, and the joint filler material. Concrete slabs used in rigid pavements may be fabricated no longer than fifteen feet, extending to twenty feet when prestressed, with a minimum length of six feet; each slab is placed one‑quarter inch thicker than the nominal design thickness. The transverse joint that separates adjacent slabs must be at least one‑quarter inch wide but shall not exceed one‑half inch, while longitudinal joints may be as wide as three‑quarters inch. The subbase on which these slabs rest must be finished to a firm surface whose grade and contour are within plus or minus one‑half inch of the design elevation.

When an expansion joint is created in a concrete overlay, full‑depth saw cuts are required. The cut depth must be no less than one‑third of the slab thickness within twelve inches of the pavement edge and at least one‑quarter of the slab thickness elsewhere, with a minimum cut width of one‑quarter inch. The resulting unbonded panels may not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness, and their length‑to‑width ratio is limited to 1.5 : 1.

For repairs that replace or intersect existing joints, the filler material thickness is prescribed. A patch that restores a transverse joint calls for a one‑half inch thick expansion‑joint material extending at least one‑half inch below the bottom of the repair; where the repair meets a longitudinal joint a one‑quarter inch preformed cellular polystyrene filler is used with the same minimum embedment. When a joint is rebuilt adjacent to an existing joint, a three‑quarters inch thick joint material is required, and for corner repairs the thickness is reduced to one‑half inch.

Premolded expansion‑joint filler placed around the periphery of structures must have a total thickness of at least one‑half inch through the full pavement depth; where the pavement abuts other rigid elements such as bridges, culverts or railway tracks the filler thickness increases to a minimum of three‑quarters inch. If construction crosses rail tracks, a three‑quarters inch thick filler is placed against each mortar‑filled rail, while along the length of the track a one‑quarter inch thick filler is provided on each rail.

For transverse joints in reinforced or plain cement concrete pavements, the filler must be supplied in lengths that match the width of the traffic lane, ramp or taper and must be at least one inch thick. Longitudinal joints in a concrete base course—or in any structural element—require filler pieces no shorter than eight feet, which are to be installed securely. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–462), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 670)

Expansion joint design for precast rigid pavements is driven by thermal‑expansion analysis, slab geometry, and grade control requirements; the concrete mix must be sized for site temperature conditions to set appropriate panel dimensions and joint locations, slabs are fabricated 1/4 inch thicker than the nominal thickness and limited to a maximum length of 15 feet (20 feet when prestressed) with a minimum of 6 feet so that transverse joints can align with existing joints, and the bedding layer is fine‑graded to achieve the required cross‑slope tolerance (no more than 1/8 inch variation over a slab length). Joint spacing is controlled by keeping transverse joint gaps between 1/4 and 1/2 inch and longitudinal joints no wider than 3/4 inch, while transitions exceeding a 2‑inch vertical drop are mitigated with temporary asphalt wedges; tie‑ins are provided through dowel bars and tie bars per the shop drawings, ensuring load transfer without exceeding a 1/4 inch vertical differential across any joint. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Materials and mixture design

Expansion joints in Portland cement concrete pavements require a rubberized joint‑sealing material as defined by the relevant specifications. The sealant is placed into a reservoir that is formed to dimensions of three‑quarters of an inch by three‑quarters of an inch. When an overlay will be applied, any severely spalled joint areas must first be cleaned and then filled with asphalt wearing‑course material having a nominal thickness of 4.75 mm or an equivalent material before the new surface is placed. Dowel‑bar retrofits call for smooth dowel bars that are coated with epoxy, positioned in slots, and backfilled with a concrete patching material. The joint filler itself must be a premolded strip whose only essential property is its thickness, and the approved filler types include cork, sponge rubber, fiber‑based fillers, and closed‑cell polypropylene foam, each conforming to its respective standard. Cork and sponge‑rubber fillers must meet AASHTO M 153, including a version that incorporates polyurethane‑bonded recycled tire rubber; fiber fillers must satisfy AASHTO M 213 with a 4‑inch compression specimen of at least 16 sq in.; and polypropylene foam must be certified to ASTM D8139. For transverse joints in plain cement‑concrete pavement the filler thickness shall not be less than one‑half inch, increasing to three‑quarters inch where the pavement abuts rigid structures such as bridges, culverts or gutters, and it may be reduced to one‑quarter inch along railway tracks. When a joint is being replaced or repaired a three‑quarter‑inch material is placed in the adjacent joint while a half‑inch material is used for corner repairs to reestablish the original transverse location. In partial‑depth concrete repairs any intersected transverse joint requires a half‑inch filler, whereas longitudinal joints receive a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene that should extend at least one‑half inch below the bottom of the new repair whenever practicable. The filler for transverse concrete joints must be supplied in lengths equal to the lane width, possess an overall minimum thickness of one inch, present a smooth top surface and include punched holes to receive dowel bars; longitudinal and structural joint pieces must be at least eight feet long and securely joined. No additional concrete mix proportions or material‑property limits are prescribed beyond these dimensional and standard requirements. [1][2]

Structural details and interfaces

In Portland cement concrete pavements the expansion joint must be cleaned of debris and sealed with a rubberized sealant, and a sealant reservoir typically sized at 0.75 in by 0.75 in is provided for pavement‑shoulder connections; transverse joints also require reservoirs shaped according to the prescribed shape factor. The joint layout includes both transverse and longitudinal configurations placed where indicated on the plans, with transverse joints aligned to any pre‑existing joints when a pavement is widened and positioned to conform to adjacent curb dimensions in plain cement‑concrete divisor areas that are installed without load‑transfer units. The width of each transverse joint gap shall not exceed one‑half inch and shall be no less than one‑quarter inch along its entire length, while longitudinal joints are limited to a maximum opening of three‑quarters inch. The cavity of the joint is filled with a premolded expansion‑joint filler whose thickness varies according to the adjoining structure—at least one‑half inch for manholes, inlets and valve boxes; three‑quarters inch where the slab meets bridges, culverts or other rigid elements; a minimum of three‑quarters inch against mortar‑filled railway rails, and only one‑quarter inch along each rail when the pavement runs alongside tracks. For gutter concrete a three‑quarter‑inch premolded filler is cut to match the cross‑section at structural interfaces and at the end of each day’s placement. In precast rigid pavements the transverse joint gap must be maintained between one‑quarter and one‑half inch, and longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters inch; load transfer across these joints is achieved with cast dowel bars and tie bars placed in slots shown on shop drawings, the portions of the dowels embedded in the slab being coated with a bond‑breaker material. Where additional sealing is required a wide foam gasket may be attached to the underside edge of each slab to create sealed grout chambers, and grout ports are provided so that dowel and bedding grouts can develop at least 2 500 psi compressive strength before traffic is allowed. When existing joints are found deficient in load transfer, an epoxy‑coated dowel bar retrofit may be installed across the joint, and cross‑stitching with epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars can be applied to longitudinal cracks or joints to obtain near‑full load transfer and prevent slab migration. Repair of a deteriorated transverse joint involves filling the joint with three‑quarters inch expansion‑joint material; Type B coated dowel bars receive bond‑breaker lubricant on at least half their length prior to placement, and tie bars are installed in 5/8‑inch holes when reestablishing corner joints using a one‑half inch filler. For Type A and B patches a full‑depth quarter‑inch polystyrene board bond breaker is placed in the longitudinal joint, whereas Type C patches employ tie bars instead of a bond breaker; all longitudinal joints are saw‑cut to a width of one‑quarter inch and a depth of one inch. Partial‑depth repairs that abut an existing transverse joint receive a half‑inch thick expansion‑joint material that extends at least half an inch below the bottom of the repaired slab whenever feasible, while repairs meeting a longitudinal joint use a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene with the same minimum embedment. In concrete overlays the existing joints are first identified and marked, then two full‑depth saw cuts are made on each side of the precut line following the underlying joint edges; the intervening concrete is removed, all transverse and longitudinal joints are cleaned of deleterious material and subsequently sealed or caulked in accordance with manufacturer recommendations. The required saw‑cut depth is at least one‑third the slab thickness within twelve inches of the pavement edge and at least one‑quarter elsewhere, with a minimum cut width of one‑quarter inch; longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths. The filler material for transverse expansion joints in reinforced or plain cement concrete pavements must span the full lane width, present a smooth top surface, be at least one inch thick, and be provided with punched holes so that dowel bars can be inserted without reducing the filler thickness; acceptable materials include cork, sponge rubber, fiber joint filler, or polypropylene foam that meet the applicable AASHTO or ASTM specifications. Longitudinal joints in concrete base courses and structural joints require premolded pieces at least eight feet long that are joined securely. [1][2]

The design of expansion‑joint interfaces begins by locating existing transverse joints on the pavement plan and, when a roadway is widened, aligning new transverse joints with those already present whenever feasible [2]. In plain‑cement concrete divisor zones the joints are placed without load‑transfer units and must conform in size and position to adjacent concrete curbs, after which the joint cavity is filled with the filler material prescribed in the specifications [2]. Concrete mix design incorporates the site’s average daily temperature range together with the AASHTO expansion coefficient so that panel dimensions and joint locations can accommodate anticipated thermal movement, and slab lengths are adjusted to match existing transverse joints while the layout of new joints permits expansion and contraction at both new and adjacent joints [2]. The spacing gap for transverse joints is limited to a minimum of one‑quarter inch and a maximum of one‑half inch along their entire length, whereas longitudinal joint gaps may not exceed three‑quarters inch; dowel bars, tie bars and appropriate sealing are provided to maintain load transfer across these interfaces [2]. Panel geometry is controlled so that the aspect ratio approaches unity and never exceeds 1.5, with panel dimensions limited to roughly eighteen times the overlay thickness, and when full‑depth cuts are required two cuts are made on each side of the joint to a depth of at least one‑third of slab thickness within twelve inches of the pavement edge and at least one‑fourth elsewhere; the resulting panels are cleaned and sealed [2]. For the pavement‑shoulder interface a sealant reservoir measuring 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch is formed before any sealing work, and a rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material is applied, while transverse joints must have reservoirs that meet the shape‑factor criteria set out in the specifications [1]. Longitudinal joints between traffic lanes and between a lane and a concrete shoulder are also cleaned and sealed on every CPR project using the same rubberized sealant [1]. When an overlay is planned any badly spalled joint or slab should be filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material before placing the new surface, after which sealing of the joints is not recommended [1]. Where a geotextile interlayer is used its edges must overlap by eight inches plus or minus two inches and extend at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, while an asphalt interlayer requires a nominal one‑inch depth of Superpave mix; at overlay limits a debonding geotextile layer is installed when existing treated base or concrete lies directly beneath [2]. Joint repair calls for a three‑quarter‑inch thick filler placed in the joint adjacent to any remaining joint, and for corner repairs a half‑inch filler reestablishes the original transverse joint location; Type B coated dowel bars receive bond‑breaker lubricant on at least one‑half of their length, are protected by a tube providing a minimum one‑inch clearance, and all dowels are anchored with material that sets within five minutes, with any exposed coated portion rendered bondless [2]. Partial‑depth repairs intersecting an existing joint treat transverse joints with a half‑inch filler and longitudinal joints with a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene filler extending at least one‑half inch below the bottom of the new concrete; if the repair material can be sawed without damage, the joint is re‑established by cutting through the full depth of the patch and then sealing as prescribed [2]. Interfaces with structures or appurtenances require premolded filler placed to the full pavement depth, with a minimum thickness of one‑half inch for manholes, inlets, valve boxes and similar features and three‑quarters inch where the pavement abuts rigid structures such as bridges or culverts; railway track interfaces are first filled with mortar and then a quarter‑inch filler is placed along each rail before concrete placement [2]. The selected premolded filler must match joint geometry, provide a continuous load‑transferring surface, and meet AASHTO or ASTM specifications; for transverse joints in reinforced or plain cement concrete pavements the filler extends the full lane (or ramp/taper) width with at least one inch thickness, possesses a smooth top face and pre‑punched dowel‑bar holes to ensure a snug fit without loss of thickness [2]. Longitudinal joint fillers for concrete base courses or structural elements are supplied in pieces no shorter than eight feet and joined securely, with acceptable materials including cork, sponge rubber, fiber, or closed‑cell polypropylene foam [2]. Concrete gutter connections are sealed with approved asphalt cement and a three‑quarter‑inch premolded expansion‑joint piece cut to match the gutter cross‑section, installed at each structural connection and at the end of daily placement, after which the joint is prepared and sealed or caulked according to the manufacturer’s recommendations [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Expansion joints are required to be incorporated into a functional drainage system that prevents water, chlorides and other incompressible materials from infiltrating the pavement structure [1][2]. To achieve this, a sealant reservoir typically sized at 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch is constructed within the joint and filled with a rubberized or rubberized‑asphalt sealing material as specified [1]. The concrete mix for the pavement is selected based on the average daily temperature and site exposure conditions, which determines appropriate panel dimensions and the spacing of expansion joints [2]. Slab layouts include blockouts that accommodate existing utilities and provide dedicated drainage structures so water can be conveyed away from the joint zone [2]. All longitudinal and transverse joints are sealed in accordance with the specified sealing procedures, and where slabs rest directly on granular or cementitious bedding an underseal of cementitious or polyurethane grout is applied to prevent moisture ingress [2][1]. The surrounding layer system is arranged to permit free drainage, with the geotextile interlayer terminated into a drainable layer or a daylighted location such as a ditch foreslope and without any obstruction to water flow [2]. Prior to overlay placement the geotextile is dampened but not saturated, and its surface temperature is limited to 120 °F to avoid adverse thermal effects [2]. If rain occurs while a patch area remains open, an outlet is excavated through the shoulder at the lowest point of the patch to drain water away from the joint zone [2]. During saw‑cutting operations cooling water, slurry and dust are kept out of traffic lanes, and joints are sawed and sealed promptly to avoid water entry [2]. Dowel‑bar anchoring is verified to eliminate voids that could admit moisture, thereby maintaining load transfer across the joint without deflection [2]. Work on concrete placement is halted when the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·h unless windbreaks or fog‑misting are employed to mitigate the rate, ensuring that excessive drying does not draw moisture from the joint area [2]. The base course must be kept moist without standing water and, if necessary, sprinkled to keep its surface temperature below 115 °F before paving, providing a stable substrate for the expansion joint [2]. Cold‑weather provisions require stopping work when ambient temperature drops to 40 °F (45 °F for accelerated concrete), while hot‑weather limits call for cessation of placement if the plastic concrete temperature rises above 90 °F or the ambient reaches 85 °F, protecting the joint from extreme thermal stresses [2]. When temperatures fall to 40 °F, insulating blankets are placed over the concrete and removed in a staged manner so that the concrete‑surface temperature does not change more than 40 °F within any one‑hour period, further safeguarding joint performance [2].

Expansion joints incorporated in Portland‑cement concrete pavements are required to satisfy a set of accessibility, safety, geometric and user criteria that together ensure durable performance and safe passage for all road users [1][2]. The joints must be positioned so that they do not intersect driving lanes or final pavement markings, thereby preserving unobstructed vehicle travel and wheelchair access [2]. Joint sealing is performed with the specified rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material and a sealant reservoir of the prescribed dimensions is provided; for pavement‑shoulder joints the reservoir must be 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch, while transverse joints must meet the shape‑factor criteria outlined in the applicable specifications [1]. The sealing work assumes an existing functional drainage system and is intended to prevent water, chlorides and incompressible material from infiltrating the pavement structure, protecting both structural integrity and user safety [1]. Transverse expansion joints are installed at the locations shown on construction drawings and, when a pavement is widened, they should be aligned with any existing joints whenever feasible; in plain‑cement divisor areas the joint is placed without load‑transfer units and must correspond to the dimensions of the adjacent concrete curb [2]. The geometry of each joint follows the aggregate thermal‑expansion coefficient prescribed in the AASHTO mechanistic‑empirical design guide, and panel dimensions and spacing are selected to accommodate anticipated temperature‑induced movement [2]. The transverse gap of a joint shall be no less than one‑quarter inch and shall not exceed one‑half inch along its entire length, while longitudinal gaps are limited to three‑quarters inch; the finished vertical differential across any joint may not exceed one‑quarter inch, limits that ensure safe vehicle passage and maintain accessibility [2]. Prior to concrete placement the joints are located, marked and precut, with two full‑depth saw cuts made on each side of the joint having a minimum width of one‑quarter inch; the cut depth must reach at least one‑third of the slab thickness within twelve inches of the pavement edge and at least one‑quarter of the thickness elsewhere [2]. Resulting concrete panels should have an aspect ratio as close to unity as practicable, not exceeding 1.5, and shall not be larger than eighteen times the overlay thickness; longitudinal joints are required to be kept out of wheel paths and any joint that would fall within one‑and‑a‑half feet of an existing crack shall be omitted [2]. When an existing joint is replaced a three‑quarter‑inch thick expansion‑joint material is installed adjacent to the remaining joint, and for corner repairs a half‑inch thick material is used to reestablish the original transverse location [2]. Coated dowel bars bordering the joint receive bond‑breaker lubricant before concrete placement, and all constructed joints are sealed in accordance with the specified procedures [2]. The filler placed within the joint must be sized to match pavement geometry; around manholes, inlets, valve boxes and similar appurtenances the premolded filler shall be at least a half‑inch thick, where the pavement meets other rigid structures such as bridges or culverts it shall be no less than three‑quarters inch, and for railway track crossings the minimum thickness is three‑quarters inch against mortar‑filled rails and one‑quarter inch along each rail [2]. For transverse joints in partial‑depth repairs the filler thickness is specified as a half‑inch, while longitudinal joints use a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene that extends at least a half‑inch below the bottom of the repair whenever feasible; in all cases the filler must present a smooth top surface and contain pre‑punched holes to receive dowel bars without loss of material thickness [2]. Filler lengths shall equal the full width of a traffic lane, ramp or taper with a minimum overall thickness of one inch, and longitudinal joints in concrete base courses require filler pieces at least eight feet long that are securely joined [2]. At gutter connections the joint shall be a three‑quarter‑inch premolded unit cut to the gutter’s cross‑section, installed at structural connections and whenever gutter work stops for the day, then sealed or caulked per the manufacturer’s instructions [2]. A construction quality‑control plan must include procedures that guarantee proper joint placement, timely sawing, correct sealing and compliance with these geometric and material requirements, thereby addressing safety, performance and accessibility for all pavement users [2].

Verification and expected performance

The joint is first examined to ensure that the transverse gap between adjacent slabs falls within the design range of one‑quarter inch minimum and one‑half inch maximum along its entire length, while any longitudinal opening does not exceed the specified maximum. The finished vertical differential across the joint must be less than one‑quarter inch; this condition is checked before the next slab is placed. Load‑transfer dowels and tie bars are inspected to verify that they have been cast in accordance with the shop drawings, positioned square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface within plus or minus one‑eighth inch of the indicated locations. The grout used in dowel slots and bedding is tested until it reaches the prescribed compressive strength, which must be at least 1,000 psi before traffic is permitted. All existing transverse expansion joints are identified and marked, and saw cuts are made to a depth of at least one‑third of the pavement thickness within twelve inches of any edge and at least one‑fourth of the thickness elsewhere. After cutting, the joint faces are cleared of all deleterious material and sealed in both the transverse and longitudinal directions. Joint spacing is verified against the project layout, and panel dimensions are confirmed not to exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness with an aspect ratio no greater than 1.5; any cracking outside the sawed joints or in wheel paths constitutes non‑compliance. The quality‑control plan submitted for the work is reviewed and a project representative inspects the installation of the specified three‑quarter‑inch expansion‑joint filler material. Where premolded filler is used, it must be placed at a minimum thickness of one‑half inch around utilities and at least three‑quarters inch adjacent to other rigid structures; for transverse joints the filler must span the full lane width with a minimum overall thickness of one inch, present a smooth top surface, and contain dowel‑bar holes that preserve its thickness. Longitudinal joint filler pieces must be at least eight feet long and securely joined. All filler material must originate from a Bulletin 15‑listed manufacturer and be an approved type—cork, sponge rubber, fiber, or polypropylene foam—that meets the referenced standards. Bond‑breaker lubricant is applied to at least half the length of each Type B coated dowel bar without using brushes, and it must be placed at least one hour before concrete placement. Random four‑inch core samples are taken to confirm that voids around each dowel do not exceed a total of one‑half inch. Surface flatness is checked with a twelve‑foot straightedge; any high point or depression of three‑sixteenths inch or more is ground out. Finally, a concrete pavement joint wipe test is performed and the joint’s certification is cross‑checked against Section 106.03(b)3 to verify that all contractual specifications for compliance, constructability, performance, and consistency with project requirements have been satisfied before opening the pavement to traffic. The expansion joint is expected to deliver long‑term load transfer, prevent joint cracking or spalling, and preserve a smooth riding surface, with traffic allowed only after the concrete meets its design strength. Load‑transfer capacity is verified by coring anchored dowels and accepting joints whose peripheral voids are less than 0.50 inch; surface flatness is checked with a 12‑foot straightedge and any deviation over 3/16 inch must be ground out; joint material must be placed to the prescribed thickness (e.g., 3/4‑inch for replacement joints) and sealed as required; finally, traffic may be opened only when cylinder tests show at least 1,000 psi compressive strength. These measurable criteria define the performance outcomes for expansion joints. [2]
