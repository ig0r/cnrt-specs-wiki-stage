Expansion Joint

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Expansion Joint is a topic under Rigid Pavement.
expansion-joints
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-30

Expansion joints are full‑depth saw cuts—at least one‑third of slab thickness—incorporated into rigid concrete pavements to provide a controlled path for thermal expansion, contraction and shrinkage movements that would otherwise generate uncontrolled stresses within the slabs [2]. By locating these cuts according to site‑specific average daily temperature, aggregate expansion coefficients, and prescribed panel dimensions (no larger than eighteen times the overlay thickness with an aspect ratio ≤ 1.5), the joints preserve structural continuity and load‑transfer capability while confining any cracking to the joint itself [2]. A sealant reservoir is formed within each joint and filled with approved rubberized or asphalt sealants together with appropriate filler material, allowing transverse openings of ¼‑½ in. to accommodate movement yet remain sealed against water and debris, thereby maintaining durability and long‑term serviceability [1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Expansion joints are incorporated in rigid concrete pavements to provide a controlled path for the thermal and shrinkage movements that otherwise generate uncontrolled stresses within slabs. [2] When these movements are not accommodated, the pavement can develop faulting, loss of load‑transfer capacity, spalling, water infiltration and other forms of distress that reduce serviceability and shorten service life. [1][2] The primary performance need therefore is to preserve structural continuity and load transfer while allowing transverse expansion and contraction without inducing random cracking. [1][2] To meet this need, designers base mix proportions on the site’s average daily temperature and exposure conditions and apply standard aggregate expansion coefficients to select appropriate panel dimensions and joint locations that give each slab sufficient room to expand. [2] Joints are created as full‑depth saw cuts at a depth of at least one‑third of the slab thickness, placed according to prescribed spacing, aspect‑ratio and size limits, which releases stresses along known lines and confines any cracking to the joint. [2] A sealant reservoir is constructed within each joint so that transverse movement can occur without loss of structural continuity, and effective sealing restores or maintains load‑transfer capability. [1][2] When existing joints exhibit poor performance such as faulting or inadequate load transfer, rehabilitation—by constructing new sealant reservoirs and resealing the joints, whether saw‑cut or metal‑plate types—is required to revitalize transverse contraction capacity and extend pavement life. [1][2] Overall, providing effective joint seals and proper joint design prevents premature or random cracking, maintains pavement durability, and ensures long‑term serviceability despite temperature fluctuations. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 403–474)

The primary functional objective of an expansion joint is to accommodate the thermal expansion and contraction of concrete pavement slabs, which requires that panel dimensions be selected on the basis of site‑specific average daily temperature and exposure conditions and that joints be located so transverse openings range from one‑quarter inch to one‑half inch while longitudinal openings do not exceed three‑quarters inch. The joint must also limit the finished vertical differential across any joint to a maximum of one‑quarter inch in order to prevent uncontrolled cracking caused by thermal movement. Structurally, the joint is required to provide reliable load transfer between adjacent slabs; this is achieved through the use of dowel‑bar retrofits or cross‑stitching where hairline cracks appear and through properly lubricated dowel bars whose surrounding voids are limited to less than 0.5 inch per core. In transverse joints a filler material of three‑quarters inch thickness is installed (or one‑half inch for corner repairs), while longitudinal joints employ a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene, and where the pavement meets rigid structures a premolded filler of at least three‑quarters inch thickness is placed; elsewhere a minimum half‑inch filler extending at least half an inch below the repair surface is required. The design assumes a stable subgrade and a functional drainage system so that water, chlorides and incompressible materials cannot infiltrate the slab or undermine drainage. Durability objectives mandate the use of approved joint material and sealants that fully close both transverse and longitudinal joints; rubberized sealants are applied in reservoirs sized at 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch for pavement‑shoulder joints, and bond‑breaker lubricant must be applied at least one hour before concrete placement around dowel bars. Joints must be cleaned and resealed at the first sign of distress to preserve serviceability, preventing faulting, spalling or slab migration that could compromise safety and overall pavement performance. Saw cuts used to create joints are limited to a maximum width of one‑half inch, with depths no less than one‑third of slab thickness within twelve inches of an edge and no less than one‑quarter elsewhere; the aspect ratio of any panel should be as close to unity as feasible and never exceed 1.5. After placement joint faces are protected from subsequent coating, a joint wipe test is performed to verify moisture tightness, and construction traffic protection measures prohibit drop‑off conditions while ensuring cut dimensions remain within prescribed limits. The finished surface must be free of high spots or depressions greater than three‑sixteenths inch; any irregularities exceeding this limit are ground away, the texture and cross‑slope are matched to the existing pavement, and longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths so that the joint does not introduce hazards. All joint work is completed before opening to traffic, supporting long‑term serviceability through proper curing practices such as removal of curing compounds prior to sawing. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 360–474)

Scope and applicability

Expansion joints are employed primarily in rigid concrete pavements, including continuous prestressed precast slab systems, reinforced or plain Portland‑cement slabs, and overlay constructions. They become necessary whenever thermal movement must be accommodated—such as when the site’s average daily temperature and exposure conditions dictate panel sizing—or when slab dimensions approach limits that would otherwise induce cracking; specifically, panels should not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and their aspect ratio should remain at or below 1.5. In new construction the joints are laid out according to a joint‑spacing plan, with saw cuts made to at least one‑third of the slab depth within twelve inches of an edge and to at least one‑quarter of the depth elsewhere. The design calls for expansion‑joint filler material in a variety of repair and detailing situations. When a patch replaces an existing joint while an adjacent joint remains, a ¾‑inch thick filler is placed in the joint nearest the remaining joint; corner repairs require a ½‑inch filler to reestablish the original transverse joint location, and partial‑depth repairs that abut an existing transverse joint also receive a ½‑inch filler extending at least ½ inch below the repair bottom. For transverse cracks or joints wider than ¼ inch, the preformed cellular polystyrene is substituted with expansion‑joint material matching the crack width, and dowel bars are installed in saw‑cut slots with 1½‑inch non‑metallic caps that provide a ¼‑inch clearance. Expansion joint material is also required where concrete slabs meet ancillary structures. A minimum of ½ inch full‑depth filler surrounds manholes, inlets, valve boxes and similar appurtenances; at bridges, culverts or other rigid elements the filler thickness must be at least ¾ inch. When pavement crosses railway tracks, a ¾‑inch filler is placed against mortar‑filled rails, while a ¼‑inch filler is used along each rail when the pavement runs parallel to the track. For transverse joints in reinforced or plain cement concrete pavements the filler must span the full lane width and be at least one inch thick; longitudinal joints in concrete base courses require filler pieces no shorter than eight feet that are securely joined. Acceptable fillers include cork, sponge‑rubber, fiber‑based products, or closed‑cell polypropylene foam that meet AASHTO M153 or equivalent ASTM specifications. Finally, the approach is applied during joint rehabilitation of existing Portland‑cement concrete pavements; it provides for the construction of a sealant reservoir and the sealing of transverse contraction or expansion joints, whether they are saw‑cut (Type 1) or metal‑plate (Type 2) joints. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 360–474), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 684)

When an existing concrete pavement shows joint faulting, excessive movement, or inadequate load transfer, the underlying support or load‑transfer system must be restored rather than relying on a conventional expansion joint [1]. In such cases slab stabilization or jacking can be employed to improve subgrade support, and a dowel‑bar retrofit may be used to reestablish proper load transfer provided drainage remains functional and the subgrade is stable [1]. If an overlay is planned, sealing of joints or joint rehabilitation is not appropriate, so alternative structural treatments become preferable [1]. Moreover, design geometry can limit the practicality of expansion‑joint layouts; panels whose aspect ratio would exceed about 1.5 or whose dimensions would be greater than roughly eighteen times the overlay thickness are considered impractical for conventional joints and should trigger a different design strategy [2]. Longitudinal joints placed directly in wheel paths are prohibited, requiring either a revised joint configuration or another accommodation method when traffic loading demands such placement [2]. When an existing crack lies within approximately one‑and‑a‑half feet of a proposed joint location, the specification calls for omission of sawing and indicates that a non‑joint solution or layout revision is required [2]. Together these conditions—structural deficiencies, overlay plans, geometric constraints, prohibited wheel‑path joints, and proximity to existing cracks—make alternative design approaches such as slab stabilization, dowel‑bar retrofits, full‑depth joint replacement, or other accommodation methods preferable to conventional expansion joints [1][2].

Design inputs and requirements

The design and construction of expansion joints in Portland‑cement concrete pavements are governed by a suite of federal and AASHTO specifications that prescribe material properties, geometric limits, sealing procedures, and quality‑control requirements [1][2]. Federal Publication 408 specifications together with the FHWA Roadway Construction Standards (Publication 72M) provide the primary regulatory framework for joint geometry, sealant reservoirs, and rehabilitation methods [1]. The concrete mix for rigid pavements must incorporate the site’s average daily temperature and exposure conditions so that panel dimensions and joint locations are compatible with the thermal‑expansion coefficients prescribed in the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide [2]. Joint geometry is tightly controlled, with transverse joints limited to a maximum width of one‑half inch and required to maintain at least a quarter‑inch length along their full extent, while longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters inch in width [2]. Panels are expected to have an aspect ratio close to unity and shall not exceed a ratio of 1.5, and the overall panel dimensions are capped at eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness [2]. Saw cuts that define joints must be no less than one‑third of slab thickness within twelve inches of a pavement edge and at least one‑fourth of the thickness elsewhere, after which all joint surfaces are to be cleaned of deleterious material before sealing [2][1]. Sealant reservoirs are sized according to the shape‑factor defined in Publication 408 Section 521 and illustrated in FHWA RC‑20M, with pavement‑shoulder joints requiring a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch reservoir that may be sealed with either rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material [1]. Joint sealing must employ the rubberized joint‑sealing material prescribed in Publication 408 Sections 512 and 521, while approved caulking compounds meeting the requirements of Sections 705.4(b)/(c) and 705.7 are used for longitudinal and transverse joints as referenced in the same publication [1][2]. Expansion‑joint fillers must meet minimum thicknesses that vary with location: at manholes, inlets, valve boxes and similar appurtenances a filler of at least one‑half inch is required; where pavement meets bridges, culverts or other rigid structures a three‑quarter‑inch filler is mandated; transverse concrete pavement joints are provided with a filler of at least one inch thickness sized to lane width and incorporating dowel‑bar holes; longitudinal joints use quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene; partial‑depth repairs call for filler extending at least one‑half inch below the repair bottom [2]. All filler materials—cork or rubber meeting AASHTO M 153, fiber‑based material meeting AASHTO M 213 with a four‑inch compression specimen, or polypropylene foam complying with ASTM D8139—must be supplied by manufacturers listed in Bulletin 15 and satisfy the certification requirements of Section 106.03(b)3 [2]. Dowel‑bar installation follows detailed tolerances: Type B coated bars require bond‑breaker lubricant on at least half their length unless pre‑lubricated, applied a minimum of one hour before concrete placement with lubricated ends protected by a one‑inch clearance tube; Type A bars need no lubricant. Chairs must provide at least a half‑inch clearance on all sides, vertical or horizontal tilt is limited to a quarter‑inch, and longitudinal or transverse translation may not exceed one inch; anchoring material must achieve an initial set within five minutes [2]. Rehabilitation of saw‑cut or metal‑plate joints is performed in accordance with Publication 408 Section 513 and the procedures outlined in FHWA Roadway Construction Standards RC‑26M, while longitudinal, transverse, and crack sealing reference the specific sections of Publication 408 such as 512, 525, 519, 590, and 1091 [1]. All joint work is a prerequisite before any concrete pavement repair or patching operation, but it is not required when an overlay is planned; compliance with the Standard Drawings and the project’s quality‑control plan (Section 106.03(a)2.a) is mandatory, and failure to meet dimensional tolerances or to prevent cracking outside sawed joints renders panels defective [1][2].

Design methods and criteria

The design and maintenance of expansion joints in Portland‑cement concrete pavements follows a largely procedural methodology that emphasizes timely resealing, joint rehabilitation that creates new sealant reservoirs, routine cleaning, and the use of rubberized or asphalt sealants sized to a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch pocket, as specified in the relevant construction standards [1]. Prior to any geometric layout, a climatic assessment is performed to obtain the average daily temperature and exposure conditions at the project site, and these data are combined with the thermal‑expansion coefficient given in the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide to determine appropriate panel dimensions and joint locations [2]. An empirical rule limits the longer dimension of any overlay panel to no more than eighteen times the concrete thickness, while the aspect ratio between the longer and shorter sides is kept as close to unity as practicable and must not exceed 1.5 [2]. Joint geometry is further constrained by minimum and maximum widths: transverse joints must be at least one‑quarter inch wide but shall not exceed one‑half inch, and longitudinal joints are limited to three‑quarters inch [2]. When panels intersect or a joint must be re‑established after repair, full‑depth saw cuts are placed according to standard drawings; the cut depth is required to be at least one‑third of the slab thickness within twelve inches of an edge and at least one‑fourth elsewhere [2]. The FHWA HIPERPAV program or any department‑approved equivalent may be employed to calculate a project‑specific sawing window for each joint, providing a mechanistic‑empirical tool that links climate data to contraction‑joint timing [2]. Construction specifications prescribe the use of premolded expansion‑joint fillers with minimum total thicknesses that vary by context: at least one‑half inch for typical structures such as manholes or valve boxes, three‑quarters inch where the pavement meets bridges, culverts, or other rigid elements, and a quarter inch when abutting railway tracks [2]. For joints that replace an existing joint, a three‑quarter‑inch filler is installed; partial‑depth repairs call for a half‑inch thick expansion material in transverse joints and a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene in longitudinal joints, both required to extend at least one‑half inch below the bottom of the repair whenever feasible [2]. Dowel bars that cross the joint are Type B coated; bond‑breaker lubricant is applied to at least half of each dowel’s length before concrete placement, and holes are drilled no larger than one‑eighth inch beyond the dowel diameter [2]. The anchoring material used with the dowels must achieve an initial set within five minutes, be rotated several times to purge air, and any excess is troweled flush; random verification of dowel anchorage is performed by coring and confirming that voids around the dowel perimeter are less than half an inch [2]. After placement, all longitudinal and transverse joints created during patching or new construction are sealed or caulked in accordance with manufacturer recommendations, and performance testing such as a concrete pavement joint wipe test is included in the contractor’s quality‑control plan [2]. Joint rehabilitation techniques described in the procedural guide revitalize existing transverse contraction, construction, or expansion joints by constructing new sealant reservoirs and resealing the joints, thereby restoring load transfer and extending service life [1]. Cross‑stitching with epoxy‑coated tie bars may be employed to achieve near‑100 % load transfer across cracks or joints, complementing dowel‑bar retrofits as part of the maintenance sequence outlined in the specifications [1].

The design of expansion joints in rigid pavements is based on temperature‑driven assumptions that use the aggregate thermal‑expansion coefficient prescribed in the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide, ensuring that panel sizing and joint placement accommodate expected daily temperature variations. [2] Joint gaps are limited to a minimum of one‑quarter inch and a maximum of one‑half inch for transverse joints, while longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters inch, and vertical differentials across any joint must be kept within one‑quarter inch to maintain structural continuity. [2] Panel geometry is constrained so that the aspect ratio (longer dimension divided by shorter) approaches unity and never exceeds 1.5, with overall panel dimensions limited to eighteen times the overlay thickness, providing a geometric safety margin against excessive thermal movement. [2] Saw cuts defining joints must penetrate at least one‑third of slab depth within twelve inches of an edge and at least one‑quarter depth elsewhere; any cracking outside these sawed lines is considered defective, establishing a reliability criterion for joint integrity. [2] Expansion‑joint filler material thicknesses are prescribed according to adjacent conditions: a minimum half‑inch for manholes and similar appurtenances, three‑quarters inch where concrete meets bridges, culverts or other rigid structures, and three‑quarters inch when crossing railway tracks (or one‑quarter inch per rail), thereby setting threshold values that protect against intrusion. [2] For transverse joints in reinforced or plain concrete pavement the filler must extend the full lane width with a minimum thickness of one inch, provide a smooth top surface, and include punched holes for dowel bars without loss of material thickness, ensuring load‑transfer reliability. [2] Longitudinal joint repairs require a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene insert that extends at least half an inch below the repair bottom, while partial‑depth repairs abutting existing joints use half‑inch expansion‑joint material (transverse) or quarter‑inch polystyrene (longitudinal), establishing clear thickness thresholds for repair reliability. [2] When a patch replaces an existing joint, three‑quarter‑inch joint material is installed, and corner repairs receive one‑half‑inch material, defining the minimum filler dimensions for those situations. [2] Dowel bar geometry must meet strict tolerances: each end may not deviate more than 0.25 inch from the midpoint, longitudinal translation relative to the intended joint location may not exceed one inch, and lateral offset across the joint is limited to one inch, providing geometric safety limits for load‑transfer devices. [2] Non‑metallic caps of 1½ inches are placed with a quarter‑inch clearance from the dowel tip to the cap bottom, and plastic or non‑metallic chairs must maintain at least a half‑inch gap to surrounding surfaces, further controlling joint geometry. [2] For joints wider than one‑quarter inch, dedicated expansion‑joint material matching the crack width replaces thin polystyrene filler to prevent concrete intrusion, reinforcing the reliability of the joint seal. [2] A bond‑breaker lubricant on Type B coated dowels must be applied at least one hour before concrete placement, and core testing verifies dowel anchorage; voids around a dowel perimeter must sum to less than 0.50 inch in any core for acceptance, establishing a factor of safety based on inspection criteria. [2] All expansion‑joint fillers must be sourced from approved manufacturers and conform to the relevant material standards—AASHTO M 153 for cork or pre‑formed rubber, AASHTO M 213 for fiber, ASTM D8139 for polypropylene foam—and be certified per Section 106.03(b)3, ensuring material reliability. [2] The installation assumes a stable subgrade and functional drainage so that water, chlorides, or incompressible materials cannot infiltrate the structure, representing an underlying design assumption that underpins joint performance. [1] Joints are to be cleaned and sealed with the rubberized joint‑sealing material prescribed in Publication 408, with the sealant reservoir constructed to the specified shape factor and typical dimensions of 0.75 in by 0.75 in; resealing is required as soon as any distress appears, even if overall serviceability remains acceptable, providing an operational reliability requirement for maintenance. [1] After placement, joints are sealed or caulked according to manufacturer recommendations, ensuring long‑term performance and meeting the reliability thresholds established for expansion joints. [2]

Structural section and dimensions

The expansion‑joint design for concrete pavements specifies that transverse joints between precast slabs shall be cut to a width of at least one‑quarter inch and not exceed one‑half inch along their full length, while longitudinal joints are limited to three‑quarters inch. Slab lengths are constrained to a minimum of six feet and a maximum of fifteen feet, extending to twenty feet when the slab is prestressed; each slab includes a sacrificial concrete layer that adds an extra quarter‑inch over the nominal thickness. Where a joint is repaired or replaced, the filler material must be three‑quarters inch thick for patches that substitute an existing joint, and one‑half inch thick for corner repairs or partial‑depth repairs, with the latter required to extend at least half an inch below the bottom of the repair whenever practicable. For rigid‑pavement overlays, panel dimensions are limited so that no individual panel exceeds eighteen times the overlay thickness and the ratio of the longer side to the shorter side does not exceed 1.5; the saw cut forming the joint must be at least one‑third of the slab thickness within twelve inches of any edge and at least one‑quarter of the slab thickness elsewhere. Premolded joint filler used around appurtenances must meet minimum total thicknesses that vary with the adjacent structure: at least one‑half inch for manholes, inlets, valve boxes and similar features; at least three‑quarters inch where the pavement meets bridges, culverts or other rigid structures and also when crossing railway tracks; and a quarter‑inch minimum along each mortar‑filled rail. In reinforced or plain cement concrete pavements, transverse joint filler must span the full width of the traffic lane, ramp or taper and be at least one inch thick, while longitudinal joints in concrete base courses require filler pieces no shorter than eight feet, with ends securely joined; the same length requirement applies to structural joints. No separate dimensional requirements are prescribed for the underlying base, subbase, treated‑soil layers, shoulders, edge supports, or reinforcement beyond those directly tied to the joint geometry and filler thicknesses described above. In designing expansion joints for precast rigid pavements and concrete overlays the thickness of the structural layer is the primary control parameter. The slab is placed one‑quarter inch thicker than the nominal design thickness, providing a sacrificial layer that accommodates early wear and thermal strain. Because the allowable panel size scales with this thickness, any concrete overlay panel may not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness, and its longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio is kept as close to unity as practicable but never larger than 1.5. These limits together dictate the maximum slab length—fifteen feet for conventional units and twenty feet for prestressed units—and consequently the spacing of expansion joints.

Joint geometry follows directly from these dimensional constraints. Transverse joints are cut so that their width lies between one‑quarter inch and one‑half inch along their entire length, while longitudinal joints may be up to three‑quarters inch wide but are avoided in wheel paths whenever possible. When project plans do not specify joint locations, a layout complying with the eighteen‑times‑thickness rule and the aspect‑ratio limit is prepared for departmental approval.

The pavement structure beneath the slab consists of a compacted subbase topped by a bedding layer whose cross‑slope is established with a laser‑controlled screeding device to satisfy the project’s slope tolerance. Any abrupt change in elevation greater than two inches is accommodated by inserting a temporary asphalt wedge transition, and at the limits of an overlay a transition detail is constructed as shown on the plans. Where such transitions rest directly on existing treated base or concrete pavement, a geotextile debonding layer is placed beneath the new slab to prevent bond‑related stresses.

Tie‑ins are provided by longitudinal tie bars installed in continuous paving sections longer than sixty‑five feet, following the fabricator’s shop drawings. These tie bars are positioned so that they do not impede the intended movement of adjacent existing joints, thereby preserving the functional expansion capability of the system. [2]

Materials and mixture design

Expansion joints in Portland cement concrete pavements are required to be sealed with a rubberized joint‑sealing material as defined in the relevant specifications, and the sealant is placed into a reservoir formed to 0.75 in by 0.75 in dimensions. When an overlay will be applied, any joints that have spalled concrete must first be cleaned and then filled with a 4.75‑mm asphalt wearing‑course material or an equivalent product before the new surface is placed. The concrete slab mix that forms each pavement panel must be proportioned to achieve at least 4,925 psi compressive strength and at least 650 psi flexural strength, with the aggregate thermal‑expansion coefficient taken from the standard values in the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide. Joint geometry is limited so that transverse gaps are no smaller than ¼ in and no larger than ½ in, while longitudinal gaps may not exceed ¾ in. The filler occupying the joint must be a premolded or preformed product supplied by an approved manufacturer and may be selected from cork, sponge rubber, fiber‑type joint filler, or closed‑cell polypropylene foam, each of which must meet its respective test method (AASHTO M153 for cork/rubber, AASHTO M213 for fiber, ASTM D8139 for foam). All fillers are required to retain an overall thickness of at least one inch; however, when the joint surrounds utilities such as manholes or valve boxes the minimum thickness is reduced to ½ in, and where the pavement abuts other rigid structures (bridges, culverts, railway tracks) a minimum of ¾ in is mandated, with gutter installations also calling for a ¾‑in premolded filler. For joint openings greater than ¼ in, an expansion‑joint material whose thickness matches the measured gap—commonly ½ in or ¾ in—is installed, whereas openings of ¼ in or less are filled with a ¼‑in preformed cellular polystyrene piece that is cut to the slot dimensions and extended at least ½ in below the repair surface. The filler pieces for transverse joints must be supplied in lengths equal to the lane width, have a smooth top surface, and include pre‑punched dowel holes; longitudinal or structural joint pieces shall be at least eight feet long and securely joined together. Where dowel bars are incorporated, epoxy‑coated smooth dowels are used for retrofits, and new Type B coated dowels receive a bond‑breaker lubricant applied at least one hour before concrete placement, after which each bar is capped with a 1½‑in non‑metallic expansion cap that leaves a ¼‑in clearance. The dowel slots are backfilled with a concrete patching material and the joint assembly is completed with an epoxy bonding compound; adjacent edges are sealed with a manufacturer‑approved caulk or, where appropriate, asphalt cement such as PG 64S‑22 or PG 58S‑28. Expansion joint fillers must be sourced from manufacturers listed in Bulletin 15 and limited to cork, sponge‑rubber, fiber‑based or polypropylene‑foam products that meet the applicable test standards (AASHTO M 153, AASHTO M 213, ASTM D8139). Structural details and interfaces

Expansion joints in Portland cement concrete pavements are first located after a thermal‑expansion analysis and must be spaced according to the department‑approved plan, limiting panel aspect ratios to no more than 1.5 and overall dimensions to eighteen times the overlay thickness. The transverse joint width is required to lie between one‑quarter and one‑half inch along its entire length while longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters inch, and all joints are sawed full depth after the concrete has set – at least one‑third of slab thickness within twelve inches of an edge and at least one‑fourth elsewhere – before being cleaned and sealed with a rubberized sealant; a sealant reservoir typically 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch is constructed for pavement‑shoulder connections and similarly shaped reservoirs are provided for transverse joints as dictated by the shape factor [1][2]. Where load transfer across a joint is inadequate, an epoxy‑coated dowel bar retrofit may be installed, and cross‑stitching with epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars can be applied to longitudinal cracks or joints to achieve near‑full load transfer and prevent slab migration; in new construction the guides require cast load‑transfer dowels and tie bars placed according to shop drawings, with Type B coated dowels receiving a bond‑breaker lubricant on at least half their length while Type A dowels need no lubricant, each dowel being anchored in holes no larger than one‑eighth inch over the bar and grouted with a rapid‑set anchoring grout that sets within five minutes [1][2]. Non‑metallic chairs must provide a minimum ½‑inch clearance around each dowel cap, and 1½‑inch non‑metallic expansion caps are installed leaving a ¼‑inch gap between the dowel end and the cap; foam gaskets may be used to create sealed grout chambers, and all load‑transfer assemblies must be in place before concrete placement, with the pavement poured onto the joint units within thirty minutes of their installation [2]. The filler material placed in the joints varies with the adjacent element: a preformed cellular polystyrene piece typically ¼ inch thick is cut to slot dimensions as a temporary filler, while for joints wider than ¼ inch an appropriate expansion‑joint material matching the joint width replaces the polystyrene; premolded filler must be at least one‑half inch thick around manholes and similar appurtenances and at least three‑quarters inch thick where pavement meets bridges, culverts or other rigid structures, with railway crossings receiving mortar‑filled spaces of three‑quarters inch across the rail web and a quarter inch along each rail. For transverse joints in reinforced or plain concrete pavement the filler is supplied in lengths equal to the lane width with a minimum thickness of one inch, and holes are punched for dowel bars so that the filler’s thickness is not reduced; longitudinal joint filler pieces must be at least eight feet long and securely joined together [2]. Transition details include a geotextile debonding layer placed at overlay limits when underlying treated base or concrete is present, and when a partial‑depth repair abuts an existing transverse joint a ½‑inch thick expansion‑joint material extending at least ½ inch below the bottom of the repair is installed; if the repaired slab can be sawed without damage the joint is re‑established by full‑depth sawing and then sealed. Corner repairs employ a ½‑inch expansion‑joint material to restore the original transverse location together with tie bars set in 5/8‑inch holes, while longitudinal joints may incorporate a ¼‑inch full‑depth polystyrene board bond breaker for Type A and B patches (Type C patches require none). Gutter installations use premolded three‑quarter‑inch expansion‑joint material trimmed to the gutter’s cross‑section and positioned at every structural interface as well as at each daily termination point, with all joint faces sealed or caulked in accordance with manufacturer recommendations and edges where the gutter meets other structures sealed with an approved asphalt cement [2]. Joint rehabilitation projects are limited to constructing a sealant reservoir and applying sealant to existing saw‑cut (Type 1) or metal‑plate (Type 2) joints; no additional reinforcement, load‑transfer devices, transitions or connections are required for those cases [2].

Design of expansion‑joint interfaces begins with selecting a concrete mix that reflects the site’s average daily temperature and exposure conditions, which governs appropriate panel dimensions and joint locations [2]. Panel geometry is limited so that the longer side does not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the aspect ratio is kept as close to unity as feasible but never greater than 1.5 [2]. Transverse joints are identified before construction, precut in the plastic concrete, and later sawed to a depth of at least one‑third of slab thickness within twelve inches of an edge and no less than one‑quarter elsewhere [2]. The gap width of transverse joints is limited to a minimum of one‑quarter inch and a maximum of one‑half inch, and the joint faces must be cleared of deleterious material before sealing [2][1]. Longitudinal joints between traffic lanes or between a lane and a concrete shoulder are limited to a maximum width of three‑quarters inch, cleaned, and sealed with a rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material on every CPR project [2][1]. For the pavement‑shoulder interface a sealant reservoir measuring 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch is formed prior to sealing [1]. Dowel bars are used to transfer load across the joint gaps; each bar is coated with a bond‑breaker lubricant covering at least half its length unless applied in the shop, and a protective tube providing a minimum one‑inch clearance is placed over each lubricated end [2]. Placement tolerances restrict vertical tilt or horizontal skew of each dowel end to no more than 0.25 inch and limit longitudinal or horizontal translation to one inch [2]. Non‑metallic expansion caps, typically 1.5 inch high with a quarter‑inch clearance from the dowel tip, are seated on plastic or non‑metallic chairs that maintain at least a half‑inch gap to all slot surfaces [2]. After the joint faces are cleared, they are filled with concrete patching material and vibrated to consolidate around the dowel assembly [2]. Where the pavement abuts other structures, premolded expansion‑joint fillers are installed through the full pavement depth; the filler thickness is at least one‑half inch adjacent to manholes, inlets or valve boxes and increases to three‑quarters inch next to bridges, culverts or other rigid elements [2]. At railway track interfaces mortar is placed beneath rail webs and flanges, followed by a minimum three‑quarter‑inch filler for crossings and a quarter‑inch filler along each side for parallel alignments [2]. Gutter terminations are sealed with approved asphalt cement and a three‑quarter‑inch premolded filler cut to the gutter’s cross‑section is placed at structural connections and daily termination points [2]. When an overlay is planned over existing pavement, any badly spalled joint or slab should be filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material before placing the new surface, after which sealing of the joints is not recommended [1]. For partial‑depth repairs that abut an existing transverse joint, a half‑inch expansion‑joint material is installed; for repairs abutting a longitudinal joint, a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene piece is used, each extending at least half an inch below the repaired slab when feasible and full‑depth saw cuts are employed to re‑establish the joint if possible [2][1]. The expansion‑joint filler material may be cork, sponge rubber, fiber‑reinforced joint filler, or closed‑cell polypropylene foam, all conforming to applicable AASHTO or ASTM specifications and having a minimum overall thickness of one inch with pre‑punched holes to receive dowel bars without loss of thickness [2]. Longitudinal filler pieces in concrete base courses or structural joints must be at least eight feet long and securely joined [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Expansion joints are required to incorporate a functional drainage system that directs water away from the pavement structure and prevents infiltration of chlorides or other incompressible materials that could degrade the base; this is achieved by providing a properly shaped sealant reservoir typically sized at 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch and sealing the joint with a rubberized (or rubberized‑asphalt) material as specified, while also ensuring the underlying subgrade remains stable to support performance [1]. The design process begins with an evaluation of the ambient average daily temperature and site exposure conditions so that panel dimensions and joint locations can accommodate anticipated thermal movement without compromising drainage functions [2]. Transverse joint gaps are limited to a minimum of one‑quarter inch and a maximum of one‑half inch, a range that supplies sufficient freedom for expansion while limiting the aperture through which water can enter the joint system [2]. Blockouts are incorporated in each slab for existing utilities and any proposed drainage structures, guaranteeing that the joint does not obstruct the pavement’s intended drainage pathways [2]. All longitudinal and transverse joints are sealed in accordance with the applicable sealing specifications, providing a continuous barrier against moisture intrusion [1][2]. During construction, if an open patch is exposed to rain, an outlet is excavated through the shoulder at the lowest point of the patch to convey water away from the joint area, thereby preventing ponding and infiltration [2]. Cooling water, slurry, and dust generated by saw cutting are prohibited from entering traffic lanes, and a bond‑breaker lubricant applied to dowel bars further inhibits moisture penetration along load‑transfer elements [2]. Prior to concrete placement the slots are rigorously cleaned to remove all loose material and slurry within twenty‑four hours, with a final cleaning completed no later than thirty minutes before placement; air‑blasting employs a dry compressed‑air stream of at least one hundred pounds per square inch that is free of oil, moisture, or other contaminants [2]. After dowel installation a caulking compound is applied to the sides and bottom of the joint to seal temporary material and keep water out, reinforcing the primary sealant system [2]. When ambient temperatures are forty degrees Fahrenheit or lower, insulating blankets are placed over the concrete surface to limit rapid heat loss; the blankets remain until the temperature rises above forty degrees Fahrenheit or the slab attains a compressive strength of 3 600 psi, and they are removed in a manner that does not allow the surface temperature to change more than forty degrees Fahrenheit within any one‑hour period [2]. The quality‑control plan incorporates an environmental control program that monitors evaporation rates and restricts concrete placement when evaporation exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·h unless windbreaks or fog‑misting systems are employed, thereby protecting the joint from premature drying and moisture loss in hot weather [2]. Sawing of joints is performed promptly and sealing follows immediately to avoid moisture ingress, while the base course beneath the joint is kept moist without forming puddles; a prescribed joint filler thickness around structures maintains drainage capacity while still permitting thermal movement [2].

Expansion joints in rigid pavements must be designed with specific geometric limits that control the spacing and alignment of adjacent concrete slabs to provide a smooth ride and protect users from abrupt surface changes [2]. The transverse gap between slabs is limited to a minimum of one‑quarter inch and a maximum of one‑half inch along its entire length, while longitudinal gaps may not exceed three‑quarters inch [2]. Across any joint the finished vertical differential is restricted to one‑quarter inch, with an allowance of up to one‑half inch only before the surface is ground to restore smoothness [2]. Panel dimensions are constrained so that the longer side does not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the aspect ratio stays as close to unity as practicable but never beyond 1.5, ensuring manageable slab sizes for construction and maintenance activities [2]. Saw cuts used to re‑establish joints must be at least one‑quarter inch wide, cut to a depth of no less than one‑third of the slab thickness within twelve inches of an edge and no less than one‑fourth elsewhere, and longitudinal joints are prohibited in wheel paths to avoid interference with vehicle tires [2]. The filler material placed in each joint is selected according to its function and location, with transverse joints that intersect traffic lanes requiring a premolded unit at least one inch thick, having a smooth top surface and pre‑punched dowel holes, and extending at least half an inch below the bottom of any repair whenever feasible [2]. Where a joint meets an existing transverse expansion joint, a half‑inch thick filler is used; longitudinal joints employ a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene or a full‑depth quarter‑inch polystyrene board as a bond breaker, and at structural interfaces such as manholes, bridges or culverts the filler thickness increases to a minimum of three‑quarters inch [2]. All longitudinal joint filler pieces in concrete base courses must be no shorter than eight feet and must be securely joined to maintain continuity under traffic loads [2]. Accessibility requirements dictate that non‑metallic chairs provide at least a half‑inch clearance around dowel caps, expansion caps are positioned a quarter‑inch from the dowel end, and dowel bars may not tilt or skew more than a quarter‑inch at either end nor shift more than one inch from the intended joint line, thereby preventing protrusions that could catch vehicle tires or impede pedestrians [2]. For Type B coated dowels, bond‑breaker lubricant must cover at least half of each bar and a tube offering a minimum one‑inch clearance is placed over the lubricated end to ensure safe handling and proper function [2]. If slabs are opened to traffic before dowel and bedding grout placement, a minimum of two incompressible shims must be installed in every transverse joint, and any temporary vertical drop exceeding two inches must be bridged with an asphalt wedge to maintain a level surface for users [2]. Traffic may not resume until the dowel‑and‑bedding grout attains a compressive strength of at least 2,500 psi and all joints are sealed, preventing premature loading that could cause joint failure and endanger road users [2]. Expansion joints must be cleaned and sealed with the specified rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material, and a sealant reservoir of the prescribed size—0.75 inch by 0.75 inch for pavement‑shoulder joints and shape‑factor‑controlled dimensions for transverse joints—must be provided to ensure long‑term protection against water, chlorides and incompressible material infiltration [1]. The sealing work assumes an existing functional drainage system and aims to prevent moisture ingress that could degrade structural performance and compromise user safety [1].

Verification and expected performance

After the concrete overlay and expansion joint have been placed, the inspector follows the approved field‑operation QC plan to verify that every aspect of the joint meets the contract requirements. The transverse gaps between slabs are measured along their full length to confirm they fall within a quarter‑inch to a half‑inch, while longitudinal gaps are checked not to exceed three‑quarters of an inch. The finished vertical differential across each joint is inspected and must be no greater than one‑quarter inch before the next slab is set. Premolded filler material is examined for thickness, requiring at least a half‑inch around utilities and three‑quarters of an inch adjacent to other rigid structures. The ¾‑inch expansion‑joint material specified in the drawings is confirmed as installed, and dowel bars, tie bars and bond‑breaker are verified against the approved shop drawings. Core drilling of three random locations checks dowel anchorage; acceptance requires that the total void around each dowel be less than 0.5 in. Grout in dowel slots and bedding must achieve a compressive strength of at least 2,500 psi before traffic is allowed, while cylinder testing of the pavement surface must show a minimum of 1,000 psi. Surface flatness is evaluated with a 12‑ft straightedge and any high or low spot exceeding 3/16 in is ground smooth. Saw cuts are inspected to ensure a depth of at least one‑third the slab thickness within 12 in of the edge and no less than one‑fourth elsewhere, and joint faces are cleared of curing compound and all deleterious material before sealant application. All longitudinal and transverse joints are sealed as required and pass the concrete pavement joint wipe test. Panel dimensions are checked for an aspect ratio close to 1.0 and not greater than 1.5, with panel length limited to eighteen times the overlay thickness; any cracking outside the sawed joints is recorded as a defect. Finally, the inspector confirms that no damage exists before opening the pavement to traffic, thereby demonstrating compliance, constructability, performance, and consistency with the project specifications.
