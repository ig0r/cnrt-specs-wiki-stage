Vehicle Classification

Part of: Traffic Loading is-subtopic-of
Vehicle Classification is a topic under Traffic Loading.
truck-classification-counttruck-classification-surveytruck-classification-breakdownstruck-classification-informationfhwa-13-vehicle-classification-breakdown
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Vehicle classification is the systematic counting and categorizing of trucks on a roadway to establish the proportion of each truck class, which supplies the rigorous 18‑kip Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) calculation required for pavement design models [1][2]. The derived truck distribution—based on current average daily traffic, projected growth, and FHWA 13 classification data or, when those data are missing, on 24‑hour portable or 8‑hour manual surveys—provides the daily ESALs that drive overlay thickness decisions, structural life requirements (minimum eight years, maximum twenty years), and performance criteria [1]. During construction warranty monitoring, repeated vehicle‑classification counts verify that cumulative ESALs remain within the projected values, ensuring that any exceedance triggers the remedial actions stipulated in the warranty provisions [2].

Purpose and design objectives

Vehicle classification counts supply the proportion of each truck class so that the rigorous 18‑kip ESAL calculation can be entered into pavement design models, allowing engineers to predict the cumulative loading a structure will experience and thereby avoid premature failure or unnecessary over‑design. The detailed truck distribution derived from the count also provides the daily 18‑kip ESALs that underlie every overlay design, which must achieve a minimum eight‑year structural life and may not exceed a maximum of twenty years, making accurate load data essential to ensure the pavement can sustain the expected traffic stresses. During construction warranty monitoring, vehicle classification counting is used to verify that the actual ESALs imposed on the pavement remain within the projected values incorporated in the design, because those projected values form the basis of the performance criteria and distress limits applied throughout the sixty‑month warranty period. If the counted ESALs exceed one hundred percent of the estimated twenty‑year design life ESALs, the contractor is relieved of responsibility for rutting or base‑condition distresses that may develop, underscoring the need to track loading against the original classification data. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Scope and applicability

Vehicle‑classification data are appropriate whenever a pavement design requires an ESAL calculation, which includes all functional classifications of highways [1]. For any pavement overlay design—such as an asphalt concrete (AC) overlay on AC, a fractured Portland cement concrete (PCC) slab, a PCC slab, an AC/PCC composite, or a bonded or unbonded concrete overlay—that is being undertaken to improve ride quality, address suspected structural inadequacy, or remedy low‑friction values, the truck classification count should be performed [1]. The process must use the best traffic information available, including current average daily traffic (ADT), projected growth and truck percentage, and a full truck distribution is required for all such overlays except when the roadway is a collector or local road, where the simple method may be used to calculate the 18‑kip ESALs provided that the resulting layer thicknesses do not exceed the minimum limits for that roadway type [1]. If RMS traffic count data are missing or appear outdated, a 24‑hour portable or an 8‑hour manual classification survey must be performed for the proposed improvement [1]. For cross routes that intersect limited‑access freeways, a minimum daily 18‑kip ESAL value from Table 7.3 is used unless the projected ADT exceeds 8,000; in that case the rigorous method with classification data must be employed [1]. Vehicle classification, implemented as traffic classification counts, is also used for asphalt‑concrete pavements covered by the Section 496 60‑month warranty; these counts are performed routinely during the warranty period to verify that the ESAL estimates incorporated in the contract documents remain within the projected values, and the results feed into the annual performance surveys to help determine whether remedial actions specified in the warranty are required [2].

[1] — Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

The Simple Method may be used only on collectors and local roads; it must not be applied to other functional classifications. If the pavement layer thicknesses obtained with the Simple Method would exceed the minimum layer thicknesss prescribed for the roadway type, or if the structural requirements on a rehabilitation project would exceed the existing pavement’s capacity, the design approach must shift to the Rigorous ESAL calculation that relies on detailed truck‑classification data. For cross routes that intersect limited‑access freeways, when the projected design‑year average daily traffic exceeds 8 000, the Rigorous Method must be used instead of the minimum‑design ESAL tables. In all other cases where those thresholds are not breached, the Simple Method may be preferred as a less data‑intensive alternative. When an overlay design is being prepared, a detailed truck classification count is required only if a truck distribution is needed; however, for projects on collector or local roads the guidance permits the simple method to compute the 18‑kip ESALs, making that approach preferable. If traffic data cannot be obtained from the Bureau of Planning and Research, the district may have to conduct its own counts, but the decision to use the simple method remains driven by roadway classification rather than by a lack of data. Under the Section 496 warranty, the vehicle‑classification based design is acceptable only while the Department’s traffic classification counts indicate that the estimated cumulative ESALs remain within the projected values for the 20‑year design life. If during the warranty period the cumulative ESALs exceed 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life, or if they increase enough to require a change in the mix design, the original vehicle‑classification approach is no longer sufficient and alternative design or remedial actions become preferable. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Design inputs and requirements

Vehicle classification for pavement design is governed by traffic data supplied by the Bureau of Planning and Research or another regional authority, and such data must contain the average daily truck percentage and its breakdown as recorded in the RMS Traffic Count Data Screen using the FHWA 13 Vehicle Classification scheme. When that information is missing or outdated, a truck classification survey—either a 24‑hour portable count or an 8‑hour manual count—must be performed and the manual count recorded on Form PS‑121 in accordance with Appendix B; only counts shown as “MACHINE” or “MANUAL” after 01/01/2008 are considered valid, and if no post‑2008 counts exist, the pre‑2008 classification count together with the ESAL factors given in Table 7.4 shall be used for design. For overlay design, a truck classification count shall be performed in accordance with Appendix B using the best available traffic data such as current ADT, projected growth, and truck percentage (or a distribution when the road is not a collector or local). The collected classification data must be submitted to the Bureau of Planning and Research for refinement and development of the truck distributions that feed into the design, and the resulting daily 18‑kip ESALs must be determined in accordance with Chapter 7. If the district lacks the necessary traffic information, it shall request the data from the Bureau of Planning and Research before proceeding. Vehicle classification is also governed by the pavement‑warranty provisions in Section 496.3(c) of the Flexible Pavements specifications, which require the Department to conduct routine traffic classification counts during the 60‑month warranty period to verify that Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) estimates remain within the values projected in the contract documents. These counts are performed annually together with automated distress surveys prescribed by Publication 336 and manual surveys for flushing and potholes, and any exceedance of the segment performance criteria listed in Table A triggers the remedial actions defined in the warranty. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Design methods and criteria

Vehicle classification for pavement design starts by acquiring truck‑percentage breakdowns from RMS traffic counts; if those data are unavailable, a 24‑hour portable or an 8‑hour manual classification count is conducted and recorded on Form PS‑121 following the instructions in Appendix B [1]. The resulting percentages are forwarded to the Bureau of Planning and Research where they are refined into truck distributions that feed the calculation of design daily 18‑kip ESALs in accordance with Chapter 7 [1]. These classified percentages are entered into the DARWin software using the Rigorous ESAL Calculation method, which requires specification of the performance period, two‑way ADT, number of lanes in the design direction, a lane distribution factor (for example 90 % for four‑lane highways), a directional distribution factor (default 50 % unless directed otherwise), the percent of ADT per vehicle class, annual growth rates for traffic volume and truck ESAL factors, and the selected compound growth rate calculation type [1]. For collectors and local roads the Simple Method may be applied provided that the resulting layer thicknesses do not exceed the minimum values in Table 9.4; if they would, the Rigorous method must be used with the annual truck‑factor growth rate set to 0 % and average initial truck factors taken from Table 7.2 [1]. Overlay designs that rely on this classification information are generated with DARWin according to the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures, and bonded concrete overlay thicknesses are determined using the bonded concrete overlay of asphalt mechanistic‑empirical design procedure (BCOA‑ME) [1]. In addition, the Department routinely schedules and performs traffic classification counts to verify that the ESAL estimates remain within the projected values specified in the contract documents [2].

Vehicle classification for pavement design follows the assumptions and criteria set out in Section 7.1 and Section 10.1. For two‑lane highways the percent of all trucks in the most heavily used lane is assumed to be 100 %; for four‑lane highways it is assumed to be 90 %; and for highways with six or more lanes it is assumed to be 80 %. The directional distribution factor defaults to 50 % unless the Bureau of Planning and Research directs otherwise or traffic data indicate a different value, and it is set to 100 % for one‑way ADT counts. All vehicle classes are required to use the same annual growth rate unless historical evidence or traffic studies justify a different rate, while the annual percent growth in truck factor shall be 0 % unless justified by historical evidence or loading studies. When the design‑year ADT on a cross route that interchanges with a limited‑access freeway exceeds 8 000, the Rigorous Method must be used to calculate daily 18‑kip ESALs rather than applying the minimum values in Table 7.3. The truck classification count shall be performed in accordance with the procedures set out in Appendix B, and the resulting data must be provided early enough to supply current ADT, projected traffic growth and truck percentage for overlay design. A full truck distribution is required unless the roadway is a collector or local road, and the collected counts are to be submitted to the Bureau of Planning and Research for refinement and for generating the truck distributions that feed the Chapter 7 determination of daily 18‑kip ESALs used in the design. All overlay designs that rely on this classification must meet the overall requirement of a minimum eight‑year structural design life and may not exceed a maximum of twenty years. [1]

[1] — Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177)

Materials and mixture design

To select and proportion materials and mixtures for an overlay, the design must first use adequate traffic information—including current ADT, projected growth, truck percentage, and a truck classification count performed in accordance with Appendix B—as the basis for determining structural demand. The suitability of leveling, wearing, and base‑course asphalt materials is then identified from Tables 10.1‑10.3 based on the highway’s ADT, while Table 10.4 supplies the allowable minimum and maximum layer thicknesses that must be observed; for example, when an asphalt overlay is placed over cement concrete or brick a minimum of 2 ½ inches of binder material is required and the wearing course shall have the minimum thickness indicated for the selected material. Finally, Table 10.5 provides the appropriate Superpave mixture size for the chosen pavement course thickness, ensuring that the resulting overlay meets structural, durability, constructability, and performance requirements.
