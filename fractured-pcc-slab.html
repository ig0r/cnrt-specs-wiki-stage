Fractured PCC Slab

Part of: Rehabilitation is-subtopic-of
Fractured PCC Slab is a topic under Rehabilitation.
fractured-pcc-slabfractured-concrete-pavementfractured-pccfractured-pcc-pavementfractured-pcc-slab-pavement
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Fractured PCC slab refers to a Portland‑cement concrete pavement that has lost its structural adequacy—exhibiting excessive vertical movement, reduced load‑transfer efficiency at joints and cracks, and degraded ride quality—and is therefore treated as a distinct failure mode requiring rehabilitation before an overlay can be placed. The prescribed remedy fractures the existing slab into controlled full‑depth pieces (12–24 in long, ≤30 in overall) using variable‑force equipment, seats the fragments with a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller, and then restores serviceability by designing and placing an asphalt‑concrete overlay whose thickness is derived from a future‑traffic structural number and the effective structural number of the fractured layer as determined by falling‑weight deflectometer testing and resilient‑modulus back‑calculation. Stabilization of any joint or crack showing a loaded‑corner deflection of 0.02 in or greater and a joint efficiency of at least 65 % is mandatory, typically achieved with cement, asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane grout injected under strict lift (≤0.05 in) and strength (≥700 psi 7‑day) limits to prevent premature distress of the new overlay. The overall treatment aims to improve ride quality, meet serviceability objectives, and provide a design life of eight to twenty years for the rehabilitated pavement.[1][2]

Purpose and design objectives

The fractured PCC slab condition is recognized as a failure mode that renders the concrete pavement structurally inadequate, providing insufficient structural capacity in jointed plain or reinforced slabs and leading to excessive vertical movement, loss of load‑transfer efficiency at joints and cracks, compromised continuity, reduced ride quality, and unstable support for any subsequent overlay. To address this inadequacy the design prescribes an asphalt‑concrete (AC) overlay as one of the allowable overlay types to restore serviceability for anticipated traffic on cracked or otherwise deficient slabs. The AC Overlay of Fractured PCC Slab method first computes a future‑traffic structural number, then determines the existing effective structural number by the Component Method, and finally selects an overlay thickness from Table 10.7 using flexible‑pavement average initial truck factors as ESAL inputs. Accurate determination of the resilient modulus of the fractured slab and underlying subgrade is required before placing the AC overlay, with deflection measurements taken approximately four feet from the load center to calculate Mr and thereby help prevent premature distress of the new layer. Under the slab‑stabilization provisions the performance need is to limit pavement lift and prevent further slab displacement; any joint or crack showing a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more with a joint efficiency of 65 % or higher must be stabilized, and upward movement during grout injection may not exceed 0.05 inch. Partial‑depth repair provisions aim to restore load‑carrying capacity and surface smoothness without full slab replacement, limiting repair depth to no more than one‑half of the slab thickness and specifying spot repairs for damage lengths between 15 inches and 6 feet and extended repairs for lengths greater than 6 feet. The unbonded concrete overlay requirements call for stabilization or repair of any unstable slabs or fragments that move or rock under normal traffic loads so that the overlay receives uniform support. Slabjacking methods provide a means to lift and stabilize fractured slabs using cement grout or high‑density polyurethane grout with mix proportions of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, an efflux time of 16 seconds to 26 seconds (initial injection 10 seconds to 15 seconds), thereby achieving the required lift. The stabilization material must attain a 7‑day compressive strength of at least 700 psi, an expansion between 0 % and 10 %, an initial setting time from 1 hour to 6 hours, and bleeding not exceeding 2.5 % of the volume, ensuring the slab gains the necessary structural stability. The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) states that a fractured PCC slab treated with an asphalt overlay must satisfy the functional goal of improving ride quality[1]. The same manual adds that the design shall meet serviceability objectives associated with that improved ride quality[1]. It also requires that structural objectives be achieved by confirming that the existing pavement is deemed adequate[1]. Safety requirements include addressing low‑friction conditions identified in the overlay design[1]. Durability is expressed by a required structural design life of at least eight years and not to exceed twenty years[1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications (Publication 408/2026) require that the fractured PCC slab be produced with equipment capable of applying a variable, controllable force so as to create continuous full‑depth cracks while preserving aggregate interlock, and the resulting cracks must be visible on the surface[2]. After fracturing, a minimum‑50‑ton pneumatic tire roller inflated to 100 psi must seat the slab using a rolling pattern that provides at least three passes for the Crack‑and‑Seat method or at least five passes for the Break‑and‑Seat method, and final seating is acceptable only when visual inspection shows no substrate movement[2]. Structural performance shall be verified on a one‑thousand‑foot test section by falling‑weight deflectometer testing with a 9 000‑pound drop weight at intervals of fifty feet or less, and the average elastic modulus must fall within the range specified in Table A; if it exceeds the upper limit the breaking pattern must be reduced and retested[2]. Safety and durability objectives include avoiding surface spalling, shattering, or damage to utilities and structures, prohibiting traffic after fracturing until an asphalt overlay is placed within one week, applying a tack coat, and preventing exposure of the seated slab to freezing conditions[2]. Any joint or crack that exhibits a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more and a joint efficiency of at least 65 % must be stabilized, and upward movement of the pavement may not exceed 0.05 inch as verified with vertical‑movement testing capable of detecting changes as small as 0.001 inch[2]. Work shall be restricted to daytime temperatures above 40 °F, deflection testing limited to air temperatures between 40 °F and 70 °F, and injection pressure shall not exceed 200 psi[2]. Safety rules for pressure equipment shall be observed and masks, gloves and layered clothing provided for hot‑asphalt workers[2]. After grout injection the slab may not be opened to traffic for less than a minimum of twelve hours (or as approved in the field QC plan), and any panel displacement above the 0.05‑inch tolerance must be ground back to grade[2]. When high‑density polyurethane grout is used, holes no larger than 5/8 in. in diameter shall be drilled vertically and round, the grout shall be injected at a controlled rate using equipment calibrated within the last twelve months that records material usage in pounds, and traffic may not be opened for less than a minimum of thirty minutes after grouting, thereby restoring functional load‑bearing capacity[2]. When hot asphalt grout is used, the asphalt shall be heated between 380 °F and 450 °F (any material above 500 °F is rejected) and pumped at a pressure of 25–45 psi; a splash shield of at least fifteen square feet shall be provided, open flame contact shall be prohibited, and the area shall remain closed to traffic for a minimum of thirty minutes after grouting while continuous monitoring of slab lift is performed[2]. Cement grout employed for stabilization must achieve a seven‑day compressive strength of at least 700 psi, an expansion limited to between 0 % and 10 %, an initial setting time ranging from one hour to six hours, bleed no more than 2.5 % of the volume, and flowability such that efflux time is within ten to fifteen seconds; the mix shall consist of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume with water adjusted to meet the efflux requirement and any admixtures approved per Section 711.3[2]. Repair work must preserve traffic flow while protecting users; excavated areas may not remain un‑patched for more than forty‑eight hours, saw cuts opened to traffic may not exceed a width of one‑half inch or be less than full depth, and the concrete must reach at least 1 000 psi compressive strength before it is released to traffic[2]. Serviceability is ensured by limiting surface irregularities to no more than three‑sixteenths inch when measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge and correcting any high or low spots by grinding, and by confirming that traffic protection measures such as drop‑off safeguards are in place before opening to traffic[2].

Scope and applicability

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10), the fractured‑PCC‑slab design approach is appropriate when an existing Portland cement concrete slab has cracked and the project calls for an asphalt‑concrete overlay. The manual lists this method among the seven overlay types that can be designed with DARWin in accordance with the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures, and it may be used to design an AC overlay for either a cracked and seated or rubblized Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement or Jointed Reinforced Concrete Pavement. The approach is especially called out when the original concrete thickness is less than eight inches or otherwise considered thin; in such cases the resilient modulus of the underlying slab is obtained by falling‑weight deflectometer testing at midslab points that are not cracked, using a 9,000‑lb load per ASTM D4694/D4695 and applying a correction factor of C = 0.25 to align the back‑calculated Mr with laboratory values, an approach identified as “Prior to AC Overlay of Fractured PCC”. When the asphalt overlay is placed over the fractured slab, the project is treated as a flexible pavement application and the AASHTO design procedure calls for using an overall standard deviation of 0.45. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that the fractured‑PCC‑slab treatment is applied when an existing concrete rigid pavement is to receive an asphalt overlay and it is appropriate for both unreinforced and reinforced concrete slabs. Under the “crack and seat” method the slab is broken into pieces 12 to 24 inches in size with a maximum dimension of 30 inches, and the average elastic modulus of the fractured layer must fall within the range given in Table A. The same treatment is required on concrete‑surfaced pavements where slabs or fragments move or rock visibly under normal traffic because such instability would cause non‑uniform support for an unbonded overlay; before placing the interlayer these areas must be stabilized by patching, slab stabilization, slabjacking, flexible‑base replacement, or shallow repairs using an approved asphalt or cementitious patching material. When a geotextile interlayer is specified any surface defect larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch must be filled. For full‑depth cracking and seating of existing concrete pavements the method may be used after removal of overlying asphalt, removal of joint seals, and saw cuts made to a maximum length of 20 feet across the lane; it is appropriate only when the subgrade will not liquefy if sonic vibratory equipment is used, utilities or structures are kept outside required clearances (no culverts, cross‑pipes or sleeves within 10 feet horizontally and 10 feet vertically, no work within 40 feet of bridge decks or approaches, and no work within 3 feet of fixed objects), and temperature limits for falling‑weight deflectometer testing between 45 °F and 90 °F are met. The seating operation requires a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller with tires inflated to at least 100 psi, and a test section of at least 1,000 feet must be established to verify the fracture pattern before production work. For slab stabilization the specifications allow treatment when joints or cracks have a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more and a joint efficiency of 65 % or more (or less than 65 % if approved); work may only start when daytime temperatures are at least 40 °F, the subgrade and base course are not frozen, and testing is performed only when air temperature is between 40 °F and 70 °F. Stabilization uses cement grout injected through vertical holes no larger than 1½ inches in diameter, with injection pressure limited to 200 psi and upward pavement movement limited to 0.05 inch. The grout may be cement‑based, asphalt‑based, or high‑density polyurethane; the cement grout mix must consist of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, water adjusted for an efflux time of ten to fifteen seconds, may include approved admixtures, shall achieve an expansion of 0 % to 10 %, an initial set between one and six hours, bleed no more than 2.5 % of the volume, and a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi based on five cylinders. The asphalt grout requires asphalt cement that meets ASTM D3141 with a flash point of at least 500 °F and approved cylindrical plugs, while the polyurethane option must be supplied by an approved manufacturer with certification confirming compliance. Partial‑depth repair of fractured PCC slabs is appropriate for rigid concrete pavements where delamination or cracking is evident, provided the required repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness; rectangular repair zones must be at least 15 inches long, 10 inches wide and 2 inches deep, removal depth shall not be less than 2 inches nor exceed half of the slab thickness, and the method may be applied as a spot repair for lengths between 15 inches and 6 feet (Type 1) or as an extended repair for lengths greater than 6 feet (Type 2). For jointed reinforced concrete pavement the repair may involve cutting exposed reinforcement, while for continuously reinforced concrete pavement reinforcement is left in place; transverse joints abutting the repair receive ½‑inch expansion joint material and longitudinal joints receive ¼‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene, after which sealing is performed per the relevant sections. The fractured PCC slab repair method may also be used on rigid, continuously reinforced concrete pavements that have failed and need patching; it may be employed when a fracture exists provided full‑depth transverse saw cuts are made from the pavement edge toward the longitudinal cut, no saw cuts extend into adjacent pavement or shoulders, and no cuts are placed within 24 inches of an existing crack, with partial‑depth cuts required to be at least 1 inch deep. Removal of the failed slab is performed with a wheel saw having carbide steel tips limited to a maximum penetration of ½ inch into the subbase, and concrete strips are broken out with jackhammers not exceeding 30 pounds maximum while preserving existing reinforcement; if reinforcement bars are cut, a full‑depth cut and an additional partial‑depth cut must be made, and the patch must include reinforcement spliced as shown on the standard drawings and may require placement of two layers of 4.0 mil minimum polyethylene sheeting as a new bond breaker. In section 6.2 Subgrade Soils of the Pavement Policy Manual, if isolated zones exhibit CBR values below the project average or laboratory results show a wide spread with the lowest‑bearing soil governing the design, subgrade improvement should be considered rather than relying on an overlay of fractured PCC slab [1]. Also in that manual, when application of the Heukelom and Klomp correlation would require a CBR greater than 20, such high values are prohibited and indicate that the fractured‑PCC approach could yield insufficient thickness, so an alternative design may be needed [1]. The same source notes that back‑calculated resilient modulus from FWD testing may be unreliable when accurate pavement layer information is unknown, when the pavement section changes frequently over the tested length, or when significant cracking exists; if back calculation yields a value above 15 000 psi, it must be verified by another form of testing before it can be used, suggesting that an alternative design should be pursued [1]. Projects that contain markedly different soil types and CBR values across large areas may also be divided into separate pavement sections instead of applying a single fractured‑PCC slab design [1]. In section 10.1 General of the Pavement Policy Manual, when the project requires a bonded concrete overlay—meaning the new concrete must adhere to an existing asphalt or composite surface—the standard AC overlay of fractured PCC slab is not suitable because the 1993 AASHTO design procedures do not account for bonding, and a different design procedure must be used [1]. The manual further states that if the required minimum binder thickness of 2 ½ inches for first‑time asphalt over concrete cannot be met or other minimum thickness requirements conflict with site conditions, an alternative overlay type should be selected and the District Pavement Management Engineer/Pavement Manager shall be consulted; in such circumstances a bonded concrete overlay (or another appropriate overlay) is preferred over the fractured PCC slab approach [1]. Section 522.3 of the Pennsylvania DOT specifications requires that slab‑fracturing not be performed when an existing concrete slab lies within 10 feet horizontally or within 10 feet from the bottom of a culvert, cross‑pipe or utility sleeve, within 40 feet of bridge decks, approaches or tunnels, or less than 3 feet from any fixed object such as a manhole; under those conditions an alternative design approach is preferred [2]. The same specification states that the use of sonic vibratory equipment can only be employed when fracturing will not cause soil liquefaction of the subgrade; if liquefaction risk exists, the unsuitable subgrade must be replaced or another treatment selected, and fracturing cannot proceed [2]. Section 525.3 specifies that slab‑fracturing cannot proceed when falling weight deflectometer testing temperatures are below 45 °F or exceed 90 °F, or when equipment would cause surface spalling, punching holes, or shattering of the slab; in such cases an alternative design should be used [2]. Section 525.1 requires that slab‑stabilization work not be started when daytime temperatures are below 40 °F or when the subgrade or base course is frozen, and also prohibits deflection testing if air temperature falls outside the 40 °F to 70 °F range; under those conditions another design may be preferred [2]. The specification further mandates that any observed vertical movement during pumping, uplift greater than 0.05 inch, or a determination by the Representative that continued material placement is not economically feasible precludes stabilization in favor of an alternative solution, and the appearance of radial cracks from injection holes or transverse cracks between adjacent holes triggers a stop‑work order or full slab replacement [2]. When the required repair depth exceeds half the slab thickness, partial‑depth repair is no longer suitable and the specification calls for full‑depth concrete pavement patches or corner repairs as outlined in Section 516; therefore joints, cracks or spalls needing deeper repair must be addressed with an alternative design approach [2]. If existing PCC slabs exhibit visible movement or rocking under normal traffic loads—unstable slabs or slab fragments—the overlay cannot be placed directly; such areas must be stabilized or repaired before any interlayer material is applied using methods such as concrete pavement patching, slab stabilization, slabjacking, flexible‑base replacement, or shallow repairs, and for a geotextile interlayer the joints must have faulting greater than 1/4 inch to be milled, while for an asphalt interlayer joint faulting exceeding 1/2 inch requires a scratch course or milling/grinding of the joints prior to placement, limiting the suitability of simply applying an unbonded concrete overlay on fractured PCC slabs and making alternative repair‑or‑preparation designs preferable [2]. When delamination extends through a pavement joint and cannot be treated with the detailed joint‑reestablishment procedure, or when removal of unsound concrete would have to exceed the allowed maximum of half the slab thickness (minimum depth 2 inches, maximum depth half the slab thickness), a full‑depth concrete pavement patch is preferred; likewise, if dowel bars are misaligned or show extensive deterioration, the design must shift to a full‑depth patch or corner repair rather than a partial‑depth repair [2]. Finally, if cement grout stabilization is selected for a fractured PCC slab, the mix must achieve a 7‑day compressive strength of at least 700 psi, limit expansion to between 0% and 10%, set within 1 to 6 hours, bleed no more than 2.5% of its volume, and have a water efflux time of 10 to 15 seconds; when these performance thresholds cannot be met, an asphalt grout or high‑density polyurethane grout may be selected instead, indicating that the original fractured‑PCC approach is not feasible [2].

Design inputs and requirements

The design of a fractured PCC slab must incorporate detailed site information such as the location of culverts, cross‑pipes, utility sleeves, bridge approaches, tunnels and other fixed objects, with fracture work prohibited within 10 feet horizontally and 10 feet from the bottom of the slab for utilities, within 40 feet of structures or bridge decks, and within 3 feet of any manhole or inlet. Traffic considerations require that no vehicular or construction traffic be allowed on the lane after fracturing and seating until the asphalt overlay is placed. Material inputs include removal of any existing asphalt courses, elimination of loose joint seals, saw‑cutting all slabs longer than 20 feet to a depth of plus or minus 1 inch of two‑thirds of the slab thickness, provision of Subbase No. 2A for backfill and Flexible Base Replacement for surface patching, and use of a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic tire roller with tires inflated to at least 100 psi. Environmental constraints dictate that falling weight deflectometer testing be avoided when pavement is frozen or air temperature is below 45 °F or above 90 °F, and that seating operations not be performed under wet conditions, while geotechnical input requires assessment of subgrade stability—sonic vibratory equipment may only be used when it will not cause soil liquefaction and unsuitable subgrade must be replaced. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

The design of an asphalt‑concrete overlay on a fractured Portland Cement Concrete slab is governed by the 1993 AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures, Part III, Chapter 5.5, which prescribes deflection measurements at midslab locations that are not cracked using a Falling Weight Deflectometer with a 9,000‑lb load in accordance with ASTM D4694 and D4695, the measurement being taken about four feet from the load centre [1]. The back‑calculated resilient modulus obtained from those measurements must be adjusted by a correction factor of C = 0.25 and may require seasonal adjustment [1]. When California Bearing Ratio values are employed, they shall not exceed 20 for the Heukelom and Klomp correlation, and any value greater than 10 shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer [1]. In the AASHTO pavement‑design process the overall standard deviation parameter that governs treatment of fractured PCC slabs when an asphalt overlay is applied should be 0.45 for flexible pavements—specifically asphalt overlays of fractured PCC pavement and flexible overlays over flexible pavements, while rigid pavement designs require a value of 0.35 and flexible pavement over rigid pavement uses a value of 0.40 [1]. Overlay designs for fractured PCC slabs are also governed by the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures as implemented in DARWin, where the AC overlay of fractured PCC slab is one of seven permitted overlay types; all such overlay designs shall be designed for a minimum structural design life of eight years and a maximum of twenty years based on current Average Daily Traffic, projected traffic growth, and truck‑percentage information obtained early in the design stage, and Form D‑4332 shall be completed for each resurfacing or overlay design and submitted to PDAU for concurrence and FHWA approval [1].

Fracturing of existing concrete pavements is governed by Section 522 of the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Rigid Pavements specifications, which requires submission and approval of a pavement‑fracturing plan together with a list of proposed equipment before construction begins [2]. The equipment must be capable of delivering a variable impact force while avoiding extensive surface spalling, punching holes or shattering of the concrete, and sonic vibratory equipment may be used only when fracturing will not cause soil liquefaction of the subgrade [2]. Prior to fracturing any overlying asphalt must be removed, joints are to be cut to a maximum length of 20 ft and to a depth of ±1 in (approximately two‑thirds slab depth), and clear zones of at least ten feet horizontally and vertically from culverts, cross pipes or utility sleeves, forty feet from bridge approaches or tunnels, and three feet from fixed objects such as manholes must be maintained [2]. Seating after fracturing shall be performed with a minimum‑50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller having four to seven wheels, tires inflated to at least 100 psi, and the method requires three passes for Crack‑and‑Seat or five passes for Break‑and‑Seat; vibratory rollers are prohibited [2]. Performance verification is required by falling weight deflectometer testing on a test section no shorter than 1,000 ft using equipment calibrated at an SHRP regional facility within the current year and following AASHTO T256, ASTM D4694, D4695 and D5858, with results required to fall within the elastic‑modulus range shown in Table A [2]. Section 522.1 classifies fracturing as Crack‑and‑Seat for unreinforced slabs or Break‑and‑Seat for reinforced slabs, and fractured pieces must be sized between twelve and twenty‑four inches with a maximum dimension of thirty inches while the average elastic modulus must meet the range specified in Table A [2].

Stabilization of fractured PCC slabs is addressed by Section 679, which mandates that qualified personnel with at least five years’ experience be assigned, that a crew supervisor submit three references, and that written submittals be delivered to the Representative at least ten working days before work begins; work may not commence when daytime temperatures are below 40°F or when the subgrade or base is frozen, and deflection testing in accordance with ASTM D4695 is prohibited if air temperature is below 40°F or exceeds 70°F [2]. Stabilization is required for any joint or crack showing a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more together with a joint efficiency of 65 % or greater, vertical movement during grout injection may not exceed 0.05 inch while pump pressure must not surpass 200 psi unless otherwise directed [2]. The field quality‑control plan shall detail hole spacing, depth, diameter (not larger than 1½ in), drilling methods, line‑and‑grade control, waste disposal, curing period and a traffic opening time of at least twelve hours after grouting or as specified [2].

High‑density polyurethane grout stabilization under Section 679.3(d) further requires the pump unit to be calibrated within the past twelve months with a calibrated meter recording material usage in pounds, drilling holes not larger than 5/8 in diameter, vertical and round, deep enough to penetrate any stabilized base, and it prohibits slab lift greater than 0.05 inch while requiring vertical movement testing capable of detecting lift to 0.001 inch; traffic may not be opened for less than thirty minutes after grouting unless the contractor’s QC plan or manufacturer recommendations permit a shorter interval [2]. Asphalt grout stabilization under Section 679.3(c) requires a fully insulated asphalt tanker with a removable access hatch, a minimum two‑inch overflow pipe, a reversible underseal pump with at least a 1½‑inch flexible metal hose, a pressure‑water distributor trailer of at least fifty gallons compatible with the tanker air system, and a compressor delivering a minimum of one hundred cubic feet per minute; the asphalt must be heated to between 380°F and 450°F (any temperature above 500°F is rejected) and injected at a pressure of 25 psi to 45 psi while monitoring slab lift, which may not exceed an upward movement of 0.05 inch; drilled holes are limited to a maximum diameter of 1½ in and traffic opening is prohibited for less than thirty minutes after grouting unless the QC plan or manufacturer recommendations allow earlier opening [2].

Partial‑depth repair of fractured PCC slabs is governed by Section 525, which limits repair depth to no greater than half the slab thickness and directs deeper interventions to the full‑depth patch provisions of Section 516; the minimum repair dimensions are fifteen inches long by ten inches wide by two inches deep, any partial‑depth areas spaced less than twenty‑four inches apart shall be combined, unsound concrete must be removed to at least two inches depth but not deeper than one half of the slab thickness, and perimeter saw cuts for Type 1 repairs shall be a minimum of two inches deep [2]. Removal may be performed with a chipping hammer weighing no more than thirty‑five pounds operated at a 30‑ to 60‑degree angle, final cleaning must occur within thirty minutes before placing repair material, and if compressed air is used the stream shall be free of contaminants and have a minimum pressure of one hundred psi measured at the source [2]. Joint restoration requires installing a ½‑inch expansion joint material in transverse joints or a ¼‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene in longitudinal joints extending at least ½ inch below the repair bottom [2].

Section 548.3 requires that any unstable slab or fragment be stabilized or repaired before placement of an unbonded concrete overlay, and surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be filled with an approved asphalt, cementitious or similar repair material; when a geotextile interlayer is used joints showing more than a ¼‑inch faulting must be milled, the geotextile must overlap edges by eight ± 2 in, may not exceed three layers, must extend at least four inches beyond the pavement edge and its surface temperature (or that of the pavement for an asphalt interlayer) must not exceed 120°F before overlay placement [2].

Slabjacking under Section 681 permits either cement grout or high‑density polyurethane grout; when cement grout is selected the mix design calls for one part cement by volume combined with three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, water adjusted so that the efflux time measured according to ASTM C939 falls within sixteen to twenty‑six seconds, although a more fluid mix achieving an efflux time of ten to fifteen seconds may be used for the initial injection in each hole and a stiffer mix shall be used if the former fails to raise the pavement slab [2].

Design methods and criteria

In the design of an asphalt concrete overlay on a fractured Portland Cement Concrete slab, subgrade evaluation begins with falling weight deflectometer testing at 9,000 lb in accordance with ASTM D4694 and D4695, with deflection measurements taken at midslab locations that are not cracked and about four feet from the load centre [1]. The measured deflections are back‑calculated to obtain the resilient modulus Mr using the procedure described in Chapter 5.5.5 (Step 4) of the 1993 AASHTO Guide, after which a correction factor C = 0.25 is applied (or less if a very stiff layer exists within about fifteen feet of the subgrade), and any dynamic k obtained from back‑calculation is converted to a static value by dividing by two; if the resulting Mr exceeds 15,000 psi it must be verified with another test before use [1]. The overall standard deviation required for such treatments is set at 0.45, reflecting all error and variability in design and construction inputs [1]. Design calculations are performed with the DARWin software applying the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures, and the “AC overlay of fractured PCC slab” is one of seven overlay configurations that DARWin can handle, requiring no separate mechanistic‑empirical model because the standard procedures are used [1]. The design sequence follows Section 10.2: first the structural number required for future traffic is computed, then the existing effective structural number is determined by applying the Component Method with sub‑base guidance from Section 10.2, after which the overlay structural number is calculated and an asphalt concrete thickness is selected using Table 10.7 together with the procedures in Section 10.2; flexible pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 are employed for ESAL calculations [1]. For rehabilitation of a fractured PCC slab, the contractor must first submit a pavement fracturing plan that specifies variable‑force equipment and pre‑fracture actions such as removal of overlying asphalt and sawcutting slabs to no more than twenty feet [2]. A minimum one‑thousand‑foot‑long test section is then established to determine the optimal fracturing pattern, after which wet fracturing is performed and the broken surface is seated with a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic tire roller applying three passes for crack‑and‑seat or five passes for break‑and‑seat methods [2]. Falling weight deflectometer testing is performed only when air temperature is between 45 °F and 90 °F, using a 9,000‑pound drop weight at intervals of fifty feet or less; the elastic modulus of the fractured concrete layer is back‑calculated in accordance with AASHTO T256, ASTM D4694, D4695 and D5858 by an approved software package such as MODULUS or EVERCALC, and the resulting average modulus must fall within the range given in Table A before production fracturing proceeds [2]. The slab‑stabilization procedure requires a field quality‑control plan that defines hole spacing, depth, diameter, drilling method and injection sequence; deflection testing of each joint or crack is performed according to ASTM D4695, and joints are selected for stabilization when the loaded slab corner deflection is 0.02 inch or more and the calculated joint efficiency—defined as (unloaded slab corner ÷ loaded slab corner) × 100—is at least 65 % (or less than 65 % if patching and stabilizing) [2]. Cement grout injection is carried out through vertical holes no larger than 1½ inches using a positive‑displacement pump that must not exceed a pressure of 200 psi; upward pavement movement is monitored with equipment capable of detecting lift to 0.001 inch and any movement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited, and the slab must remain closed to traffic for at least twelve hours after grouting [2]. When high‑density polyurethane grout is used, injection holes are drilled vertically, round and no larger than 5/8 inches in diameter to a depth that penetrates the underlying base; the components are mixed per the manufacturer’s specification and injected while lift is continuously measured, stopping immediately if upward movement exceeds 0.05 inch, with traffic closure for at least thirty minutes [2]. When asphalt grout is used, vertical holes no larger than 1½ inches are drilled to a depth that penetrates any stabilized base; compressed air is blown for several seconds while the drill is withdrawn, the asphalt must be heated to between 380°F and 450°F (asphalt at 500°F or more will be rejected), circulated before injection, and pumped through a tapered nozzle at a pressure of 25 psi to 45 psi with water applied around each hole to prevent seepage; lift is monitored as described for the other grout types and traffic may reopen after a minimum of thirty minutes [2]. Prior to placement of an unbonded concrete overlay, any fractured PCC slab showing visible movement or rocking under normal traffic must be stabilized or repaired by methods such as concrete pavement patching, slab stabilization, slabjacking, flexible‑base replacement, or shallow repairs with approved asphalt or cementitious materials; if a geotextile separation layer is used its edges are overlapped by eight inches ± 2 inches, limited to three layers, and the free edge extends at least four inches beyond the pavement while being kept clean, damp but not saturated [2]. Joint faulting exceeding one‑half inch requires a scratch course or milling to reduce the faulting [2]. The cement grout mix design calls for one part cement and three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume; water is added to obtain an efflux time of sixteen seconds to twenty‑six seconds (ASTM C939), although a more fluid mix with an efflux time of ten seconds to fifteen seconds may be used for the initial injection in each hole [2]. The mix must furnish an expansion of 0 %–10 % (ASTM C940), an initial setting time of one hour to six hours (AASHTO T 131), bleed loss no more than 2.5 % of the volume (ASTM C940), and achieve a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi based on the average of five test cylinders; independent laboratory testing must report one‑, three‑, and seven‑day strengths, flowability, shrinkage/expansion results, and initial set time [2]. Concrete pavement partial‑depth repair begins with an approved QC plan that details removal of unsound concrete, mixing and curing, joint sawing and sealing, and testing before traffic opening; delaminated areas are sounded using a steel bar, drag chains or hammer, rectangular repair limits are laid out extending about three inches beyond the edges with each repair at least fifteen inches long, ten inches wide and two inches deep, and areas less than twenty‑four inches apart are combined [2]. Unsound concrete is removed to a minimum depth of two inches and not more than half the slab thickness using a chipping hammer ≤35 lb at a 30‑60° angle or a milling machine with an appropriate head; edges are roughened for bonding, final cleaning must occur within thirty minutes before placing repair material, compressed air used must be oil‑free and at least one hundred psi, mixing equipment may be volumetric or hand‑batched drum/paddle mixers, and joints are treated with one‑half inch expansion joint material for transverse joints or one‑quarter inch preformed cellular polystyrene for longitudinal joints extending at least one‑half inch below the repair [2]. When repairing a fractured PCC slab by continuously reinforced concrete pavement patching, full‑depth transverse saw cuts are made from the pavement edge toward the longitudinal cut; a second transverse saw‑cut of at least one inch depth is placed at each end of the patch area parallel to and outside the first cut as shown on the Standard Drawings, and these cuts must not intersect existing cracks or be located within twenty‑four inches of an existing crack, while reinforcement bars may not be cut during the partial‑depth cuts [2]. The concrete bounded by the full‑depth cuts is removed preferably with a wheel saw equipped with carbide steel tips limiting penetration into the subbase to a maximum of one‑half inch; any remaining strips are broken out with jackhammers limited to thirty pounds maximum, and if reinforcement bars are inadvertently cut the partial‑depth cut must be made full‑depth and an additional partial‑depth cut placed as specified [2]. After removal, two layers of at least 4.0‑mil polyethylene sheeting are installed as a new bond breaker when needed, and reinforcement is placed in the patch area according to Section 1002.3 with tied or welded splices lapped as shown on the Standard Drawings, welding the splice at the patch center to accommodate expansion [2].

Structural details and interfaces

The fractured PCC slab treatment requires removal of any joint seals that become debonded and are left loose on the surface, and before fracturing the existing slabs must be saw‑cut so that no piece exceeds a maximum length of 20 feet across the lane width; for Break‑and‑Seat the saw cut shall be made to a depth of plus or minus 1 inch of two‑thirds of the slab depth. Where fracturing is not allowed, the uncracked panels are full‑depth sawcut into segments about 5 feet longitudinally by 6 feet transversely, and any reinforcing steel or steel plates that become exposed in the broken pavement or at original transverse joints shall be cut off and removed. Each joint or crack must be evaluated by deflection testing and any that exhibit a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 in or more shall be stabilized; joints with a joint efficiency of at least 65 % receive grout stabilization, while those below 65 % are also patched if approved. The field QC plan shall detail hole spacing, depth, diameter and the method for locating any reinforcement before drilling, and grout‑injection holes shall not exceed 1½ in in diameter, be drilled vertically to a depth that penetrates the stabilized base, with grouting pressure not exceeding 200 psi and upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 in prohibited. After grouting the holes are removed and patched full‑depth with rapid‑set non‑shrink concrete flush with the surface, and traffic may be reopened after a minimum of 12 hours. When an unbonded concrete overlay is placed over a fractured PCC slab, any area where the slab or fragments move or rock under normal traffic shall be stabilized or repaired before the interlayer is placed; if a geotextile separation layer is used joints showing more than 1/4‑inch faulting shall be milled, the geotextile edges shall overlap by 8 inches ± 2 inches, no more than three layers may overlay in one spot, and the free edge shall extend at least 4 inches beyond the pavement edge. If an asphalt interlayer is specified and joint faulting exceeds 1/2 inch, a scratch course or milling/grinding of the joint shall be required to reduce the faulting. For a fractured PCC slab undergoing partial‑depth repair where delamination extends through an existing joint, the area shall be repaired by maintaining the joint with two separate minimum repair areas unless the QC plan includes a detailed procedure for reestablishing the joint; any reinforcement exposed after removal of delaminated concrete in jointed reinforced concrete pavement shall be cut off and removed, while reinforcement in continuously reinforced concrete pavement shall not be cut or removed. Exposed dowel bars that show little sign of corrosion shall receive a bond‑breaking material applied to all exposed surfaces, whereas dowel bars deemed unacceptable because of misalignment or extensive deterioration shall be replaced with a full‑depth concrete patch. At an existing transverse joint a 1/2‑inch expansion joint material shall be installed extending at least 1/2 inch below the bottom of the repair whenever possible, and at an existing longitudinal joint a 1/4‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene shall be installed with the same minimum extension; if the repair material can be sawed without damage the joint may be re‑established by sawing through the full depth of the repair, and longitudinal and transverse joints shall be sealed as specified. Repairing a fractured PCC slab requires initial full‑depth transverse saw cuts from the pavement edge toward the longitudinal cut, followed by a second transverse cut at each patch end that is at least 1 inch deep and positioned according to the standard drawing for the rebar splice type; no saw cuts may be placed within 24 inches of an existing crack or across an existing crack, and any reinforcement inadvertently cut shall be corrected by converting the partial‑depth cut to a full‑depth cut and adding another minimum‑1‑inch cut as specified. Reinforcement in the patch shall follow Section 1002.3 and the standard drawings, with bars firmly supported and tied at every intersection, using either tied or welded splices that are lapped with the existing reinforcement as shown; weld splices shall comply with Section 1105.03(m) and lap‑tie bars at the patch centre to accommodate expansion. Concrete pavement patching for a fractured PCC slab must incorporate full‑depth longitudinal and transverse saw cuts that do not exceed 1/2 inch in width, with transverse cuts allowed to extend up to full depth + 2 inches provided dowel bars are not damaged; where the repair replaces an existing expansion joint a 3/4‑inch expansion joint material shall be installed in the adjacent joint and Type B coated dowel bars shall receive bond‑breaker lubricant applied at least one hour before concrete placement with a minimum 1‑inch clearance tube over the lubricated end. Reinforcement is provided by drilled holes sized no more than 1/8 inch larger than the coated dowel bars, into which dowels are anchored using an approved material that sets within five minutes and the exposed portion is rendered bondless; for one‑lane or Type A/B patches a 1/4‑inch full‑depth polystyrene board bond breaker shall be placed in the longitudinal joint while Type C patches receive tie bars instead. Load‑transfer units are installed adjacent to existing joints as specified, and all constructed longitudinal and transverse joints shall be sealed after placement. [2]

Before placing an asphalt‑concrete overlay on a fractured PCC slab, the interface is evaluated by performing Falling Weight Deflectometer testing at midslab locations that are not cracked using a 9,000 lb load in accordance with ASTM D 4694 and D 4695; the deflection measured about four feet from the load center is used to back‑calculate the subgrade resilient modulus, which then must be adjusted with a correction factor of C = 0.25 (and any seasonal adjustment) so that it matches the laboratory value employed in the flexible‑pavement design equation [1]. If the back‑calculated modulus exceeds 15,000 psi, it must be verified by another form of testing before being used in design [1]. Where the new overlay meets an existing pavement having a different total depth, a granular transition layer should be provided to create a gradual subgrade change and avoid abrupt discontinuities [1]. During fractured PCC slab construction the overlying asphalt is removed and all existing slabs are saw‑cut to a maximum length of 20 feet with a saw depth of plus or minus 1 inch of two‑thirds of slab thickness, and fracture operations must be kept at least 10 feet horizontally and 10 feet from the bottom of any culvert, cross pipe or utility sleeve, at least 40 feet from bridge or tunnel approaches, and at least 3 feet from fixed objects such as manholes; where fracturing is prohibited the uncracked panels are saw‑cut full depth into segments that measure 5 feet longitudinally by 6 feet transversely [2]. Joint or crack testing is performed only when air temperature is between 40 °F and 70 °F; any joint that exhibits a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more must be stabilized if its joint efficiency is at least 65 % or patched and stabilized if the efficiency is less than 65 %, all subject to representative approval [2]. Grout injection used to stabilize the interface to the underlying base requires vertical holes not larger than 1½ inches in diameter drilled deep enough to penetrate any stabilized base, asphalt heated to 380 °F–450 °F injected through an approved tapered nozzle at a pressure of 25 psi to 45 psi while continuously monitoring slab lift with equipment capable of detecting 0.001 inch, upward pavement movement must not exceed 0.05 inch and pressure must not exceed 200 psi; traffic may be reopened no sooner than 12 hours after grouting and the pavement may not be opened to traffic for a minimum of 30 minutes after grouting [2]. For fractured PCC slabs that will receive an unbonded concrete overlay any slab that moves or rocks under normal traffic must be stabilized or repaired before the interlayer is placed; surface defects larger than 4 sq in and deeper than 1 in are filled with an approved patching material, then the pavement is swept clean [2]. A geotextile separation layer is laid flat on the repaired surface, its edges overlapped by 8 in ± 2 in, no more than three layers overlapping, and the free edge extended at least 4 in beyond the pavement to a drainable or daylighted location; the fabric must be kept clean, damp but not saturated, and its temperature must not exceed 120 °F [2]. If an asphalt interlayer is used it is placed to a nominal depth of 1 in, joint faulting greater than ½ in requires a scratch course or milling, and surface temperature must not exceed 120 °F [2]. When a fractured PCC slab is repaired with a partial‑depth patch the interface to any existing transverse joint shall be treated by installing 1/2‑inch expansion joint material in the joint, and the interface to an existing longitudinal joint shall receive 1/4‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene; both materials should extend at least 1/2 inch below the bottom of the repair whenever possible [2]. If the repair material can be sawed without damage, the joint is re‑established by sawing through the full depth of the repair and the joints are then sealed as specified for longitudinal and transverse joints [2]. In continuously reinforced concrete pavement patching the interface to the surrounding pavement must be protected by keeping all saw cuts within the area to be removed; no cut may extend into adjacent pavement or shoulders and cuts shall not be placed within 24 inches of an existing crack, full‑depth transverse cuts are made from the outer edge toward the longitudinal cut, followed by a second transverse cut of at least 1 inch depth at each end of the patch, and any partial‑depth cuts that inadvertently sever reinforcement must be upgraded to full‑depth cuts with an additional minimum‑depth cut as specified [2]. If the original slab was on a bond breaker, two layers of ≥4.0 mil polyethylene sheeting are installed over the prepared subbase before new concrete is placed, wheel‑saw penetration into the subbase is limited to a maximum of ½ inch while any disturbed subbase deeper than 1 inch is re‑compacted or replaced; reinforcement continuity across the patch is achieved by lapping new bars with existing ones and using tied or welded splices [2]. For patches abutting an existing expansion joint a 3/4‑inch expansion joint filler is placed in the adjacent joint, while corner repairs use 1/2‑inch material; Type B coated dowel bars receive bond‑breaker lubricant on at least one‑half of their length before being anchored with a fast‑setting grout (initial set within five minutes), and a ¼‑inch polystyrene board bond breaker is installed in the longitudinal joint of Type A and B patches (no bond breaker for Type C) [2]. All longitudinal joints are saw‑cut ¼‑inch wide and 1 inch deep, sealed after placement, and the assembled interface must meet the specified tolerances before opening to traffic [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

In Section 522—Slab Fracturing of Existing Concrete Pavements, drainage is provided by installing pavement base drains and outlets before fracturing, and any damaged drainage facilities are repaired at no additional cost. Moisture control during the fracture operation includes wetting the slab to aid visual inspection, but the roller for seating operations shall not be operated under wet conditions, and the fractured surface must not be left exposed to freezing conditions; falling‑weight deflectometer testing is prohibited when the pavement surface is frozen or when air temperature falls below 45 °F or exceeds 90 °F. In Section 679—Slab Stabilization, work may not begin if daytime temperatures are below 40 °F or if the subgrade or base course material is frozen. Deflection testing is allowed only when air temperature is between 40 °F and 70 °F, and it shall not be performed during spring‑thaw conditions or when the subgrade is frozen. During grout injection upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 inch is not permitted, and traffic may not be reopened for less than 12 hours after grouting. In Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfaced Pavements, the interlayer shall be terminated in a drainable layer or at a daylighted location without impairing free drainage of the geotextile. The geotextile is to be dampened but not saturated before overlay placement, and the surface temperature of both the fabric and the pavement must not exceed 120 °F prior to overlay placement; temperature control may be achieved by misting, white‑pigmented curing compound, lime‑water whitewashing, or another method approved by the Representative. In Section 516—Concrete Pavement Patching, the construction plan must provide drainage and moisture‑control measures; if rain occurs while the patch area is open, an outlet shall be excavated through the shoulder at the lowest point of the patch and any resulting shoulder damage repaired. Cooling water, slurry, and dust from saw‑cutting operations shall not enter open traffic lanes, and the excavated patch area may not remain un‑patched for more than 48 hours or over weekends or holidays. Verification and expected performance

To verify that a completed fractured PCC slab meets project requirements, the pavement must be tested with a Falling Weight Deflectometer at midslab locations that are not cracked using a 9,000‑pound load and deflection measured about 4 feet from the load centre. The resulting back‑calculated resilient modulus is then adjusted with a correction factor of C = 0.25 (and any seasonal adjustment) to align it with the laboratory value used in the flexible pavement design equation. The dynamic k obtained from the FWD must be converted to a static k by dividing by two, while any back‑calculated Mr exceeding 15,000 psi must be confirmed by an additional test before being accepted for design. Compliance of a completed fractured PCC slab is first verified by confirming that the Representative has approved the field quality‑control plan and all required submittals, as stipulated in Section 679.3. The contractor must also submit a pavement fracturing plan together with an equipment list for approval before any fracturing operation begins, per Section 522.3. Deflection testing is performed in accordance with ASTM D4695 under air temperatures between 40 °F and 70 °F; any joint or crack that exhibits a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more and a joint efficiency of 65 % or higher must be stabilized or patched as directed, and any panel raised beyond the 0.05‑inch allowable tolerance is ground to grade. A minimum one‑thousand‑foot‑long test section is established to determine the optimal fracturing pattern, and all full‑depth cracks must be visible on the top surface of the fractured pavement. After fracturing, the slab is seated with a minimum fifty‑ton pneumatic tire roller; at least three passes are required for a Crack and Seat operation or at least five passes for a Break and Seat operation, and vertical deflection under the roller must show no substrate movement. Falling‑weight deflectometer testing is then conducted in accordance with AASHTO T256, ASTM D4694, D4695 and D5858 using a 9,000‑pound drop weight at intervals of fifty feet or less, and the average elastic modulus of the fractured concrete layer must fall within the limits shown in Table A; any exceedance requires adjustment of the breaking pattern, retesting, and approval before production can resume. When high‑density polyurethane grout is used, the pump unit must be certified as calibrated within the last twelve months and its display must record the pounds injected; vertical movement testing equipment capable of detecting lift to 0.001 inch is employed while the grout is placed, and slab lift may not exceed 0.05 inch, with pumping stopped immediately if upward movement is observed. For asphalt grout stabilization, injection temperature must be between 380 °F and 450 °F and pressure maintained between 25 psi and 45 psi; vertical movement testing equipment detecting lift to 0.001 inch confirms that upward movement does not exceed 0.05 inch. All drilled injection holes are required to be vertical, round, and no larger than 5/8 inch in diameter, ensuring constructability compliance with the specified pattern. Prior to acceptance, the surface is measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge and any high points or depressions of 3/16 inch or more are ground out; dowel bar anchorage is inspected by core drilling and accepted only when peripheral voids are less than 0.50 inch per core. The slab may be opened to traffic only after compressive‑strength testing of cylinders shows at least 1,000 psi as required, and traffic is prohibited until the surface has been swept clean, a tack coat applied, and an asphalt overlay placed within one week while avoiding freezing conditions. The slab may not be opened to traffic for a minimum of twelve hours after grouting operations have been completed unless a later retest shows acceptable performance. When high‑density polyurethane grout is used, traffic may not be allowed onto the slab until at least thirty minutes have elapsed after grouting or as permitted by the contractor’s approved field QC plan and the manufacturer’s recommendations. For asphalt grout stabilization, traffic may not be reopened to the slab until at least thirty minutes have passed after the injection operation. [1][2]

For an AC overlay applied to a fractured PCC slab, the performance expectation is defined by a structural design life that shall be at least eight years and shall not exceed twenty years; compliance with this lifespan criterion serves as the measure of acceptable performance for the treatment. For fractured PCC slabs constructed under Section 522, the expected performance includes continuous full‑depth cracking with aggregate interlock and visible surface cracks, and the slab must be seated so that vertical deflection observed under the roller indicates no substrate movement. Structural performance for those slabs is quantified by an average elastic modulus that must fall within the range set in Table A, a condition verified on a minimum one‑thousand‑foot test section. For slabs that are subsequently stabilized under Section 679, the expected performance is to limit upward movement to no more than 0.05 inch and to keep loaded‑slab‑corner deflection at less than 0.02 inch while achieving a joint efficiency of at least 65 % for slabs approved for stabilization (or lower if patched).
