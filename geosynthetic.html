Geosynthetic

Part of: Pavement Structure is-subtopic-of
Geosynthetic is a topic under Pavement Structure.
geosyntheticgeosynthetics
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Geosynthetics are synthetic polymer‑based sheet or grid products—such as geotextiles and geogrids—that are incorporated into pavement structures to enhance overall performance[1][2]. They provide physical separation of subgrade and base layers, drainage, erosion and sediment control, and reinforcement of weak soils by means of a permeable fabric network (geotextiles) or mechanical interlock of net‑like apertures (geogrids)[1][2]. Their behavior is governed by class‑type designations that define composition, aperture geometry, tensile strength and installation tolerances, and they must be protected from temperatures above 140 °F, direct sunlight and physical damage to preserve durability[1][2].

Material purpose and applicability

Geosynthetics are synthetic materials—such as geotextiles, geogrids, or other sheet or grid products—that are incorporated into pavement structures to enhance performance. [1][2] In PennDOT projects they are employed primarily for drainage, layer separation, erosion control, sediment control, and to reinforce soft subgrade soil or aggregate layers, thereby adding structure to the pavement. [1] Geotextiles are permeable polymer fabrics that may be used to separate subgrade and subbase layers; a Class 4 Type A fabric separates the two over the full lane width (8 ft or greater), while a Class 4 Type C fabric provides both separation and lateral restraint, reducing load stresses on subgrades whose CBR value is less than three. [1] Geogrids may be used at the subgrade‑aggregate interface; because their apertures interlock with surrounding unbound material they function primarily as reinforcement that stabilizes a weak subgrade. [1] In a Geosynthetic Stabilized Bridge Approach (Section 224), a Class 4 Type A separation geosynthetic is placed over the prepared subgrade and excavated layback slope to isolate the subgrade from backfill, and all geosynthetics must be positioned within ±1/2 inch of plan elevation. [2] A stabilization geosynthetic such as a Class 2 Type A biaxial geogrid provides reinforcement of the fill layers in that bridge approach. [2] In the context of a prefabricated pavement base drain (Section 610), a geosynthetic is a synthetic non‑woven, needle‑punched fabric made from virgin resin that is wrapped around the inert plastic core, and its function is to filter water entering the drain so that soil particles are excluded while drainage flow is maintained. [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 62–63), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 202–204), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 518–521)

Geogrids may be used at the interface between subgrade and aggregate layers to stabilize weak pavement subgrades, while Class 4—Stabilization (Type C) geotextiles are specified for subgrades with a CBR value less than three to provide separation and lateral reinforcement; Class 4—Layer Separation (Type A) geotextile is required as a separation layer in all treatments. When the subgrade CBR exceeds three or when alternative measures such as removal of frost‑susceptible soils, stronger base courses, or conventional subgrade improvement are feasible, another material rather than a geosynthetic may be selected. The source does not address traffic conditions, climate zones, broader soil classifications beyond CBR, or exposure conditions for geosynthetic use. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Composition and characteristics

Geosynthetics such as geogrids and geotextiles are synthetic polymer‑based materials engineered for pavement applications, with the polymeric composition defining their fundamental nature. A geogrid is a net‑like geosynthetic whose apertures are sized to interlock with surrounding unbound soils, rock, or aggregate, providing primary reinforcement at the subgrade‑aggregate interface; its structure consists of a regular network of integrally connected longitudinal and transverse polymer tensile elements forming the aperture geometry. Geotextiles are permeable fabrics consisting of long‑chain polymeric filaments or yarns formed into a stable network so that the filaments retain their relative position to each other, allowing them to separate subgrade and subbase layers. In the geosynthetic‑stabilized bridge approach (GSBA) the constituent material consists of synthetic sheet or grid products classified by class and type, for example a Class 4 Type A separation geotextile, a Class 2 Type A biaxial geogrid, and a Class 4 Type C geotextile. These class and type designations prescribe both composition and structural form; the material must be free of tears, punctures, flaws, deterioration, or damage arising from manufacture, transport, storage, or installation. The geosynthetic must be protected from temperatures greater than 140 °F, direct sunlight and debris; storage is required elevated off the ground, placement flat and pulled tight, anchored within ±½‑inch of plan elevation, with adjacent rolls overlapped a minimum of 12 inches and no splicing permitted, while any repair patch must use the same material and extend at least 3 feet beyond the damaged area. Each roll supplied for stabilization is required to be clearly identified by the manufacturer with product model designation, manufacturing plant, and lot number, and must meet one of the prescribed material categories: a Class 2 Type A geogrid as defined in Section 738.3 or a Class 4 geotextile of Type A or Type C as defined in Section 735; these categories define the polymer composition and fabric or grid architecture. Class 1 uni‑axial geogrids, a subset of the geogrid family, consist of a regular network of integrally connected longitudinal and transverse polymer tensile elements whose apertures are sized to permit mechanical interlock with surrounding material; their performance is characterized by an ultimate tensile strength expressed as a minimum average roll value, a creep‑reduction factor based on the ratio of ultimate to creep‑limiting strength, an installation‑damage reduction factor that must never be less than 1.3, and a durability reduction factor, together limiting the allowable long‑term tensile strength while restricting strain to 5 percent for a 100‑year design life as required by Section 738.2. For bridge‑approach slabs the specified geosynthetic is polyethylene sheeting used as a bond breaker; it must be installed in two layers, each having a minimum thickness of 4.0 mil, providing a smooth surface without excessive depth, with seams placed longitudinally and overlapped at least 12 inches, and manufacturer certification confirming compliance. In prefabricated pavement‑base drains the geosynthetic comprises an inert plastic core that must resist common chemicals and meet defined physical thresholds: a minimum tensile strength of 1,200 psi, water absorption not greater than 0.01 % after 24 hours, no fungal growth, crush strength of at least 40 psi, and a flow capacity of at least 15 gallons per minute per foot of width; the core must provide open areas of at least 6 % on the pavement‑side face and 3 % on the shoulder side face. The inert plastic core is completely wrapped by a non‑woven, needle‑punched geotextile fabric made from virgin resin and conforming to Table A, which supplies filtration. [1][2]

Key material properties

The performance of geosynthetic materials in pavement applications is governed by a set of physical, mechanical, chemical and thermal characteristics that must be satisfied under the conditions specified for each product type. For bridge‑approach stabilization, the material must be protected from temperatures greater than 140 °F, from direct exposure to sunlight, and from debris that may cause tears or punctures; it also must be stored elevated from the ground surface and any geosynthetic showing defects, tears, punctures, flaws, deterioration, or damage incurred during manufacture, transport, storage, or installation must be rejected. Uni‑axial geogrids classified as Class 1 are required to consist of a regular network of integrally connected longitudinal and transverse polymer tensile elements whose aperture geometry permits mechanical interlock with the surrounding material; their performance hinges on an ultimate tensile strength expressed as a minimum average roll value that is reduced by creep, installation‑damage and durability factors to yield an allowable long‑term tensile strength, the installation‑damage factor must never be less than 1.3, and the combined reduction factor must keep geogrid strain within a maximum of 5 percent for a 100‑year design life. Polyethylene sheeting used as a bond breaker beneath bridge approach slabs must be provided in two layers with a minimum thickness of 4.0 mil, present a surface that is smooth without excessive depth, and any seams must be installed in the longitudinal direction of the bridge and overlapped by at least 12 inches. The inert plastic core of a prefabricated pavement base drain must exhibit high mechanical strength and chemical stability, being required to have a tensile strength of at least 1,200 psi, a crush strength of no less than 40 psi, water absorption limited to 0.01 % after 24 hours, and no fungal growth; the surrounding geotextile fabric is specified as non‑woven, needle‑punched material made from virgin resin to ensure consistent filtration performance. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 202–204), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 387), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 518–521), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 720–722)

In determining geosynthetic design parameters, the Pavement Policy Manual requires a project‑by‑project approach that consults Publication 408 specifications, FHWA’s Geosynthetic Design and Construction Guidelines, NTPEP data, the manufacturer’s published product data, and any other appropriate technical references. The same manual classifies geosynthetics by product type and functional class, describing geogrids as net‑like materials whose apertures interlock with surrounding soil or aggregate and defining their characteristics through Publication 408 Sections 221 and 738 together with Publication 35 and manufacturer data, while geotextiles are identified as permeable fabrics divided into Class 4—Layer Separation (Type A) for separating dissimilar layers and Class 4—Stabilization (Type C) when both separation and lateral restraint are required. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications further classify geosynthetics by a class‑type designation that defines function, specifying that bridge‑approach separations must use a Class 4 Type A material and that stabilization may be provided with either a Class 2 Type A biaxial geogrid or a Class 4 Type C geotextile, with acceptance samples sized and packaged according to Publication 2 Part B Section 6. For Class 1 uni‑axial geogrids the manual prescribes measurement of ultimate tensile strength (TU) from a twelve‑month quality‑control data set, calculating the minimum average roll value by subtracting two standard deviations and conducting the test in accordance with ASTM D6637 Method B. The same specification derives the creep reduction factor (RFCR) as the ratio of ultimate strength to creep‑limiting strength using AASHTO R 69, ASTM D5262 and ASTM D6992, obtains the installation damage reduction factor (RFID) from Table B unless a higher product‑specific value is justified but never allowing it to be less than 1.3, and adopts durability reduction factor (RFD) values from the minimums in Table B. The allowable long‑term tensile strength (TL) is then calculated by dividing TU by the combined reduction factor (RFCR × RFID × RFD), and a maximum allowable strain of 5 percent is prescribed for a 100‑year design life. For prefabricated pavement base drains, performance verification requires laboratory tests that follow specific ASTM methods, with tensile strength required to reach at least 1,200 psi as measured by ASTM D638, water absorption limited to 0.01 % after 24 hours per ASTM D570, fungal resistance confirmed by ASTM G21 showing no growth, crush strength a minimum of 40 psi according to *GRI‑GC4, and flow capacity at least 15 gpm per foot of width as determined by ASTM D4716; the geotextile wrapper is classified as a non‑woven, needle‑punched fabric made from virgin resin unless otherwise approved, and each roll must carry certification identifying its type, thickness, height, length and weight. [1][2]

Behavior and mechanisms

In the bridge‑approach stabilization (Section 224), geosynthetic materials must be protected from temperatures greater than 140 °F, direct sunlight and debris, and any sheet exhibiting tears, punctures, flaws, deterioration or other damage incurred during manufacture, transport, storage or installation shall be rejected and replaced. For Class 1 uni‑axial geogrids (Section 738.2), the response to loading is governed by the ultimate tensile strength (TU) taken as a minimum average roll value reduced by two standard deviations, with the allowable long‑term tensile strength (TL) obtained by dividing TU by the combined reduction factor (RFOV = RFCR × RFID × RFD); the creep reduction factor must meet at least the value specified in Table B, installation damage is accounted for with an installation‑damage reduction factor that cannot be less than 1.3, durability over service life is represented by a durability reduction factor taken from Table B unless higher project‑specific values are justified, and strain is limited to 5 percent for the 100‑year design life when the combined reduction factor is applied. In the prefabricated pavement base drain (Section 610.2), the inert plastic core is described as resistant to commonly encountered chemicals and substances, must achieve a minimum tensile strength of 1,200 pounds per square inch, a water absorption of 0.01% after 24 hours, fungus resistance with no growth, and a crush strength of at least 40 pounds per square inch, thereby allowing it to sustain mechanical loads without significant deformation; the surrounding non‑woven needle‑punched geotextile is required to remain attached and not deform under continuous loading so that flow capacity is maintained. [2]

Geosynthetic behavior in pavement design is governed by mechanical interlock and physical separation mechanisms (Pavement Policy Manual, section 6.2) [1]. Geogrids function primarily as reinforcement because their net‑like apertures are sized to interlock with surrounding unbound soils, rock or aggregate, thereby stabilizing weak subgrades [1], while geotextiles may be used to separate subgrade and subbase layers; they consist of long‑chain polymeric filaments formed into a stable network that retains filament positions while permitting water flow [1]. In the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications for geosynthetic stabilized bridge approaches (Section 224), the behavior is governed primarily by physical exposure limits—protection from temperatures greater than 140 °F, direct sunlight and debris—and mechanical handling requirements such as placement flat, pulled tight and anchored until backfill is placed, prohibition of vehicle traffic until a minimum of 6 inches of loose backfill has been placed, overlaps of at least twelve inches, and complete removal and replacement of any damaged area larger than three square feet [2]. For Class 1 uni‑axial geogrids (Section 738), the physical network of integrally connected longitudinal and transverse polymer tensile elements whose aperture geometry is sufficient to permit mechanical interlock with the surrounding material controls performance; mechanical performance is further controlled through ultimate tensile strength, a creep reduction factor derived from the ratio of ultimate to creep‑limiting strength, an installation damage reduction factor (TU/Tdam) that shall never be less than 1.3, durability reduction, and a combined reduction factor used to compute allowable long‑term tensile strength, with the maximum allowable strain limited to 5 percent for a 100‑year design life [2]. In the specifications for pipe underdrain and pavement base drain geosynthetics (Section 610), physical attributes such as a minimum tensile strength of 1,200 psi (ASTM D638) and a crush strength not less than 40 psi (GRI‑GC4), together with a required flow capacity of 15 gallons per minute per foot of width and defined open‑core percentages, govern behavior; chemically, the material must be inert to commonly encountered substances, absorb no more than 0.01 % water after 24 hours (ASTM D570) and exhibit no fungal growth (ASTM G21); mechanically, the geosynthetic and its surrounding geotextile must retain integrity when flattened to prescribed limits and sustain stiffness under specified deflection criteria so that filtration and drainage functions remain effective [2].

Durability and deterioration

In SECTION 224—GEOSYNTHETIC STABILIZED BRIDGE APPROACH, geosynthetics must be shielded from temperatures greater than 140 °F, direct sunlight, and debris because exposure to heat or UV and contact with sharp objects can initiate tearing, puncturing, or other deterioration during manufacture, transport, storage, or installation. The specification requires that any geosynthetic showing defects, tears, punctures, flaws, deterioration, or damage be rejected and replaced; damaged areas smaller than 3 square feet must be patched with the same material extending at least 3 feet beyond the defect on all sides, while larger damaged zones must be removed and replaced. Vehicle traffic is prohibited on exposed geosynthetic until a minimum of 6 inches of loose backfill covers it, preventing wrinkling or distortion that could compromise performance. In SECTION 738—GEOGRIDS for Class 1 uni‑axial geogrids, the long‑term behavior is governed by three principal deterioration mechanisms: time‑dependent creep, damage that can occur during placement, and overall loss of material durability over the service period. Each mechanism is represented by a specific reduction factor—RFCR for creep, RFID for installation damage, and RFD for durability—and these factors are applied to the ultimate tensile strength to obtain the allowable long‑term tensile strength. In SECTION 610—PIPE UNDERDRAIN AND PAVEMENT BASE DRAIN, durability of the geosynthetic component depends on maintaining its tensile strength of at least 1,200 psi, crush strength of at least 40 psi, and limiting water absorption to 0.01% after 24 hours while ensuring no fungal growth; the core material must also resist commonly encountered chemicals. The geotextile must be a virgin‑resin, non‑woven needle‑punched fabric that provides at least 6% open core area on the pavement side and 3% on the shoulder side, and allows unobstructed inflow through at least 50% of its area on the pavement face and 12% on the shoulder face. Seams should be made with a non water‑soluble adhesive and spot‑bonded so that under continuous loading the geotextile does not deform enough to reduce flow capacity below the specified minimum rate, and improper handling that stretches or damages the pipe or geosynthetic can also compromise performance. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 202–204), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 518–521), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 720–722)

In SECTION 224—GEOSYNTHETIC STABILIZED BRIDGE APPROACH, geosynthetic durability is compromised when the material is exposed to temperatures greater than 140 °F, direct sunlight, or contact with debris that can damage it; these conditions increase susceptibility to deterioration. Any pre‑existing defects such as tears, punctures, flaws, or damage incurred during manufacture, transportation, storage, or installation are to be rejected because they also raise the risk of failure. Storing geosynthetics elevated off the ground and shielding them from heat, UV light, and debris therefore reduces the likelihood of deterioration. In SECTION 610—PIPE UNDERDRAIN AND PAVEMENT BASE DRAIN, the susceptibility of the geosynthetic components is reduced when the core material is an inert plastic that resists commonly encountered chemicals and possesses a minimum tensile strength of 1,200 pounds per square inch, water absorption not exceeding 0.01 % after 24 hours, fungus resistance with no growth, and a crush strength of at least 40 pounds per square inch. The surrounding geotextile must be non‑woven, needle‑punched and made from virgin resin unless approved, and seams should be bonded with a non‑water‑soluble adhesive to prevent degradation under loading. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 202–204), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 518–521)

Material interactions and compatibility

Geogrids are installed at the subgrade‑aggregate interface where their net‑like apertures interlock with surrounding soil, rock, and aggregate, thereby reinforcing weak pavement subgrades; this mechanical interlocking allows load transfer and improves stability without altering material chemistry. [1] Geotextiles function as permeable fabrics that separate the subgrade from the subbase, preserving each layer’s integrity, and when specified as a Class 4 Type C stabilization fabric they also provide lateral restraint that reduces load stresses on the subgrade, effectively combining separation with reinforcement. [1] In Geosynthetic Stabilized Bridge Approach construction, the geosynthetic is laid flat over a prepared and proof‑rolled subgrade and serves as a separation layer between the subgrade and the backfill aggregate. [2] The geosynthetic must be positioned within ±½ inch of the plan elevation, overlapped on parallel edges by at least 12 inches, and protected from temperatures exceeding 140 °F, sunlight, and debris. [2] Aggregate is required to be placed in a non‑destructive manner—by hand placement, light rain from an excavator bucket, or gradual blading—so that it does not puncture the exposed sheet. [2] Vehicles are prohibited on the geosynthetic until at least 6 inches of loose backfill have been placed, after which successive 6‑inch compacted lifts of coarse aggregate are built up over it to keep the material undistorted. [2] For a prefabricated pavement base drain, the geosynthetic is a non‑woven needle‑punched geotextile that completely wraps an inert plastic core to act as a filter and must be bonded to the core’s protrusions and spot‑bonded on the shoulder face so that loading does not deform the fabric and reduce the specified flow capacity. [2] The design calls for at least 6 % open area on the pavement side of the core and at least 3 % on the shoulder side, while the geotextile must allow unobstructed inflow through a minimum of 50 % of its area on the pavement side and 12 % on the shoulder side, ensuring that fine aggregate backfill (Type A sand) can surround the drain without clogging it. [2] The resin of the geotextile is required to be virgin unless otherwise approved, guaranteeing chemical resistance and compatibility with the surrounding materials. [2]

The GSBA specifications require that the foundation be prepared free of deleterious or unsuitable soils because such materials can undermine the performance of the separation geosynthetic placed on the subgrade. Geosynthetics must also be protected from temperatures greater than 140 °F, direct sunlight and debris, and any material showing defects, tears, punctures, flaws, deterioration, or damage incurred during manufacture, transportation, storage or installation must be rejected and replaced. During construction the exposed geosynthetic may not be subjected to direct dumping of aggregate from a haul truck; aggregates must be placed in a non‑destructive manner to avoid puncturing or wrinkling the membrane, and vehicles are prohibited until at least six inches of loose backfill cover the fabric. The document does not address compatibility issues specific to concrete (sulfates, organics, clays, reactive aggregates, corrosion, shrinkage restraint, chemical attack) or asphalt (moisture susceptibility, binder modifiers, RAP/RAS stiffness, fuel/solvent contamination, temperature susceptibility). Influence of production and placement

Geosynthetic performance on a bridge approach depends heavily on how the material is stored, handled, and placed: temperatures must be kept below 140°F and direct sunlight avoided to prevent thermal degradation, while storage off the ground protects against heat and moisture; any geosynthetic showing tears, punctures, or other damage from manufacture, transport, storage, or installation must be rejected because such defects compromise reinforcement and separation functions; during placement the sheet must lie flat, be pulled tight and anchored before backfill, and aggregate must be introduced gently—hand‑placed, lightly rained from an excavator bucket, or bladed out gradually—rather than dumped directly from a haul truck to avoid puncturing or tearing the membrane; compaction is performed in two 6‑inch lifts of coarse aggregate for each layer, creating a stepped fill and preserving the geosynthetic’s integrity, and no vehicles may travel on the geosynthetic until at least 6 inches of loose backfill covers it. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 202–204)

The long‑term performance of a geosynthetic is most compromised when construction introduces physical damage or improper handling. In bridge‑approach applications (Section 224) any defect, tear, puncture, flaw, deterioration, or other damage incurred during manufacture, transportation, storage, or installation must be rejected and replaced; splicing of the sheet is prohibited, placement outside the ±1/2‑inch elevation tolerance, dumping aggregate directly onto an exposed sheet, or permitting vehicle traffic before a minimum 6 inches of backfill can create wrinkles or punctures that affect durability. Damaged areas less than 3 sq ft may be patched, whereas damaged areas greater than 3 sq ft must be completely removed and replaced. For Class 1 uni‑axial geogrids (Section 738) the installation damage reduction factor (RFID), which reflects puncture or tearing introduced during placement, must never be less than 1.3 and is taken from Table B unless project‑specific testing indicates a higher value, especially when surrounding material exceeds a maximum particle size of 1.5 inches; the durability reduction factor (RFD) also defaults to the minimum in Table B unless a higher product‑specific value is justified. In bridge‑approach slab construction (Section 505) the polyethylene bond‑breaker sheet must lie on a smooth surface, must not be built up beyond the required thickness, and seams must run in the longitudinal direction with an overlap of at least 12 inches; otherwise gaps or misalignment can allow water infiltration and loss of separation function, and the material must meet the manufacturer’s certification for a minimum 4.0‑mil thickness. For pipe underdrain and pavement‑base drain systems (Section 610) perforations must be cut cleanly so as not to restrict flow, circular or slotted perforations may be provided but slotted perforations shall not be used in smooth‑walled pipe; pipes sized 4 inches through 10 inches must have a stiffness of at least 30 pounds per square inch at 10 % deflection and must not be stretched or damaged during handling. In prefabricated base drains the geotextile must be wrapped completely around the drainage core with the seam along the length of the drain, seams must be securely bonded with a non‑water‑soluble adhesive, and attachment to the core protrusions must be sufficient; otherwise deformation under load can diminish the required open‑core percentages and flow capacity. Selection, specification, and improvement

In PennDOT experimental pavement construction, the first criterion for selecting a geosynthetic is the specific function it is intended to perform—geotextiles may be specified only for drainage, layer separation, erosion control or sediment control, whereas geogrids are limited to reinforcement of soft subgrade soil or aggregate layers and any other use is treated as experimental. The designer must establish design parameters on a project‑by‑project basis in accordance with Publication 408 specifications and shall consult FHWA’s Geosynthetic Design and Construction Guidelines, the National Transportation Product Evaluation Program, the manufacturer’s published data, and other relevant technical references to define properties, design specs, product selection and construction procedures. For subgrade soil applications the selection is again driven first by the required function—whether reinforcement, separation, or both—and second by measured subgrade conditions such as a CBR value less than three that indicate weak support. When a separation fabric is required, the specification calls for a Class 4‑Layer Separation (Type A) geotextile to be placed on all completed subgrades that span an entire lane width of eight feet or greater, with special provisions when the widening is less than eight feet or when patching exceeds twenty feet in length. For dual‑purpose fabrics that must both separate and provide lateral restraint, a Class 4‑Stabilization (Type C) geotextile is specified on subgrades having a CBR value less than three or where sufficient depth of undercutting cannot be achieved. Geogrids may be used at the interface between subgrade and aggregate layers to stabilize weak pavement subgrades, provided that the net‑like geosynthetic has apertures of sufficient size to interlock with surrounding soil, rock or aggregate, and their design must follow Publication 408 sections 221 and 738 together with Publication 35 and the manufacturer’s product data. Within the Geosynthetic Stabilized Bridge Approach specifications, a geosynthetic must be identified by its class and type that correspond to the intended function; for example a Class 4 Type A separation geotextile is required for subgrade coverage and a Class 2 Type A biaxial geogrid is required when the geosynthetic serves as the stabilization layer together with a backing Class 4 Type A geotextile. Selection is limited to products that satisfy one of the prescribed material classes—either a geogrid classified as Class 2 Type A according to Section 738.3 or a geotextile classified as Class 4 Type C in accordance with Section 735—and each roll must be supplied in the lengths called for and clearly identified by manufacturer, product model designation, plant and lot number. For uni‑axial geogrids designated as Class 1, the material must provide a regular network of integrally connected longitudinal and transverse polymer tensile elements whose aperture geometry is sufficient to permit mechanical interlock with the surrounding material, and its ultimate tensile strength must meet the calculated TU defined as the minimum average roll value obtained by subtracting two standard deviations from the twelve‑month average of manufacturer quality‑control tests. The creep, installation damage and durability reduction factors (RFCR, RFID, RFD) must satisfy the minimum values in Table B, with RFID never being less than 1.3, and an allowable long‑term tensile strength is obtained by dividing TU by the combined reduction factor RFOV (= RFCR × RFID × RFD); the geogrid must not be strained more than five percent for a one‑hundred‑year design life when this combined factor is applied. All geosynthetic materials must be protected from temperatures greater than 140 °F, from direct exposure to sunlight, and from debris that may damage the material; any damaged material must be rejected and replaced, and electronic submission of manufacturer material information—including identification samples and all data necessary to demonstrate compliance with the specified requirements—shall be provided. PennDOT requires that geosynthetic design parameters be established on a project‑by‑project basis using Publication 408, FHWA’s Geosynthetic Design and Construction Guidelines, the National Transportation Product Evaluation Program, manufacturer product data, and any other appropriate technical references; this systematic selection and construction procedure serves as the primary quality‑control measure. The agency limits geotextile applications to drainage, layer separation, erosion control and sediment control and limits geogrid applications to reinforcement of soft subgrade soil and aggregate layers, treating any other reinforcement use as experimental; confining uses to these proven functions or following the detailed design‑parameter process for new uses can improve performance and mitigate potential weaknesses. For pavement treatments a Class 4 Type A geotextile must be installed as a separation layer in all treatments, while a Class 4 Type C geotextile is recommended on subgrades whose CBR value is less than three to provide both separation and lateral restraint. Geogrids may be placed at the subgrade‑aggregate interface to reinforce weak subgrades, and when low CBR values are encountered subgrade strengthening such as cement or lime stabilization or rock capping modeled with a CBR of 20 can be used as alternative measures that reduce reliance on geosynthetics and improve overall pavement stability. All material selections and design parameters shall follow Publication 408 specifications (sections 221, 738, 210.3(b), 212, 735) and be verified against manufacturer product data before installation. For bridge‑approach applications the contract requires that every roll be clearly identified by the manufacturer with its model designation, plant of manufacture and lot number, providing traceability throughout handling, placement and repair. Materials must be stored elevated and protected from temperatures greater than 140 °F, direct sunlight and debris; any sheet exhibiting defects, tears, punctures, flaws, deterioration or damage incurred during manufacture, transportation, storage or installation shall be rejected and replaced at no cost. When a Class 2 Type A biaxial geogrid is used as the stabilization geosynthetic it must be backed by a Class 4 Type A geotextile that extends at least four feet at the top and two feet at the bottom of the face wrap, whereas a Class 4 Type C geotextile may be employed without supplemental wrap. Overlap of adjacent rolls shall be at least twelve inches and splicing is prohibited to preserve continuity; localized damage smaller than three square feet may be repaired with a patch of the same material extending a minimum of three feet beyond the damaged area on all sides, while larger damaged areas must be removed and replaced. For projects that specify geogrids, tightening manufacturer testing so that the ultimate tensile strength is derived from the average of the last twelve months and reduced by two standard deviations yields a reliable MARV, and products whose creep‑reduction factor, installation‑damage reduction factor and durability‑reduction factor exceed the minimum values in Table B should be selected. The installation‑damage reduction factor must never be less than 1.3, and the combined reduction factors are applied to compute an allowable tensile strength that does not surpass the required design strength while keeping strain below the five percent limit for a one‑hundred‑year design life. When geosynthetic performance beneath bridge‑approach slabs is required, two layers of polyethylene sheeting at least 4.0 mil thick shall be installed on a smooth surface with seams running in the longitudinal direction and overlapped by a minimum of twelve inches; certification from the manufacturer confirming compliance with these material requirements must be provided, and the use of a geosynthetic‑stabilized subbase further stabilizes the subbase layer. The prefabricated pavement base drain may be enhanced by specifying an inert plastic core that meets a minimum tensile strength of 1,200 psi (ASTM D638), water absorption not greater than 0.01 % after twenty‑four hours (ASTM D570), crush strength at least 40 psi (*GRI‑GC4) and a flow rate of at least fifteen gallons per minute per foot of width (ASTM D4716); open‑core areas shall be no less than six percent on the pavement side face and three percent on the shoulder side face, and the surrounding geotextile shall be non‑woven, needle‑punched, made from virgin resin, wrapped completely around the core with a seam sealed by a non‑water‑soluble adhesive and spot‑bonded to protrusions. Each roll must be certified with lot number, manufacturer information, product type, thickness, height, length and weight; alternative pipe materials such as corrugated polyethylene, PVC or ABS may be used provided they satisfy the stiffness requirements, for example thirty psi at ten percent deflection for four‑ to ten‑inch PE pipe. Performance evaluation and implications

The long‑term performance of Class 1 uni‑axial geogrids is established through a sequence of laboratory investigations and the application of reduction factors that together constitute durability evidence. The ultimate tensile strength is obtained from twelve months of manufacturer quality‑control data and confirmed by testing in accordance with ASTM D6637, Method B. Creep behaviour is quantified by determining a creep reduction factor using AASHTO R 69 together with ASTM D5262 and ASTM D6992, while installation damage and material durability are represented by additional reduction factors prescribed by AASHTO R 69. The allowable long‑term tensile strength (TL) is calculated by dividing the ultimate tensile strength by the product of the creep, installation‑damage and durability reduction factors, with the explicit condition that the installation‑damage factor must never be less than 1.3 and that the geogrid strain may not exceed five percent for a design life of one hundred years. For the geosynthetic elements incorporated in a prefabricated pavement base drain, performance verification relies on specific laboratory test results. Tensile strength of the geotextile must meet or exceed 1,200 psi as measured by ASTM D638; water absorption after twenty‑four hours is limited to 0.01 % according to ASTM D570; crush strength must be at least 40 psi as determined by *GRI‑GC4; and flow capacity must not be less than fifteen gallons per minute per foot of width measured with ASTM D4716. The core geometry must provide a minimum open area of six percent on the pavement side face and three percent on the shoulder side face, and at least fifty percent of the geotextile area on the pavement side and twelve percent on the shoulder side must remain unobstructed to inflow. Pipe sections that form part of the drainage system are subjected to a parallel‑plate load test per ASTM D2412 with stiffness requirements such as thirty psi at ten percent deflection for four‑to‑ten inch pipes, and an infiltration rate of not less than ten gallons per minute per linear foot is confirmed using AASHTO M 176M. Acceptance is based on certified test data supplied by the manufacturer or independent testing performed by LTS, and the geotextile must be a non‑woven needle‑punched fabric made from virgin resin unless an alternative is approved. The net‑like apertures of geogrids allow them to interlock with surrounding soil, rock or aggregate at the subgrade‑aggregate interface, providing reinforcement that stabilizes weak subgrades and thereby improves pavement performance and durability; this function is described in the design specifications for geogrid use. Geotextiles, being permeable polymer fabrics, are placed between subgrade and subbase to maintain separation of the two materials and, when specified as Class 4‑Stabilization (Type C), also supply lateral restraint that reduces load stresses on low‑CBR subgrades, which influences design thickness, construction detail and long‑term maintenance. Both products must be installed according to Publication 408 specifications to achieve the intended performance benefits. In Section 224 the geosynthetic’s susceptibility to heat, UV light and physical damage requires that it be stored elevated, shielded from temperatures greater than 140 °F and direct sunlight; any material showing tears or defects must be rejected and replaced at no extra cost. During construction the sheet must be laid flat, pulled tight and anchored within ±½‑inch of the design elevation, with adjacent rolls overlapping a minimum of 12 inches to maintain continuity and required vertical spacing between layers. Backfill is placed in thin lifts without dumping aggregate directly onto the exposed geosynthetic, and vehicles are prohibited until at least 6 inches of loose backfill cover the sheet, preventing premature puncture or wrinkling that would compromise stability. If damage occurs, patches using the same material must extend at least 3 feet beyond the defect for areas under 3 square feet, while larger damaged sections require full removal and replacement, ensuring long‑term durability of the stabilized pavement. In Section 738.2 the design of pavement reinforcement with Class 1 uni‑axial geogrids is based on the ultimate tensile strength (TU), defined as a minimum average roll value obtained by subtracting two standard deviations from the twelve‑month manufacturer test average; the allowable long‑term tensile strength (TL) is derived by applying three reduction factors—creep reduction factor (RFCR), installation damage reduction factor (RFID), and durability reduction factor (RFD)—so that TL = TU / RFOV, or T5 whichever is less, where RFOV = RFCR × RFID × RFD. The RFID shall never be less than 1.3, and the RFD is taken from Table B unless project‑specific data justify a higher value; the resulting TL must meet or exceed the required design tensile strength (Td) and stay within a maximum allowable strain of 5 percent for a 100‑year design life, directly influencing pavement performance and maintenance expectations. In Section 610.2 the geotextile that wraps the inert plastic core of a prefabricated pavement base drain must have a tensile strength of at least 1,200 psi (ASTM D638) and a crush strength of no less than 40 psi (GRI‑GC4), which help the drain resist deformation under traffic loads. Water absorption shall be 0.01 % after 24 hours (ASTM D570), preventing swelling that could block flow paths, and a minimum flow capacity of 15 gpm per foot of width together with at least 6 % open core area on the pavement side face is required to ensure sufficient water removal, reducing subgrade moisture and extending service life; proper seam placement and attachment to protrusions are required so the geotextile does not deform under continuous loading and compromise flow.
