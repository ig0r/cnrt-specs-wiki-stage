Interlayer

Part of: Asphalt Overlay is-subtopic-of
Interlayer is a topic under Asphalt Overlay.
interlayerinterlayersasphalt-paving-interlayerstress-absorbing-interlayerstress-absorbing-membrane-interlayer
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Interlayer is a stress‑absorbing membrane installed directly on an existing pavement surface—whether flexible or concrete—to provide uniform support where unstable slabs, slab fragments, or joint faulting would otherwise cause loss of support or separation of a new overlay[2]. It acts as both a waterproofing barrier and a stress‑relieving layer that limits moisture intrusion and mitigates reflective (alligator) cracking caused by age‑oxidation, thereby improving load transfer and extending the service life of the overlay[1]. Proper performance depends on rigorous surface preparation—repairing all potholes, cracks larger than 4 sq inches and deeper than 1 inch, removing rutting, ensuring a dry, clean substrate, meeting fault‑thresholds (≥¼‑inch for geotextile, ≥½‑inch for asphalt), applying tack coat within the 290–320 °F window, and providing at least a 1½‑inch overlay thickness to achieve reliable bonding[1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Interlayer” also include the following subtopics: Paving Fabric.

Paving Fabric serves as the specific material implementation of an interlayer by functioning as a needle-punched geotextile that saturates with asphalt binder to create a moisture barrier and stress-absorbing membrane. [1][2] This fabric limits reflective cracking through its thermal stability and chemical resistance while requiring application temperatures between 290 °F and 320 °F to ensure proper bonding and durability within the overlay system. [2]

Purpose and treatment objectives

Interlayer is applied where the existing pavement exhibits conditions that could lead to loss of support or separation of an overlay, such as unstable slabs or slab fragments that would otherwise produce non‑uniform support [2]. For flexible pavements it targets situations in which reflective cracking may develop when a new overlay is placed over an existing surface that contains cracks, including alligator‑type cracking that could propagate through the wearing course [1]. The treatment also addresses moisture intrusion by providing a waterproofing membrane that limits surface water penetration and helps prevent moisture‑related deterioration of the underlying pavement [2][1]. It is intended for pavements where surface defects larger than 4 sq inches and deeper than 1 inch must be patched, and where joints exhibit faulting greater than ¼ inch (for a geotextile interlayer) or greater than ½ inch (for an asphalt interlayer), because such faulting can cause cracking or loss of uniform load transfer [2][1]. The interlayer further mitigates stress‑induced cracking that can arise from the addition of new layers, acting as a stress‑relieving membrane [2]. In addition, it helps prevent fabric pickup by vehicle tires caused by high ambient temperatures or tack overspray bleed‑through, which is a distress associated with overlay installations on flexible pavements [1]. For projects with grades steeper than 5 percent the interlayer system requires that the asphalt mix be proportioned to improve compaction and shear‑rut stability, addressing potential rutting problems under steep slopes [1]. Prior to placement all potholes, cracks as specified in Section 469.3(d), and any local distresses related to structural or subgrade failures must be repaired, and existing rutting must be corrected by milling or a leveling course so that a clean, moisture‑free surface is available for proper adhesion and to avoid delamination [1]. Proper temperature and application rate of the asphalt material are required to achieve waterproofing performance without debonding; excessive tack coat or asphalt may cause slippage at higher temperatures while insufficient coverage can lead to poor adhesion [1].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 62–63), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–121), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 317), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

The asphalt paving interlayer is described as being used primarily to extend the service life of an overlay by mitigating reflective cracking and by providing a moisture barrier that limits water intrusion, which together help prevent water‑related deterioration such as stripping or freeze‑thaw damage; the specification further requires that when grid systems are milled the overlying asphalt have a minimum thickness of 1 ½ inches to ensure successful milling [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that the interlayer is installed primarily to repair damage that would otherwise compromise the new overlay, requiring unstable slabs or slab fragments that move under traffic to be stabilized or repaired before placement and mandating that any surface defect larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch be filled with an approved patching material; by placing a geotextile flat on the repaired surface, overlapping edges by 8 inches ± 2 inches and extending at least 4 inches beyond the pavement edge while keeping the fabric temperature below 120°F, the interlayer restores proper support for the overlay [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Distress characterization and causes

The distress that the interlayer is intended to mitigate occurs at concrete pavement joints where measurable faulting is present; joints with greater than 1/4‑inch faulting are milled and a geotextile interlayer is placed, and if joint faulting exceeds 1/2 inch a Superpave wearing course is installed or the joint is milled/grinded to reduce faulting. The condition is described as unstable (visibly rocking) slabs, indicating a severity that can affect ride quality and safety. No progression rate for the faulting is given in the source. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (p. 185)

Reflective cracking of existing cracked surfaces and age‑related oxidation that produces alligator cracking are identified as primary distress mechanisms that an asphalt interlayer is intended to counter [1]. Water intrusion into the structural section—whether from surface water, springs, standing water in ditches, or a non‑drainable wet pavement surface—is described as causing loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation and freeze‑thaw deterioration, and active pumping signals a weak subgrade that must be treated before an interlayer is placed; the interlayer therefore provides a moisture barrier and stress‑absorbing reinforcement to reduce infiltration and slow expansion‑contraction cycles [1][2]. Residual dirt, water, oil or other foreign material on the existing pavement prevents proper adhesion and can lead to delamination during or after construction, so the surface must be dry, clean and free of contaminants and installation shall not occur during precipitation [1][2]. Visibly rocking slabs, unstable slab fragments that move under normal traffic loads, and joint faulting greater than ¼ inch (requiring a geotextile interlayer) or greater than ½ inch (requiring a Superpave wearing course, scratch mix or joint milling/grinding) indicate inadequate uniform support and create conditions for water infiltration if drainage is not provided; surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be filled before the interlayer is placed [1][2]. Existing rutting must be removed by milling or a leveling course because uncorrected structural failures can cause premature cracking of the overlay, and on grades steeper than 5 % the asphalt mix should be proportioned to improve compaction and shear‑rut stability [1][2]. Improper temperature control during placement—such as an asphalt material applied below 290 °F or above 320 °F, a tack coat that is too heavy at high ambient temperatures, or placement of fiberglass mesh grid with self‑adhesive glue outside the range of 40 °F to 140 °F—can result in loss of waterproofing, debonding, slippage problems and thermal‑related loss of bond strength [1][2]. The first lift of an asphalt overlay must be compacted to a minimum thickness of 1½ inches to provide adequate structural capacity, and insufficient compaction can accelerate distress [2]. Drainage impairment accelerates distress; the interlayer must be terminated in a drainable layer or at a daylighted location and must not impede free drainage of the geotextile [1][2]. Storage of interlayer materials must be protected from precipitation, ultraviolet light, chemicals and sparks to prevent material degradation prior to placement [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–121), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 317–319), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Applicability and treatment selection

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, an interlayer such as a stress‑absorbing membrane (SAMI) may be employed only on flexible pavements that have flexible bases and whose existing surface does not exhibit excessive cracking or extensive alligator cracking, and the spacing between thermal cracks must not exceed 15 feet. The manual further states that the interlayer should be placed directly on the pavement and the design depth must accommodate at least two courses as recommended by the manufacturer. In the overlay context of the same source, an asphalt paving interlayer is appropriate when a structurally sound pavement shows reflective or age‑oxidation induced alligator cracking and an overlay of at least 1 ½ inches is planned, because that minimum thickness permits milling of the grids. The manual notes that the treatment is considered when climate and traffic conditions warrant additional crack control, especially in freeze‑thaw environments where a thicker overlay with interlayer can delay cracking, and when moisture intrusion could accelerate distress provided any subgrade water problems such as active pumping or underground springs are corrected before installation. The Pennsylvania specifications require that for flexible pavements the surface be prepared to a dry, clean, drainable condition free of dirt, gravel, vegetation, oil, loose debris, and that all open cracks or potholes be sealed or repaired; the surface must also be free of water before placing the interlayer. The first lift of overlay over the interlayer area must achieve at least 1 ½ inches compacted thickness, and traffic is limited to construction vehicles only until the final asphalt overlay is placed. Environmental limits in that specification include maintaining tack coat temperature between 290°F and not exceeding 320°F, keeping pavement surface temperatures within the ranges specified for each product, and avoiding rain before overlay placement. For concrete‑surfaced pavements, the specifications state that the existing concrete must be stabilized if slabs are unstable, and surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be repaired; a geotextile interlayer is specified when milled joints exhibit greater than 1/4‑inch faulting, whereas an asphalt interlayer is applied when joint faulting exceeds 1/2 inch after a scratch course or milling to reduce the fault. The concrete‑surfaced pavement requirements also call for sweeping debris and limiting surface temperature of the fabric to not exceed 120°F before overlay placement, with construction traffic restricted to protect the newly installed material. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 62–63), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 317–319), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Under the experimental pavement construction criteria (section 2.9), an interlayer such as a stress‑absorbing membrane (SAM) or stress‑absorbing membrane interlayer (SAMI) should not be used when the existing pavement does not meet its experimental requirements, and it is unsuitable on any pavement other than flexible pavements with flexible bases where the surface exhibits excessive cracking or extensive alligator cracks, and where thermal cracks are spaced more than 15 feet apart. [1] If the design cannot provide the manufacturer‑specified depth of material over the membrane together with at least two courses of overlay, a conventional seal coat, another preservation treatment, or an alternative rehabilitation approach should be selected instead. [1] For overlay applications (section 5.12), an interlayer should not be used when the distress is rutting, bleeding, raveling, or when active pumping indicates a poor subgrade condition; those situations require other repair or drainage treatments such as a structural overlay, milling, or subgrade improvement. [1] Water intrusion that originates from underground springs or standing water in ditches cannot be mitigated with an interlayer, so drainage work must be performed before any rehabilitation. [1] Regarding surface preparation (section 5.12 >a), an interlayer cannot be expected to adhere and may delaminate when unrepaired potholes, cracks, or other structural distresses remain; when dirt, water, oil, or any foreign material is present on the surface; when rutting has not been removed by milling or a leveling course; when moisture is present or precipitation is occurring; or when the pavement temperature is outside the range of 40 °F to 140 °F for fiberglass mesh grid with self‑adhesive glue, another repair, preservation, rehabilitation or maintenance method should be used instead. [1] An interlayer should not be used when the existing concrete cannot provide uniform support because any area with unstable slabs and slab fragments that move or rock under normal traffic loads must be stabilized or repaired before an interlayer is placed. [2] When a geotextile interlayer is specified, all surface defects larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch must be patched prior to placement; otherwise those defects require repair before the interlayer. [2] For an asphalt interlayer, shallow defects may only be filled with the interlayer material if they can be properly compacted – otherwise they require repair before the interlayer. [2] If joint faulting exceeds ½ inch, a scratch course or milling must be performed before installing the asphalt interlayer; other maintenance actions such as concrete patching, slab stabilization, slabjacking, flexible‑base replacement, or shallow repairs are required instead of relying solely on an interlayer. [2]

Condition assessment and timing

To determine whether an interlayer is required, a visual inspection must first confirm that the pavement surface does not exhibit excessive cracking or extensive all‑area alligator cracks, because such conditions preclude the use of SAMs and SAMIs which are intended only for flexible pavements with a flexible base [1]. The inspector also records moisture or water damage, prevailing climate, and traffic loading, and must verify that the planned overlay will achieve the recommended minimum thickness of 1 ½ inches when grids are to be milled before considering an interlayer [1]. Measurement of the spacing between thermal cracks is required, and an interlayer may be considered only when the distance between thermal cracks does not exceed 15 feet [1]. A detailed pavement condition survey records the type and severity of existing distresses, giving particular attention to all‑area alligator cracking that indicates age oxidation and raises the likelihood of reflective cracking in a new overlay, as well as any active pumping that would signal subgrade problems requiring repair before an interlayer can be placed [1]. For concrete pavements, the crew must visually inspect for unstable slabs or slab fragments that move or rock under normal traffic, and such areas must be stabilized or repaired prior to placing an interlayer [2]. Surface defects in the traffic lanes are measured, and any defect exceeding four square inches in area and one inch in depth must be filled with an approved patching material before interlayer installation [2]. Joint faulting is measured, and geotextile interlayers are required where milled joints show more than a quarter‑inch of faulting, while asphalt interlayers are called for when joint faulting exceeds a half‑inch; in the latter case the appropriate preparation such as a scratch course or milling/grinding of the joint must be performed [1][2]. For existing concrete pavements where joints that have been milled show greater than ¼‑inch faulting, a geotextile interlayer may be used, whereas faulting exceeding ½‑inch calls for placement of a Superpave wearing course (Scratch) or milling/grinding of the joint [1]. Accurate depth verification supporting these determinations is obtained by providing cross‑sections every 50 feet and adjusting core collection where the cross‑section varies [1].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 62–63), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

For flexible pavements with flexible bases, a stress‑absorbing membrane interlayer may be used only when the existing surface does not exhibit excessive cracking or extensive alligator cracking and when thermal cracks are spaced no more than 15 feet apart [1]. For asphalt rehabilitation overlays, an interlayer should be placed when reflective (alligator) cracking caused by age oxidation is present but the remainder of the surface remains sound; it shall not be used for distresses such as rutting, bleeding or raveling and shall not be applied where active pumping indicates a poor subgrade or where drainage problems from underground springs or standing water exist without separate corrective work [1]. Prior to interlayer placement on flexible pavements, all potholes, cracks defined in Section 469.3(d), and any local distresses tied to structural or subgrade failures must be repaired; rutting must be corrected by milling or a leveling course; the pavement surface must be free of dirt, water, oil and other foreign material, moisture‑free, and installation may not occur during precipitation [1]. For unbonded concrete overlays over existing concrete pavements, an interlayer is called for when joint faulting exceeds ¼ inch; if joint faulting exceeds ½ inch the specification requires adding a Superpave wearing course or grinding the joints before the interlayer is placed, and a geotextile (or asphalt concrete) interlayer is installed to separate the layers and proper drainage must be provided [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require that an interlayer be placed only after all pre‑overlay repairs and surface cleaning have been completed, and when the existing concrete shows joint faulting that exceeds the specified limits; for a geotextile interlayer the joints must be milled if they have greater than ¼‑inch faulting, and for an asphalt interlayer any joint faulting over ½ inch requires a scratch course or milling before the interlayer is laid [2]. Additionally, all surface defects larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch must be patched prior to interlayer placement, and the pavement must be swept clean of loose debris by an approved method such as power broom or vacuum truck [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 62–63), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Materials and repair details

The experimental guidance limits interlayer applications to stress‑absorbing membrane (SAM) or stress‑absorbing membrane interlayer (SAMI) systems that are installed full‑width over the pavement surface. [1] A SAMI must be placed directly on an existing flexible pavement with a flexible base, and any leveling courses or grade adjustments are applied on top of it; the design shall provide at least two overlay courses whose depth satisfies the manufacturer’s recommendation. [1] Acceptable pavements for SAMI use may not exhibit excessive cracking or extensive alligator cracking, and the spacing between thermal cracks must not exceed 15 feet. [1] A seal coat that meets the design intent may be used as a more cost‑effective alternative to an asphalt/rubber SAMI. [1] For conventional overlay projects the interlayer material shall be compatible with the surrounding mix, thermally stable, physically durable, and capable of providing a strong internal adhesive or mechanical bond, with specific tack coat and asphalt mix requirements confirmed with the supplier. [1] Acceptable products include a needle‑punched nonwoven geotextile fabric that becomes an asphalt‑saturated membrane, a high‑strength open fiberglass mesh grid either pre‑coated with pressure‑sensitive adhesive or carried on lightweight fabric bonded with tack coat, and composite reinforcing grids composed of polyolefin, polyester, or polyamide needle‑punched nonwoven material reinforced with glass filaments. [1] When an interlayer is used beneath an overlay the pavement should have a minimum thickness of 1 ½ inches to allow milling of the grids, as required for mitigation of reflective cracking and provision of a moisture barrier. [1] Prior to placement all potholes and cracks identified in Section 469.3(d) must be repaired, the surface cleared of dirt, water, oil or other foreign material, and any rutting corrected by milling or by placing a leveling course. [1] A leveling course is required on concrete pavements and for all paving‑grid applications except composite grids. [1] The existing pavement must be dry, clean, free of debris, and any cracks sealed or potholes repaired before placement; shallow defects larger than four square inches and one inch deep shall be filled using an approved asphalt, cementitious or similar patching material, and unstable slabs must be stabilized. [2] The interlayer material shall remain wrapped, dry, and labeled in accordance with ASTM D4873 while stored. [2] Installation shall not occur during precipitation, the pavement must be moisture‑free, and ambient air temperature must satisfy the limits in Section 413.3(b). [1] For fiberglass mesh grid with self‑adhesive glue the pavement surface temperature at placement shall be between 40 °F and 140 °F; for newly placed asphalt surfaces the temperature must be allowed to cool below 140 °F before the adhesive grid is installed. [1][2] For geotextile fabric and any other interlayer the pavement surface temperature shall not exceed 120 °F. [2] When an asphalt tack coat is required it must be a manufacturer‑approved product applied with a calibrated distributor at rates that fully saturate the selected reinforcement: 0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard for geotextile fabric, 0.02 to 0.05 gallons per square yard for fiberglass mesh grid, and 0.19 to 0.28 gallons per square yard for composite reinforcing grid. [2] The tack coat shall be applied over a width equal to the product width plus six inches on each side, and its temperature must not fall below 290 °F nor exceed 320 °F. [2] A tack coat or asphalt material shall be selected in consultation with the interlayer manufacturer, with type, temperature and application rate adjusted to existing pavement condition, overlay and ambient temperature. [1] During installation clean blotting sand or broadcast asphalt pavement material should be spread over any area where fabric pickup or tack bleed‑through is a concern, and excess sand removed before the asphalt is placed. [1] The reinforcement is laid on the still‑tacky tack coat using smooth mechanical or manual equipment with brooming or rolling to ensure contact; any damaged or non‑adhered sections are removed and replaced, and hand‑placed asphalt is used at all joints and repairs identified by the technical representative. [2] Overlaps for reinforcement shall be at least one inch unless the manufacturer specifies otherwise, and wrinkles larger than one inch are to be sliced and overlapped. [2] For a geotextile interlayer the fabric is laid flat with edges overlapped eight inches plus or minus two inches on each side, limited to no more than three layers in any location, and its free edge must extend at least four inches beyond the pavement edge while avoiding folding or traffic damage. [2] When a geotextile interlayer is used in an unbonded PCC overlay of PCC pavement, existing concrete joints showing faulting greater than one‑quarter inch shall be milled; if joint faulting exceeds one‑half inch a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch), 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix should be placed at the joint or the joints milled/grinded to reduce faulting, and a nominal depth of one inch of the approved Superpave mix design shall be placed as the asphalt interlayer. [1][2] Proper drainage of the interlayer must be provided by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain, and the interlayer must extend through the shoulder when shoulders are constructed or reconstructed and be tied to the mainline. [1] For projects on grades steeper than 5 % the asphalt mix should be proportioned to aid compaction and shear rut stability, and adhesion problems may be corrected by warming the material or increasing the application rate. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 62–63), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–121), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 317–319), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Interlayer performance is governed by material properties, compatibility with the overlay, and the condition of the existing pavement. For stress‑absorbing membrane (SAM) or SAMI systems placed on flexible pavements with flexible bases they should be used only when the surface does not exhibit excessive cracking or extensive alligator cracks and when thermal cracks are spaced no more than 15 feet apart [1]. The design must provide a sufficient depth of material over the membrane to satisfy the manufacturer’s recommendations and must include at least two courses of material [1]. When an interlayer is installed beneath an asphalt overlay the interlayer material must be thermally stable and physically durable so that it does not lose its properties over time, and it must be compatible with the asphalt to create a strong internal adhesive or mechanical bond within the asphalt matrix [1]. The specification generally recommends an overlay of at least 1½ inches in thickness when milling grids, which also allows use of a thinner overlay if a paving fabric is employed; the same minimum overlay thickness of not less than 1½ inches is required for the asphalt paving interlayer system described in Section 466 [1][2]. Successful placement requires a clean, dry substrate; the pavement must be free of dirt, water, oil or other foreign material and installation during precipitation is prohibited [1]; the Pennsylvania specification adds that the surface must also be free of gravel, vegetation, loose debris, depressions, and any unsealed cracks or potholes, and all crack‑filling material must be sealed and allowed to cure before placement [2]. Any existing rutting must be removed by milling or by providing a leveling course before the interlayer is placed, and a leveling course is mandatory on concrete pavements as well as on all paving‑grid applications except composite grids; for unbonded concrete overlays a leveling course is also required [1][2]. For fiberglass mesh grids with self‑adhesive glue pavement temperature shall be between 40 °F and 140 °F (and below 140 °F on newly laid asphalt) to achieve proper bonding, and the same temperature range is specified for placement of a fiberglass mesh grid in the Pennsylvania interlayer system [1][2]. The application of tack coat or asphalt must be at a temperature that permits waterproofing while avoiding debonding; the asphalt binder used as tack coat shall be kept between a minimum temperature of 290 °F and not exceed 320 °F, and an overly heavy coating may produce slippage at higher temperatures [1][2]. The required application rate depends on the interlayer type; for geotextile fabric the applied asphalt rate must be sufficient to fully saturate the material, specified as 0.22‑0.28 gal/yd², and for fiberglass mesh it is 0.02‑0.05 gal/yd², both values being approved by the manufacturer [2]; the Pavement Policy Manual notes that the rate also depends on whether the existing pavement has been milled rough or smooth and that self‑adhesive paving grids installed on the same day as a leveling course may not need an additional coating [1]. Geotextile fabric must be placed flat, overlapped by 8 inches ± 2 inches, limited to three layers, extended at least 4 inches beyond the pavement edge, kept clean and damp but not saturated, and its surface temperature must not exceed 120 °F before concrete placement, and an asphalt interlayer requires a nominal depth of 1 inch of Superpave mix design, and joint faulting greater than ½ inch must be corrected by a scratch course or milling [2]; the Policy Manual similarly requires that joints with faulting greater than ¼ inch be milled before the interlayer is placed and if faulting exceeds ½ inch a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix must be placed at the joint or the joint must be milled/grinded to reduce faulting [1]. Surface defects larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch must be patched before a geotextile is laid, while shallow defects may be filled only if they can be properly compacted with the overlay when an asphalt interlayer is used [2]; the Policy Manual lists existing distresses—including types and severity levels—as influencing performance but does not give numeric thresholds beyond cracking and rutting [1]. Proper drainage should be achieved by daylighting the interlayer or installing a vertical subdrain, and the interlayer must extend through the shoulder when shoulders are constructed or reconstructed and be tied to the mainline [1]; the Pennsylvania specification also requires that the surface be drainable before placement [2]. Pavement temperature, the presence of sharp curves or slopes, and grades steeper than 5 % influence mix proportioning to support compaction and shear‑rut stability during installation of the overlay [1].

Treatment procedures

The preparation for an interlayer requires that all potholes and cracks be repaired, as mandated by both the Pavement Policy Manual and the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications. The Pavement Policy Manual further specifies that repairs must address any structural or subgrade‑related distresses in accordance with Section 469.3(d). The Pennsylvania specification requires cracked joints to be sealed and allowed to cure, and potholes to be repaired so the surface becomes drainable and free of depressions. After repairs, the existing pavement surface must be dried and cleaned of all dirt, water, oil, gravel, vegetation, and loose debris, using brooming, air‑cleaning, power brooms, vacuum trucks, or other approved means. The Pavement Policy Manual requires that excess blotting sand be removed before placement of asphalt after spreading clean blotting sand or broadcast material over the area. Both documents require that the pavement be moisture‑free and that installation not occur during precipitation, with any rainfall‑induced moisture allowed to dry before proceeding. The Pavement Policy Manual states that rutting must be corrected by milling or by placing a leveling course, and if milling is performed a leveling course may be required before the interlayer is placed. The Pennsylvania specification requires that for geotextile interlayers joints exhibiting more than ¼‑inch faulting be milled, while for asphalt interlayers on concrete joints with faulting greater than ½ inch a scratch course or milling/grinding shall be provided. Both sources require the use of a manufacturer‑specified tack coat or asphalt material applied at the rate prescribed by the interlayer manufacturer. The Pennsylvania specification further requires that the tack coat be applied at a temperature not below 290 °F and not exceeding 320 °F using a calibrated distributor truck spray bar or hand application where necessary. The interlayer is then placed over the prepared surface and compacted, with the Pavement Policy Manual noting that on grades steeper than 5 % the mix shall be proportioned for compaction and shear‑rut stability. The Pennsylvania specification requires the fabric or grid to be laid using mechanical or manual equipment capable of a smooth installation with minimal wrinkling or folding, and brooming or rubber‑tire rolling (or both) is used to press it into full contact before the asphalt cement cools. For geotextile interlayers the Pennsylvania specification mandates that edges be overlapped 8 in. ± 2 in., limited to three layers, extended at least 4 in. beyond the pavement edge, and secured according to the manufacturer’s instructions while being kept clean and damp but not saturated. The Pennsylvania specification calls for a nominal one‑inch depth of the specified Superpave mix to be placed over an asphalt interlayer and compacted with a rubber‑tire roller, and the overlay must be completed the same day and compacted to a minimum first‑lift thickness of 1½ inches, with joints overlapped at least one inch. The Pavement Policy Manual requires that the surface temperature be maintained between 40 °F and 140 °F for fiberglass mesh grids with self‑adhesive glue, and that newly placed asphalt be allowed to cool below 140 °F before interlayer placement; no further finishing, cooling or curing steps are prescribed beyond meeting these temperature limits. The Pennsylvania specification additionally requires that the surface temperature of the interlayer be kept at or below 120 °F prior to overlay placement, which may be achieved by misting, applying a white‑pigmented curing compound, lime‑water whitening, or another approved method, and any excess asphalt bleeding through the fabric is blotted with sand. Quality and workmanship

Inspection of an interlayer begins with verification that the supplied material is appropriate for the pavement type and meets the required specifications; the product must be labeled in accordance with ASTM D4873 according to the asphalt paving interlayer system requirements (source: Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATION Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS) [2]. The material itself shall be compatible with the asphalt mix or concrete substrate and provide a strong internal adhesive or mechanical bond, must be thermally stable and physically durable so that it does not exhibit property or performance breakdown during placement, and when a fiberglass mesh grid with self‑adhesive glue is used the pavement temperature shall be between 40 °F and 140 °F (source: Pavement Policy Manual) [1]. For geotextile interlayers the nominal thickness of the overlying Superpave Wearing Course shall be one inch using a 9.5 mm Fine‑Graded mix, while an asphalt interlayer requires a minimum compacted first lift thickness of 1½ inches (source: Pavement Policy Manual; Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATION Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS) [1][2].

Prior to placement the inspector shall confirm that all potholes, cracks identified in Section 469.3(d), and any local structural or subgrade‑related distresses have been repaired, that the pavement surface is completely free of dirt, water, oil, gravel, vegetation and other foreign material, and that it has been swept clean of loose debris by an approved method (source: Pavement Policy Manual; Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATION Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS) [1][2]. Rutting must be eliminated either through milling or by installing a leveling course, and any surface depressions larger than four square inches and one inch deep shall be filled with an approved repair material (source: Pavement Policy Manual; Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATION Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS) [1][2]. The pavement must be dry, and its temperature before overlay placement shall not exceed 120 °F for both geotextile and asphalt interlayers; ambient air temperature must also satisfy the requirement set out in Section 413.3(b), and no precipitation may occur during installation (source: Pavement Policy Manual) [1].

During installation the presence of a trained manufacturer’s technical representative shall be confirmed, calibrated distributor equipment shall be used, and the interlayer must be laid with smooth, wrinkle‑free contact aided by brooming or rubber‑tire rolling (source: Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATION Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS) [2]. The asphalt tack coat temperature shall be kept at least 290 °F and shall not exceed 320 °F, and the application rate must meet the manufacturer‑approved gallons per square yard for the specific fabric, typically between 0.22 and 0.28 g/yd² (source: Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATION Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS) [2]. Overlaps shall receive at least a one‑inch asphalt bond; geotextile edges shall be overlapped eight inches ± two inches, no more than three layers may overlap in any location, and the free edge shall extend a minimum of four inches beyond the pavement while remaining clean, damp but not saturated (source: Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATION Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS) [2]. For grades steeper than five percent the asphalt mix proportions should be examined to ensure adequate compaction and shear‑rut stability, and blotting sand or broadcast asphalt shall be spread cleanly over areas where fabric pickup is a risk, with excess material removed before the asphalt is laid (source: Pavement Policy Manual) [1]. If milling of the existing surface has produced a rough texture, a leveling course may be required before the interlayer is placed (source: Pavement Policy Manual) [1].

After placement the completed work shall be examined for a sound bond and intact reinforcement, joints and overlaps shall be inspected for proper asphalt fill, excess tack coat bleed‑through shall be sanded off, and traffic shall be limited to construction vehicles only (source: Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATION Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS) [2]. The first lift of overlay must meet the minimum compacted thickness requirement, and the overall installation shall comply with all specified performance criteria, including proper drainage verified by daylighting or a vertical subdrain, continuity through shoulders, and cross‑sections at every fifty feet for material quantity estimation together with core sampling where depth varies (source: Pavement Policy Manual) [1].

A satisfactory stress‑absorbing membrane interlayer (SAMI) installed on a flexible pavement with a flexible base shall be placed directly on the existing surface, and any leveling or grade‑adjustment courses shall be applied on top; the existing surface must not exhibit excessive cracking or extensive alligator cracks, and the spacing of thermal cracks in the underlying concrete may not exceed 15 feet. Design documents shall specify a material depth over the SAM/SAMI that follows the manufacturer’s recommendations and shall provide at least two courses of overlay material. For general overlays the interlayer material must be compatible with the asphalt to provide a strong internal adhesive or mechanical bond within the asphalt matrix, and it must be thermally stable and physically durable so that it does not exhibit property or performance breakdown over time. Prior to placement the existing pavement surface shall be completely clean—free of dirt, water, oil and any other foreign material—and moisture free; installation shall not occur during precipitation. For fiberglass‑mesh grids with self‑adhesive glue the pavement temperature shall be between 40 °F and 140 °F, and on newly placed asphalt surfaces the temperature must be allowed to cool below 140 °F before placing the adhesive grid. The asphalt material applied over the interlayer shall be at the proper temperature to achieve waterproofing performance and avoid debonding, and the tack‑coat or asphalt rate shall not be excessive because a too heavy coat could cause slippage at higher temperatures. Any excess blotting sand must be removed before placement, and if the milled surface is rough a leveling course may be required to ensure adequate bonding. For self‑adhesive paving grids the manufacturer‑approved tack coat shall be used in accordance with the manufacturer’s requirements, and when grades exceed 5 % the mix shall be proportioned to achieve proper compaction and shear‑rut stability. Indicators of an unsatisfactory interlayer include slippage at high temperature or lack of adhesion, which suggest that the asphalt is too cold or the application rate is insufficient. Within an unbonded Portland cement concrete overlay joints with faulting greater than 1/4‑inch shall be milled, and when joint faulting exceeds 1/2‑inch a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix shall be placed at the joint or the joint shall be ground to reduce the faulting. Proper drainage of the interlayer shall be provided either by daylighting the interlayer or installing a vertical subdrain, and the interlayer must extend through any reconstructed shoulder and be tied into the mainline. In the Asphalt Paving Interlayer System (Section 466) a satisfactory interlayer installation is confirmed when the pavement surface is dry, clean and free of debris, oil, vegetation and sealed cracks that have cured. The asphalt material applied must meet temperature limits of at least 290 °F but must not exceed 320 °F. The application rate must saturate the specific product—geotextile fabric requires 0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard with a target width extending six inches beyond each side, fiberglass mesh grid requires 0.02 to 0.05 gallons per square yard, and composite reinforcing grid requires 0.19 to 0.28 gallons per square yard—as approved by the manufacturer’s technical representative. All joints and overlaps must meet a 1‑inch minimum and any wrinkle larger than one inch must be sliced and overlapped flat. The first lift of overlay over the interlayer must have a compacted thickness that will not be less than 1 ½ inches. No traffic other than construction vehicles is permitted until curing is complete. In the Unbonded Concrete Overlay (Section 548) a satisfactory geotextile interlayer must be laid flat on a repaired surface with its edges overlapped by eight inches ± two inches and the free edge extending at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, while ensuring the fabric remains clean, damp but not saturated and its surface temperature does not exceed 120 °F before concrete placement. An asphalt interlayer is acceptable when placed to a nominal one‑inch depth of the specified Superpave mix and likewise must be installed before the pavement surface temperature exceeds 120 °F. Performance and service life

The asphalt paving interlayer placed between two asphalt layers helps to mitigate reflective cracking and provides a moisture barrier that limits water intrusion, which otherwise can cause loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, crack propagation and freeze‑thaw damage; by mechanically interlocking aggregate and reinforcing the overlay it increases bearing capacity and load transfer, thereby improving pavement functionality and durability. The specification recommends a minimum overlay thickness of 1 ½ inches when using an interlayer so that the grids can be milled, and this thinner yet reinforced structure may reduce the overall initial cost while extending the design life, which may also decrease the life‑cycle cost by prolonging service life. Interlayers are intended for distresses such as reflective cracking or alligator cracking caused by age oxidation rather than rutting or bleeding, and they should be selected based on existing distress type, climate, traffic, moisture conditions and cost considerations. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

Reflective cracking is identified as a common problem when an asphalt overlay is placed over an existing cracked pavement, and interlayers are intended to reduce this type of crack reflection but do not address other distresses such as rutting, bleeding or raveling. Water intrusion into the structural section is described as a major cause of loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, localized failures and further crack propagation, especially in freeze‑thaw environments where expansion and contraction can accelerate damage; the development of these moisture‑related distresses is influenced by existing pavement condition (for example alligator cracking from age oxidation), climate, traffic conditions and any active pumping that signals poor subgrade requiring treatment before an interlayer can be effective. Delamination is reported as the primary distress observed when an interlayer fails to bond to the existing pavement; this occurs when the surface contains dirt, water, oil or other foreign material, when asphalt is sprayed onto such contaminants, or when moisture remains on the pavement during installation, and the requirement that the pavement be moisture‑free and that placement not occur during precipitation further limits the risk of delamination. Loss of bond is also listed as a typical distress for asphalt paving interlayers, and visible wrinkles or folds larger than one inch are cited as additional failure modes when surface preparation is inadequate, weather limits are ignored, storage permits moisture or UV exposure, or the temperature of the asphalt exceeds the allowable range. The specifications require the pavement to be dry, clean and free of debris before a tack coat is applied within a 290 °F‑320 °F temperature window; overlaps must be at least one inch with asphalt fill, any wrinkle larger than one inch must be sliced and overlapped flat, and damaged interlayer material must be removed and replaced. Improper temperature—outside the 40 °F to 140 °F range for fiberglass mesh grid with self‑adhesive glue—or placing the interlayer over uncorrected rutting can impair bonding and lead to premature failure, while asphalt material temperatures exceeding a 320 °F ceiling can cause damage to the interlayer system. Typical fabric‑related distresses include the fabric being picked up by tires, slippage of the layer at high temperatures, and loss of bond (debonding) between the interlayer and existing pavement; these problems are driven primarily by excessive ambient heat or overspray that causes tack‑coat bleed‑through, a tack coat that is too heavy for the temperature conditions, asphalt applied too cold or at an insufficient rate, and inadequate surface preparation such as a rough milled base that lacks a leveling course. Unstable concrete slabs that move or rock under normal traffic loads create non‑uniform support for the interlayer and therefore must be stabilized or repaired before placement; surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be filled with a patching material and compacted to a flat, tight surface to prevent voids beneath a geotextile or asphalt interlayer. When a geotextile interlayer is used, the specifications prohibit more than three layers in any location, require avoidance of folds or tears, limit exposure to construction traffic, and set a maximum fabric temperature of 120 °F before overlay placement; for an asphalt interlayer, joint faulting greater than one‑half inch must be corrected and the pavement surface temperature also must be kept below 120 °F to ensure proper bonding. Steeper grades greater than five percent require specially proportioned mixes to maintain compaction and shear‑rut stability, otherwise bonding may suffer. [1][2]

Monitoring and follow-up

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, before an interlayer is placed the pavement surface must be free of potholes, cracks as defined in Section 469.3(d), and other local distresses associated with structural or subgrade failure; the presence of these defects indicates that the existing pavement can no longer support the new layer and further maintenance would be required [1]. The same manual requires that the surface be clean of dirt, water, oil, or any foreign material and that it be moisture‑free at the time of placement, because moisture on the pavement or precipitation during installation is an additional sign that corrective work is needed to avoid delamination of the interlayer [1]. Rutting that has not been removed by milling or a leveling course likewise indicates inadequate treatment and must be corrected before the interlayer is placed [1]. Ambient air temperature must be between 40 °F and 140 °F for fiberglass‑mesh grid with self‑adhesive glue, and the newly placed asphalt surface must be cooled below 140 °F before the adhesive mesh is laid; temperatures outside these limits indicate that placement should be postponed to avoid premature failure [1]. Joint faulting observed at the interlayer that exceeds the design limits—greater than ¼‑inch for a geotextile interlayer and especially greater than ½‑inch for an asphalt interlayer—requires additional repair such as placing a Superpave wearing course or milling/grinding the joints to reduce faulting, signaling the need for further maintenance or rehabilitation [1][2]. Inadequate drainage through the interlayer is also a trigger for remedial action; when drainage is insufficient the interlayer should be daylighted or a vertical subdrain installed because these conditions indicate that the interlayer is no longer performing its intended function [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications add that visible movement or rocking of concrete slabs or slab fragments under normal traffic loads constitutes unstable pavement and signals the need for additional maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation before an interlayer can be installed [2]. Surface defects larger than four square inches in area and deeper than one inch must be filled; any such unfilled defect remaining after preparation indicates that further repair is required prior to interlayer placement [2].
