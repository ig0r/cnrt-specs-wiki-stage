Pavement Condition Assessment

Part of: PennDOT Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242) is-subtopic-of
Pavement Condition Assessment is a topic under PennDOT Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242).

Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-29

Pavement Condition Assessment is a systematic evaluation of the functional state of existing roadway pavements—both rigid concrete and flexible asphalt—and their supporting base, sub‑base and subgrade soils[2]. It combines visual surveys of surface distresses such as cracking, rutting, raveling, joint spalling, potholes and drainage deficiencies with quantitative measurements including pavement thickness, compressive strength, air‑content percentages, deflection testing and International Roughness Index values to determine whether the structure meets design tolerances and serviceability targets[1][2]. The process applies explicit severity thresholds (e.g., rut depth < 1½ in., crack depth < 1 in. with width ≤ ½ in., Percent Within Limits ≥ 55 % for strength or air content) and integrates the results into treatment matrices that guide preservation, rehabilitation, or reconstruction decisions[2][1].

PennDOT publications covering “Pavement Condition Assessment” also include the following subtopics: Deflection, Distress, Falling Weight Deflectometer, Functional Condition, Pavement Condition Survey, Pavement Core, Ride Quality, Skid Resistance, and Structural Capacity.

Pavement Condition Assessment integrates visual Distress observations and ride quality metrics from the Pavement Condition Survey with structural inputs derived from Deflection testing and Skid Resistance measurements. [1][2] The process relies on data from the Falling Weight Deflectometer and Pavement Core samples to calculate Structural Capacity, which determines whether Functional Condition thresholds require preservation or reconstruction. [1][2]

Purpose and investigation objectives

Pavement Condition Assessment evaluates the functional state of existing roadway pavements, encompassing both rigid concrete and flexible asphalt surfaces, together with their supporting base, sub‑base and subgrade soils [2]. It also checks whether a concrete pavement meets its design thickness, compressive‑strength and air‑content limits as specified for new installations [1][2]. The assessment records observable surface distresses such as transverse, longitudinal or miscellaneous cracking, rutting, raveling or weathering, potholes, edge deterioration, shoving, slippage, bleeding, minor corrugation, joint spalling and faulting, and notes any drainage deficiencies [2]. Each distress type is quantified against prescribed severity thresholds: rut depth shall be less than 1½ in., transverse and miscellaneous crack depth less than 1 in. with an average crack width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ in., and raveling/weathering limited to the medium rating [2]. For concrete pavements, additional material‑condition indicators are evaluated, including percent patching, compressive strength, air content, and visual signs of durability problems such as aggregate deficiencies (e.g., Vanport Limestone or alkali‑silica reaction) or frost damage [1][2]. Core samples are taken near deteriorated joints and at random locations to ascertain the condition of underlying subgrade or subbase soils [2]. The investigation must answer whether the average pavement depth falls short of the design thickness by more than a half‑inch, and whether any individual sublot is deficient by that amount [1]. It must also verify that the Percent Within Limits for concrete compressive strength or plastic air content meets or exceeds the 55 % threshold [1]. The assessment asks which distress types are present, how severe they are relative to the thresholds, and whether any observed distress cannot be repaired to acceptable standards [1][2]. It inquires whether tolerances for depressions or high points exceed specified limits and whether edge‑slumping requirements are satisfied [1]. The evaluation determines the dominant mechanisms causing distress—load‑related, drainage‑related, material‑related (including prohibited materials or durability problems) [2]. It queries whether pavement thickness meets minimum requirements relative to required milling depth and whether the base, subbase and subgrade are sound, adequately drained, and possess sufficient in‑situ stiffness to support construction equipment and traffic [1][2]. The assessment also asks for estimates of future traffic loading and the segment’s ranking in overall condition expressed by the Overall Pavement Index and the percentage of mileage in excellent or good condition [2]. Ride quality is examined through International Roughness Index measurements, which are compared with Publication 408 smoothness criteria to verify that existing, resurfaced or newly constructed sections satisfy acceptable IRI limits [2]. Finally, the investigation must determine whether the Present Serviceability Index derived from roughness, crack, patch and rut data meets or exceeds the Terminal Serviceability Index for the road’s functional classification, thereby guiding the preliminary engineering choice between preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction [2].

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 389); [2] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 5 (pp. 121–133), Ch. 6 (pp. 150–151), Ch. 11 (p. 197), Ch. 13 (pp. 216–221), App. J (pp. 267–272)

Publication 242’s condition assessment converts measured physical properties—average pavement depth, compressive‑strength and plastic‑air‑content percentages—and observed surface defects into a binary defect determination using explicit thresholds; a lot is flagged as defective when its average thickness falls below the design thickness by at least one‑half inch or any sublot is that thin, when the percent within limits for strength or air content drops beneath 55 %, or when rain damage, improper joint work, curing flaws, cracking, honeycombing, depressions, high points, or edge‑slumping cannot be satisfactorily repaired. These defect determinations immediately inform planning and design by pinpointing locations where removal of at least ten feet between transverse joints or full panel replacement is required, dictating joint reconstruction methods, and establishing the priority of preservation versus rehabilitation actions. Complementary to the material‑property checks, the STAMPP automated surveys conducted each year record observable distresses such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling, joint spalling and faulting, quantifying rut depth, crack depth and width, extent of raveling, potholes and drainage deficiencies. The quantified values are entered into pavement‑condition matrices that incorporate severity thresholds—rutting less than 1½ inches, transverse cracking depth under 1 inch with average widths no greater than ½ inch, minor corrugation or shoving under 1½ inches, and asphalt thickness at least 1½ inches beyond any milling depth—and the matrices rapidly assign a treatment category ranging from routine maintenance to preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction. The assigned category drives planning schedules, budgeting allocations, and design specifications because it determines whether an overlay, structural reinforcement, or full reconstruction is warranted. In‑situ nondestructive testing such as falling weight deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer and lightweight deflectometer supplies layer‑wise stiffness and strength data that confirm whether existing layers can support the proposed structure, guide the sizing of reclaimed materials, inform selection of stabilization additives, and enable mix designs that satisfy projected traffic loads. Specific structural criteria—rutting under a quarter inch for high‑friction surface treatments, a structurally sound base, adequate drainage, and an expectation of no major repairs for at least six years—further refine the selection of preservation versus rehabilitation strategies and dictate overlay type, thickness and material. Network‑level metrics such as mileage “out of cycle,” pavement surface age, structure age, and the Overall Pavement Index provide a system‑wide view; an OPI greater than 85 percent denotes excellent or good condition while less than 75 percent signals poor condition, and segments where two or more distress components receive poor ratings are flagged for higher‑priority interventions to satisfy FHWA limits on interstate lane‑miles in poor condition. Traffic considerations, for example avoiding cold recycling on sections carrying 200 or more daily ESALs, are incorporated into the decision process to ensure that selected treatments will perform under anticipated loads. The effective structural capacity derived from the combined distress survey and layer‑analysis (Deff or SNeff) is compared against the same severity thresholds to decide whether an overlay can provide sufficient remaining life or whether additional structural capacity must be added, thereby guiding the choice between preservation and rehabilitation and specifying the required overlay thickness and material. International Roughness Index measurements, entered within four to six weeks for existing work and within three months for new pavement, give an objective gauge of ride quality that allows planners to verify compliance with Publication 408 smoothness criteria, monitor deterioration rates, feed treatment matrices, and improve life‑cycle cost predictions for budgeting and design. For concrete pavements the assessment also includes core sampling near deteriorated joints and at random locations together with deflection or faulting tests; when the percent of pavement requiring patching exceeds ten percent a cost‑effectiveness analysis is required before proceeding, and the nature of the distress (load‑related versus climate/material) directs selection among slab stabilization, partial‑depth repair, dowel retrofits, concrete overlay or asphalt overlay, each evaluated through life‑cycle cost comparisons between preservation and rehabilitation alternatives. The aggregated roughness, crack, patch and rut measurements together with nondestructive test results generate a Present Serviceability Index that is compared to the Terminal Serviceability Index for the functional classification; when PSI falls below TSI planners recognize that the pavement no longer meets user needs and must consider preservation or rehabilitation while screening design alternatives to achieve at least the required terminal serviceability level. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

Pavement Condition Assessment is appropriate for existing roadways constructed of rigid pavements—including reinforced cement concrete panels and plain cement concrete slabs—as well as flexible asphalt‑concrete surfaces and unpaved surfacing that form part of the state highway network, encompassing National Highway System routes and representative non‑NHS sections. For rigid pavements the assessment checks whether the surface meets design specifications for depth, material quality, and integrity, flagging a lot as defective when the average thickness is more than one‑half inch below the design thickness, when the percent within limits for compressive strength or plastic air content falls under 55 percent, or when rain‑induced damage, improper joint construction, curing errors, poor final finish, cracking, honeycombing, excessive depressions or high points, and edge slumping cannot be satisfactorily repaired. For flexible pavements the condition rating relies on International Roughness Index values together with measured percentages of surface cracking and rut depth, while concrete sections are rated by IRI, crack percentage and joint faulting. The survey records a broad suite of observable distresses—including fatigue, transverse thermal and reflection cracks; rutting; edge deterioration; raveling or weathering; joint spalling; faulting; potholes; shoulder drop‑off; and drainage problems—each quantified by severity. In addition to the visual inventory, in‑situ testing of all structural layers—surface course, base, subbase and subgrade—is performed using falling weight deflectometers, dynamic cone penetrometers or lightweight deflectometers, with targeted coring used to verify layer thicknesses, material composition and moisture conditions. The methodology works best when a minimum wearing course is present and the underlying base or subgrade is structurally sound and well‑drained, conditions that are essential for preservation options such as cold recycling which is limited to pavements carrying fewer than roughly 200 equivalent single axle loads per day. Limitations arise on ramp sections, which are not captured by the automated STAMPP surveys, on pavements with poor drainage or overly coarse underlying material, and on concrete slabs that exhibit premature mid‑slab cracking caused by inadequate subbase material or problematic aggregates such as Vanport Limestone or alkali‑silica reaction. When these pavement types, materials, layers, observable distresses and favorable site conditions are present, the assessment provides the data needed to select between preservation and rehabilitation strategies and to verify that new or rehabilitated surfaces meet the smoothness criteria of Publication 408. [1][2]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 389); [2] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 5 (pp. 121–126), Ch. 13 (pp. 216–221), Ch. 14 (p. 227), App. J (pp. 267–272)

The pavement‑condition assessment framework described in Publication 242, which relies on the STAMPP automated distress surveys and visual inspections to record cracking, rutting, raveling, potholes, joint spalling, faulting and drainage problems against severity thresholds, provides a valuable snapshot of surface performance but it has several inherent limitations. Because the data are essentially surface‑level indicators, they can overlook critical sub‑surface conditions such as inadequate subbase material that leads to premature mid‑slab cracking in jointed concrete pavements, or aggregate‑related pathologies like Vanport Limestone concerns and alkali‑silica reaction, which are not detectable by STAMPP alone. Ramp pavements are also excluded from the automated inventory, leaving a gap for those sections. Moreover, the Overall Pavement Index and other matrix‑driven treatment recommendations do not capture underlying structural deficiencies; interim maintenance or prior preservation work may improve only the riding surface, causing the matrix to trigger a lower‑level treatment while the deeper condition that drives future performance remains unaddressed. The International Roughness Index component further depends on complete and timely Pavement History data – without accurate construction, reconstruction or resurfacing dates, IRI trends cannot be meaningfully interpreted, and measurements taken before a project reopens to traffic or on routes not scheduled for annual testing fail to reflect the true post‑construction condition.

Consequently, whenever any of these hidden issues are suspected, a complementary investigation should be undertaken before finalizing preservation versus rehabilitation decisions. Structural capacity and uniformity assessments such as Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing, dynamic cone penetrometer or lightweight deflectometer measurements provide rapid insight into in‑situ stiffness and support conditions across the pavement cross‑section. Core sampling near deteriorated joints and at random locations is required to evaluate subbase or subgrade soil properties, especially when full‑depth reclamation, high traffic loads, or drainage problems are central to the design. Deflection tests, faulting measurements and other nondestructive techniques help identify which joints need replacement or slab stabilization. When the percentage of pavement that would require patching exceeds ten percent – excluding existing concrete patches in good condition – a more detailed analysis, including cost‑effectiveness assessment and possibly alternative treatment strategies, becomes necessary. In cases where prior surface‑only preservation has been performed, or when ancillary surveys (e.g., guide‑rail condition) have been collected by non‑professional staff, the data should be verified by qualified personnel before being used for critical decisions. Finally, if IRI values cannot be linked to a reliable Pavement History or are obtained under construction traffic controls, supplemental investigations such as post‑construction roughness testing after reopening to traffic must be employed to achieve an accurate condition evaluation. Existing information and records

The reviewer begins by extracting the most recent pavement‑condition records from PennDOT’s Roadway Management System. These include the Present Serviceability Rating (PSR) or Present Serviceability Index numbers together with each segment’s Maintenance Functional Classification, and they are supplemented by the automated STAMPP surveys that capture roughness, cracking, rut depth, edge deterioration, raveling, joint spalling, faulting, potholes and drainage deficiencies. The condition data are compared to the severity thresholds set out in Publication 242, and the Overall Pavement Index percentages are examined to confirm whether more than 85 % of the mileage is classified as Excellent or Good. International Roughness Index values from the annual NHS inventory and the biennial non‑NHS sample are retrieved, along with post‑construction IRI measurements taken within three months of reopening, to verify compliance with the smoothness criteria of Publication 408. Non‑destructive testing results—single‑locked‑wheel skid‑friction values, high‑speed profiler ride‑quality readings, and structural capacity indicators from Falling Weight Deflectometer or lightweight deflectometer tests—are incorporated to describe the current functional state of the surface and subgrade stiffness. Historical maintenance information stored in the RMS is reviewed for every construction, reconstruction, resurfacing, crack sealing, joint sealing, spall repair, patching and other preservation actions. The dates, pavement‑surface age, structure age and any mileage that is out‑of‑cycle according to Interstate treatment cycles are noted because they trigger preservation or rehabilitation decisions. Laboratory sampling reports for the surface, base, subbase and subgrade provide material properties, layer thicknesses and drainage conditions, while in‑situ strength data from FWD, dynamic cone penetrometer or lightweight deflectometer tests supply subgrade uniformity information prior to any rehabilitation. Traffic loading records are compiled, including average daily traffic counts and Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) figures. Segments that experience 200 or more ESALs per day receive special attention because heavy truck traffic influences treatment selection and may preclude certain recycling methods. Projected future traffic for the design life is also considered. Ancillary inventories such as guide‑rail, drainage and shoulder drop‑off reports generated by Access tools are examined to assess features that affect overall pavement performance. All of these records feed the treatment matrices that determine whether preservation or rehabilitation is appropriate and ensure that any new or rehabilitated surface meets the required ride‑quality standards. [2]

[2] — Ch. 5 (pp. 121–133), Ch. 6 (pp. 150–151), Ch. 10 (pp. 177–179), Ch. 11 (p. 197), Ch. 13 (pp. 219–221), Ch. 14 (p. 227), App. J (pp. 267–272)

Field investigation must close a series of record deficiencies that undermine the reliability of pavement condition assessment under Publication 242. The STAMPP surveys, while providing systematic distress data for the NHS, omit several critical observations: premature mid‑slab cracking in jointed concrete—often linked to inadequate subbase—is not captured; aggregate‑related pathologies such as Vanport limestone concerns and alkali‑silica reaction remain invisible; ramp pavements are excluded from the automated program; and the biennial coverage of non‑NHS segments leaves up to a year of unverified distress. Moreover, the existing databases frequently lack documentation of the underlying distress that originally justified a surface treatment, causing the treatment matrix to misclassify needs, and they do not consistently reflect interim maintenance recorded in the Roadway Management System within the District Interstate plan, which is essential for accurate OPI percentages and poor‑condition mileage.

Compounding these gaps are timing and completeness issues with pavement history and smoothness data. Pavement History entries are often delayed—four weeks for Department‑force projects and six weeks for contractor projects—or missing altogether, resulting in IRI measurements that may be taken before construction, omitted due to traffic‑control constraints, or absent on non‑NHS routes outside the annual testing cycle. This misalignment obscures true ride quality at opening, hampers evaluation of construction effectiveness, and impedes prediction of deterioration rates needed for preservation versus rehabilitation decisions.

Additional uncertainties stem from insufficient forecasts of future traffic loading, outdated distress surveys, and a lack of measured in‑situ stiffness of pavement layers and subgrade. Representative sampling to define material properties, layer thicknesses, drainage conditions, and the presence of wet or water‑damaged subgrade is often inadequate. The drainage inventory excludes features larger than eight feet clear span, and guide‑rail condition records rely on nonprofessional temporary staff, further eroding confidence in ancillary data that influence pavement performance. Finally, because the Present Serviceability Rating ceased incorporating STAMPP condition data after 1995, current PSR values may no longer reflect actual cracking, rutting, or other distress, and the Maintenance Functional Classification required to compute the Terminal Serviceability Index is sometimes absent from the RMS. Field investigations therefore need to quantify visible distress, obtain core samples for subbase/subgrade verification, conduct deflection and faulting tests, assess concrete properties (percent patching, compressive strength, air content), evaluate friction adequacy, ride‑quality indices, and potential polishing‑susceptible aggregates, and verify functional classifications before the treatment matrices can be populated with confidence. [2]

[2] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 6 (pp. 150–151), Ch. 11 (p. 197), Ch. 13 (pp. 219–221), Ch. 14 (p. 227)

Visual inspection and condition assessment

During a Pavement Condition Assessment the inspector records all visible surface defects defined in the standards, which include various forms of cracking, honeycombing, rain‑induced damage, and deficiencies related to joint construction, curing, or final finish [1][2]. Cracking is catalogued by type—longitudinal, transverse, fatigue, thermal, reflection and any patched areas that expose prior repairs—and each crack is measured for depth not exceeding one inch and for average width limited to a medium rating of half‑inch or less [2][1]. Joint spalling is noted wherever concrete joints show loss of material, and faulting is quantified as the elevation difference across transverse joints in inches [2][1]. Geometric irregularities such as depressions, high points, edge slumping, longitudinal‑edge spalling between lanes or outside pavement edges, and shoulder drop‑off are recorded as signs of lateral failure [1][2]. Rutting is measured as the depth of surface depressions; a general tolerance of up to one‑and‑a‑half inches is allowed, but any rut deeper than a quarter‑inch triggers the requirement for surface preparation before applying high‑friction surface treatments [2][1]. Raveling or weathering is evaluated against a medium rating that describes a rough, pitted texture with possible loose particles, and its severity must not exceed this medium level [2][1]. Minor surface corrugation, shoving, slippage or isolated bleeding may be present provided their average depth does not exceed about one‑and‑a‑half inches [2]. Potholes and any drainage deficiencies—including poor water runoff that can accelerate deterioration—are identified because they influence both the condition rating and eligibility for recycling treatments [2][1]. Additional material‑related criteria such as depth shortfalls relative to design thickness, low compressive strength, or excessive air‑content percentages are also considered defects that render a lot unsatisfactory [1]. The International Roughness Index, derived from measured pavement roughness, is treated as an observable defect and feeds the treatment matrices that determine preservation versus rehabilitation decisions [2][1].

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 389); [2] — Ch. 5 (pp. 121–133), Ch. 6 (pp. 150–151), Ch. 13 (pp. 216–221), Ch. 14 (p. 227), App. I (p. 264), App. J (pp. 267–272)

The assessment begins with a field distress survey that classifies every observed defect by its physical form, enumerating cracking, honey‑combing, edge slumping, depressions or high points, improper joint construction, curing defects, surface rain damage, as well as rutting, raveling, potholes and drainage‑related problems [1][2]. For analytical purposes each distress is also assigned to one of two cause categories—load‑related or climate/materials (including drainage)—to aid in interpreting the underlying mechanisms [2]. Severity is quantified against explicit thresholds; a pavement depth that falls more than one‑half inch below the design thickness triggers a severe rating, as does a Percent Within Limits for compressive strength or plastic air content under 55 %, and any defect judged unreparable by the representative’s judgment [1]; similarly the guides require that the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues be specifically noted according to prescribed limits [2]. The extent of each distress is measured along the affected segment, with the exact quantity—length, width or area—recorded and entered into tabular form to support treatment matrices and preliminary engineering decisions between preservation and rehabilitation [2]. Location is documented by referencing the lot, sub‑lot or panel in which the defect occurs, noting longitudinal position (e.g., between transverse joints) and transverse position such as wheel‑path versus non‑wheel‑path, and by indicating whether the distress lies at an edge or interior portion of the slab [1][2]. Verification of underlying conditions is achieved through core sampling taken near deteriorated joints and at random locations, while non‑destructive methods such as deflection testing and faulting measurements identify which joints require replacement or slab stabilization [2]. All recorded information—type, measured severity, quantified extent, and precise location—is compiled into a summary report that documents the level of distress and corresponding observations, providing the quantitative basis for subsequent analysis and treatment selection [1][2].

Field measurements and testing

Pavement condition assessment integrates a suite of field tests, measurements, and nondestructive evaluation methods that together quantify structural capacity, surface integrity, ride quality and safety. [1][2] For rigid pavements the assessment begins with direct measurement of slab thickness, where an average depth that falls more than a half‑inch below the design thickness flags the pavement as defective. [1] Core samples are taken to determine compressive strength and plastic air content, and the results are expressed as a Percent Within Limits; values below 55 % for either parameter indicate unacceptable material quality. [1] Visual inspection records surface defects such as rain‑induced damage, improper joint construction or curing, honeycombing, depressions, high points, edge slumping and cracking, each of which signals a condition that cannot be satisfactorily repaired. [1] A separate visual distress survey, performed in accordance with Publication 336, Publication 343 or comparable procedures such as MicroPaver, documents the type and severity of cracking, rutting, raveling, potholes and drainage problems. [2] The survey measures transverse crack depth to be less than one inch, limits average crack width to the medium rating of half an inch, restricts rut depth and corrugation or shoving depth to no more than one‑and‑a‑half inches, and records these values for input into treatment matrices. [2] The observed distresses are entered into the STAMPP automated video‑log system, which captures cracking, joint spalling, faulting, rutting, raveling and potholes on both asphalt and jointed concrete surfaces and compares them against predefined severity thresholds. [2] Falling Weight Deflectometer testing provides vertical deflection responses that quantify the in‑situ stiffness of surface layers and subgrade, indicate joint efficiency in concrete pavements, and supply data used to estimate required patching volumes. [2] To characterize unbound base and subgrade materials a dynamic cone penetrometer is employed; its blows‑per‑inch rate is correlated with California Bearing Ratio or resilient modulus values. [2] A lightweight deflectometer supplies shallow elastic response measurements that can also be related to CBR, offering an additional means of assessing the strength of subgrade soils. [2] Ground‑penetrating radar surveys are used to locate and describe subbase or subgrade conditions without excavation, providing a rapid subsurface profile. [2] Ride quality is evaluated by measuring the International Roughness Index with high‑speed laser profilers; the IRI value expressed in inches per mile quantifies longitudinal roughness and is checked against Publication 408 smoothness criteria within three months of pavement entry. [2] The Present Serviceability Index combines the IRI‑derived roughness with observed crack density, patch area and rut depth to produce a single numeric expression of overall serviceability for use in treatment decisions. [2] Skid resistance is assessed on segments identified as having inadequate friction by using a Single Locked Wheel Skid Tester, which yields friction values that reflect surface safety. [2] Additional nondestructive tools such as the MIRA system evaluate both the condition of the existing surface and the underlying subsurface, while MIT‑Scan 2 verifies dowel bar alignment within concrete joints, MIT‑Scan T2 determines concrete slab thickness, and a tensile bond tester measures the bond strength of repaired concrete sections. [2] The collection of these measurements feeds the treatment matrices and informs the preliminary engineering choice between preservation actions and full rehabilitation. [1][2]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 389); [2] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–133), Ch. 6 (pp. 150–151), Ch. 10 (pp. 177–179), Ch. 11 (p. 197), Ch. 13 (pp. 216–221), Ch. 14 (p. 227), App. J (pp. 267–272)

Pavement condition assessment under Publication 242 is governed by a coordinated set of equipment and procedures that capture surface distress, structural capacity, and ride quality. The cornerstone of the program is a contracted video‑logging system described in Publication 336; it conducts semi‑automated surveys each year on the entire National Highway System and on half of the non‑NHS network, with the remaining non‑NHS sections completed on a two‑year cycle. These automated surveys record cracking, rutting, spalling, joint faulting and other observable distresses. When skid‑resistance deficiencies are identified, additional friction testing is performed by BOMO using standard skid‑friction equipment.

Structural evaluation relies on in‑situ devices such as the Falling Weight Deflectometer, Dynamic Cone Penetrometer and Light Weight Deflectometer. The DCP uses a 17.6 lb hammer dropped 22.6 in onto a 0.75‑in cone, while the LWD employs a drop weight ranging from 22 to 66 lb applied through load plates of roughly 4 or 12 in diameter with a maximum drop height of 33.5 in. The FWD can be operated as a moving test, which requires traffic control but minimizes disruption to travel.

Sampling for these point‑load tests follows a spacing rule of approximately 500 ft between locations within each lane, and no fewer than three samples per lane are permitted; on long uniform sections the minimum may be reduced to one sample per mile. Testing must consider site conditions: active traffic is managed through appropriate control measures, drainage problems are avoided, and subgrade moisture is monitored so that wet or saturated soils do not compromise measurements. The overall testing regime repeats annually for the video‑logging surveys, while structural tests are performed as needed in conjunction with project design cycles; routine crack sealing on asphalt pavements is scheduled every three to five years and joint sealing on concrete pavements every five years. [2]

Sampling and laboratory investigation

The assessment program requires that material from every pavement layer that could be reclaimed be sampled. For flexible pavements this includes the asphalt wearing course, the underlying base course and, where relevant, the subgrade; for rigid pavements cores are taken from the concrete slab itself, especially at joints showing visible distress, as well as from the subbase or subgrade soils beneath the slab. Sampling locations are established on a regular grid but with random selection to avoid bias: typically one sample is collected every 500 ft along each lane, never fewer than three samples per lane for a given project, and in sections that are uniform and extend more than a mile the interval may be relaxed to approximately one sample per mile. When concrete joints are examined, cores are obtained both adjacent to deteriorated joints and at randomly chosen points so that hidden deterioration can be detected throughout the slab thickness. The depth of each boring or test pit is set to 1.5 times the estimated pulverization depth in order to capture the full extent of the layer under investigation, while concrete cores are extracted through the entire slab to expose the underlying material. At every sampling station a quantity sufficient for laboratory testing is required; the guideline is to retrieve at least 100 lb of material from flexible‑pavement samples, and core specimens must be large enough to permit the prescribed tests on the slab and sub‑soil. The total number of samples depends on the length of the project, the number of lanes, and the observed variability in subgrade or subbase conditions. Sampling frequency is finalized during preliminary engineering and is revisited whenever nondestructive‑test results—such as deflection, faulting, or ground‑penetrating radar—indicate a change in performance, or when distress metrics such as percent patching approach decision thresholds (for example, 10 % patching of concrete). [2]

[2] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), App. J (pp. 267–272)

A Pavement Condition Assessment incorporates laboratory testing of representative samples taken from every pavement layer to quantify structural capacity and material condition. [2] For concrete pavements the protocol requires extracting cores both from visibly distressed joints and from random locations within the slab, after which the specimens are submitted to an accredited laboratory for compressive‑strength determination and measurement of entrapped (plastic) air content. [2][1] The results are evaluated against a Percent Within Limits threshold of 55 % for each property; values below this level flag the concrete as defective because insufficient strength or improper air entrainment can lead to cracking, honeycombing, or other durability problems. [1] In addition to strength and air content, a visual durability inspection is performed on the cores to identify aggregate deficiencies or frost‑related damage that may compromise long‑term performance. [2] Granular and soil layers are subjected to a combined gradation (particle‑size) analysis, moisture‑content determination at the time of testing, and moisture‑density (compaction) testing that establishes relative density for coarse‑grained materials. [2] Classification procedures then include textural classification, determination of Atterberg limits—liquid, plastic, and shrinkage—and assignment to an AASHTO soil class, thereby characterizing composition, stiffness, strength potential, and susceptibility to moisture‑induced deterioration. [2] The subbase and subgrade material recovered with the cores are likewise evaluated to reveal underlying soil conditions and any concealed deterioration beneath the surface, providing essential data for selecting preservation versus rehabilitation treatments. [2] Together these laboratory evaluations provide the quantitative basis for selecting between preservation treatments and more extensive rehabilitation, ensuring that structural capacity and long‑term performance criteria are met. [2][1]

Structural and functional performance

The assessment must first confirm that the pavement’s structural capacity satisfies the design thickness, measuring the in‑place depth and allowing a tolerance of one‑half inch below the specified value. [1] In addition, the material strength is checked by determining the Percent Within Limits for compressive strength (or plastic air content) and ensuring it does not fall beneath the 55 % minimum required by the guidelines. [1] Core drilling provides direct measurements of existing layer thicknesses, which together with the depth verification form the basis for evaluating whether sufficient structural layers remain. [2] Deflection testing with a Falling Weight Deflectometer supplies pavement and subgrade deflection values that are interpreted as overall load‑carrying strength and bearing modulus, while complementary Dynamic Cone Penetrometer or Light Weight Deflectometer tests yield CBR‑or‑resilient‑modulus equivalents for the subgrade. [2] Joint efficiency and load‑transfer capability are assessed through faulting measurements on concrete slabs and by deriving joint efficiency indices from FWD data. [2] Surface friction is quantified with a single locked‑wheel skid tester to verify that skid resistance meets the required standards. [2] Ride quality and roughness are expressed through the International Roughness Index, which is obtained from high‑speed laser profiling of the longitudinal wheel track and compared against the smoothness criteria set out in Publication 408 for new or rehabilitated surfaces. [2] The severity of observable distress—cracking, rutting, raveling, potholes, spalling, and joint faulting—is recorded as percentages or inch measurements so that the combined distress data can be integrated with thickness, stiffness, friction and roughness results in treatment matrices for preservation versus rehabilitation decisions. [2] Traffic exposure is represented by Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) figures, providing a demand context for interpreting the structural and functional measurements. [2]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 389); [2] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 6 (pp. 150–151), Ch. 10 (pp. 177–179), Ch. 13 (pp. 216–221), App. J (pp. 267–272)

Annual automated surveys and visual inspections capture the observable distresses—cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling, joint spalling and faulting—that are processed through treatment matrices built on the original structural capacity assumptions and the forecast ESAL traffic loads [2]. These measured conditions are first checked against design‑based tolerances for rigid pavements: if the average slab depth falls below the specified thickness minus a half‑inch, or if the percent within limits for compressive strength or air content drops under 55 %, the segment is classified as defective and presumed to lack sufficient remaining service life [1]. Surface‑defect thresholds also guide the decision process; rut depths greater than 1½ in., transverse cracks deeper than 1 in. or wider than ½ in., and raveling that exceeds a medium rating are identified in Publication 336 as indicators that the pavement is approaching or exceeding its design capacity [2]. When patching requirements exceed ten percent of the pavement area, preservation is deemed no longer cost‑effective and structural rehabilitation becomes the preferred strategy, reflecting that actual traffic loads are outpacing the original design assumptions [2][1]. Specific overlay treatments have additional limits: a high‑friction surface treatment may not be placed directly over pavements with rutting greater than ¼ in., while high‑intensity‑rubber overlays are acceptable for any ADT provided the underlying distress thresholds are satisfied, confirming alignment with expected traffic loading [2]. The ride‑quality component is expressed through the International Roughness Index and incorporated into a present serviceability index (PSI); if the PSI remains above the terminal serviceability index (TSI) defined for the road’s functional class, the remaining service life is considered adequate, whereas a PSI below TSI signals that rehabilitation or replacement is required to meet design performance criteria [2]. Across both guides, when any of these quantitative thresholds are not met—whether slab depth, material quality percentages, distress magnitudes, or serviceability indices—the pavement is judged to have insufficient residual capacity relative to its design traffic assumptions and is slated for removal, structural improvement, or full reconstruction [1][2].

Data interpretation and diagnosis

Visual inspections systematically record all observable distresses—cracking, rutting, raveling, potholes, joint spalling, faulting and drainage problems—and immediately relate each defect to its likely cause while comparing the measured severity with the limits set out in Publication 242 such as a rut depth less than one‑and‑a‑half inches, crack depth under one inch, average crack width not exceeding a half‑inch, and a concrete patching limit of ten percent [2]. The visual survey also notes surface conditions that cannot be satisfactorily repaired, including rain‑induced damage, improper joint construction or curing, honeycombing, depressions or high points as defined in the applicable specifications [1][2]. Each year the visual data are supplemented by STAMPP automated surveys that capture the same distress types on a network scale, providing quantitative confirmation of the field‑observed conditions [2]. Field measurements supply numeric values that corroborate the visual findings; Falling Weight Deflectometer tests performed before and after construction quantify structural stiffness, dynamic cone penetrometer or lightweight deflectometer readings assess layer uniformity, and additional measurements of pavement thickness, faulting and deflection are cross‑checked against observed distress [2]. Skid‑resistance testing adds safety information, while the measured average pavement depth is compared to the design thickness minus one‑half inch, with a lot deemed defective when this threshold is not met [1][2]. Representative cores taken from deteriorated joints and random locations are sent to an accredited laboratory where compressive strength, plastic‑air content, moisture‑density, CBR or resilient modulus and percent patching are determined; the lot is classified as defective if the Percent Within Limits for compressive strength or plastic air content falls below 55 percent [1][2]. Performance data—including Equivalent Single Axle Load traffic histories, historical construction and maintenance records, and ride quality expressed as the International Roughness Index—are layered onto the combined visual‑measurement‑lab dataset to generate a Present Serviceability Index that is compared with terminal serviceability thresholds for decision making [2]. Post‑rehabilitation IRI measurements are then used to verify compliance with Publication 408 smoothness criteria, completing the assessment loop and informing whether preservation treatments such as hot in‑place recycling or full rehabilitation are appropriate [2]. All of these data streams—visual observations, automated surveys, field nondestructive tests, laboratory results and performance histories—are integrated within treatment matrices and severity thresholds to determine the most suitable engineering response for each pavement segment [1][2].

Observed distress is commonly linked to construction‑related problems and material deterioration, including surface damage caused by rain, improper joint construction, inadequate curing or final finish, cracking and honeycombing, indicating poor workmanship and early breakdown of materials. [1] In addition, insufficient drainage that allows excess moisture to infiltrate the base and subgrade erodes structural capacity and accelerates cracking, rutting and other distresses. [2] Weak or non‑uniform support layers identified through deflection testing such as FWD reveal soft subbase material, which predisposes jointed concrete pavements to premature mid‑slab cracking and contributes to surface rut depths that approach the allowable limit of 1½ inches. [2] Overloading beyond design traffic, particularly heavy truck volumes of about 200 ESALs per day or more, imposes fatigue stresses that manifest as cracking, raveling, and rutting when the observed rut depth nears the 1½‑inch threshold. [2] Material degradation further contributes, with aggregate issues such as Vanport limestone susceptibility, alkali‑silica reaction, frost damage, high void content and low abrasion resistance of cold‑recycled mixes producing rough, pitted surfaces that may exceed the medium rating for raveling/weathering. [2] Construction defects amplify these effects; excessive Portland cement in the base can cause shrinkage cracking, while insufficient curing time or thickness leaves cold‑recycled layers with low early strength and compressive strength or plastic air content below 55 % of limits. [1][2] Inadequate pavement depth—average thickness less than the design thickness minus ½ inch—also reflects substandard construction practices that reduce structural capacity. [1] Because surface treatments cannot compensate for soft or unsound bases, the presence of any underlying support or drainage weakness points directly to these causes rather than to aging alone. Findings, confidence, and next actions

The Pavement Condition Assessment generated through the STAMPP program provides a quantitative, network‑wide picture of present serviceability and structural health. By systematically recording surface‑visible distresses such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling, joint spalling and faulting, and by measuring those items against FHWA severity thresholds, analysts can assign each segment to a good, fair or poor category. A segment is deemed poor when at least two of its three component scores—International Roughness Index, crack percentage, and either rut depth for asphalt or faulting for concrete—are rated poor. This classification supports the treatment‑matrix process that ranges from routine preservation to full rehabilitation, and it also enables planners to calculate the share of the Interstate system in excellent or good condition (greater than 85 % rating) versus poor condition (less than 75 % rating). The resulting mileage percentages can be compared with the FHWA requirement that no more than five percent of a state’s interstate lane‑miles be in poor condition, and out‑of‑cycle segments can be flagged for priority action. In‑situ investigations such as falling weight deflectometer profiles, dynamic cone penetrometer blows, targeted core sampling near deteriorated joints, and other non‑destructive techniques provide insight into subgrade uniformity and the feasibility of full‑depth reclamation. Sampling protocols require at least three samples per lane spaced at 500‑foot intervals, which together with the distress inventory feed the development of recommended treatments for each segment. Although these treatment recommendations are not intended as final design solutions, they give a consistent basis for evaluating the magnitude of required preservation or rehabilitation work and for estimating construction effectiveness. Post‑construction ride quality is verified by measuring IRI after reopening to traffic; compliance with Publication 408 smoothness criteria offers an objective check on whether the new surface meets prescribed roughness limits. Serviceability can also be expressed through the Present Serviceability Index, which combines measured roughness, crack density, patch area and rut depth into a 0‑to‑5 scale. When a segment’s PSI falls below the terminal serviceability level defined for its functional classification, engineers infer that preservation actions are no longer sufficient and rehabilitation should be considered. Nevertheless, several uncertainties constrain the conclusions that can be drawn. The automated surveys capture only surface‑visible distress; underlying mechanisms such as premature mid‑slab cracking caused by inadequate subbase material or aggregate reactions remain hidden unless supplemental investigations are performed. The sampling density may not fully represent spatial variability, and point‑based test results are relative rather than absolute, leaving ambiguity about unobserved damage and future traffic loads. Treatment matrices derived from the condition data are intentionally less extensive than detailed engineering designs, creating uncertainty about the true scope of rehabilitation required. Reliable use of IRI trends also depends on timely and complete Pavement History records; missing or delayed entries—particularly for non‑NHS routes—can cause pre‑opening IRI values to misrepresent current conditions. Finally, the classification scheme relies on only four distress metrics (IRI, cracking, rutting/faulting, occasionally spalling) and omits other defects such as potholes, while the PSI/PSR models are regression‑based and, since 1995, PSR has been reduced to a function of roughness alone, reducing the amount of condition information incorporated into serviceability estimates. [2]

[2] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 6 (pp. 150–151), Ch. 11 (p. 197), Ch. 13 (pp. 216–221), Ch. 14 (p. 227), App. J (pp. 267–272)

When a Pavement Condition Assessment identifies distress, the first engineering response is to verify that the measured pavement depth does not fall below the design thickness minus one‑half inch and that compressive‑strength and plastic‑air‑content samples show at least fifty‑five percent of values within the specified limits; a detailed visual inspection also confirms the absence of rain damage, joint or curing defects, cracking or honeycombing that cannot be repaired, any lot failing these checks being classified as defective【1】. The segment in which the distress occurs must then be entered into the Roadway Management System within four weeks for department‑managed projects or six weeks for contractor‑executed work, and a baseline International Roughness Index measurement is taken no later than three months after entry so that smoothness can be compared with Publication 408 criteria; subsequent IRI collections are scheduled annually or biennially on non‑NHS routes to monitor performance over time【2】. Prior to selecting a preservation or rehabilitation strategy, confirmatory nondestructive testing is performed: skid‑friction values are obtained with a Single Locked Wheel Skid Tester, ride quality is captured by high‑speed laser profilers, and structural capacity is evaluated using a Falling Weight Deflectometer, while subgrade strength is measured with Dynamic Cone Penetrometer or Light Weight Deflectometer tests to provide the modulus or CBR values needed for mechanistic design calculations【2】. Representative material samples are taken at intervals of no more than five hundred feet per lane, with a minimum of three cores per lane, and these cores are sent to the laboratory for grading, moisture‑density, and strength testing; the same cores also support rejuvenator dosage design for high‑intensity reclamation projects【2】. Field verification of existing layer thickness confirms that any asphalt layers exceed the planned milling depth by at least one‑and‑a‑half inches and that the top two inches contain no rubber or fiber material before milled‑recycled or HIR treatments are applied, while cold‑recycling projects require a minimum of two inches of remaining asphalt, traffic volumes below two hundred ESALs per day, and polymer‑modified emulsions when average daily traffic exceeds roughly three thousand vehicles or truck percentages are high; drainage problems must be corrected before reclamation, base repair, or overlay work proceeds【2】. The test results are used to compute effective structural capacity—D_eff for rigid/composite pavements or SN_eff for flexible pavements—and these values are compared with the required capacity for projected traffic to ensure that an inappropriate overlay, such as a bonded PCC over severely cracked concrete, is avoided; if rut depth exceeds one‑quarter inch, surface preparation is required either by milling and shot blasting to achieve a Concrete Surface Profile of five or by applying a wearing course at least thirty days prior to high‑friction surface treatments on open‑graded or gap‑graded pavements【2】. Preservation actions driven by severity ratings include cyclic crack sealing, joint sealing, micro‑surfacing, diamond grinding, and targeted milling, whereas more extensive distress such as premature mid‑slab cracking may trigger base repair or a full‑depth overlay; load‑related distress leads to structural preservation measures such as slab stabilization, slabjacking, partial‑depth repair, dowel retrofits, or cross‑stitching, and problems linked to drainage, joint condition, or material durability call for drainage improvements, joint cleaning and sealing, diamond grinding, or replacement of deficient concrete【2】. An out‑of‑cycle analysis using the Overall Pavement Index flags mileage with Excellent/Good scores above eighty‑five percent versus Poor scores below seventy‑five percent, guiding prioritization of corrective measures; when the total percent of patching exceeds ten percent (excluding existing concrete patches in good condition), a cost‑effectiveness analysis is required to determine whether an alternative treatment would be more appropriate【2】. Additional field investigations include coring near deteriorated joints and at random locations to assess subbase or subgrade conditions, fault‑measurement surveys, ground‑penetrating radar scans, and specialized concrete scans such as MIT Scan 2, MIT Scan T2, and tensile bond tests; design checks must ensure that any overlay or reconstruction meets at least the Terminal Serviceability Index for the segment’s Maintenance Functional Classification, with a higher TSI permissible for high‑traffic facilities, while other design thresholds—maximum allowable air content, minimum compressive strength, and friction adequacy—are also verified【2】. If any lot is found to be defective based on depth, material quality, or unrepairable surface defects, it must be removed and replaced by a minimum of ten feet between transverse joints for reinforced cement concrete pavements or an entire panel for plain cement concrete pavement, using in‑kind joint construction; longitudinal edge spall repairs are not acceptable for edge slumping or spalling issues【1】. Post‑construction monitoring is carried out through repeat Falling Weight Deflectometer testing and IRI measurements to verify that the rehabilitated surface satisfies the smoothness criteria of Publication 408, with all data entered into the RMS and Access tools to track distress trends, guide‑rail and drainage inventories, and support future asset‑management decisions【2】.
