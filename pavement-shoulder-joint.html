Pavement-Shoulder Joint

Part of: Longitudinal Joint is-subtopic-of
Pavement-Shoulder Joint is a topic under Longitudinal Joint.
pavement-shoulder-jointpavement-shoulder-joints
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

The Pavement‑Shoulder Joint is the longitudinal interface between a concrete pavement slab and an adjacent asphalt shoulder that is treated as a joint in rigid‑pavement specifications[1][2]. In every Concrete Pavement Restoration project it must be cleaned, a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir formed, and the joint sealed with rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material to keep water, chlorides and other incompressible materials out of the structure while providing load‑transfer continuity[1]. The joint is required to limit edge slumping and longitudinal surface deviation to no more than a quarter inch as measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge (or a four‑foot straightedge when cross‑slope changes), thereby ensuring a smooth vehicle transition, proper load transfer, and durability[2].

Purpose and design objectives

The Pavement‑Shoulder Joint is incorporated and sealed so that water, chlorides and other incompressible materials cannot infiltrate the concrete pavement at the interface with an asphalt shoulder, a condition that would otherwise compromise structural integrity and durability; during each CPR project the joint must be cleaned, a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir constructed, and the joint sealed with rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material, whether or not the shoulder is being rebuilt [1]. The joint is also installed to control the elevation change where the main slab meets the shoulder so that the longitudinal joint does not develop an excessive step or dip; under Section 501.3(o) the surface at all longitudinal joints, including the pavement‑shoulder joint, must be within 1/4 inch when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge and any deviation beyond this is considered defective, limiting edge slumping to no more than 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders and thereby ensuring a smooth vehicle transition and proper load transfer across the joint [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

The Pavement‑Shoulder Joint must be cleaned and sealed in every CPR project, with a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir filled using rubberized/asphalt joint sealing material, to ensure that water, chlorides, and incompressible materials cannot infiltrate the pavement structure; this action supports durability and preserves structural integrity while also requiring a stable subgrade and functional drainage for an effective CPR project [1]. Resealing is required as soon as joints begin to exhibit distress, even if serviceability appears satisfactory at that time, thereby meeting safety and serviceability objectives [1]. In addition, the joint must be constructed so that edge slump measured on the six‑inch zone at the pavement edge does not exceed one‑quarter inch between lane and shoulder during placement and after hardening, with any excess corrected, to provide functional continuity and structural alignment [2]. After curing, the longitudinal joint at the pavement‑shoulder interface must meet a surface‑tolerance limit of no more than one‑quarter inch as measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge across or perpendicular to the joint, and depressions of three‑sixteenths inch or greater between adjacent lanes are considered defective; these limits furnish durability against edge slumping, safety through acceptable ride quality, and serviceability by maintaining prescribed surface smoothness [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

Scope and applicability

The pavement‑shoulder joint design is appropriate for concrete pavements that incorporate an asphalt shoulder and must be addressed in every Concrete Pavement Restoration (CPR) project. [1] It should be performed whether or not the asphalt shoulder will be reconstructed, using a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir and sealing the joint with rubberized/asphalt joint sealing material. [1] The joint is also specified for rigid pavements, specifically plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements covered by Section 500 and Section 501, where longitudinal joints are required at the interface between the main slab and the shoulder. [2] For these rigid pavements the joint must satisfy the surface‑tolerance limits for longitudinal joints set out in Section 501.3(o), which require that the joint deviation not exceed ¼ inch when there is no change in cross‑slope, and the same limit applies when a cross‑slope change exists. [2] In addition, edge‑slump control during placement applies to the pavement‑shoulder interface, with allowable slump not exceeding ¼ inch between lanes and shoulders. [2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

When a concrete pavement is slated for an overlay, the guidelines state that sealing of joints—including the pavement‑shoulder joint—is not recommended, so an overlay design supersedes joint sealing. Likewise, if the joint exhibits faulting or poor load transfer and the underlying drainage is functional and the subgrade is stable, the preferred remedy shifts to restoring support (slab stabilization or jacking), installing dowel bars, or replacing the joint with a full‑depth concrete patch rather than merely cleaning and resealing it. Joint rehabilitation is limited to restoration projects and therefore is not used when an overlay is planned. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90)

Design inputs and requirements

The pavement‑shoulder joint must be cleaned and sealed in accordance with Publication 408 specifications, specifically Section 512 for longitudinal joints, and the seal must be placed in a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch reservoir using rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material; this treatment is required as part of every concrete pavement rehabilitation (CPR) project regardless of whether the asphalt shoulder will be reconstructed. The pavement‑shoulder joint must meet the requirements set out in Section 501.3 of the Rigid Pavements specifications. Under the slip‑form paving provisions of Section 501.3(g), edge slumping at the lane‑to‑shoulder interface may not exceed 1/4 inch when measured over a six‑inch width adjacent to the pavement edge with a 12‑foot straightedge before the concrete becomes non‑plastic, and if it cannot be controlled the paving must be suspended after 200 feet; any excess slump must be corrected. The longitudinal joint tolerance described in Section 501.3(i) requires that the surface at all longitudinal joints—including the joint between pavement and shoulder—be within 1/4 inch when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge centered across and perpendicular to the joint, or with a 4‑foot straightedge on each side if the cross‑slope changes. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

Design methods and criteria

In every Concrete Pavement Rehabilitation (CPR) project the pavement‑shoulder joint must be cleaned and sealed, with a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir constructed and the joint sealed using rubberized/asphalt joint sealing material, and this work should be performed whether or not the asphalt shoulder will be reconstructed [1]. The edge slumping at the pavement edge is measured before the concrete becomes non‑plastic with a 12‑foot straightedge, and the allowable slump is limited to 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes and 1/4 inch between a lane and its shoulder [2]. After hardening, the surface of every longitudinal joint—including the joint between pavement and shoulder—is rechecked with a 12‑foot straightedge; where there is no change in design cross‑slope the deviation must not be greater than 1/4 inch measured across and perpendicular to the joint [2]. When saw‑cutting joints that involve the shoulder, the cut shall be made to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness (D/3) and a width of 1/8 inch [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

For each Concrete Pavement Restoration (CPR) project the pavement‑shoulder joint must be cleaned and sealed, regardless of whether the asphalt shoulder is being reconstructed. The cleaning and sealing work requires constructing a sealant reservoir that measures 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch and filling it with rubberized or asphalt joint sealing material as specified in Publication 408. During slip‑form paving, edge slumping measured over a six‑inch width at the pavement edge shall not exceed one‑quarter inch between lanes and shoulders (or ramps and shoulders). If such slumping cannot be prevented within a 200‑foot length, the section is evaluated for compliance. After concrete hardens, the longitudinal joint that includes the pavement‑shoulder interface must have a surface tolerance of no greater than one‑quarter inch when checked with a twelve‑foot straightedge across a joint where the design cross‑slope does not change. When a change in design cross‑slope exists, the surface tolerance shall also be no greater than one‑quarter inch measured with a four‑foot straightedge on each side of the joint. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

Structural section and dimensions

The guidelines for a pavement‑shoulder joint specify that the joint be cleaned and sealed by constructing a sealant reservoir that measures 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch, after which rubberized or asphalt sealing material is applied. The source does not provide any additional dimensional requirements for the pavement, base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab, shoulder, edge‑support, or reinforcement associated with this joint. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90)

Materials and mixture design

For a concrete pavement rehabilitation project, the pavement‑shoulder joint shall be cleaned and then a sealant reservoir measuring 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch shall be formed before the joint is filled with rubberized/asphalt joint sealing material; all joints and cracks are to be sealed with rubberized joint sealing material as referenced in Publication 408 Sections 512 and 521. The joint must also incorporate load‑transfer units (dowel bars) installed according to the standard drawings, and any Type B coated dowel bar shall receive a bond‑breaker lubricant applied to at least one‑half of its length at least one hour before concrete placement, whereas Type A bars require no lubricant. After the transverse joint is sawed, an oversized backer rod whose width equals the cut width plus 50 percent shall be placed in the joint cavity. Once the surrounding concrete has cured, the joint faces shall be cleaned, dried and pass a quality‑control wipe test before being sealed with an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant, a neoprene preformed seal, or other manufacturer‑approved material. Any reinforcement that may appear in the joint area shall be free of rust, dirt, oil, grease, or any foreign substance that could impair bond to the concrete. No separate concrete mixture proportions are prescribed for the pavement‑shoulder joint itself; the specifications focus on the listed materials and their handling. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

Structural details and interfaces

The Pavement Policy Manual states that the pavement/shoulder joint should be cleaned and sealed during every CPR project, regardless of whether the asphalt shoulder is being reconstructed; this treatment includes constructing a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir and sealing the joint with rubberized/asphalt joint sealing material, and it applies to the longitudinal joint between the traffic lane and a concrete shoulder [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications treat the pavement‑shoulder interface as a longitudinal joint and require that after the concrete has hardened the surface be checked with a straightedge, with any deviation not exceeding 1/4 inch when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge across the joint or, where the cross‑slope changes, with a 4‑foot straightedge on each side of the joint [2]. Tie bars may be placed in this longitudinal joint but must be located at least 24 inches from any dowelled contraction joint [2]. Load‑transfer units such as dowel bars are installed for transverse joints and must be secured before paving so that saw cuts fall over their centers, while edge slumping at the pavement‑shoulder edge shall not exceed 1/4 inch [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), the pavement‑shoulder interface is treated as a joint that must be cleaned and sealed on every concrete pavement rehabilitation project; the design specifies constructing a sealant reservoir measuring 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch at the joint and filling it with the prescribed rubberized or asphalt joint sealing material, and this work must be performed whether or not the adjacent asphalt shoulder is being rebuilt [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification (Publication 408/2026, Section 501.3(g)) requires that edge slumping at the pavement‑shoulder joint be limited to no more than a quarter inch between lanes and shoulders as measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge before the concrete loses its plasticity, and it further mandates that the longitudinal joint surface tolerance at the interface may not exceed a quarter inch—measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge when cross‑slope is unchanged or with a four‑foot straightedge on each side of the joint when cross‑slope changes—to ensure a smooth transition and structural continuity [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

The pavement‑shoulder joint is required to be cleaned and sealed during every Concrete Pavement Restoration effort, using a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir filled with rubberized or asphalt joint sealing material so that water, chlorides and incompressible materials are kept out of the structure [1]. This requirement presumes that the underlying subgrade is stable and that the existing drainage system is functional, conditions identified as necessary for an effective CPR project [1]. During new construction the joint must be protected from moisture and temperature extremes; rain must be guarded against by covering edges and the surface before the concrete sets, and any edge slumping adjacent to shoulders may not exceed 1/4 inch, with greater slump triggering suspension of paving for evaluation [2]. Joint preparation calls for thorough cleaning, media blasting of the reservoir sidewalls, a wipe test in the presence of the Representative, and placement of an oversized backer rod whose width is plus 50 % of the joint, but only after the joint faces are free of moisture; sealing may not occur until the joint is dry [2]. For cold‑weather conditions the concrete temperature must be kept at least 40 °F; if a high‑low thermometer reads between 40 °F and 35 °F the cure period shall be extended one day, whereas any reading below 35 °F renders the concrete defective [2]. Curing methods such as white polyethylene sheets or monomolecular film must be applied continuously and must not allow the concrete temperature to rise above 160 °F, with insulation removed only when temperature changes do not exceed 40 °F per hour [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

The pavement‑shoulder joint must be cleaned and sealed during every Concrete Pavement Restoration project, even when the asphalt shoulder is not being reconstructed, to keep water, chlorides, and incompressible materials out of the pavement structure and thereby support safety and performance [1]. This treatment requires constructing a sealant reservoir that measures 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch and applying rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material over the joint [1]. The joint must be built so that edge slumping along the joint does not exceed ¼ inch between lanes and shoulders (or ramps and shoulders), as measured on a 12‑foot straightedge across a 6‑inch width adjacent to the edge [2]. The longitudinal surface tolerance at the joint may not be greater than ¼ inch when the cross‑slope is unchanged, again verified with a 12‑foot straightedge centered across and perpendicular to the joint [2]. In addition, the shoulder portion of the joint shall receive a transverse texture consisting of rectangular grooves that are from 3/32 inch to 3/16 inch in width and from 1/8 inch to 3/16 inch in depth to provide adequate skid resistance for users [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

Verification and expected performance

The completed pavement‑shoulder joint is verified by straightedge testing after the concrete has hardened; a 12‑foot straightedge held across and perpendicular to the joint must not show more than 1/4 inch deviation (or, when cross‑slope changes, the same 1/4 inch limit measured with a 4‑foot straightedge). Edge slumping is measured with a 12‑foot straightedge before the concrete becomes non‑plastic and again after hardening, and the slump between lane and shoulder may not exceed 1/4 inch over the 6‑inch width adjacent to the edge. Load transfer units or dowels placed in the joint must be positioned so that vertical tilt or horizontal skew does not exceed 0.25 inch, longitudinal translation does not exceed 1 inch, and horizontal translation does not exceed 1 inch. All measurements, saw‑cutting, cleaning, wipe testing and sealing of the joint are performed in the presence of the Representative to ensure constructability, performance and consistency with project requirements. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

The Pavement‑Shoulder Joint is required to stay within tight geometric tolerances and texture limits that serve as its performance criteria: edge slumping at the shoulder must not exceed one‑quarter inch measured over a six‑inch strip adjacent to the pavement edge with a twelve‑foot straightedge before the concrete loses plasticity (and again after hardening); the longitudinal joint surface, including the pavement‑shoulder interface, must be smooth enough that a twelve‑foot straightedge (or a four‑foot straightedge when cross‑slope changes) shows no more than one‑quarter inch deviation; and the finished surface must achieve at least a 0.040‑inch mean texture depth, verified by ASTM E965 testing at a minimum of four locations per lane. These measurements directly define the joint’s expected serviceability, distress resistance, and ride quality. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)
