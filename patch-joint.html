Patch Joint

Part of: Concrete Patching is-subtopic-of
Patch Joint is a topic under Concrete Patching.
patch-joints
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

Patch Joint is a concrete‑pavement repair technique that removes locally deteriorated or broken slab sections and replaces them with new concrete to restore the structural condition and functional performance of the roadway [1][2]. The method uses full‑depth (or, when appropriate, partial‑depth) saw cuts extending beyond the distressed zone, removes unsound material, installs load‑transfer dowels anchored with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders, seals longitudinal and transverse joints, and places a Class AA Modified, accelerated or rapid‑set concrete mix that is finished to match the existing surface [2]. It is applied where severe joint loss, spalling beyond the top half of the slab, transverse‑joint faulting, voids at the slab‑subbase interface, corner breaks or overall settlement are observed, and it may be combined with slab‑stabilization by injecting cement grout, hot‑asphalt or high‑density polyurethane to re‑establish support [1][2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

In the concrete‑pavement patching context, Patch Joint is applied when a slab is badly deteriorated or broken and localized distress such as major working cracks, crushed or deteriorated joints, or loss of load transfer at transverse joints threatens serviceability [1]. The method also requires full‑depth replacement where spalling extends beyond the top half of the slab depth because partial‑depth repair would not correct the underlying joint problem [1]. In the slab‑stabilization context, Patch Joint is used where voids have formed at the slab‑subbase interface, producing a loss of support that appears as transverse‑joint faulting, fines or small depressions (blow holes) near joints or cracks, corner breaks and overall slab settlement [1]. For pavements in which a crack, spall, or break‑back occurs at any location before final inspection, the damaged concrete is removed and replaced with a full‑depth Type A (or B) patch anchored by dowel bars using full‑depth saw cuts that extend beyond the affected area [2]. When concrete delamination reaches an existing joint, the specification calls for removal of unsound concrete to at least 2 inches depth—never exceeding half the slab thickness—and the establishment or sealing of the joint after placing a partial‑depth repair with limits extending approximately 3 inches beyond the edges and dimensions of at least 15 inches long, 10 inches wide and 2 inches deep [2]. The technique is also specified for joints or cracks that exhibit excessive vertical movement, namely when a loaded slab corner deflection reaches 0.02 inch or more and joint efficiency falls below 65 %, in which case the deficient joint should be patched and stabilized if approved by the representative [2]. For localized damage at longitudinal joints such as break‑back or spalling, full‑depth transverse saw cuts are made beyond the damaged zone, unsuitable subbase material is removed, and any surface irregularities exceeding 1 inch in depth are corrected before placing a new concrete patch that restores joint continuity [2]. Finally, for localized surface distress on rigid pavements, the partial‑depth Patch Joint repair involves cleaning the area, applying grout or epoxy bonding compound, and placing fresh concrete or rapid‑set patching material into saw cuts that extend beyond the corners of the distressed zone so the patch blends with the surrounding pavement [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–429), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, the patch‑joint treatment for concrete pavement is intended principally to extend the roadway’s useful life by removing deteriorated sections and placing new concrete that should provide adequate long‑term performance [1]. The same manual notes that this replacement also inherently repairs the damaged area while restoring load transfer through dowelled joints, thereby recovering functionality of the repaired segment [1]. In slab‑stabilization applications the manual states that a patch joint is applied to fill voids beneath the slab so that full support is restored and the pavement’s structural integrity can be regained, which constitutes repair of damage and restoration of functionality [1]. The Department of Transportation specifications describe the Patch Joint as a repair‑oriented method whose objective is to restore functionality of the pavement by repairing damaged concrete or joints [2]. For partial‑depth repairs (Section 525) the specification requires locating delamination, laying rectangular limits that extend about 3 inches beyond the defect, removing all unsound concrete to a minimum depth of 2 inches and a maximum depth of half the slab thickness, installing joint material when the repair abuts an existing transverse joint, and placing the repair mix so that the repaired area regains structural continuity, all actions aimed at repairing damage and restoring functionality [2]. For slab‑stabilization (Section 679) the specification applies the patch joint to joints or cracks that have a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more and whose joint efficiency meets the specified thresholds (65 % or more, or less than 65 % if approved), stabilizing them with grout injection and then fully patching the drill holes with rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete to restore pavement performance [2].

Distress characterization and causes

The distress that a patch‑joint repair targets occurs beneath the concrete slab where small voids form near transverse joints and cracks, especially at outside slab corners, and can also appear as blow holes in the shoulder at those locations. These voids cause loss of slab support, producing excessive deflections and stresses that first manifest as joint faulting, then progress to corner breaks, diagonal cracking, and ultimately complete breakup of the slab. Because the resulting faulting, cracks and depressions compromise ride quality and can expose traffic to hazardous surface irregularities, the condition has clear safety and operational consequences for road users. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

The distress that patch‑joint repairs target originates primarily from loss of load transfer across transverse joints and severe joint deterioration, which concentrate damage at slab bottoms near joints and cracks and permit spalling and bottom‑up cracking. When high early strength concrete is used, excessive shrinkage cracking may develop, and poor placement quality can produce non‑uniform support beneath the slab. Water infiltration exacerbates the problem by creating pumping action; subgrade consolidation and settlement can generate small voids beneath the slab, especially adjacent to transverse joints and corners, leading to loss of support, excessive deflections and stresses, joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and eventual slab breakup. The resulting slab movement—rocking, pumping or faulting—is linked to inadequate subdrainage and weak or unstable support, while material aging contributes to surface spalling. To counter these mechanisms full‑depth patching removes the deteriorated concrete and replaces it with new material, securing dowels along their entire embedded length to restore proper load transfer. Stabilization of the slab is required when movement or voids are present; this may be achieved by filling each identified void with a cement grout mixture at a rate of 1 cubic foot of grout per hole, applying hot‑asphalt undersealing, or using high‑density polyurethane as prescribed. Joint and crack sealing must be performed together with slab stabilization to limit water entry, and when no preliminary testing has been conducted at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized. Prior to any bonded overlay, adequate subdrainage and a stable subgrade must be ensured, because bonded JPCP overlays are not appropriate for pavements with unresolved unstable support or drainage deficiencies. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98)

Applicability and treatment selection

Patch Joint, a full‑depth concrete pavement patching technique, is deemed appropriate for rigid pavements in which the existing slab is badly deteriorated or broken, or where spalling is confined to the upper half of the slab and not caused by severe joint loss; under these circumstances the manual recommends full‑depth replacement rather than partial‑depth repair except when surface spalling is limited. The method is also specified for Class AA cement concrete pavements, including Modified, Accelerated, or rapid‑set concrete patching materials, whenever a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair is required. For slab‑stabilization applications the technique may be used on Portland‑cement concrete pavements that exhibit loss of support manifested as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks, small depressions at shoulders, corner breaks, or general slab settlement, conditions that indicate underlying voids suitable for filling with cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane. When the repair is performed on a high‑traffic route an early opening to traffic is advantageous because accelerated concrete can achieve usable strength in four to seven hours. Traffic may be allowed onto the repaired area for up to seventy‑two hours after saw cutting provided that any opened cuts do not exceed one‑half inch in width, and excavated sections must be backfilled within forty‑eight hours, excluding weekends or holidays, with the return‑to‑service time considered before reopening. During slab‑stabilization work traffic should be restricted until the selected sealing material cures—twelve hours for cement grout or a minimum of thirty minutes for hot asphalt or polyurethane—and the operation should be confined to a single lane using appropriate temporary traffic control. Placement of the patch material is permitted only when ambient air and surface temperatures are forty degrees Fahrenheit or greater and rising; placement must cease if temperature falls below forty degrees, and the repaired area must be protected from freezing for at least twenty‑four hours. If during curing the ambient temperature drops below eighty degrees Fahrenheit insulation or heating shall be provided to maintain acceptable cure conditions. Environmental conditions that promote water infiltration must be mitigated by sealing joints and cracks and maintaining subsurface drains. Site preparation shall avoid damage to adjacent pavement and limit disturbance of the subbase, and any sub‑base surface irregularities exceeding one inch in depth must be corrected before new concrete is placed. Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only for concrete patches longer than sixty‑five feet. If intermittent patches are needed over a length of approximately five hundred feet with less than ten feet of original pavement remaining between them, the entire section should be removed and reconstructed rather than patched. Expansion‑joint material together with preformed cellular polystyrene forms that extend to the pavement surface shall be used so that the new concrete does not intrude on existing joints. When no preliminary testing has been performed, at least twenty‑five percent of transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 416–429)

When the repair area extends roughly ±500 feet and the remaining intact pavement between individual patches is 10 feet or less, patch joints are considered unsuitable and the entire section should be removed and reconstructed. Likewise, if more than 500 feet of pavement in all lanes must be replaced, a full design analysis for slab thickness is required rather than isolated patching. For projects that will receive an overlay, it may be preferable to clean and fill spalled concrete with asphalt or use leveling courses instead of performing partial‑depth repairs or joint rehabilitation because only a uniformly supporting base is needed. Patch Joint repair should be avoided when the pavement problem includes surface depressions that need correction, a requirement to increase the design structural capacity, or the need to eliminate faulting, because slab stabilization does not correct those conditions; in such situations another maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair approach must be selected. Under Section 525 of the Pennsylvania specifications, a partial‑depth patch joint is unsuitable when delamination extends through an existing pavement joint and the repair cannot be performed by maintaining the joint as two separate minimum repair areas unless a detailed re‑establishment procedure is included in the QC Plan. The method also should not be used if dowel bars exposed in the transverse joint are misaligned or extensively deteriorated, because the Representative must then specify a full‑depth concrete pavement patch or corner repair. If removal of unsound concrete becomes excessive or damages the dowel bars such that the repair area effectively turns into a full‑depth patch, a full‑depth concrete pavement patch as described in Section 516 must be constructed. The patching method may not be applied when the required saw cuts would intersect or lie too close to existing cracks; saw‑cuts must not be located within 24 inches of an existing crack and must not be cut across an existing crack. When a cut would damage reinforcement bars, corrective action requires converting the partial‑depth cut to a full‑depth cut and adding another partial‑depth cut as specified; otherwise the patch is unsuitable. Use of the wheel saw shall be discontinued if unsatisfactory results are obtained as determined by the Representative. Patch Joint should not be used when the area to be repaired is narrower than one lane width, because the specification explicitly prohibits patching less than one lane width. For Type A patches, the required length must be between 6 feet and 20 feet when tied splices are used or between 4 feet and 20 feet when welded splices are used; if these dimensional criteria cannot be met, another maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair approach should be considered. For Type B patches, the required length must be between 20.1 feet and 65 feet; when this range cannot be satisfied, another approach should be considered. For Type C patches, the required length must be between 65.1 feet and 500 feet; when the dimensional criteria are not met, another maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair approach should be considered. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 414–428)

Condition assessment and timing

Determining whether a patch joint is required begins with a visual inspection of the pavement surface to locate existing transverse or patch joints and any loss‑of‑support symptoms such as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes), corner breaks, or overall slab settlement. For concrete pavements the inspector must also measure the total length of the distressed roadway; if the damaged stretch is about ±500 feet and the spacing between individual patches is ten feet or less, the entire section should be removed and reconstructed. When more than five‑hundred feet of pavement in all lanes requires replacement, a pavement design analysis shall be performed to decide whether a thicker slab depth is needed. The length of each proposed patch must be measured because longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only for concrete patches that exceed sixty‑five feet. In the partial‑depth repair specification an inspector, in the presence of the Representative, sounds the concrete with a steel bar, drag chains or a hammer to locate delamination before any measurement is taken. The identified delaminated area is then measured and rectangular limits are laid out extending approximately three inches beyond the edges, with each repair rectangle required to be at least 15 inches long, 10 inches wide and 2 inches deep. If two partial‑depth areas are spaced less than 24 inches apart they shall be combined into a single repair. When delamination crosses an existing joint the inspector confirms that the joint will be retained as two separate minimum repair areas unless the QC Plan provides a re‑establishment procedure. Before deciding on a patch joint, visual verification of joint condition shall be performed when air temperature is between 40 °F and 70 °F; testing shall not be performed if temperature is below 40 °F or exceeds 70 °F. If no preliminary testing has been performed, each joint and crack shall be tested if directed by the Representative in accordance with ASTM D4695, and the test data must include the loaded slab corner deflection and the calculated joint efficiency for each joint or crack. Any joint or crack that shows a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more and a joint efficiency of less than 65 % shall be patch‑and‑stabilized, whereas joints or cracks with a joint efficiency of 65 % or more shall be stabilized only; the Representative must review and approve the test results before work proceeds. When no preliminary testing has been performed, the guidelines advise that at least twenty‑five percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints be stabilized. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 427–428), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Patch Joint is applied when localized pavement distress is observed and quantified through actual field measurements, and the quantity of patch material shall anticipate any additional deterioration that may develop between the design field view and construction (section 3.3) [1]. For concrete pavements, full‑depth Patch Joint repair should be used when the pavement is badly deteriorated or slabs are broken, and it is required where joint deterioration at transverse‑longitudinal intersections extends beyond the mid‑slab depth, or where spalling penetrates deeper than the top half of the slab or is accompanied by loss of load transfer; in those situations partial‑depth repair is not appropriate (section 4.2) [1]. Patch Joint repair is also indicated when a slab has lost support, which is signaled by transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes) at a joint or crack, corner breaks, or general slab settlement; under those conditions all patch joints should be stabilized and, if no preliminary testing has been performed, the guidance calls for stabilizing every patch joint and at least 25 percent of transverse joints (section 4.5) [1]. When concrete delamination is detected by sounding the slab with a steel bar, drag chains, or a hammer, a partial‑depth Patch Joint should be placed over a rectangular repair area that extends approximately three inches beyond the edges of the delaminated zone and meets minimum dimensions of fifteen inches in length, ten inches in width, and two inches in depth; if separate delaminations are spaced less than twenty‑four inches apart they are combined into one repair (section 525.3) [2]. If the delamination runs through an existing pavement joint, two minimum repair areas are maintained to preserve the joint unless a detailed joint‑reestablishment procedure is included in the quality‑control plan (section 525.3) [2]. Additionally, under slab stabilization requirements, a Patch Joint should be applied to any joint or crack that exhibits a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more and a joint efficiency of less than 65 percent, provided the representative approves the treatment (section 679.3) [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 427–428), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Materials and repair details

Patch‑joint repair begins with thorough cleaning and sealing of both longitudinal and transverse concrete joints, and an asphalt tack coat is applied when required while heavy‑duty membranes are used as necessary [1]. A sealant reservoir is then constructed at the joint and the joint is sealed before any new material is placed [2]. Load‑transfer devices are required at the transverse joints and all patch joints shall be dowelled; the dowels are anchored to the existing pavement with epoxy, polyester binders or vinylester binders that must be bonded along their entire embedded length [1]. Coated dowel bars are installed in holes no larger than ⅛ inch greater than the bar diameter and the anchoring material must reach an initial set within five minutes; a bond‑breaker lubricant is applied to at least one‑half of the length of Type B coated dowel bars at least one hour before concrete placement [2]. For reinforced concrete pavements, designers specify new joint items as either skewed or non‑skewed baskets in accordance with RC‑26M, and joints are spaced at 30 feet with perpendicular orientation for jointed reinforced concrete pavement and at 15 feet for jointed plain concrete pavement [1]. The length of each patch is limited by type: Type A patches may be between 6 feet and 20 feet, Type B between 20.1 feet and 65 feet, and Type C between 65.1 feet and 500 feet, and every patch must be at least one lane width wide [2]. Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only when the concrete patch exceeds 65 feet in length [1]. Full‑depth saw‑cuts are made at the existing pavement/patch interface; cuts opened to traffic may not exceed ½ inch in width and a temporary rigid separator greater than ⅛ inch thick shall not be used [2]. When rebuilding an expansion joint, ¾‑inch expansion joint material is installed (½‑inch for corner repairs) and coated dowel bars are placed as described above [2]. For partial‑depth patches the damaged area is laid out as a rectangle extending approximately three inches beyond delaminated edges with minimum dimensions of 15 inches long by 10 inches wide by 2 inches deep; if the repair abuts an existing transverse joint, a ½‑inch expansion joint material is installed, and if it abuts a longitudinal joint a ¼‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene form extending at least ½ inch below the repair bottom is used [2]. The patch material for partial‑depth repairs is Class AA cement concrete—either a modified mix, an accelerated variant, or a rapid‑set concrete patching material—placed after exposed surfaces are cleaned and coated with a thin layer of grout or epoxy bonding compound; fresh concrete is consolidated with a spud vibrator (or equivalent) and forced into the saw cuts before being struck off to match the surrounding pavement profile, including wheel ruts [2]. Continuously reinforced patches require full‑depth, continuously reinforced cement concrete extending at least one lane width; reinforcement is installed per Section 1002.3 using tied or welded splices that lap new bars with existing ones, and weld splices are placed at the patch centre to accommodate expansion [2]. Slab stabilization may be incorporated by filling voids beneath the concrete with either a cement grout estimated at 1 cubic foot per hole (or 0.25 bag of cement per hole), hot‑asphalt undersealing typically between 30 and 50 gallons per hole, or high‑density polyurethane applied at about 0.4 pounds per square foot of slab; when no preliminary testing has been performed, at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized [1]. For stabilization of joints or cracks showing a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more and a joint efficiency of less than 65 percent (or greater than 65 percent for stabilization only), full‑depth holes no larger than 1½ inches in diameter are drilled vertically to a depth that penetrates any stabilized base, filled with cement grout injected by a positive‑displacement pump at pressures not exceeding 200 psi while an expanding rubber packer is lowered but not extended below the pavement surface; each hole is then patched full depth with rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete patching material flush with the surrounding pavement and traffic may not be reopened until the selected material has cured, cooled or hardened as specified for that product (no sooner than 12 hours after grouting) [2][1]. After sawing operations, traffic may use the area for no more than 72 hours, excavated zones must be patched within 48 hours, and any disturbed subbase greater than 1 inch is re‑compacted with small vibratory compactors or replaced; full‑depth saw cuts at repair limits may extend transversely into adjacent pavement up to full depth plus 2 inches provided dowel bars in the adjacent lane are not damaged [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–429), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

The repair must be matched to the measured condition of the existing pavement by using the same type and depth of asphalt or concrete as the surrounding surface, with field measurements dictating the quantity of material placed. When the underlying slab has lost support, slab stabilization is required before the patch is applied, and an asphalt tack coat may be used if adhesion is a concern. Proper cleaning and sealing of longitudinal, transverse, and existing patch joints, employing heavy‑duty membranes as needed, ensures compatibility between the new overlay and the existing pavement, while continuous base drainage helps limit further deterioration. Adequate load transfer at transverse joints is achieved by dowelling every joint and anchoring the dowels with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders that must be bonded along their entire embedded length. Patches longer than 65 feet require longitudinal tie bars or bolts to maintain stability. High early‑strength concrete should be avoided because of its potential for excessive shrinkage cracking; accelerated or standard concrete mixes are preferred when early traffic opening is needed. The grout selected must possess properties that allow it to fill voids effectively, with options including fluid cement grout pumped under the slab, hot asphalt that must cool before traffic returns, and high‑density polyurethane—a low‑viscosity two‑component material that expands into a lightweight rigid closed‑cell foam and is resistant to water and chemical attack with long‑term durability. Compatibility also requires joint and crack sealing be performed together with the stabilization, and subsurface drains must remain in good condition; otherwise water will continue to enter and cause pumping. For pavements where no preliminary testing has been performed, at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints shall be stabilized according to the slab‑stabilization guidelines. The anchoring material used to embed coated dowel bars must achieve an initial set within five minutes. Concrete placed for the patch must reach at least 1,000 psi compressive strength before traffic is allowed. Rapid‑set patching compounds must be cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. A bond‑breaker lubricant must be applied to at least half of each Type B dowel bar. Expansion joints must receive the prescribed 3/4‑inch (or 1/2‑inch for corner repairs) joint material. Full‑depth saw cuts may not exceed a width of ½ inch, and subbase irregularities deeper than one inch must be corrected before placement. Any voids around anchored dowels must be less than 0.50 inch. Surface high points or depressions greater than 3/16 inch must be ground before opening to traffic, and excavated areas may not remain un‑patched for more than 48 hours. For partial‑depth repairs the distressed area shall be laid out at least three inches beyond the edges with minimum dimensions of 15 × 10 × 2 inches; when distress crosses a transverse joint, the joint must be retained by installing 1/2‑inch expansion joint material, and when it crosses a longitudinal joint, the joint must be retained by installing 1/4‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene that extends at least one‑half inch below the repair bottom. The repair mix must be placed after a final cleaning using a compressed‑air stream free of oil and moisture with a minimum pressure of 100 psi measured at the source, and any exposed dowel bars that are not severely corroded require a bond‑breaking coating to ensure proper bonding. Grout or epoxy bonding compound must be applied to all exposed surfaces immediately after final cleaning, and fresh concrete must be placed before the grout dries or the epoxy becomes non‑tacky. Placement is permitted only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F and rising; work must be discontinued if temperatures fall below 40 °F or outside the manufacturer’s recommended range, a minimum of 24 hours of protection from freezing is required, and during curing of Class AA accelerated repairs insulation or heating is required if ambient temperature drops below 80 °F while temperature changes may not exceed 40 °F in any one‑hour period. Slab stabilization requires a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete patching material applied full depth and struck flush; work may not start if daytime temperatures are below 40 °F or the subgrade/base is frozen, joints must exhibit a loaded slab corner deflection of at least 0.02 inch, joint efficiency may be 65 % or more for stabilization alone or less than 65 % when patching and stabilizing, upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 inch during grout injection is not allowed, traffic may not be permitted until at least 12 hours after grouting, and wooden plugs must be removed and drill holes patched full depth with rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete. Continuously reinforced concrete pavement patching requires saw cuts to be kept at least 24 inches away from any existing crack, full‑depth cuts must avoid cutting reinforcement bars, if a break back exceeds 2 inches or reinforcement is damaged a new full‑depth transverse cut must be made, wheel‑saw penetration of the subbase may not exceed ½ inch, and any disturbance deeper than 1 inch requires removal of the affected material and replacement with concrete. When a bond breaker is present it must be removed and replaced with two layers of polyethylene sheeting that are at least 4.0 mil thick before new concrete is placed, and reinforcement in the patch must be tied or welded and lapped to the existing bars as shown on the standard drawings. Full‑depth longitudinal joint repair limits traffic use of saw‑cut repair areas to a maximum of 72 hours, any saw cuts wider than ½ inch must not be opened to traffic, excavated zones must be repatched within 48 hours (including weekends or holidays), the subbase must be free of surface irregularities greater than 1 inch and any disturbance deeper than that must be removed and the area re‑compacted before concrete placement, and when a patch is adjacent to a longitudinal joint repair it must be combined with that repair and all original joints sealed as specified without sealing the patch perimeter itself. Treatment procedures

To perform a patch joint the existing pavement surface is first cleaned and any longitudinal or transverse joints are prepared by removing loose material; for concrete pavements all crushed or deteriorated joints and major working cracks must be removed, and the designer should determine appropriate patch boundaries so that the entire deteriorated pavement is removed, with the area possibly sawed and sealed before the patch is placed. For asphalt repairs the patch thickness is determined from actual field measurements and should match the depth of the surrounding asphalt course, with an asphalt tack coat applied when necessary. The contractor must first prepare and submit a QC Plan that describes mixing, curing, joint sawing and sealing; traffic protection is required and excavated patch areas may not remain un‑patched for more than 48 hours, with any longer exposure requiring graded fill to prevent movement. Qualified personnel having at least five years’ experience must be assigned and written documentation submitted ten working days in advance, and work may not start when daytime temperatures are below 40°F or the subgrade is frozen. Full‑depth diamond‑tipped transverse saw cuts are made around the patch with a cut width that shall not exceed ½ inch, and the loosened concrete is removed in one or more pieces without using drop hammers; disturbed subbase must be re‑compacted or replaced as needed, and saw cuts opened to traffic may not exceed ½ inch in width nor remain exposed for more than 72 hours. In partial‑depth repairs the existing concrete is sounded and rectangular repair limits are laid out extending approximately 3 inches beyond delaminated edges, with a minimum size of 15×10 inches and at least 2 inches deep; all unsound concrete is removed to a minimum depth of 2 inches and not more than half the slab thickness using a chipping hammer weighing not more than 35 pounds operated at a 30–60° angle to create a rough bonding surface and square corners. Holes are drilled in the full‑depth saw‑cut faces a maximum of 1/8 inch larger than the coated dowel bars, anchoring material that sets within five minutes is injected before inserting the dowels, and bond‑breaker lubricant is applied at least one hour prior to concrete placement; if a bond breaker is present it is removed and replaced with two layers of at least 4.0‑mil polyethylene sheeting cut to the patch dimensions before new concrete is placed. Reinforcement is installed in the patch area as specified in Section 1002.3 and shown on the Standard Drawings, with tied or welded splices and proper lap. Forms are set on all sides not against existing pavement, the specified concrete is mixed, placed, struck off and textured to match the surrounding pavement. The area is finally cleaned with an oil‑, moisture‑ and contaminant‑free compressed air stream at a minimum pressure of 100 psi within 30 minutes before placing the concrete, and immediately after this cleaning grout or epoxy bonding compound is brushed onto all exposed surfaces in a thin layer without puddling; concrete must be placed before the grout dries or while the epoxy remains tacky. After finishing a permitted curing compound is applied, grout is placed around the patch before opening to traffic, joints are sealed, surface tolerance is checked with a 12‑foot straightedge and any high points exceeding 3/16 inch are ground. Class AA Modified concrete receives a white membrane forming curing compound applied with an atomizing spray tank; accelerated concrete also receives the same compound but requires insulation or heating if ambient temperature drops below 80°F and hourly monitoring to ensure the repair area does not experience a curing temperature change in excess of 40°F within any one‑hour period, while rapid‑set material is cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Repairs are protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours, and traffic may not be opened for a minimum of 12 hours after grouting operations have been completed; upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed while monitoring pressure shall not exceed 200 pounds per square inch, and the Representative determines when pavement may be opened to traffic based on attainment of a compressive strength of at least 1,000 psi as measured by compressive strength testing of cylinders. For concrete patches dowel bars are installed across transverse joints and must be bonded along their entire embedded length using epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders; longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only when the patch exceeds 65 feet in length, and all joints must be detailed as either skewed or non‑skewed per RC‑26M, with finishing providing a uniform surface ready for any required sealant before curing proceeds according to the specified time. Accelerated concrete can achieve the minimum compressive strength for traffic within 4 to 7 hours, whereas standard pavement concrete typically requires 24 to 48 hours. Before stabilizing a patch joint the slab should be inspected for voids and any joints or cracks sealed to limit water entry; holes are then drilled in a two‑hole pattern adjacent to the patch, and grout—cement mix (about 1 cubic foot per hole), hot asphalt (30–50 gallons per hole) or high‑density polyurethane (approximately 0.4 lb per square foot)—is pumped into the holes. Traffic may not be reopened until the material has cured: cement grout can be 12 hours, hot asphalt requires a minimum of 30 minutes for cooling, and polyurethane a minimum of 30 minutes for expansion and hardening. Patching less than one lane width shall not be performed. [1][2]

Implementation of a patch joint begins with removal of the deteriorated slab and placement of load‑transfer dowels that are anchored with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders; for patches longer than 65 ft longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts must be provided, while shorter patches omit them[1]. Accelerated‑setting concrete may be used when early traffic reopening is needed, allowing the lane to be opened after 4 to 7 hours, whereas conventional concrete typically requires 24 to 48 hours to reach opening strength[1]. Heavy precast slab units often require special hauling permits, designated haul routes, and dedicated unloading/installation equipment, and their dimensions are limited to a minimum of 6 ft length and up to 15 ft (20 ft for prestressed) to fit within a lane width[1]. On high‑ADT highways the contract may include liquidated damages or lane‑rental charges for each hour the lane remains closed beyond the specified opening time, and two‑lane facilities must maintain traffic unless an approved detour exemption is obtained[1]. The work is sequenced by drilling the pavement just prior to stabilization so that both crews can share the same Maintenance and Protection of Traffic (MPT) setup, and drilling and stabilizing are confined to a single lane at any one time[1]. Safety equipment is required to protect traffic and crew when handling hot liquid asphalt[1]. For concrete pavement patching the specification requires a saw equipped with a diamond‑tipped blade, a blade guard, alignment guides, a water cooling system and cut‑depth controls; surface marking must be performed before sawing[2]. Full‑depth longitudinal cuts shall be made prior to transverse cuts, and open excavations shall not exceed 48 hours (or grading/compacting shall be done if longer)[2]. Traffic may be allowed on the existing pavement for a maximum of 72 hours after cutting, but saw cuts wider than 1/2 inch or that are not full depth must not be opened to traffic[2]. Patches shall be constructed one lane at a time when two‑lane work would interfere, and drop‑off protection shall be provided; if rain occurs while the patch area is open an outlet shall be excavated through the shoulder at the lowest point of the patch[2]. The sequence consists of making full‑depth saw cuts at the interface between existing pavement and the new patch, installing load‑transfer dowels in the transverse faces of the existing concrete, constructing a sealant reservoir and sealing the joint[2]. A wheel saw having carbide steel tips may be used for the full‑depth transverse cuts, but penetration of the wheel into the subbase shall not exceed 1/2 inch[2]. Jackhammers limited to a maximum size of 30 pounds shall be used to remove concrete strips at each end of the patch area, and any disturbed subbase surface shall be re‑compacted with small vibratory compactors[2]. Reinforcement shall be placed after the concrete is removed and any bond breaker shall be replaced with two layers of polyethylene sheeting at least 4.0 mil thick before new concrete is placed[2]. For partial‑depth repairs a chipping hammer weighing not more than 35 pounds or a milling machine with a “V”, flat, or rounded head shall be used to remove unsound concrete; the material shall be mixed in a volumetrically‑mixed concrete system or with a power‑driven revolving and tilting drum mixer sized for the batch[2]. The surface shall be cleaned and a final cleaning method deployed within 30 minutes before placing the repair, and compressed air used for cleaning shall be free of oil, moisture and other contaminants and shall have a minimum pressure of 100 psi measured at the source[2]. Placement of Class AA cement concrete or rapid‑set patching material may only occur when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40°F or greater; placement shall be discontinued if temperatures fall below 40°F, repairs shall be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours, insulation or heating shall be provided if the ambient temperature drops below 80°F during curing, and the temperature change shall not exceed 40°F in any one‑hour period[2]. Slab stabilization requires a qualified crew and a supervisor with at least five years’ experience, and written submittals listing equipment such as supply pumps, valves, mixing machines, injection guns, a positive‑displacement cement pump, water tanker, core drill with diamond‑tip barrels, pressure gauges and vertical‑movement testing devices must be delivered ten working days before start[2]. Work may not commence when daytime temperatures are below 40°F or when the subgrade or base is frozen, and deflection testing shall not be performed if air temperature is below 40°F, exceeds 70°F, during spring thaw, or on frozen ground[2]. The stabilization sequence proceeds with drilling holes to the specified pattern, mixing grout at 800‑2000 rpm, injecting while monitoring pressure so that it does not exceed 200 psi and stopping pumping when any vertical movement is observed, followed by a curing period before traffic returns; traffic may not be allowed on the repaired slab for at least 12 hours after grouting unless the approved QC plan or manufacturer’s guidance permits an earlier opening[2]. For full‑depth longitudinal concrete joint repair the same saw equipment described for Section 516 shall be used, patches shall be constructed before any longitudinal joint work, traffic may be permitted on the repaired area for a maximum of 72 hours after sawing, saw cuts wider than 1/2 inch must not be opened to traffic, excavated repair areas shall not remain un‑patched for more than 48 hours or over weekends or holidays, and drop‑off protection shall be provided as specified[2].

Quality and workmanship

During a Patch Joint inspection the reviewer must verify that dowels and their anchoring binders—epoxy, polyester or vinylester—are present and fully bonded along the entire embedded length to provide load transfer[1]. The repair shall use one of the specified concrete materials—Class AA Modified cement concrete, Accelerated cement concrete, or rapid‑set concrete patching material—as required for the selected patch type[2]. Where joint sealing is needed a half‑inch expansion joint material shall be used in transverse joints or a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene extending at least one‑half inch below the repair[2], and a bond breaker shall consist of two layers of polyethylene sheeting at least 4.0 mil thick[2]. For slab‑stabilization patches the crew may select among cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing or high‑density polyurethane, choosing the material that matches site conditions[1]. Prior to placement all deteriorated concrete and crushed joints must be removed; unsound concrete shall be chipped away to a minimum depth of two inches using a chipping hammer not exceeding thirty‑five pounds operated at a thirty‑to‑sixty degree angle, leaving a rough surface for bonding[2]. The repair limits are to be laid out as a rectangular area extending approximately three inches beyond the edges of delaminated concrete, with minimum dimensions of fifteen by ten inches and a depth of two inches[2]. Final cleaning shall be performed within thirty minutes before placing the repair material using oil‑free compressed air at a minimum pressure of one hundred psi[2]. Full‑depth transverse saw cuts shall be made from the pavement edge, a second transverse cut at each end shall have a depth of at least one inch, no cut may extend into adjacent pavement or shoulders, and no cut shall be located within twenty‑four inches of an existing crack; saw‑cut width shall not exceed one‑half inch and cutting equipment must include a diamond‑tipped blade, blade guard, alignment guides, water cooling system and depth controls[2]. A Quality‑Control plan covering concrete mixing, curing, joint sawing, sealing, any required drilling or deflection testing must be prepared, submitted and approved at least two weeks before work begins[2]. Concrete material shall be accepted by lot according to the acceptance criteria of Section 704.1(d)4, and where a flexible base replacement is used density acceptance sampling shall be performed as required by Publication 408 Section 316 to ensure the completed patch meets the accepted quality criteria[1][2]. During placement the bond coat must remain tacky and concrete shall be placed before any grout has dried; ambient air and surface temperatures shall be at least forty degrees Fahrenheit (or rising) and the repair shall be protected from freezing for a minimum of twenty‑four hours[2]. For slab stabilization, mixed cement grout may not be held in the mixer longer than one hour, flowability measurements must be taken at least twice each shift, pressure shall not exceed two hundred psi, and pumping shall stop if upward pavement movement exceeds zero point zero five inch or if radial cracks around injection holes or transverse cracks between holes appear[2]. Cement grout shall not be opened to traffic until it cures, which can be twelve hours, while hot asphalt and polyurethane shall not be opened until they cool or expand and harden, a minimum of thirty minutes after pumping[1]. Dowel‑bar anchoring shall be inspected by coring, and the Representative shall accept the anchor only when the summation of voids around each dowel is less than zero point five inch per core; any deviation shall trigger additional evaluation[2]. After placement the surface shall be tested with a twelve‑foot straightedge and any high points or depressions of three‑sixteenths inch or more shall be ground smooth before traffic is allowed[2]. Cylinders shall be cured and compressive strength testing performed; traffic may be opened only when the concrete attains at least one thousand pounds per square inch as measured by PTM No. 604[2]. Deflection testing of each joint or crack shall be performed in accordance with ASTM D4695 when air temperature is between forty and seventy degrees Fahrenheit, and any joint exhibiting a loaded slab‑corner deflection of zero point zero two inch or more shall be stabilized; joint efficiency thresholds of sixty‑five percent shall guide whether full stabilization or patch‑and‑stabilize is required[2]. The inspector shall verify that voids are filled, slab support is restored and no surface depressions remain before releasing traffic, and that all original longitudinal and transverse joints as well as any saw cuts extending beyond the patch are properly resealed[1][2]. The finished patch surface shall be textured to match the existing pavement cross‑section[2].

A satisfactory patch joint requires that load transfer at the transverse joints be provided by dowelling all patch joints, anchoring the dowels with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders and ensuring that the dowels are bonded throughout their entire embedded length; the new joints must follow the spacing requirements of the applicable standard—30‑foot joint spacing for JRCP or 15‑foot joint spacing for JPCP—with perpendicular orientation, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals (Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are only required in concrete patches more than 65 feet long) [1]. For concrete pavement patching under Section 516, a satisfactory patch joint must meet the dowel‑bar anchoring verification of Section 516.3(e)(2.a) by having the summed voids around a dowel’s perimeter be less than 0.50 inch on any core and it also must satisfy the surface‑flatness tolerance of Section 516.3(n), so that after grinding no high points or depressions exceed 3/16 inch when checked with a 12‑foot straightedge; the concrete may be opened to traffic only after the Representative confirms that the material has achieved a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi as measured on test cylinders [2]. If accelerated concrete is used, the patch must attain the minimum compressive strength within the 4 to 7 hour window specified in Publication 408 Section 501 [1]. Under Section 525 for partial‑depth repair, each repair area shall be laid out rectangularly with minimum dimensions of 15 inches by 10 inches and at least 2 inches deep; unsound concrete must be removed to no less than 2 inches depth but not deeper than one half of the slab thickness; final cleaning of the substrate must occur within 30 minutes before placing the repair mix; any compressed‑air stream used shall be oil‑free, moisture‑free and delivered at a minimum pressure of 100 psi measured at the source; when delamination crosses an existing pavement joint the joint must be preserved as two separate minimum repair zones unless the QC Plan details a re‑establishment procedure; adjacent transverse or longitudinal joints must receive 1/2‑inch expansion material or 1/4‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene respectively extending at least 1/2 inch below the repair bottom, and when feasible the joint should be re‑sawed through the full depth of the repair to restore continuity; the repair surface must be free of running or puddled grout or epoxy before concrete placement, the concrete must be placed while the bonding agent remains tacky and before it dries, the repaired area must be protected from freezing for at least 24 hours, the curing temperature may not change by more than 40°F within any one‑hour period, and the repair must be resounded in the presence of the Representative with any delaminated sections replaced at no cost to the Department [2]. According to Section 679, a satisfactory patch joint after cement grout injection is achieved when the loaded slab corner deflection is reduced to less than 0.02 inch and the joint efficiency meets the approved threshold—at least 65 percent for standard stabilization or less than 65 percent only if specifically authorized; no upward pavement movement exceeds 0.05 inch during injection, the injection pressure does not surpass 200 psi, and the drilled holes are filled full‑depth with rapid‑set non‑shrink concrete flush with the surrounding surface; any joint still deflecting 0.02 inch or more must be regrouted and retested, with acceptance possible only after two proper groutings or replacement as directed [2]. For continuously reinforced concrete pavement patching under Section 518, a satisfactory patch joint is considered achieved only when all saw‑cut and removal tolerances are met: no saw cuts will be allowed to extend into the adjacent pavement or shoulders that is to remain in place; limit penetration of wheel into the subbase to a maximum of 1/2 inch; do not locate saw‑cuts within 24 inches of an existing crack; limit jackhammer size to 30 pounds maximum; break‑back of the underside of existing pavement must not be greater than 2 inches; any reinforcement bar damage requires corrective full‑depth cuts; and if subbase disturbance exceeds 1 inch it must be removed and replaced with concrete together with installation of a new bond breaker of at least two layers of 4.0 mil polyethylene [2]. For full‑depth longitudinal concrete joint repair under Section 519, a satisfactory patch joint requires that traffic may be restored on the repaired area for a maximum of 72 hours; any saw cuts opened to traffic do not exceed 1/2 inch in width; excavated areas must be patched within 48 hours and not remain open over weekends or holidays; full‑depth transverse cuts at the patch limits may extend into adjacent pavement up to full depth plus 2 inches provided dowel bars are not damaged; any subbase disturbance greater than 1 inch must be removed and replaced with concrete during paving; all surface irregularities exceeding 1 inch must be corrected and compacted; the final surface finish must match the existing cross‑section and all original longitudinal and transverse joints must be reestablished and sealed without sealing of the patch perimeter [2].

Performance and service life

A full‑depth patch joint that follows the specified design steps—removing all deteriorated concrete, replacing it with new concrete, and providing load transfer at transverse joints by dowelling and securely anchoring the dowels with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders—restores the pavement’s structural condition and functional performance. The use of standard concrete, which may be opened to traffic in 24‑48 hours, or accelerated concrete, which may be opened in 4‑7 hours, helps maintain durability, whereas high early strength (HES) concrete should be avoided because of its potential for excessive shrinkage cracking. Longitudinal tie bars are required only for patches longer than 65 feet, preventing unnecessary stress. When a significant length of roadway (±500 feet) requires intermittent patches with 10 feet or less of original pavement remaining between them, the section should be removed and reconstructed for its entire length, ensuring adequate long‑term performance and thereby extending the expected service life. Patch joints are prone to void formation beneath the slab that can appear as transverse joint faulting, fines, blow‑holes, corner breaks or settlement. Slab stabilization addresses these voids by drilling and filling them with cement grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane in accordance with Publication 72M/RC‑26M. When all patch joints—and at least 25 % of transverse joints when no testing has been performed—are stabilized and the patch is grouted using a two‑hole pattern, the lost support is recovered, deflections are reduced and the pavement’s structural integrity is restored, which lowers the likelihood of future pumping, faulting and cracking. Although stabilization does not increase the design structural capacity or eliminate existing surface depressions, the improved support enhances overall condition, functionality and durability, thereby contributing to a longer expected service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92)

The most frequent distress observed in a patch joint is loss of load transfer at the transverse joint, which occurs when dowels are not fully bonded along their embedded length; this can produce surface spalling and, if high‑early‑strength concrete is used, excessive shrinkage cracking. Tie‑bar movement in partially set patch concrete further degrades performance, particularly for patches that are not more than 65 feet long where longitudinal tie bars are not required. When intermittent patches are placed less than 10 feet apart over a roadway segment of about ±500 feet, the tendency toward recurring joint failures often forces full‑width reconstruction. Voids may develop beneath the slab as a result of pumping action, subgrade consolidation, and settlement, especially near transverse joints, cracks, and outer corners; the resulting loss of support generates excessive slab deflection and stress that manifest as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines at joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder, corner breaks, slab settlement, diagonal cracking, and ultimately complete breakup of the slab. The development of these distresses is governed by factors such as concrete type, proper dowel anchoring, traffic loading during setting, adherence to specified joint spacing and tie‑bar requirements, water infiltration that promotes pumping, inadequate joint sealing, and poor subsurface drainage. For repairs covered by Section 516, break back or spalling may develop at saw‑cut edges when the cut width exceeds the permitted 1/2 inch, and the specification requires a new full‑depth transverse saw‑cut beyond the damaged zone. Cracks or spalls that appear before final inspection also constitute a failure, as does the presence of voids around the perimeter of dowel bar anchoring larger than 0.50 inch on any one core. These distresses are generally caused by improper cutting, inadequate removal techniques, insufficient compaction, exposure to rain while the patch area remains open, and poor anchoring practices. Saw cuts greater than 1/2 inch in width or less than full depth shall not be opened to traffic. In Section 525, delamination that extends through a pavement joint is identified as a failure that must be replaced at no cost to the Department; delamination is also considered defective when the curing temperature of the concrete patch changes by more than 40 °F within any one‑hour period. This condition is driven primarily by inadequate temperature control, requiring ambient air and surface temperatures of at least 40 °F and rising, protection from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours, and insulation or heating if the temperature drops below 80 °F. When dowel bars in a transverse joint are exposed and show little sign of corrosion or other damage, a bond‑breaking material shall be applied to all exposed surfaces; if the dowel bars are unacceptable because of misalignment or extensive deterioration, a full‑depth concrete pavement patch shall be constructed. Excessive removal of concrete beyond the required minimum depth of 2 inches or exceeding half of the slab thickness, as well as damage to dowel bars during removal, can unintentionally turn a partial‑depth repair into a full‑depth patch and must be treated accordingly. Proper joint treatment—installing 1/2‑inch expansion material in transverse joints and 1/4‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene in longitudinal joints—and cleaning the surface within 30 minutes of placement further affect long‑term performance. Section 679 notes that radial cracks spreading outward from grout injection holes indicate poor quality or improper methods, and transverse cracks that develop between adjacent grout injection holes require replacement of the entire slab at no additional cost to the Department. Upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch will not be allowed, and panel displacement raised above the 0.05‑inch allowable tolerance shall be ground to grade. These failures are typically caused by poor grout quality or improper injection methods, exceeding pressure limits, or inadequate control of temperature and subgrade conditions that can lead to frozen material or insufficient curing. Section 519 adds that break back or spalling can develop when saw cuts exceed the permitted 1/2 inch, and damage to adjacent pavement may arise if drop hammers or hydro‑hammers are used; such damage shall be repaired as specified in Section 516.3(b) at no additional cost to the Department. Leaving excavated repair areas un‑patched for more than 48 hours, including weekends or holidays, or allowing traffic on fresh saw cuts beyond a maximum of 72 hours can promote distress, and disturbance of the subbase greater than 1 inch must be corrected by re‑compaction to avoid settlement problems. Monitoring and follow-up

After the patch‑joint work is finished, the contractor should first perform a visual verification of pumping quality when temperature limits prevented testing; otherwise a deflection test in accordance with ASTM D4695 shall be done and any joint that shows a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more must be regrouted and retested. If the Representative directs it, a formal retest is required 24 hours after grouting and before acceptance, and traffic may not be allowed onto the repaired area for at least 12 hours following the grout placement. After the concrete has cured, the patch area is inspected by testing the compressive strength of QC cylinders; the Representative will allow traffic only when the concrete reaches the minimum required compressive strength of 1,000 psi as measured according to PTM No. 604. In addition, the surface of the patched section must be checked with a 12‑foot straightedge in both longitudinal and transverse directions, and any high points or depressions of 3/16 inch or more must be ground smooth and retested before acceptance. After the patch joint is placed, the repair must be resounded in the presence of the Representative to verify its integrity, and during curing the temperature must be controlled and monitored at least hourly so that the repair area does not experience a curing temperature change in excess of 40 °F within any 1‑hour period; repairs should also be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours.
