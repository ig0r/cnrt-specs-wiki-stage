Patch Joint

Part of: Concrete Patching is-subtopic-of
Patch Joint is a topic under Concrete Patching.
patch-joints
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Patch Joint is a full‑depth concrete pavement repair technique applied to rigid (concrete) pavements when the slab shows severe deterioration, broken or crushed joints, spalling that penetrates beyond the top half of the slab, or loss of load transfer that threatens serviceability [1], [2]. The procedure removes all unsound concrete within a rectangular repair area (minimum 15 × 10 inches by 2 inches deep), installs dowel bars anchored with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders to re‑establish load transfer across transverse joints, and—if voids beneath the slab are present—stabilizes support by injecting cement grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane before placing a new concrete patch [1], [2]. By restoring full structural support and improving joint efficiency (typically above 65 % and reducing loaded corner deflection below 0.02 inch), the repair extends the pavement’s useful life and returns it to acceptable functional performance [1], [2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Patch Joint is employed when a concrete pavement slab exhibits severe deterioration or breakage, including major working cracks, crushed or otherwise deteriorated joints, and loss of load transfer at transverse joints that jeopardize serviceability [1]. The treatment also targets spalling that extends beyond the top half of the slab depth, because partial‑depth repair would not resolve the underlying joint problem [1]. In addition, localized distress such as cracks, spalls, break‑back damage, and corner deterioration that require removal of unsound concrete and replacement with a full‑depth patch are specified conditions for Patch Joint application [2]. When delamination occurs and the separation reaches or passes through an existing joint, Patch Joint is indicated as a repair measure [2]. The method is further required where voids have developed at the slab‑subbase interface, producing loss of support that manifests as transverse‑joint faulting, fines or small depressions near joints or cracks, corner breaks, and overall slab settlement; filling those voids restores full support and reduces deflections and the potential for additional pumping, faulting, and cracking [1]. For slab‑stabilization purposes, Patch Joint is applied to pavement joints or cracks that show excessive movement, defined as a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more together with a joint efficiency below 65%, indicating poor joint performance if approved by the representative [2]. The guidelines also advise that all patch joints be stabilized and that at least 25 percent of transverse joints receive treatment when no preliminary testing has been performed [1]. Finally, when full‑depth longitudinal concrete joint repair is undertaken, Patch Joint is used adjacent to the repaired joint to remediate localized distress such as break‑back, spalling, or other deterioration and to correct subbase irregularities [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–418), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

For concrete pavement patching, the patch joint work is intended principally to extend the roadway’s useful life by removing deteriorated sections and placing new concrete that should provide adequate long‑term performance; this replacement also inherently repairs the damaged area while restoring load transfer through dowelled joints. In slab stabilization, a patch joint is applied to fill voids beneath the slab so that full support is restored and the pavement’s structural integrity can be regained, thereby repairing damage and restoring functionality. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92)

Distress characterization and causes

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION, the distress that a patch‑joint repair targets is located beneath the concrete slab where small voids develop near transverse joints and cracks, particularly at outside slab corners, and may also appear as blow holes in the shoulder at those locations [1]. These voids cause loss of slab support, producing excessive slab deflections and stresses that first manifest as joint faulting, then progress to corner breaks, diagonal cracking, and ultimately complete breakup of the slab, compromising ride quality and exposing traffic to hazardous surface irregularities [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications (Publication 408/2020), section SECTION 679—SLAB STABILIZATION > 679.3 CONSTRUCTION, define the extent of the distress in terms of a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more at joints or cracks [2]. Within that same specification, severity is evaluated by joint efficiency, with values of 65 % or higher qualifying for stabilization and values below 65 % requiring patch‑and‑stabilize treatment if approved by the representative [2]. The Pennsylvania specification does not provide a progression rate for this distress [2]. Because patch‑joint work involves pressure pumps and hot‑asphalt hoses, normal safety rules must be observed, extra precautions taken in handling the equipment, and pumping stopped whenever vertical movement is noted or the representative deems continued placement uneconomical, indicating operational safety consequences of the distress [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

The distress that patch‑joint repairs target originates primarily from loss of load transfer across transverse joints and severe joint deterioration, which concentrate damage at slab bottoms near joints and cracks and permit spalling and bottom‑up cracking. When high early strength concrete is used, excessive shrinkage cracking may develop, and poor placement quality can produce non‑uniform support beneath the slab. Water infiltration exacerbates the problem by creating pumping action; subgrade consolidation and settlement can generate small voids beneath the slab, especially adjacent to transverse joints and corners, leading to loss of support, excessive deflections and stresses, joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and eventual slab breakup. The resulting slab movement—rocking, pumping or faulting—is linked to inadequate subdrainage and weak or unstable support, while material aging contributes to surface spalling. To counter these mechanisms full‑depth patching removes the deteriorated concrete and replaces it with new material, securing dowels along their entire embedded length to restore proper load transfer. Stabilization of the slab is required when movement or voids are present; this may be achieved by filling each identified void with a cement grout mixture at a rate of 1 cubic foot of grout per hole, applying hot‑asphalt undersealing, or using high‑density polyurethane as prescribed. Joint and crack sealing must be performed together with slab stabilization to limit water entry, and when no preliminary testing has been conducted at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized. Prior to any bonded overlay, adequate subdrainage and a stable subgrade must be ensured, because bonded JPCP overlays are not appropriate for pavements with unresolved unstable support or drainage deficiencies. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98)

Applicability and treatment selection

Patch Joint repairs are intended for rigid (concrete) pavements where full‑depth longitudinal joint work is required [2]. In concrete pavement patching, Patch Joint (full‑depth concrete pavement patching) is appropriate when the existing slab is badly deteriorated or broken, or when spalling is confined to the upper half of the slab and not caused by severe joint loss; in those cases full‑depth replacement should be used, while partial‑depth repair may be considered only for limited surface spalling [1]. Patch Joint repair as a slab‑stabilization measure is appropriate on Portland‑cement concrete pavements that exhibit loss of support manifested by transverse joint faulting, fines accumulating near joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes) at the shoulder, corner breaks, or overall slab settlement [1]. The method becomes especially suitable on high‑traffic routes where an early opening to traffic is required because accelerated concrete can achieve strength in 4 to 7 hours [1]. Following saw‑cutting, traffic may be allowed on the repaired sections for up to 72 hours provided that any opened saw cuts do not exceed one‑half inch in width, and the excavated repair areas must be re‑patched within 48 hours and may not remain open over weekends or holidays [2]. The treatment should be applied when traffic can be restricted until the selected material cures—12 hours for cement grout or at least 30 minutes for hot‑asphalt or polyurethane—and work is limited to a single lane using suitable temporary traffic control [1]. Work may commence only when daytime temperatures are above 40 °F and the subgrade or base course material is not frozen, and any required deflection testing must be performed with air temperatures between 40 °F and 70 °F while avoiding spring‑thaw conditions [2]. Environmental conditions that encourage water infiltration must be addressed by sealing joints and cracks and by maintaining functional subsurface drainage [1]. If intermittent patches are needed over a length of approximately ±500 feet with less than ten feet of original pavement remaining between them, the whole section should be removed and rebuilt instead of being patched, and longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only for concrete patches exceeding sixty‑five feet in length [1]. The treatment is considered appropriate when a loaded slab‑corner deflection equals or exceeds 0.02 inch and the joint efficiency is below 65 percent, subject to approval by the Representative, and it may only proceed if qualified personnel possessing at least five years of comparable project experience are present [2]. The concrete in the repair area must be removed in one or more pieces while minimizing disturbance to the subbase, subgrade, and adjacent pavement, prohibiting the use of drop hammers or hydro‑hammers, and limiting any subbase disturbance to no more than one inch unless the disturbed material is excavated and replaced [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 407–408), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

When the repair area extends roughly ±500 feet and the remaining intact pavement between individual patches is ten feet or less, patch joints are considered unsuitable and the entire section should be removed and reconstructed (section 4.2) [1]. Likewise, if more than five hundred feet of pavement in all lanes must be replaced, a full design analysis for slab thickness is required rather than isolated patching (section 4.2) [1]. For projects that will receive an overlay, it may be preferable to clean and fill spalled concrete with asphalt or use leveling courses instead of performing partial‑depth repairs or joint rehabilitation because only a uniformly supporting base is needed (section 4.2) [1]. Patch Joint repair should be avoided when the pavement problem includes surface depressions that need correction, a requirement to increase the design structural capacity, or the need to eliminate faulting, because slab stabilization does not correct those conditions (section 4.5) [1]. For concrete pavement partial‑depth repairs under Section 525, Patch Joint is unsuitable when delamination extends through an existing joint and the work cannot be performed as two separate minimum repair areas or without a detailed QC plan for reestablishing and protecting the joint; in that case another method such as full‑depth patching must be used (section 525.3) [2]. The approach also becomes inappropriate if removal of unsound concrete exceeds the maximum depth of one half of the slab thickness, or if excessive removal or damage to dowel bars converts the repair into a full‑depth condition; under those circumstances a full‑depth concrete pavement patch per Section 516 is required (section 525.3) [2]. Additionally, when dowel bars in a transverse joint are misaligned or extensively deteriorated, the partial‑depth repair should be abandoned in favor of a full‑depth patch (section 525.3) [2]. For slab stabilization under Section 679, Patch Joint should not be used when a joint or crack meets the loaded slab corner deflection threshold of 0.02 inch and has a joint efficiency at sixty‑five percent or more, because plain slab stabilization is directed in that case (section 679.3) [2]. The technique may be applied only when the same deflection exists and the joint efficiency is less than sixty‑five percent, provided the work is approved (section 679.3) [2]. Work—including patching—must be deferred if daytime temperatures are below forty °F, if the subgrade or base course material is frozen, or if air temperature for deflection testing is below forty °F or exceeds seventy °F; alternative repair methods should then be considered (section 679.3) [2]. For continuously reinforced concrete pavement patching under Section 518, Patch Joint is not appropriate when the distressed area is narrower than one lane width, because the specification explicitly prohibits patches of less than that width (section 518) [2]. The method also applies only to full‑depth patches whose lengths fall within the defined ranges—four feet to twenty feet (or six feet to twenty feet) for Type A, twenty‑point‑one feet to sixty‑five feet for Type B, and sixty‑five‑point‑one feet to five hundred feet for Type C—and if a repair requirement lies outside those limits another maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair approach should be used (section 518) [2].

Condition assessment and timing

Determining whether a patch joint is required begins with a visual inspection of the pavement surface to locate existing transverse or patch joints and to look for loss‑of‑support symptoms such as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes) at joints or cracks, corner breaks and overall slab settlement. The inspector also sounds the concrete with a steel bar, drag chains or a hammer to assess the extent of delamination before laying out rectangular repair limits that extend approximately three inches beyond the identified edges; these limits shall not be smaller than fifteen inches long by ten inches wide by two inches deep, and any partial‑depth areas spaced less than twenty‑four inches apart are combined into one repair area. Exposed dowel bars encountered during this sounding must be inspected for corrosion or misalignment so that bond‑breaking material can be applied or a full‑depth patch constructed if the dowels are unacceptable. For concrete pavements, field measurements of the total length of distress are taken; when the damaged stretch is about ±500 feet and the spacing between individual patches is ten feet or less, the entire section should be removed and reconstructed. If more than five‑hundred feet of pavement in all lanes requires replacement, a pavement design analysis is required to decide whether a thicker slab depth is needed. The length of each proposed patch is measured because longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are only required for concrete patches that exceed sixty‑five feet. Deflection testing on each joint and crack in accordance with ASTM D 4695 may be performed provided the air temperature is between forty and seventy degrees Fahrenheit, not during spring thaw, and when the subgrade is not frozen; if those limits are exceeded, visual verification of pumping or poor joint performance shall be used before specifying slab stabilization. The test yields loaded and unloaded slab‑corner deflections, and any location with a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more becomes a candidate for treatment. Joint efficiency is calculated as the unloaded‑to‑loaded deflection ratio multiplied by one hundred, and joints whose efficiency is less than sixty‑five percent shall be patched and stabilized, while joints whose efficiency is sixty‑five percent or more may be stabilized subject to Representative approval. When no preliminary testing has been performed, the guidelines advise stabilizing at least twenty‑five percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 417–418), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Patch Joint is applied when localized pavement distress is observed and quantified through actual field measurements, and the quantity shall anticipate any additional deterioration that may develop between the design field view and construction (section 3.3) [1]. For concrete pavements, full‑depth Patch Joint repair should be used when the pavement is badly deteriorated or slabs are broken, and it is required where joint deterioration at transverse‑longitudinal intersections extends beyond the mid‑slab depth, or where spalling penetrates deeper than the top half of the slab or is accompanied by loss of load transfer; in those situations partial‑depth repair is not appropriate and full‑depth patching must be performed (section 4.2) [1]. Patch Joint repair is also indicated when a slab has lost support, which is signaled by transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes) at a joint or crack, corner breaks, or general slab settlement; under those conditions all patch joints should be stabilized, and if no preliminary testing has been performed the guidance calls for stabilizing every patch joint and at least 25 percent of transverse joints (section 4.5) [1]. In the Pennsylvania specifications, Patch Joint is used whenever a crack or spall appears at any location before the pavement’s final inspection; in that case the damaged concrete must be removed, full‑depth saw cuts placed around the area with cuts not exceeding ½ inch in width, and a new joint created by drilling holes into the saw‑cut face, inserting coated dowel bars anchored with material that reaches an initial set within five minutes, after which the joint is sealed and the concrete must attain at least 1,000 psi compressive strength before traffic is opened (section 516.3) [2]. The same specifications prescribe partial‑depth Patch Joint repair when concrete delamination is detected—unsound concrete identified by sounding the slab with a steel bar, drag chains, or a hammer—and the repair area must be laid out rectangularly extending about three inches beyond the delaminated edges and be at least 15 inches long, 10 inches wide, and 2 inches deep, with all unsound concrete removed to a minimum depth of 2 inches but not deeper than half the slab thickness; if separate delamination zones are spaced less than 24 inches apart they are combined into a single repair area (section 525.3) [2]. Additionally, a joint or crack should be patched and simultaneously stabilized when the loaded slab‑corner deflection measured at that location is 0.02 inch or more and the calculated joint efficiency is less than 65%, provided the representative approves the treatment after field testing confirms these values (section 679.3) [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–418), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Materials and repair details

Patch‑joint repair begins with cleaning and sealing both longitudinal and transverse concrete joints, applying an asphalt tack coat when required and using heavy‑duty membranes as necessary; the patch material is then placed according to actual field measurements and quantities are sized to anticipate additional deterioration before construction, after which the overlay is sawed and sealed over the existing transverse and patch joints [1]. Load‑transfer devices are required at the transverse joints and all patch joints shall be dowelled, with dowels securely anchored to the existing pavement using epoxy, polyester binders or vinylester binders that must be bonded along their entire embedded length [1]. Designers must specify new pavement joint items as either skewed or non‑skewed baskets in accordance with RC‑26M; for jointed reinforced concrete pavement the joints are spaced at 30 feet with perpendicular orientation, whereas jointed plain concrete pavement calls for 15‑foot spacing [1]. Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only when the concrete patch exceeds 65 feet in length, and full‑depth patches must have all major working cracks and crushed joints removed before new concrete is placed as prescribed by Publication 408 Section 516 [1]. Slab stabilization may be accomplished by filling voids beneath the concrete with one of three grout materials: a cement grout estimated at 1 cubic foot per hole (or 0.25 bag of cement per hole), hot‑asphalt undersealing typically between 30 and 50 gallons per hole, or high‑density polyurethane applied at about 0.4 pounds per square foot of slab [1]. When no preliminary testing has been conducted, at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized; full‑depth patches are commonly grouted using a two‑hole pattern drilled adjacent to the patch, and traffic may not be reopened until the selected material has cured, cooled or hardened as specified for that product [1]. Patch‑joint repair calls for a single‑course, full‑depth cement concrete patch of either normal or accelerated strength; the patch must be at least one lane wide and is classified by length: Type A patches are built between 6 feet and 20 feet long, Type B between 20.1 feet and 65 feet, and Type C between 65.1 feet and 500 feet [2]. When the patch is continuously reinforced, a one‑course, full‑depth, continuously reinforced cement concrete patch is used; partial‑depth saw‑cuts are provided at the pavement/patch interface, a sealant reservoir is constructed and sealed, and the same length categories apply, with Type A patches ranging from 6 feet to 20 feet when tied splices are used or from 4 feet to 20 feet when welded splices are used [2]. Reinforcement must be placed as specified in Section 1002.3 and shown on the Standard Drawings, with bars firmly supported and tied at every intersection using either tied or welded splices; new reinforcement is lapped with existing bars according to the splice type shown, weld splices follow Section 1105.03(m), and tie bars are lapped at the center of the patch to allow expansion [2]. Full‑depth saw cuts defining the perimeter are made with diamond‑tipped blades equipped with blade guards, alignment guides, water cooling and cut‑depth controls; these cuts must be full depth, may not exceed ½ inch in width, and longitudinal joint cuts are limited to ¼ inch wide and 1 inch deep [2]. Saw‑cut openings opened to traffic may not exceed ½ inch in width and traffic may remain on the repaired area for no more than 72 hours after cutting; excavated areas must be patched within 48 hours, any subbase disturbance deeper than 1 inch is removed and replaced with concrete while irregularities up to 1 inch are compacted with small vibratory compactors [2]. Full‑depth saw cuts at the patch limits may extend transversely into adjacent pavement up to full depth + 2 inches provided dowel bars in the adjacent lane are not damaged [2]. When a patch replaces an existing expansion joint, 3/4‑inch expansion joint material is installed; for corner repairs a ½‑inch expansion joint material reestablishes the original transverse joint location, and if the repair abuts an existing transverse joint, ½‑inch expansion joint material is installed, while for a longitudinal joint a ¼‑inch pre‑formed cellular polystyrene is placed extending at least ½ inch below the bottom of the repair [2]. Coated dowel bars are placed in holes drilled no more than ⅛ inch larger than the bar, anchored with material that reaches initial set within 5 minutes, and bond‑breaker lubricant is applied to at least half of each Type B dowel bar [2]. A temporary rigid separator not greater than ⅛ inch thick may be used between lanes, and a ¼‑inch full‑depth polystyrene board bond breaker is required in the longitudinal joint of Type A and B patches [2]. Forms are placed on all sides not touching existing pavement; rapid‑set concrete or the mix specified in Section 501.3 is placed, textured to match the surrounding surface, and cured with an approved liquid membrane‑forming curing compound [2]. For partial‑depth repairs the rectangular patch extends about 3 inches beyond delaminated edges, meets a minimum size of 15 inches long by 10 inches wide by 2 inches deep, and any separate patches spaced less than 24 inches apart are combined; unsound concrete is removed to at least 2 inches depth but not deeper than half the slab thickness, and the perimeter is saw‑cut a minimum of 2 inches using a chipping hammer no heavier than 35 lb operated at a 30–60° angle or a milling machine with a V, flat or rounded head [2]. The repair material shall be volumetrically mixed concrete (or hand‑batched with a suitable drum or paddle mixer), and the final cleaning must occur within 30 minutes before placement using an oil‑, moisture‑free compressed‑air stream at a minimum pressure of 100 psi [2]. After slab‑stabilization work any wooden plugs used in grout injection holes are removed and each hole is filled full depth with rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete patching material, the patched surface being struck flush to the surrounding pavement; the field quality‑control plan must specify hole spacing, depth, diameter, drilling methods and injection sequence [2]. The patch‑joint repair may use Class AA cement concrete modified, accelerated, or rapid‑set concrete patching material. For the modified mix a white membrane‑forming curing compound is applied with an atomizing spray tank that has an adjustable nozzle and the solution is agitated before each use; the accelerated mix uses the same compound but requires insulation or heating when ambient temperature drops below 80 °F and hourly monitoring to keep any curing temperature change within 40 °F in a one‑hour period [2]. Rapid‑set material is cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, and joint sealing follows the provisions of Section 525.3(e) [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–419), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

The performance of a patch joint is first controlled by how well the repair material matches the measured condition of the existing pavement; the repair should use the same type and depth of asphalt or concrete as the surrounding surface, with field measurements dictating the quantity placed [1]. Where the underlying slab has lost support, slab stabilization must be performed before the patch is applied, and an asphalt tack coat may be used when adhesion is a concern [1]. Proper cleaning and sealing of longitudinal, transverse, and existing patch joints, employing heavy‑duty membranes as needed, provides compatibility between the new overlay and the existing pavement, while continuous base drainage helps limit further deterioration [1]. Load transfer at transverse joints is achieved by dowelling every joint and anchoring the dowels with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders that must be bonded along their entire embedded length; patches longer than 65 feet require longitudinal tie bars or bolts to maintain stability [1]. High early‑strength concrete should be avoided because of its potential for excessive shrinkage cracking, whereas accelerated or standard concrete mixes are preferred when early traffic opening is needed [1].

For concrete pavement patches the mix may be normal strength or accelerated strength cement concrete and must be placed in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations; rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete is required for drill‑hole fills and shall be placed full depth and struck flush with the surrounding surface [2]. The anchoring material used to secure dowels must achieve an initial set within five minutes and contain voids no larger than 0.50 inch around the dowel perimeter [2]. Class AA cement concrete shall be paired with the white membrane‑forming curing compound specified in Section 516.3(j) and applied with an atomizing spray tank whose nozzle is adjustable and whose solution is agitated before each use; if ambient air temperature drops below 80 °F during curing, insulation or heating shall be provided, and the repair area may not experience a curing temperature change in excess of 40 °F within any one‑hour period, otherwise the work is considered defective [2]. Reinforcement follows Section 1002.3, with bars firmly supported and tied at every intersection; splices may be tied or welded, and weld splices must lap tie bars at the center of the patch to allow expansion [2].

Compatibility requirements also include dowel‑bar preparation: Type B coated dowels require a bond‑breaker lubricant applied to at least one‑half of their length at least one hour before concrete placement, whereas Type A bars do not require this treatment; exposed dowels that show little corrosion shall receive a bond‑breaking coating on all exposed surfaces, and severely deteriorated bars demand a full‑depth patch [2]. Load‑transfer dowels are installed in the transverse faces of the existing pavement, a sealant reservoir is constructed and sealed, and joint filler materials must incorporate 1/2 inch expansion joint material in transverse joints and 1/4 inch preformed cellular polystyrene in longitudinal joints, each extending at least 1/2 inch below the repair bottom; the repair material shall be sawable without damage to permit joint re‑establishment [2].

Existing pavement conditions further affect performance. The condition of the slab beneath the joint governs success; pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement may create small voids that appear as transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks, blow‑hole depressions, corner breaks or general slab settlement, requiring grout that can effectively fill those voids such as cement grout, hot asphalt (which must cool before traffic returns) or high‑density polyurethane—a low‑viscosity two‑component material that expands into a lightweight rigid closed‑cell foam and is resistant to water and chemical attack with long‑term durability [1][2]. All deteriorated concrete and crushed joints must be removed so that new dowel‑bars can engage sound substrate, and for pavements where no preliminary testing has been performed at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints shall be stabilized according to slab‑stabilization guidelines [1]. Saw cuts defining a patch may not exceed ½ inch in width and must be full depth; any cooling water, slurry or dust from cutting may not enter an open lane, and saw‑cuts extending beyond the patch limits shall be sealed [2]. Surface tolerances greater than 3/16 inch must be ground before traffic is allowed, and patches may be opened to traffic only after cylinders show a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi [2]. Excavated areas may not remain un‑patched for more than 48 hours, subbase disturbance deeper than 1 inch requires hand‑tool removal and replacement before concrete placement, and the disturbed surface shall be recompacted with small vibratory compactors; work shall not commence when daytime temperatures are below 40 °F or when the subgrade or base course is frozen [2]. Loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more triggers stabilization of joints or cracks that have a joint efficiency of at least 65 percent, and patch‑and‑stabilize for those with joint efficiency less than 65 percent if approved by the representative [2]. Patch dimensions must extend approximately 3 inches beyond delaminated edges, meet minimum dimensions of 15 × 10 inches by 2 inches depth, be between 6 feet and 20 feet long, and have a width not less than one lane width [2].

Treatment procedures

Preparation begins with a quality‑control (QC) plan that the contractor must submit describing mixing, curing, joint sawing and sealing actions and must protect traffic so that excavated areas are not left open more than 48 hours. [2] For concrete pavement patch joints the existing surface is first cleaned and any longitudinal or transverse joints are prepared by removing loose material; all crushed or deteriorated joints and major working cracks must be removed, and the designer should determine appropriate patch boundaries so that the entire deteriorated pavement is removed. [1] If required, the area may be sawed and sealed before the patch is placed, with saw cuts made to full depth of the slab and no wider than ½ inch; the perimeter cut extends about 3 inches beyond the distressed edges and must meet a minimum size of 15 inches by 10 inches by 2 inches for partial‑depth repairs. [1][2] For partial‑depth concrete repair the contractor sounds the slab with a steel bar, drag chains or hammer to locate delamination, then lays out rectangular repair limits that extend approximately 3 inches beyond the edges of the delaminated area and combines separate patches that are less than 24 inches apart. [2] Unsound concrete is removed to at least a 2‑inch depth but not deeper than one half of the slab thickness by saw‑cutting the perimeter for Type 1 repairs and chipping with a hammer no heavier than 35 lb held at a 30–60° angle, or by milling with a V‑head, flat‑head or rounded‑head machine; corners are squared with an additional 2‑inch cut. [2] All exposed reinforcement in jointed reinforced pavement is cut and removed; dowel bars that are damaged are either protected with bond‑breaking material or the area is upgraded to a full‑depth patch, and dowel bar holes are drilled to within 1/8 inch larger than the coated bars. [2] The surface must be final‑cleaned within 30 minutes before placing the repair mix; compressed air used for cleaning must be oil‑free, moisture‑free and at least 100 psi. [2] For asphalt patch joints the required thickness is determined from actual field measurements and should match the depth of the surrounding asphalt course, with an asphalt tack coat applied when necessary. [1] Placement of concrete involves using rapid‑set or specified concrete mix placed per Section 501.3, struck off, finished to match the surrounding cross‑section and textured to the adjacent surface; dowel bars are installed across transverse joints and must be bonded along their entire embedded length with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders, while longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only when the patch exceeds 65 feet in length. [1] For asphalt placement the overlay is sawed and sealed over the repaired joint to restore continuity after the new material has been placed. [1] After concrete placement the surface is finished and textured to match the existing cross‑section, a date is scribed, and all original longitudinal and transverse joints are resealed; sealant is also applied to any saw cuts extending beyond the patch. [2] Accelerated concrete may achieve minimum compressive strength for traffic within 4 to 7 hours, whereas standard pavement concrete typically requires 24 to 48 hours; a white membrane‑forming curing compound is applied with an atomizing spray tank that has an adjustable nozzle and the solution is agitated before each application. [1][2] When using accelerated Class AA cement concrete, insulation or heating must be provided if ambient temperature falls below 80 °F and the repair area’s temperature must be monitored at least hourly to ensure the curing temperature does not change by more than 40 °F within any one‑hour period; rapid‑set concrete is cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. [2] Traffic may be reopened only after cylinders show at least 1,000 psi compressive strength for concrete patches, after cement grout has cured for 12 hours, after hot asphalt has cooled a minimum of 30 minutes, or after polyurethane expands and hardens a minimum of 30 minutes. Implementation of a patch joint begins with removal of the deteriorated slab followed by placement of dowels that are anchored with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders; for patches longer than 65 ft longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts must be provided, while shorter patches omit them. Accelerated‑setting concrete may be used when early traffic reopening is needed, allowing the lane to be opened after 4 to 7 hours, whereas conventional concrete typically requires 24 to 48 hours to reach opening strength. Heavy precast slab units often demand special hauling permits, designated haul routes, and dedicated unloading/installation equipment, and their dimensions are limited to a minimum of 6 ft length and up to 15 ft (20 ft for prestressed) to fit within a lane width. On high‑ADT highways the contract may include liquidated damages or lane‑rental charges for each hour the lane remains closed beyond the specified opening time, and two‑lane facilities must maintain traffic unless an approved detour exemption is obtained. Implementation of a slab‑stabilization repair requires drilling the concrete and then pumping a grout material—either cement grout, hot liquid asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane—into the voids beneath the slab; the work is sequenced by drilling the pavement just prior to stabilization so that both crews can share the same Maintenance and Protection of Traffic (MPT) setup, and drilling and stabilizing are confined to a single lane at any one time. Safety equipment is required to protect traffic and crew when handling hot liquid asphalt. The slab may not be opened to traffic until the selected grout has cured or cooled: cement grout may require up to 12 hours, hot asphalt needs a minimum of 30 minutes for cooling, and polyurethane must expand and harden for a minimum of 30 minutes. Implementation requires submitting a QC plan at least two weeks before work begins and written documentation confirming qualified personnel, including a crew supervisor with at least five years’ experience, submitted at least ten working days before work begins. Saw cutting must be performed with a diamond‑tipped blade saw equipped with a blade guard, alignment guides, water cooling system and cut‑depth controls, and saw cuts opened to traffic may not exceed one‑half inch in width. The representative must surface‑mark repair areas prior to sawing, and when multiple lanes are patched the work must proceed one lane at a time to avoid traffic interference. Excavated patch areas may not remain un‑patched for more than 48 hours or over weekends or holidays; if they must stay open longer adjacent lanes shall be provided with graded, compacted fill or other approved protection. After saw cutting the existing pavement, traffic may be allowed on the repair areas of existing pavement for a maximum of 72 hours. Rain during an open patch obliges the crew to excavate a drainage outlet through the shoulder at the lowest point of the patch and to repair any resulting shoulder damage. The damaged slab is removed with a chipping hammer not exceeding 35 lb operated at a 30–60° angle, or, where specified, with a milling machine equipped with a V‑head, flat head or rounded head. Mixing is performed with volumetric mixers in accordance with Section 704.3 or with a power‑driven drum/paddle mixer sized within the manufacturer’s batch limits. The final cleaning of the prepared surface must be completed within 30 minutes prior to placing the repair material, and if compressed air is used the stream must be oil‑ and moisture‑free and have at least 100 psi pressure measured at the source. All required equipment, including a 35‑pound maximum jackhammer for corner removal, small vibratory compactors for subbase correction and sufficient hauling units, must be on site before concrete placement to maintain continuity. Curing requires an atomizing type spray tank with an adjustable nozzle, and the solution must be agitated before each application; if ambient air temperature drops below 80 °F, insulation or heating must be provided to the repair area, and the curing temperature must be monitored at least hourly so that any change does not exceed 40 °F within a one‑hour period, exceeding which renders the work defective. Work may not start when daytime air temperature is below 40 °F or when the subgrade or base course material is frozen; deflection testing is prohibited if air temperature is below 40 °F, exceeds 70 °F, during spring thaw, or when the subgrade is frozen. Pumping must be stopped immediately if any vertical movement is detected. Opening the repaired section to traffic is permitted only after the required sampling and testing stipulated in the QC plan have been satisfied. [1][2]

Quality and workmanship

The inspector must verify that load‑transfer dowels are installed and that the anchoring binders—epoxy, polyester, or vinylester—are present and fully bonded along the entire embedded length of each dowel [1]. In addition, the QC Plan required by Section 516.3(a) shall be reviewed to confirm that the concrete mix has been accepted and that the anchoring material achieves an initial set within five minutes, with expansion‑joint material thicknesses and bond‑breaker lubricant application documented [2]. All deteriorated concrete and any crushed joints must be removed, and the resulting patch boundaries must meet design limits including a minimum patch length of six feet [1][2]. Preparation inspections shall confirm surface markings, provide traffic protection for unpatched periods longer than forty‑eight hours, and correct any subbase compaction disturbances greater than one inch; if the disturbance is deeper than one inch the material shall be removed with hand tools and replaced with concrete during paving [2]. The repair area must be laid out with rectangular limits extending approximately three inches beyond the damaged edges and must be at least fifteen by ten by two inches in size, with all unsound concrete removed to a depth of at least two inches and the remaining surface roughened [2]. For slab‑stabilization patches the inspector shall look for loss‑of‑support indications such as transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at the transverse joint or crack, corner breaks and slab settlement, and then select a grout material that matches site conditions—cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane [1]. The hole pattern prescribed in Publication 72M must be used, with drilling performed immediately before stabilization and temporary traffic control established according to the guidelines [1]. Joint spacing, dowel‑bar coating, and sealant installation shall follow the specified intervals, for example thirty‑foot or fifteen‑foot spacing, as required by the project specifications [1]. Saw‑cut equipment must not produce cuts wider than one‑half inch, and full‑depth transverse cuts may not extend more than two inches into adjacent pavement; saw‑cuts may remain on the repaired area for no longer than seventy‑two hours before traffic is permitted [2]. Dowel‑bar holes shall be drilled no larger than one‑eighth inch beyond the bar diameter, and anchoring voids must be less than 0.50 inch per core as measured during inspection [2]. Forms and concrete placement shall conform to Section 501.3(a)–(h), and the fresh concrete must attain the minimum compressive strength within the time period prescribed in Publication 408 Section 501 [1]. When a flexible base replacement is used, density acceptance sampling shall be performed as required by Publication 408 Section 316 to ensure the completed patch meets accepted quality criteria [1]. Reinforcement shall be placed in the patch area as specified in Section 1002.3 and shown on the Standard Drawings, with bars firmly supported and tied at every intersection; splices shall be tied or welded according to the drawings, with weld splices following Section 1105.03(m) [2]. Joint filler material shall be installed as specified—half‑inch expansion material in transverse joints or quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene in longitudinal joints extending at least one‑half inch below the repair—and a sealant reservoir shall be constructed and the joint properly sealed [2]. The final cleaning of the repaired surface must be performed within thirty minutes using oil‑free compressed air at a minimum pressure of one hundred pounds per square inch [2]. During installation, pumping shall be stopped whenever vertical movement is observed, radial cracks from grout holes or transverse cracks between adjacent holes must be inspected and corrected, any panel raised more than 0.05 inch above the allowable tolerance shall be ground to proper grade, and each joint or crack shall be tested by deflection in accordance with ASTM D 4695 when air temperature is between forty°F and seventy°F [2]. For cement‑grout stabilization the patch may not be opened to traffic until the grout has cured, which can require twelve hours; for hot‑asphalt or polyurethane the material must cool, expand, and harden for a minimum of thirty minutes after pumping before traffic is allowed [1]. After the work is completed the inspector shall verify that all voids are filled, slab support has been restored, and no surface depressions remain before releasing traffic [1]. Completed‑work testing shall include a surface tolerance check with a twelve‑foot straightedge, and any high points or depressions of three‑sixteenths inch or more must be ground away [2]. Cylindrical specimens shall be tested for compressive strength to at least one thousand pounds per square inch before traffic can be opened [2]. After grouting, drill holes shall be patched full depth with a rapid‑set non‑shrink concrete material and struck flush; a retest twenty‑four hours later must confirm that deflection no longer exceeds zero point zero two inch before acceptance [2]. The specified Class AA cement concrete material shall be used together with a white membrane‑forming curing compound applied by an atomizing spray tank, and the curing temperature shall be monitored at least hourly so that the repair area does not experience a temperature change in excess of forty°F within any one‑hour period; if such a change occurs the work is considered defective [2]. After curing, the completed patch shall be resounded in the presence of the Representative to detect delamination, and any delaminated sections shall be replaced at no cost to the Department [2]. Patch dimensions must fall within the prescribed limits—between six feet and twenty feet long for Type A, between twenty‑point‑one feet and sixty‑five feet long for Type B, or between sixty‑five‑point‑one feet and five hundred feet long for Type C—and no patch shall be less than one lane width [2][1]. The surface of the patch shall be finished and textured to match the existing pavement cross‑section, and all saw‑cuts extending beyond the patch limits shall be sealed; if diamond grinding is performed, the pavement surface must be tested in the longitudinal direction as required [2].

Performance and service life

A full‑depth patch joint that follows the design steps—removing deteriorated concrete, replacing it with new concrete, and providing load transfer at transverse joints by dowelling and anchoring the dowels with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders—restores the pavement’s structural condition and functional performance. Using standard concrete, which may be opened to traffic in 24‑48 hours, or accelerated concrete, which may be opened in 4‑7 hours, helps maintain durability, whereas high early strength (HES) concrete should be avoided because of its potential for excessive shrinkage cracking. Longitudinal tie bars are required only for patches longer than 65 feet, preventing unnecessary stress. When a significant length of roadway (±500 feet) requires intermittent patches with 10 feet or less of original pavement remaining between them, the section should be removed and reconstructed for its entire length to ensure adequate long‑term performance and thereby extend the expected service life. Patch joints are prone to void formation beneath the slab that can appear as transverse joint faulting, fines, blow‑holes, corner breaks or settlement. Slab stabilization addresses these voids by drilling and filling them with cement grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane in accordance with Publication 72M/RC‑26M. When all patch joints—and at least 25 % of transverse joints when no testing has been performed—are stabilized and the patch is grouted using a two‑hole pattern, the lost support is recovered, deflections are reduced and the pavement’s structural integrity is restored, which lowers the likelihood of future pumping, faulting and cracking. Although stabilization does not increase the design structural capacity or eliminate existing surface depressions, the improved support enhances overall condition, functionality and durability, thereby contributing to a longer expected service life. Under the slab‑stabilization specification, any joint or crack showing a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more must be treated according to its joint efficiency; joints with efficiency at 65 % or more are stabilized, while those below 65 % are patched and stabilized, provided the representative approves. After grout injection, the drill holes are removed and filled full‑depth with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete patching material that is struck flush to the surrounding surface; retesting 24 hours later confirms whether movement remains within limits, and if excessive movement persists after two groutings the slab must be replaced. By meeting these deflection and joint‑efficiency thresholds and ensuring a flush, cured patch, the treatment restores the pavement’s structural response and functional performance as defined by the acceptance criteria. The most frequent distress observed in a patch joint is loss of load transfer at the transverse joint when dowels are not fully bonded along their embedded length, which can generate surface spalling and, if high‑early‑strength concrete is used, excessive shrinkage cracking. Tie‑bar movement in partially set patch concrete further degrades performance, particularly for patches that are not more than 65 feet long where longitudinal tie bars are not required. When intermittent patches are placed less than 10 feet apart over a roadway segment of about ±500 feet, the tendency toward recurring joint failures often forces full‑width reconstruction. Voids may develop beneath the slab as a result of pumping action, subgrade consolidation, and settlement, especially near transverse joints, cracks, and outer corners; the resulting loss of support generates excessive slab deflection and stress that manifest as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines at joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder, corner breaks, slab settlement, diagonal cracking, and ultimately complete breakup of the slab. The development of these distresses is governed by concrete type, proper dowel anchoring, traffic loading during setting, adherence to specified joint spacing and tie‑bar requirements, water infiltration that promotes pumping, inadequate joint sealing, and poor subsurface drainage. Edge slumping that exceeds the limits of no more than 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and no more than 1/4 inch between a lane and a shoulder is considered unacceptable for patch‑joint repairs. Voids or spalls larger than the width permitted in Section 525 that are left unfilled also constitute a failure of the repair. Dowel‑transfer units misaligned beyond the tolerances of 0.25 inch maximum vertical tilt or skew at each end and more than 1 inch longitudinal or horizontal translation relative to the intended joint location cause the patch joint to fail. Inadequate curing, such as concrete temperatures dropping below 35 °F during cold‑weather cure, renders the concrete defective. A temperature change exceeding 40 °F within any one‑hour period also makes the work defective, and ambient air falling below 80 °F requires insulation or heating while the curing temperature is monitored at least hourly to keep changes within the 40 °F limit. Failures are driven by poor reinforcement cleanliness, insufficient compaction or vibration, improper saw cutting or joint preparation, exposure to adverse temperature swings, and failure to apply bond‑breaker lubricant or seal joints correctly. Spalling or break‑back at the saw‑cut edges, cracks that develop before final inspection, dowel bar anchoring voids larger than 0.50 inch, and surface tolerances exceeding the permitted 3/16 inch deviation are additional distresses that can cause a patch‑joint repair to be declared defective. These problems often arise when saw cuts exceed one half inch in width or are not full depth, when aggressive removal methods damage adjacent pavement or subbase, when protection of open patches is delayed beyond forty‑eight hours, when compaction is inadequate, when bond‑breaker lubrication is insufficient, or when anchoring material does not set within the required five minutes. Repairs that are less than 24 inches apart must be combined into a single partial‑depth repair area. All unsound concrete in the designated repair area must be removed to a minimum depth of 2 inches and never beyond half of the slab thickness; failure to meet these requirements can lead to recurring separation. Dowel bar condition also governs distress; exposed bars with little corrosion require a bond‑breaking coating, while misaligned or extensively deteriorated bars trigger conversion to a full‑depth patch. During slab stabilization after a patch‑joint treatment, radial cracks spreading outward from grout injection holes, transverse cracks forming between adjacent holes, vertical movement of the material while pumping, and panel displacement that exceeds the allowable tolerance of 0.05 inch are considered failures. Any loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more after grouting also constitutes a failure. These stabilization problems are typically triggered by poor quality or improper methods, inadequate temperature control (work may not begin below 40 °F and testing must be between 40 °F and 70 °F), frozen subgrade, or incorrect drilling and injection sequencing. Break‑back or spalling at saw‑cut edges, damage to adjacent pavement during concrete removal, disturbance of the subbase beyond one inch, and prolonged exposure of an excavated area for more than forty‑eight hours can develop when improper equipment such as drop hammers or hydro‑hammers is used, when cuts exceed one half inch in width, when the removal technique unsettles the subbase without immediate recompaction, or when the excavated area remains unpatched beyond the allowed time. Monitoring and follow-up

After the patch joint is placed, the quality‑control plan must require sampling and testing before traffic, including compressive‑strength cylinders that must reach at least 1,000 psi measured per PTM No. 604; the finished surface must be checked with a 12‑foot straightedge and any high or low spots greater than 3/16 in must be ground smooth; dowel‑bar anchorage must be verified by coring three random locations and is accepted only when voids around the perimeter are less than 0.50 in, and the date of placement must be scribed on the shoulder edge to provide a reference for future monitoring. For slab stabilization under Section 679, after the wooden plugs are removed and the drill holes are filled full depth with rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete that is struck flush with the surrounding pavement, a deflection test may be performed 24 hours after grouting and before acceptance; if the loaded corner deflection is 0.02 inch or more, the slab must be re‑grouted and retested following the same procedure, and the Representative may accept the slab after two properly performed groutings but otherwise may require full‑depth replacement. Under Section 525, the curing temperature of the repair area must be controlled and monitored at least hourly so that it does not experience a change in excess of 40 °F within any one‑hour period; if such a change occurs the work is considered defective. [2] A temperature swing greater than 40 °F within any one‑hour period during curing classifies the work as defective, and any completed repair area that shows delamination at the required resounding inspection must be replaced.
