Structural Layer Coefficient

Part of: Structural Number is-subtopic-of
Structural Layer Coefficient is a topic under Structural Number.
structural-coefficientstructural-coefficientsstructural-layer-coefficientslayer-coefficientsstructure-layer-coefficient
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

The Structural Layer Coefficient is a design parameter that quantifies the relative structural capacity of individual pavement layers based on their material type and stabilization method, enabling engineers to translate a required Design Structural Number into specific layer thicknesses. By applying coefficient values drawn from prescribed tables (e.g., Table 9.3) in the structural‑number equation, it captures variations such as brick pavements over rigid or flexible bases, lime‑ or cement‑stabilized subgrades, and reclaimed or cold‑recycled materials, thereby ensuring that each course contributes appropriately to overall load‑bearing performance. This coefficient is central to full‑depth reclamation, overlay, and thin‑asphalt designs, where it drives placement‑depth calculations, minimum/maximum thickness limits, and verification of structural adequacy [1].

Purpose and design objectives

The Structural Layer Coefficient is used in full‑depth reclamation designs to capture the variation in structural capacity that results from different stabilization materials and processes, allowing engineers to quantify how a reclaimed layer supports traffic loads; recommended coefficient values are provided in Table 9.3 and, in general, the range of coefficients varies from values typical of subbase material to values representative of stabilized materials. For historic brick pavements the coefficient is used to meet the engineering need for accurate structural analysis when designing an overlay, ensuring that the differing support conditions of the underlying base are properly accounted for; if the brick pavement rests on a concrete (rigid) base the analysis shall be done using the structural coefficient for “Brick with Rigid Base”, and if it sits on stone or another flexible base the structural coefficient for “Brick with Flexible Base” is used, with the coefficient applied only to the depth of the brick layer while a separate coefficient is applied only to the depth of the base material. When a deflection‑based design program is used for FB surfaces the depth of the FB overlay must be adjusted because the program assumes Superpave, and a structural coefficient of 0.20 must be used for FB courses when designing FB overlays. In overlay design the Structural Layer Coefficient quantifies the relative strength of pavement layers so that engineers can select appropriate minimum and maximum thicknesses for scratch, leveling or wearing courses, thereby correcting structural deficiencies and providing sufficient load‑bearing capacity and ride quality; tables such as 9.3, 9.5, 10.4 and 10.5 supply the coefficient numbers that drive placement‑depth calculations. For lime‑ or cement‑stabilized subgrade layers a Structural Coefficient of 0.12 is used to model the relative stiffness, providing a way to account for subgrade strengthening when determining appropriate pavement thickness and material quantities. The a_i values (Structural Layer Coefficients) are used to convert the Design Structural Number, which depicts the required strength the proposed pavement must provide, into individual layer thicknesses, ensuring that each course contributes appropriately to meet the overall strength needed for traffic loading; the coefficients are obtained from Table 9.3. In AC‑overlay design the structural coefficients are used to compute the effective Structural Number of the existing pavement layers so that engineers can compare it with the required Structural Number for future traffic, thereby determining whether the current structure can support anticipated loads and sizing the overlay thickness accordingly; the calculated SN must be greater than the Overlay (Design) SN for the overlay to be structurally adequate. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–62), Ch. 5 (pp. 103–120), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 9 (pp. 172–173), Ch. 10 (p. 180)

In an AC‑overlay of an existing AC pavement the primary performance requirement linked to the Structural Layer Coefficient is structural adequacy: the overlay’s calculated structural number must be greater than the Overlay (Design) SN so that the structure can carry future traffic, and the design must accommodate the difference between the SN for Future Traffic and the Effective Existing SN. No other functional, durability, safety or serviceability objectives are specified in this section. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (p. 180)

Scope and applicability

The Structural Layer Coefficient is employed across a variety of pavement design contexts. For full‑depth flexible pavements the coefficient values (ai) taken from Table 9.3 are used in the structural number equation SN = Σ ai di mi when the structure includes an aggregate/cement or aggregate/lime/pozzolan base course and the design is performed with the Specified Thickness Design or Optimized Thickness Design methods in DARWin; drainage coefficients mi shall be set to 1.0 and the Layered Analysis method must not be used. For full‑depth reclamation projects that recycle the entire flexible pavement section the coefficient depends on the stabilization material: basic pulverization yields a value comparable to a standard 2A subbase, calcium‑chloride or similar additives give a slightly higher value, asphalt‑stabilized layers are treated as cold‑recycled material, and chemical stabilization with cement, lime or similar additives provides stiffness equal to or slightly better than that of asphalt‑stabilized layers; recommended values are listed in Table 9.3. When subgrade strengthening is achieved through lime or cement stabilization the stabilized portion is modeled with a coefficient of 0.12 while the native subgrade retains its project‑specific CBR value. For brick pavements the coefficient is applied only to the depth of the brick layer and must be selected according to the type of base: “Brick with Rigid Base” when the bricks rest on a concrete base, and “Brick with Flexible Base” when they are over stone or other aggregate; the underlying base material receives its own separate coefficient. In deflection‑based design programs for FB surface courses (FB‑1, FB‑2, FB Modified) used to design FB overlays a structural layer coefficient of 0.20 must be used because the program assumes the overlay material is Superpave. Thin Superpave asphalt overlays—specifically the 6.3 mm Thin Asphalt Overlay or the 9.5 mm Fine‑Grade Wearing Course—may use the Structural Layer Coefficient when the existing surface is sound asphalt, less than 2 % of the pavement requires patching, both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded to good, and no structural upgrade of the pavement is required; designers reference Tables 9.3, 9.5, 10.4, and 10.5 for the appropriate coefficient number, placement depths, and course thicknesses. For premium Superpave mixtures such as asphalt rubber gap‑graded (AR‑GG) wearing courses the structural layer coefficients supplied in Table 10.6 are appropriate for new construction or overlay projects on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements that satisfy Interstate or Interstate look‑alike criteria of greater than 25,000 ADT, cumulative ESALs exceeding 10 million over a 20‑year design life, and a project area larger than 50,000 square yards; the coefficient should not be used for shoulders unless lane and shoulder are placed in a single pass, nor for temporary lanes or crossovers. Similarly, for crumb rubber modified asphalt binder (CRMAB) dense‑graded courses the Table 10.6 coefficients may be used for new construction or overlays on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements that meet the same > 25,000 ADT, > 10 million ESALs (20‑year), and > 50,000 sq yd thresholds, and are recommended for the upper layers of the pavement section where CRMAB performance is best utilized. Structural coefficients are appropriate when designing an asphalt‑concrete (AC) overlay on an existing AC pavement; in this application the effective structural number of the existing layers is obtained either with the Component Method or with the Non‑Destructive Testing Method, and for the Component Method the depths of each layer and their corresponding structural coefficients from Table 9.3 are entered into DARWin with a drainage coefficient of 1.0. For Interstate projects the existing subbase may be omitted from the evaluation because it may be in poor condition, and on non‑Interstate roads the subbase is excluded when the design 18‑kip ESALs exceed 5,000,000 unless its condition has been verified through investigation. In Section 5.12 Overlays, a Structural Coefficient‑based thin overlay design is considered unsuitable when the existing surface is not asphalt, when more than 2 % of the pavement requires patching indicating it is not structurally sound, when surface or subsurface drainage is poor and will not be upgraded, or when a structural upgrade is required; under those circumstances another design approach such as a thicker overlay, a different material, or additional rehabilitation must be considered. When large areas of a project contain significantly different soil types and CBR values, designers may split the alignment into a limited number of subsections each with its own pavement design rather than rely on a single Structural Coefficient (Section 6.2 Subgrade Soils). Likewise, if the existing subgrade CBR is at or below 5 and the pavement quantity reaches at least 25 000 SY on a Traffic Pattern Group of 1 to 4, a cost‑benefit analysis may favor subgrade strengthening techniques such as lime or cement stabilization, which are modeled with a Structural Coefficient of 0.12; rock‑capped sections in the same project would instead be represented by a CBR of 20. In Section 10.2 AC Overlay of AC Pavement, the preferred design approaches are the Component Method or the Non‑Destructive Testing Method rather than other techniques, and the Remaining Life Method is therefore not chosen in this context. If pavement cores cannot be obtained, the design must rely on Pavement History data from the Roadway Management System. Additionally, subbase material should be excluded from the structural evaluation on Interstates or when the 18‑kip ESALs exceed 5 000 000 on other roadways unless a specific investigation confirms its condition. Design inputs and requirements

Structural Layer Coefficient application for CRMAB mixtures is limited to sites larger than 50,000 square yards that carry more than 25,000 ADT and generate over 10 million ESALs in a 20‑year design life; the material must be a dense‑graded wearing or binder course using a polymer‑modified PG 64E-22 binder produced by the wet process (RAP/RAS may be included as specified) with layer thickness not exceeding 2 ½ inches, and it is intended for new construction or overlays on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements but should not be used where extensive handwork, temporary lanes, or separate shoulder paving is required. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 130–131)

In PennDOT brick pavement projects, the structural analysis shall follow Chapter 2 Project Considerations subsection 2.8 and apply the appropriate structural coefficient from Table 9.3 based on the base material; a concrete (rigid) base requires the “Brick with Rigid Base” coefficient, while a stone or other flexible base requires the “Brick with Flexible Base” coefficient, and the underlying base material has its own separate coefficient applied only to its depth. When a deflection‑based design program is used for FB surface courses, the overlay depth must be adjusted because the program assumes Superpave, and a structural coefficient of 0.20 must be used for FB courses; additional coefficients for other paving materials are provided in Chapter 9. For overlay design, the structural layer coefficient number, its allowable minimum and maximum placement depths, and the thicknesses of scratch or leveling courses shall be taken from Tables 9.3, 9.5, 10.4, and 10.5; applicable average daily traffic limits are drawn from Table 10.1 or Table 10.2; overall pavement depth limits are those in Table 9.5 and the weather provisions of Publication 408 Section 413.3(b); on Interstates a thin Superpave wearing‑course overlay less than 1 ½ inches may be placed only with prior PDAU approval, while on non‑Interstate routes the same thickness is permitted only when the existing surface is asphalt, structurally sound, has good drainage and requires no structural upgrade. For an AR‑GG wearing course, the structural layer coefficients provided for Superpave courses in Table 10.6 should be used for the AR‑GG layer in the structural pavement design. For CRMAB layers, the structural layer coefficient must be taken from the Superpave values listed in Table 10.6 as required by the asphalt concrete pavement guidelines. When lime or cement stabilization is applied to subgrade soils, the designer shall model the stabilized layer using a Structural Coefficient of 0.12 together with the original project‑specific CBR value for the underlying subgrade. In full‑depth flexible pavement design, the structural coefficients (a_i) for each layer shall be obtained directly from Table 9.3 and all layer drainage coefficients shall be set to 1.0; the selection of a_i values must also satisfy the minimum and maximum course depths listed in Tables 9.4 and 9.5 while considering surface, base and subbase costs per inch. For AC overlay of existing AC pavement, the structural coefficients from Table 9.3 are entered together with layer depths into DARWin and all drainage coefficients shall equal 1.0; for Interstates the existing subbase is excluded from the structural evaluation, and on non‑Interstate routes the subbase may be omitted when the design 18‑kip ESALs exceed 5,000,000 unless a subbase investigation confirms its quality and drainability. [1]

Design methods and criteria

For brick pavements, the structural analysis shall use the coefficient from Table 9.3 appropriate to the base condition: “Brick with Rigid Base” when the bricks rest on a concrete (rigid) base and “Brick with Flexible Base” when they rest on stone or another flexible base; in either case the coefficient is applied only to the depth of the brick layer, while a separate coefficient is assigned to the underlying base material and applied only to its depth. When a deflection‑based design program is employed for FB surface courses, the overlay depth must be adjusted because the program assumes a Superpave material, and a structural coefficient of 0.20 shall be used for the FB courses to determine the required overlay thickness. In overlay design, the Structural Layer Coefficient is obtained by consulting Tables 9.3, 9.5, 10.4 and 10.5, which provide the coefficient value together with the allowable minimum and maximum placement depths and the thicknesses for scratch or leveling courses; Table 10.1 (or Table 10.2) supplies the average daily traffic limits that must be satisfied for a given application, and the design procedure consists of selecting the appropriate coefficient from those tables and then checking that the proposed overlay depth and traffic conditions fall within the tabulated ranges. For an AR‑GG wearing course, the structural layer coefficients provided for Superpave courses in Table 10.6 shall be used as the design input for the AR‑GG layer. In subgrade‑strengthening design, a Structural Layer Coefficient of 0.12 is applied as a modeling parameter for lime or cement stabilized subgrades while retaining the project‑specific CBR value for the underlying native subgrade; this coefficient is incorporated into the pavement design calculations performed for the selected strengthening technique to evaluate thickness and material requirements for the treated layer. In full‑depth flexible pavement design, the structural coefficient for each layer (ai) is applied in the structural number equation SN = a1 d1 + a2 d2 m2 + a3 d3 m3 + … an dn mn, where all drainage coefficients mi shall be set to 1.0 and the ai values are taken from Table 9.3; the calculated SN must exceed the required design SN, and layer thicknesses are obtained by using either the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method available in DARWin, while the Layered Analysis Thickness Design method is not used. For AC overlay of an existing AC pavement, the Component Method (or the Non‑Destructive Testing Method) shall be used by entering each layer’s depth and its coefficient from Table 9.3 into the DARWin program, which then computes the effective structural number using the structural‑number equation of Section 9.8 with drainage coefficients set to 1.0; average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 provide the ESAL inputs for future traffic calculations, and after the Future Traffic SN and Effective Existing SN are obtained, DARWin calculates the required overlay SN and a thickness is selected using either the specified or optimized thickness design methods, ensuring that the calculated SN exceeds the design SN; subbase layers are omitted from the structural evaluation on Interstates and other roadways when the design 18‑kip ESALs exceed 5,000,000 unless subbase quality has been verified. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (p. 62), Ch. 5 (pp. 103–129), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 9 (pp. 172–173), Ch. 10 (p. 180)

In sections dealing with different pavement types, the design criteria for the structural layer coefficient are defined explicitly. For FB surface courses, when a deflection‑based design program is applied, the overlay depth must be adjusted because the program assumes Superpave material, and the design criteria require that a structural coefficient of 0.20 be used for FB courses when designing FB overlays. For Superpave thin asphalt overlays, the design must reference the prescribed tables to obtain an appropriate Structural Coefficient number and to verify that the overlay thickness lies within the minimum and maximum placement depths listed in those tables; specifically, for 6.3 mm overlays the applicable tables are 9.3, 9.5, 10.4 and 10.5, while for 9.5 mm fine‑grade overlays the applicable tables are 9.5, 10.4 and 10.5. For an AR‑GG wearing course, the guidance requires that the Structural Layer Coefficient values listed for Superpave courses in Table 10.6 be applied to the AR‑GG layer in the structural pavement design, and no other specific safety factors, reliability thresholds, or additional assumptions are stipulated for the coefficient itself. In the structural number design procedure, the coefficients a_i for each pavement layer shall be taken directly from Table 9.3; alternative values are not permitted, and the procedure does not impose additional thresholds, safety factors, or reliability limits on the coefficients themselves, although they are used together with drainage coefficients m_i that shall be set to 1.0 in the structural number equation. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 103–129), Ch. 9 (pp. 172–173)

Structural section and dimensions

For AR‑GG wearing courses (section 5.16) the structural layer coefficient is applied and the layer thickness should not exceed 2 ½ inches; the section does not provide any other dimensional requirements for base, subbase, treated‑soil, edge‑support or widening. For CRMAB dense‑graded courses (section 5.17) the structural layer coefficients provided for Superpave courses in Table 10.6 should be used and the thickness of any CRMAB course must not exceed 2 ½ inches; likewise no dimensional requirements for base, subbase, treated‑soil, edge‑support or widening are specified. For AC overlays on existing asphalt pavements (section 10.2) the structural layer coefficient is employed by entering the depth of each pavement layer—including overlay, base, subbase and any other layers—together with its coefficient from Table 9.3; the specification does not prescribe numeric thicknesses but requires that all layer depths be supplied for the effective structural number calculation, and when the existing subbase may be in poor condition it shall not be included in the structural evaluation for Interstate overlay designs. For structural design the structural layer coefficients listed for Superpave courses in Table 10.6 are to be used for the CRMAB layer. [1]

Materials and mixture design

In section 2.1 GENERAL, the Structural Layer Coefficient must be tied to the type of stabilization material used in a pavement layer; basic pulverization will produce a product similar in support characteristics to a standard 2A subbase, calcium‑chloride or similar additives will be slightly improved, asphalt‑stabilized layers are treated like cold‑recycled mixes, and chemical stabilization with cement, lime or related additives provides stiffness equal to or marginally better than that of asphalt‑stabilized material. In section 6.2 SUBGRADE SOILS, when a pavement design includes a subgrade that has been stabilized with lime or cement, the Structural Layer Coefficient must be assigned a value of 0.12; this coefficient is used together with the original project‑specific CBR for the underlying native soil to represent the strengthened layer, while rock‑capped sections are modeled using a CBR of 20 instead of a structural coefficient, and no additional mixture proportions or detailed material property specifications are required beyond identifying the stabilization type (lime or cement) and applying the prescribed coefficient value. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

In overlay design the appropriate material and mixture are identified by consulting the prescribed tables that provide the Structural Coefficient number together with the allowable minimum and maximum placement depths and the required scratch or leveling‑course thicknesses, thereby aligning the selected mix with structural needs while fitting within the existing pavement profile. Only Superpave 6.3 mm wearing courses may be placed on sound surfaces and shall use a PG 64E‑22 binder; the 9.5 mm Fine Grade mix is also limited to sound pavements but shall not use that binder, and both mixes must respect the depth limits shown in Tables 9.3, 9.5, 10.4, and 10.5 as well as any ADT restrictions from Tables 10.1/10.2. When a leveling course is required it shall be at least 1 inch thick and may be counted as a one‑inch paving layer in the structural calculations. When an asphalt rubber gap‑graded (AR‑GG) wearing course is chosen, the designer should apply the Structural Layer Coefficients listed for Superpave courses in Table 10.6 to the AR‑GG layer in the structural pavement design; the mixture must be placed as a wet‑process, gap‑graded wearing course whose thickness does not exceed 2½ inches and is limited to projects that would normally use a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, have an average daily traffic greater than 25,000 vehicles, cumulative ESALs greater than 10 million over a 20‑year design life, and an area larger than 50,000 square yards, while also being cost‑effective given its premium price. The mix may include RAP or RAS as specified, but should not be used where significant handwork is required, in shoulders unless the lane and shoulder are paved in one pass, or in temporary lanes or crossovers. When a crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder (CRMAB) mixture is incorporated, the Structural Layer Coefficient values tabulated for Superpave courses should be applied to the CRMAB layer in the structural design. The mixture is recommended only for the upper wearing and binder courses of new construction because its superior performance justifies the higher cost; placement in lower layers provides no added benefit. Thickness must not exceed 2½ inches and it should not be used where significant handwork or raking is required due to stiffness. Use of CRMAB is appropriate on Interstates or Interstate‑look‑alikes with greater than 25,000 ADT, greater than 10 million ESALs (20‑year design life), and greater than 50,000 square yards, but it should not be used in shoulders unless the lane and shoulder are paved in one pass, and never in temporary lanes or crossovers. The mixture is required to be produced using the wet process only. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–131)

Structural details and interfaces

When designing an interface that involves a historic brick pavement, the type of underlying base shall dictate which structural coefficient is used for the brick layer; a concrete (rigid) base shall require the “Brick with Rigid Base” coefficient while a stone or other flexible base shall call for the “Brick with Flexible Base” coefficient. In either case the chosen coefficient applies only to the depth of the brick itself, and the underlying base material has a separate structural coefficient that is applied solely to the depth of that base. Design of the interfaces between pavement layers relies on the Structural Coefficient values supplied in the design tables; the appropriate coefficient shall determine the allowable minimum and maximum placement depths and the required thicknesses for scratch or leveling courses that form the bond between existing and new material. For Superpave thin‑asphalt overlays Tables 9.3, 9.5, 10.4 and 10.5 are consulted to select the correct coefficient and thus set the depth limits and course thicknesses, and a separate leveling course of minimum 1‑inch thickness may be placed and counted as a structural layer in the calculations. The same procedure applies to the 9.5‑mm fine‑grade overlay, using Tables 9.5, 10.4 and 10.5 for its coefficient‑driven depth and thickness requirements. [1]

Verification and expected performance

The completed Structural Layer Coefficient is checked by confirming that the design employs the coefficient numbers and the associated minimum and maximum placement depths shown in Tables 9.3, 9.5, 10.4 and 10.5 together with the allowed scratch or leveling‑course thicknesses; the overlay depth must meet the required minimum 1‑inch thickness for a leveling course and, where a Superpave Wearing Course is specified, it shall not be less than 1 ½ inches unless an exception is granted, and the existing pavement must satisfy the soundness criteria (surface asphalt, structurally sound with less than 2 % patching required, and drainage good or to be upgraded) before a thin overlay is placed. In addition, compliance with performance criteria is confirmed by first calculating the pavement’s Design Structural Number (SN) and then converting it to individual layer thicknesses using SN = a1d1 + a2d2m2 + … ; the calculated SN must be greater than the required Design SN for the pavement to be considered structurally adequate. Thicknesses are obtained only by applying either the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method in DARWin, with all layer drainage coefficients set to 1.0 and structural coefficients taken from Table 9.3, which satisfies constructability and consistency rules. Constructability is verified by using a separate leveling course rather than combining it with a binder layer, ensuring uniform depth placement of the wearing and/or binder courses, and applying any interlayer or safety‑edge details within their specified thickness limits; the proposed layer depths must also fall within the minimum and maximum course‑depth limits given in Tables 9.4 and 9.5. Performance compliance is further supported by selecting the prescribed mix (e.g., PG 64E‑22 for a 6.3 mm Wearing Course) and meeting the drainage and structural conditions outlined for thin overlays. Consistency with project requirements is achieved by documenting the Structural Coefficient number, depth limits and material selections in the contract items as required by the overlay specifications, and by evaluating cost considerations for surface, base and subbase materials to confirm that the design aligns with overall project requirements.
