Rigid Pavement

Rigid pavement consists of a continuous Portland‑cement concrete slab that furnishes the structural capacity required to sustain present and projected traffic loads while delivering a durable, load‑bearing surface[1][2]. It achieves long‑term serviceability by controlling excessive slab deflection, corner stresses and loss of subgrade support through sufficient slab thickness, appropriate joint spacing, dowelled transverse joints, tied shoulders and an effective drainage system that together provide reliable load transfer across working cracks[1][2]. The design is based on mechanistic‑empirical procedures that incorporate traffic demand (e.g., 18‑kip ESALs), subgrade reaction, concrete modulus of rupture and a prescribed reliability level, resulting in slab thicknesses typically ranging from four to eighteen inches with compressive strengths of 4 000–10 000 psi[1][2]. Strict quality‑control measures—such as minimum opening strength, controlled curing, joint sealing and limits on edge slumping—ensure a uniformly supported pavement that minimizes maintenance, controls distress mechanisms and maintains rideability throughout its design life[1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Rigid Pavement” also include the following subtopics: Composite Pavement, Contraction Joint, Dowel Bar, Expansion Joint, Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement, Load Transfer, Longitudinal Joint, Portland Cement Concrete, Roller-Compacted Concrete, Tie Bar, Transverse Joint, and Widened Lane.

Purpose and design objectives

Rigid pavement is intended to provide the structural capacity required to sustain present and projected traffic loads while delivering a durable, load‑bearing concrete slab that can safely support vehicles after construction.[1][2] The primary engineering problem it addresses is the control of excessive slab deflection, high corner stresses, and loss of subgrade support that would otherwise lead to joint faulting, edge cracking, differential heaving, and eventual slab breakup.[1] To meet this need the design supplies sufficient slab thickness, appropriate joint spacing, dowelled transverse joints, tied concrete shoulders, and a functional drainage system that together ensure effective load transfer across working transverse cracks and reduce edge and corner stresses.[1] By basing calculations on saturated subgrade strength tests the pavement retains reserve capacity to withstand periods of reduced support such as spring thaw, thereby limiting settlement and void formation beneath the slab.[1] Performance criteria are expressed through initial and terminal serviceability levels and quantified traffic demand in equivalent single axle loads, for example an 18‑kip ESAL design over the initial performance period.[1] For low‑volume roads defined as average daily traffic ≤ 2,000 vehicles and speeds ≤ 55 mph, roller‑compacted concrete offers a high‑strength, low‑maintenance alternative that does not require forms, reinforcement, or dowel bars.[1] The design also mandates strict quality‑control procedures, including methods to determine opening‑to‑traffic strength and measures to prevent damage before service, ensuring the slab achieves the required structural integrity at the time it is placed in service.[2] Concrete placement must be halted when the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·h unless mitigation actions are applied, protecting the mix from premature drying and loss of strength.[2] Together these provisions address the fundamental performance need of a long‑lasting, uniformly supported concrete pavement that minimizes maintenance, controls distress mechanisms, and maintains rideability throughout its design life.[1][2]

Functional objectives for rigid pavement require that the structure be capable of safely supporting the anticipated traffic volume—limited to an average daily traffic of 2 000 vehicles or less—and design speeds up to 55 mph while also permitting incident‑management activities without excessive deformation [1]. Additionally, the slab may not be placed in service until the concrete has attained the required compressive strength, typically a minimum of 1 000 psi as confirmed by cylinder testing and certified by the Representative before opening to traffic [2]. Structural objectives call for a concrete slab thickness ranging from 4 in. to 18 in. with a compressive strength between about 4 000 and 10 000 psi, joint spacing governed by the 15–20‑ft rule for slabs thinner than eight inches or by a thickness‑times‑2.5‑ft rule for thicker sections, and high‑performance dowel bars placed with a low water‑to‑cement ratio to provide adequate pull‑out resistance [1][2]. Edge slumping is limited to no more than one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and one‑quarter inch where lanes meet shoulders, and longitudinal joints are required to be positioned away from the driving lane and correctly aligned with dowel bars that satisfy the same pull‑out criteria; reinforcement must be clean, fully embedded, and tie bars must meet identical pull‑out requirements before finishing [2][1]. Full‑depth replacement is prescribed for slabs eight inches thick or greater, and strict control of subgrade preparation, concrete placement, jointing, finishing, curing and sawing is mandated to preserve structural integrity; partial‑depth repairs are confined to a depth not exceeding one‑half the slab thickness and to patches covering no more than ten percent of the slab area unless a cost‑effectiveness analysis justifies larger intervention, with adequate spacing between patch and adjacent slab and the use of rigid polystyrene boards or premolded expansion‑joint material equal in width to the existing joint to prevent compression failure by point loading [1]. Durability objectives require effective drainage of surface and subsurface water, provision of subdrainage where needed, and reserve structural capacity to accommodate reduced support during spring thaws, while frost‑heave protection is achieved through shoulder design rather than solely increasing slab thickness [1]. Prescribed curing regimes, complete joint sealing, protection of the work from rain, and recompaction of any disturbed subbase further enhance durability, and all saw cuts must be full depth and no wider than one‑half inch before they are opened to traffic [2][1]. Voids encountered during construction or rehabilitation are to be filled with durable grout—either high‑density polyurethane or cementitious grout—that cures for at least twelve hours before the pavement is reopened, thereby restoring full support and reducing the potential for pumping, faulting, and cracking [1]. Safety objectives include providing tied concrete shoulders to reduce lateral edge stresses and lower loss‑of‑control risk, while surface friction must be ensured by achieving the required texture through diamond grinding on exposed surfaces where posted speeds are 35 mph or higher or by grooving to obtain a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 in for radii under fifty feet; any high points or depressions exceeding three‑sixteenths of an inch must be corrected by grinding before the pavement is placed in service [1][2]. Construction safety is further protected by eliminating drop‑off hazards, preventing exposure of incomplete cuts to vehicles, and prohibiting traffic reopening until stabilizing materials have fully cured [2][1]. Serviceability objectives demand a smooth ride with acceptable roughness as measured by ride‑quality assessments rather than the International Roughness Index for payment incentives, and the finished surface must remain within three‑sixteenths of an inch straightedge tolerance both parallel and perpendicular to the centerline; the mean texture depth requirement of at least 0.040 in for tight curves ensures adequate skid resistance and the surface texture should be matched to existing pavement conditions [2][1].

Scope and applicability

Rigid pavement, most commonly Portland‑cement concrete, is appropriate wherever the structural system relies on a concrete slab substrate and the project seeks durability, load distribution, or long service life advantages [1]. It is expressly suitable for jointed plain concrete (JPCP), jointed reinforced concrete (JRCP) and, in special cases approved per the 1993 AASHTO Guide, continuously reinforced concrete (CRCP) [1]. The design approach also applies to reinforced or plain concrete traffic lanes used as base courses or structural elements that require joint treatment, with transverse joints employing premolded expansion‑joint fillers spanning the full lane width and at least one inch thick, and longitudinal or structural joints using filler pieces of minimum eight‑foot length securely joined [2]. Rigid pavement may be employed for sidewalks and curb‑ramps constructed as four‑inch slabs on a compacted six‑inch aggregate bed over a ten‑inch foundation, incorporating transverse dummy joints spaced about five feet apart, each one‑eighth inch wide and extending at least one inch into the slab, together with three‑quarter‑inch premolded expansion joints; the concrete mix is limited to a slump of five inches, curing begins within thirty minutes using a Type 2 white pigmented liquid membrane compound, and backfilling may occur after a minimum cure period of seventy‑two hours [2]. The material is preferred for full‑depth repairs or reconstruction of deteriorated slabs when sections are roughly five hundred feet or longer, as well as for lane‑width patches, shoulders, drainage improvements, slabjacking, stabilization, dowel‑bar retrofit, diamond grinding, joint and crack repair, friction enhancement, or cross‑stitching, provided a prepared surface, continuous placement operations, and a uniform nominal depth covering at least 5,600 square yards are available [1]. For widening or lane‑addition projects on existing uniform‑depth rigid pavements built since 1945, full lane additions of ten feet or more should be designed to the same thickness as the original pavement unless heavier traffic dictates otherwise; partial widenings less than ten feet must duplicate the existing pavement type, depth, and joint spacing, with transverse joints limited to a minimum of ten feet and a maximum of seventeen feet when the original joints exceed twenty feet [1]. Concrete shoulders may be provided on interstates and other limited‑access freeways where excavation or scarifying of the existing shoulder is required; shoulder joint spacing shall not exceed twenty feet and a minimum three‑foot wide concrete shoulder of Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S, or 6‑SP may be used when only a narrow portion of a high‑type paved shoulder shows distress [1]. Rigid pavement overlays are appropriate in two principal forms [1]. Bonded Portland‑cement concrete overlays are suitable when the underlying slab is in fair to excellent condition, the subgrade is stable and drainage functional, and any significant cracking or faulting has been remedied so that the new layer can act monolithically with the old slab [1]. Unbonded overlays employing a separation layer such as a geotextile or asphalt interlayer are appropriate for moderately to severely deteriorated pavements where the existing slab can serve as a relatively uniform subbase, provided field‑weight‑deflectometer testing confirms adequate support; asphalt‑concrete overlays may also be placed over JPCP, JRCP, CRCP, or concrete slabs previously covered by an asphalt layer that is stripped prior to the new overlay [1]. Roller‑compacted concrete is suitable for low‑volume roadways with average daily traffic of 2,000 vehicles or less and speed limits of 55 mph or lower, serving as a primary wearing surface, a concrete shoulder adjacent to reinforced or plain cement‑concrete pavements, a temporary pavement during construction, or a base course beneath an asphalt layer [1]. Long‑Life Concrete Pavement projects are acceptable when slab thickness is at least eight inches, joints are unbonded, high‑performance dowel bars and other high‑quality materials are used, and the site can provide a central mix plant and slip‑form paver; these conditions also support Restricted Performance Specification sections, while LLCP is not appropriate for thin (less than eight inches) or bonded concrete applications [1]. Preservation treatments such as full‑depth patching limited to ten percent of the total area unless cost‑effectiveness can be demonstrated, joint rehabilitation, diamond grinding, micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, and thin Superpave asphalt overlays within prescribed thickness limits are permissible on structurally sound concrete slabs that do not require a full redesign; dowel‑bar retrofits and other Concrete Pavement Restoration techniques may also be employed [1]. Finally, rigid pavement is typically specified for high‑volume corridors such as interstates and expressways where the slab rests on an asphalt‑treated or cement‑treated permeable base over a 2A subbase, with drainage layers limited to ATPB or CTPB, open‑graded subbases prohibited, and loss‑of‑support factors applied to account for potential erosion or pumping of the subbase material [1].

Alternative design approaches are preferred whenever the existing rigid pavement cannot be restored to a serviceable condition within the practical limits of standard remedial actions. If more than ten percent of the surface requires new patches—excluding sound existing repairs—the economics of extensive patching deteriorate and an overlay or full reconstruction becomes advisable. Likewise, when a deteriorated segment extends roughly five hundred feet or longer and intermittent patches would leave less than ten feet of original slab between them, piecemeal repair is ineffective and a full‑width reconstruction should be pursued. Slab stabilization is limited to about twenty‑five percent of transverse joints and does not eliminate surface depressions, increase structural capacity, or correct faulting; projects that need additional load‑carrying ability or extensive fault correction therefore require another pavement type. Bonded rigid overlays are only suitable when the existing concrete exhibits minimal cracking or faulting; significant distress, unstable subgrade support, stripping of asphalt, or unresolved drainage problems transmit through a bonded overlay and degrade performance, making unbonded concrete or flexible asphalt overlays the preferred alternatives. Roller‑compacted concrete is restricted to roads with an average daily traffic of two thousand vehicles or less and posted speeds of fifty‑five miles per hour or lower, and it should be avoided where the International Roughness Index will be used for payment because RCC surfaces tend to produce higher roughness values. Long‑Life Concrete Pavement is unsuitable when slab thickness would be under eight inches, when a bonded system is required, when site constraints prevent provision of roughly four acres for an on‑site mix plant, or when the three‑foot side clearances needed by slip‑form pavers cannot be met; in such cases conventional concrete or flexible pavement should be selected. In regions expecting differential frost heave, increasing slab thickness offers only limited mitigation and the standard rigid‑pavement design does not incorporate specific frost‑design provisions, prompting consideration of alternative pavement types or additional frost‑control measures. Continuously reinforced concrete pavement (CRCP) is confined to special cases; it must be designed according to the 1993 AASHTO Guide using DARWin, require special contract provisions, and obtain director approval—otherwise a jointed plain concrete or flexible pavement design is appropriate. When the existing slab shows visible rocking or instability that makes full‑depth repairs impractical, an unbonded concrete overlay can be used if the remaining pavement can serve as a relatively uniform subbase; however, if fracturing of the slab is preferred, the design should shift to an asphalt overlay using the AC‑over‑fractured‑PCC module. Preservation treatments are also limited by thickness: scratch overlays exceeding four and a half inches over a Superpave 9.5 mm wearing course or five inches over a Superpave 12.5 mm mix disqualify the project from pavement‑preservation criteria and require full 3R design, and concrete patching that exceeds ten percent of the total pavement area is ineligible for preservation unless a cost‑effectiveness analysis demonstrates otherwise. In all such situations a life‑cycle cost analysis is recommended to compare restoration, concrete overlay, asphalt overlay, or full reconstruction alternatives and identify the most economical solution. Design inputs and requirements

Designing a rigid pavement begins with a thorough characterization of the site. The classification of the location—whether it is a shoulder, a temporary median crossover, or a low‑volume collector road—guides many subsequent decisions, and the existing pavement must be evaluated for its overall condition, including the functionality of drainage systems and any visible distress that can be quantified through core sampling, deflection testing, or other non‑destructive techniques. The assessment also records the percent of concrete that is patched; if this exceeds ten percent, a supplemental cost‑effectiveness analysis becomes necessary. Geotechnical information focuses on the prepared subgrade and subbase, confirming that they are capable of sustaining the compaction requirements of roller‑compacted concrete and providing adequate friction and ride quality. Subgrade stiffness is expressed through an effective k‑value, and the suitability of the foundation is verified during design.

Traffic inputs combine both volume and loading characteristics. For low‑volume applications the average daily traffic should not exceed two thousand vehicles and the design speed should be fifty‑five miles per hour or less, while the structural analysis also incorporates a standardized load expressed as eighteen‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) over the initial performance period. Serviceability targets are established for the beginning and end of the pavement’s design life, allowing reliability levels and overall standard deviations to be factored into the prediction of performance.

Material requirements are defined by concrete mix specifications and mechanical properties. The mix must contain cement, aggregate, and water with a water‑cement ratio typically ranging from 0.30 to 0.40, and the maximum aggregate size is limited to less than one inch. Desired compressive strengths fall between four thousand and ten thousand pounds per square inch, while the 28‑day mean modulus of rupture and elastic modulus provide the stiffness parameters needed for slab analysis. Additional concrete quality metrics include air content, durability observations, and the susceptibility of aggregates to polishing. Joint behavior is captured through a load‑transfer coefficient, and moisture control is addressed by an overall drainage coefficient that reflects joint performance under varying environmental conditions.

Environmental considerations are woven throughout the design process. Climate‑related distress mechanisms such as frost damage and inadequate drainage are identified as potential causes of pavement deterioration, prompting designers to incorporate appropriate moisture‑control measures and to evaluate how local weather patterns may affect concrete durability. Existing‑condition details also note whether the new slab will be placed on an untreated surface, a rehabilitated base, or a newly constructed base, ensuring that the interaction between the concrete and its foundation is fully understood before construction proceeds. The design of rigid‑pavement projects is governed by a comprehensive suite of state publications and national design procedures that together prescribe geometric, material, drainage, structural and performance criteria. All concrete work must conform to the Publication 408 specifications, which set mix‑design requirements, strength development schedules, joint sealing materials, slab‑stabilization methods, overlay thickness limits and provisions for long‑life concrete pavement [1]. In parallel, the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures for rigid pavements provide the structural design framework, requiring an overall standard deviation of 0.35 and inputs such as projected 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads, target serviceability levels, mean concrete modulus of rupture, effective subgrade k‑value, reliability level, load‑transfer coefficient J and drainage coefficient C_d [1]. Joint spacing is limited to 30 feet for jointed reinforced slabs and 15 feet for jointed plain slabs, with shoulder joints not exceeding 20 feet; longitudinal joints must fall within twelve inches of the final pavement markings and may not cross a driving lane unless expressly shown [1][2]. When lanes are widened, the extension into the shoulder is limited to two feet if average daily traffic exceeds 5 000 vehicles or the product of ADT and truck percentage exceeds 500, otherwise only one foot may be added [1]. Shoulder types follow definitions in Design Manual Part 2, with full‑depth Portland cement concrete shoulders required on urban Interstate and Expressway sections and tied or dowelled configurations permitted elsewhere; plain cement concrete shoulders are mandatory for Long‑Life Concrete Pavement projects [1]. Drainage design follows Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards, which require continuous edge and median drains placed along the outer pavement edge and on the low side of superelevated sections, sized according to the base‑drain methodology in Design Manual Part 2 Chapter 10 Section 10.3.D, with all outlets within mowable areas protected by headwalls [1][2]. Positive drainage may be achieved with a treated permeable base and under‑drain system, allowing a drainage coefficient of 1.1; otherwise the coefficient is taken as 1.0 for pavements on unbound aggregate alone [1]. Subbase construction for Interstate and Expressway projects must consist of an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base over a 2A sub‑base; open‑graded sub‑base is prohibited, the elastic modulus of the sub‑base is prescribed at 15 000 psi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30 000 psi for normal summer/fall conditions and 50 000 psi when frozen, and the ratio of sub‑base to underlying soil resilient modulus may not exceed four [1][2]. The minimum pavement area for new or reconstructed slabs is 5 600 square yards and slab thicknesses are rounded to the nearest half‑inch, except when the slab exceeds seven inches in depth and the total area is less than 30 000 square yards, in which case rounding is to the nearest inch; final thickness must fall within the limits given in the design tables [1]. Concrete mix designs must meet the combined gradation limits of PTM No. 529, with daily sieve analyses for projects larger than fifteen thousand square yards, a slump not exceeding five inches, and placement temperatures maintained between fifty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit; placement is prohibited when ambient temperature falls below forty degrees (forty‑five for accelerated‑strength mixes) or when the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·h unless mitigation measures are applied [2]. Minimum compressive strength of one thousand psi, measured per PTM No. 604, is required before traffic may be opened, and curing must commence within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates using a Type 2 white pigmented liquid membrane compound; forms may not be removed until twelve hours after placement and backfilling is permitted only after at least seventy‑two hours of cure [2]. Edge slumping limits are one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and one‑quarter inch between lanes and shoulders, verified with a twelve‑foot straightedge before the concrete loses plasticity, while surface flatness is accepted only when variation measured with a straightedge parallel to the centreline does not exceed three‑sixteenths inch and macrotexture achieves a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 inch [2]. Dowel bars and load‑transfer units must be installed so that tilt does not exceed 0.25 inch and translation does not exceed one inch, with tie bars required to resist pull‑out at a compressive strength of 3 500 psi using a hydraulic jack up to 12 000 lb [2]. A comprehensive quality‑control plan must be submitted for review at least two weeks before paving begins in accordance with Section 106.03(a)2.a, and all work must be placed on a prepared subgrade; proof rolls are required if a subbase remains over winter without protective cover [2]. Rehabilitation projects are first evaluated against the PCCP guidelines; if concrete patching exceeds ten percent of the slab area (excluding sound existing patches), a cost‑effectiveness analysis is mandatory, and partial‑depth repairs may not exceed one‑half of the existing slab thickness, with deeper distress requiring full‑depth patching per Publication 408 Sections 516 and 519 [1]. Overlay design uses average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 to calculate ESALs, applies future‑traffic pavement thickness tables together with existing effective pavement thickness tables, and rounds the required overlay thickness up to the nearest half‑inch while ensuring it falls within the minimum and maximum limits in Table 10.10; bonded overlays are permitted only when underlying concrete exhibits limited cracking, whereas unbonded overlays require a geotextile interlayer or an asphalt separation layer and joints showing more than a quarter‑inch of faulting must be milled prior to placement [1]. Long‑Life Concrete Pavement designs follow Publication 408 Section 530, requiring high‑performance dowel bars, prohibiting thin slabs under eight inches or bonded applications, mandating full‑depth replacement with subgrade preparation, and providing an incentive equal to four percent of the estimated LLCP paving cost along with a mandatory ride‑quality incentive [1]. Continuously Reinforced Concrete Pavement is designed according to the 1993 AASHTO Guide using DARWin software, requires approval by the Director of the Bureau of Development and Design, and has contract provisions defining materials and methods because standard details are omitted from Publications 72M and 408; temporary traffic‑control requirements are detailed in Publication 213 [1]. All rigid‑pavement designs must satisfy these integrated standards, specifications, performance thresholds and project constraints while adhering to any geometric or bridge design exceptions documented in the Design Manual Part 1C.

Design methods and criteria

Rigid‑pavement design is carried out through a formal pavement‑design analysis that integrates mechanistic‑empirical procedures with empirical traffic and material relationships. The primary design tool is the PennDOT concrete‑pavement methodology presented in Chapter 8 of the manual, which incorporates the AASHTO 1993 Pavement Design Procedures and uses an overall standard deviation (So) of 0.35 for rigid pavements as a key input to the calculations. In practice the designer assembles a set of inputs that includes the projected 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) for the initial performance period, the initial and terminal serviceability indices, the 28‑day mean modulus of rupture and elastic modulus of Portland‑cement concrete, a mean effective subgrade reaction coefficient (k), a chosen reliability level, the load‑transfer coefficient (J), and a drainage coefficient (C d). The k value is derived from the resilient modulus of the road‑bed soil and may be adjusted seasonally; it is further modified by a weighted loss‑of‑support factor calculated as LS₁·D₁/(D₁+D₂)+LS₂·D₂/(D₁+D₂), where LS represents the loss‑of‑support coefficient for each layer and D its thickness. Base elastic moduli are assigned according to climatic condition—15 psi for spring‑thaw, 30 psi for normal, and 50 psi for frozen conditions—and the ratio of subbase modulus to soil modulus is limited to four. The load‑transfer coefficient J reflects lateral support at slab corners; values are selected from prescribed ranges based on measured joint efficiency and are increased when k is low or truck percentages are high, and decreased when k is high or truck traffic is low. Drainage quality enters the model through C d, with a value of 1.0 for pavements on unbound aggregate alone and 1.1 for those that incorporate a treated permeable or dense‑asphalt base with an underdrain system; rehabilitation projects without major drainage improvements retain C d = 1.0.

Using the AASHTOWare® DARWin® 3.01 software, the assembled inputs generate a required slab thickness that typically falls between four and eighteen inches. For roller‑compacted concrete the water‑cement ratio is limited to 0.30–0.40 and compressive strength to 4,000–10,000 psi. Joint spacing is governed by slab thickness: sections thinner than eight inches receive control joints spaced fifteen to twenty feet apart, while thicker slabs use a spacing equal to the slab thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet. Publication 72M specifies that reinforced‑concrete (RC‑21M) sections employ thirty‑foot joint spacing with coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, whereas plain concrete (RC‑27M) uses fifteen‑foot spacing. Joint rehabilitation, dowel‑bar retrofits, cross‑stitching, slab stabilization (drilling holes and filling them with approximately one cubic foot of cement grout, 30–50 gallons of hot asphalt, or about 0.4 lb of high‑density polyurethane per square foot), and sealant reservoirs (0.75 in × 0.75 in) are prescribed maintenance actions.

When the calculated percent patching exceeds ten percent—excluding existing patches in good condition—a cost‑effectiveness analysis followed by a life‑cycle cost analysis is required to determine whether restoration, concrete overlay, or asphalt overlay provides the most economical solution. Rehabilitation begins with a preliminary engineering phase that quantifies distress through destructive core sampling and non‑destructive techniques such as deflection or faulting measurements, ground‑penetrating radar, friction testing, roughness surveys, MIRA, MIT Scan 2/T2, and tensile bond testing. If an overlay is selected, Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing of the existing slab is performed, and the design follows Chapter 10 of the PCCP guidelines, which are based on the AASHTO method.

Overlay design starts with an empirical traffic‑loading calculation that applies the average initial truck factors listed in Table 7.1 to projected volumes to obtain cumulative ESALs. For a bonded overlay these ESALs are entered into DARWin; the program’s pavement‑thickness model predicts the required concrete depth and, through its condition‑survey algorithm, determines the effective existing slab thickness. The resulting thickness is rounded up to the nearest half‑inch and checked against minimum and maximum limits in Table 10.10. For an unbonded overlay the designer consults Table 10.8 for the future‑traffic thickness and Table 10.9 for the existing effective thickness, sums the two values, and verifies compliance with the same minimum‑thickness table; a separation layer—either a geotextile interlayer or an asphalt concrete course—is specified to isolate the new slab and provide drainage.

Preservation treatments are governed by procedural limits rather than additional equations. Full‑depth concrete patching is permitted up to ten percent of the slab area unless a cost‑effective justification for exceeding that limit is demonstrated. Other accepted techniques include joint rehabilitation, diamond grinding, micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, and thin asphalt overlays combined with slab stabilization or jacking. Overlay thicknesses that exceed the allowable scratch thickness—four and one‑half inches when a Superpave 9.5 mm mix is used or five inches for a Superpave 12.5 mm mix—are excluded from preservation work. All structural treatments are verified with FWD testing to confirm pavement soundness.

Shoulder widening is tied to traffic levels: when average daily traffic exceeds 5,000 vehicles or the truck‑adjusted traffic (ADT multiplied by the percentage of trucks, buses, and recreational vehicles) exceeds 500, the widening extends two feet into the shoulder; otherwise it extends one foot. The appropriate shoulder type is then selected according to functional class. Special situations such as continuously reinforced concrete pavement (CRCP) follow the same AASHTO guide but require additional approvals, and design of a rigid‑pavement asphalt overlay begins with ESAL calculation using the average initial truck factors before employing DARWin’s secondary screen to determine overlay thickness for projected traffic. Rigid‑pavement design must satisfy a suite of performance‑based criteria that together assure structural adequacy, durability, rideability and safety [1][2]. The slab thickness is selected between four and eighteen inches, rounded to the nearest half inch for most slabs or to the nearest whole inch when the slab exceeds seven inches and the total area is under thirty thousand square yards, and must lie within the minimum‑maximum limits tabulated for concrete pavements while also meeting a compressive strength requirement of four‑thousand to ten‑thousand psi [1]. Traffic assumptions limit conventional reinforced cement concrete sections to an average daily traffic of two thousand vehicles or less with a posted speed not exceeding fifty‑five miles per hour; higher traffic volumes invoke the design procedures for heavier loads [1]. The AASHTO 1993 rigid‑pavement procedure requires specification of a reliability level and adopts an overall standard deviation of 0.35 to capture variability in inputs, with the load‑transfer coefficient J reflecting lateral support conditions [1][2]. Joint spacing is governed by slab thickness: control joints on slabs thinner than eight inches are spaced fifteen to twenty feet apart, whereas for slabs eight inches or thicker the spacing equals two‑point‑five times the slab thickness expressed in feet; reinforced concrete pavements require thirty‑foot transverse joint spacing with perpendicular, dowelled and sealed joints, while plain concrete pavements use fifteen‑foot spacing under the same detailing requirements, and concrete shoulders may not exceed twenty feet between joints [1][2]. Load transfer across working transverse cracks is provided by coated dowel bars in conventional sections and by high‑performance dowels in long‑life concrete pavement (LLCP) applications, with the load‑transfer coefficient J reflecting lateral support; tie bars or tie bolts are required only for patches longer than sixty‑five feet, and each tie bar must achieve a pull‑out resistance of twelve thousand pounds or a slip of one‑thirty‑second inch after the concrete reaches at least three‑thousand‑five hundred psi compressive strength [1][2]. The effective subgrade reaction modulus k is derived from the resilient modulus of the roadbed soil and adjusted for subbase type, thickness, loss‑of‑support factors and any shallow rigid foundation within ten feet, with a prescribed subbase‑to‑soil modulus ratio limited to four; base elastic moduli are set at fifteen thousand psi for spring‑thaw conditions, thirty thousand psi for normal conditions and fifty thousand psi for frozen conditions [1]. Drainage assumptions affect the drainage coefficient Cd, which is taken as 1.0 for fair drainage and increased to 1.1 when a treated permeable base or dense asphalt over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system provides improved moisture control [1]. For slip‑form construction, edge slump may not exceed one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes and one‑quarter inch between a lane and the shoulder measured over a six‑inch strip; dowel bars must be installed so that vertical tilt or horizontal skew does not exceed 0.25 in and longitudinal or horizontal translation is limited to one inch [2]. The finished surface must have high points or depressions no greater than three‑sixteenths inch, and for pavements subjected to traffic radii under fifty feet the mean texture depth shall be at least 0.040 in; curing temperatures are constrained between fifty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit for concrete and ambient air temperature must remain above forty degrees (forty‑five for accelerated mixes), with temperature changes not exceeding forty degrees per hour, and a minimum compressive strength of one thousand psi is required before opening to traffic [2]. Saw cuts forming joints may not be wider than one‑half inch, must cut through the full slab depth, and any new cuts may support traffic for no more than seventy‑two hours after placement; concrete patches must be placed within forty‑eight hours of excavation to avoid deterioration [2]. Rehabilitation design begins with Falling Weight Deflectometer testing to verify slab condition and joint integrity, and slabs exhibiting movement or faulting are stabilized by slab jacking before overlay placement; bonded concrete overlays may be applied only when the existing pavement is in good to fair condition, the subgrade is stable and drainage functional, with thin‑bonded applications ranging from two‑point‑five to four inches and standard bonded overlays from four to eight inches [1]. Unbonded overlays require a minimum one‑inch asphalt or geotextile separation layer and must follow joint spacing tables appropriate to pavement type and thickness; faulting thresholds dictate that joints with more than a quarter‑inch of faulting be milled, while faulting exceeding half an inch requires placement of a wearing course or grinding operation, and diamond grinding is permitted only when wheel ruts are less than half an inch deep [1]. Partial‑depth repairs are limited to a maximum depth of one‑half the existing slab thickness and are classified as Type 1 spot repairs between fifteen inches and six feet in length or Type 2 extended repairs longer than six feet, using Class AA cement‑concrete modified material for regular patches and accelerated mix for expedited work, with tie bars required only for repairs exceeding sixty‑five feet [1][2]. When replacement of more than five hundred feet of pavement across all lanes is required, a full pavement design analysis is triggered and may call for increased slab thickness; concrete patching is limited to ten percent of the total pavement area unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided [1]. When the average daily traffic exceeds five thousand vehicles or the truck‑adjusted traffic index exceeds five hundred, the widening structure must extend two feet into the shoulder; otherwise a one‑foot extension is permitted, and joint spacing in widened sections follows the original pattern: if the original transverse joint spacing is twenty feet or less, the new slabs use the same length, while if it exceeds twenty feet, jointed plain cement‑concrete pavement with spacings between ten and seventeen feet is employed, inserting intermediate joints as needed [1]. Long‑life concrete pavement requires a minimum slab thickness of eight inches, high‑performance dowel bars, full‑depth replacement under strict subgrade preparation, controlled curing and joint sawing, and compliance with Publication 408 Section 530 specifications; LLCP is not appropriate for thin (less than eight inches) or bonded concrete applications [1]. The design process incorporates rounding rules—nearest half inch for most slabs and nearest whole inch for thick slabs in small areas—and acknowledges that frost‑design provisions are not explicit; when differential frost heave is anticipated, designers must consult the relevant frost‑design guidelines [1]. Sidewalk sections of rigid pavement require a ten‑inch foundation depth parallel to the finished surface, a six‑inch compacted aggregate bed extending six inches beyond each side, four‑inch concrete placement with transverse dummy joints spaced five feet apart, joint width approximately one‑eighth inch and depth at least one inch; concrete mix slump may not exceed five inches, vibratory screeds are prohibited when slump exceeds three inches, premolded expansion joint material of three‑quarters inch must occupy the full slab depth, forms remain in place for a minimum twelve hours before removal, curing begins immediately after finishing, and backfilling is permitted only after at least seventy‑two hours of cure [2].

In determining the required slab thickness for rigid‑pavement projects, designers first apply the PennDOT concrete‑pavement design procedures, which produce a thickness that can vary from four to eighteen inches depending on anticipated traffic levels and concrete compressive strength in the range of 4 000 to 10 000 psi [1]. When a reconstruction segment extends beyond approximately five hundred feet, a full pavement‑design analysis is performed to verify whether a slab thicker than the baseline result is warranted [1]. For lane additions or widenings that are ten feet wide or greater, the thickness is normally taken from the existing pavement unless the new lane will be subjected to heavier loads, in which case the design thickness is increased to reflect the actual traffic conditions; narrower widenings simply match the existing type and depth [1]. In addition to these performance‑based calculations, standard drawings prescribe a typical layer configuration consisting of a prepared foundation ten inches deep, a compacted aggregate bed six inches thick placed over that foundation, and a concrete slab poured four inches thick [2]. Joint configuration follows both the slab‑thickness rule and the established joint pattern of the adjoining concrete; control joints for slabs thinner than eight inches are spaced at fifteen to twenty feet, while slabs eight inches or thicker receive transverse joints spaced at a distance equal to 2.5 times the slab thickness expressed in feet [1]. The guides also specify that jointed reinforced‑concrete pavements commonly use a thirty‑foot transverse joint spacing and jointed plain‑concrete pavements a fifteen‑foot spacing, with all joints cut perpendicular to traffic flow and provided with coated dowel bars [1]. Where the existing transverse joint spacing is twenty feet or less, new work retains the same slab length as the original pavement; if the legacy spacing exceeds twenty feet, the design limits slab lengths to between ten and seventeen feet by inserting intermediate joints so that slab lengths remain roughly equal [1]. For reconstruction of a single lane, new joints are aligned with pre‑existing joints, and an additional joint is introduced whenever the legacy spacing exceeds twenty feet [1]. The guides also require formation of transverse dummy joints at five‑foot intervals, each approximately one‑eighth inch wide and at least one inch deep, and where expansion joints are needed a three‑quarter‑inch premolded material is used [2]. Cross‑slope, transition geometry, and other secondary pavement features are not prescribed in these sources and must be addressed by the applicable overall design criteria outside the presented excerpts [1][2]. Tie‑ins are achieved by aligning shoulder joints with the adjacent mainline pavement and by providing longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts on concrete patches longer than sixty‑five feet; shorter patches do not require such reinforcement, and all lane additions and widenings must be tied into the existing pavement structure to maintain continuity [1].

Materials and mixture design

In rigid‑pavement projects the designer must identify every material that composes the structural system and prescribe its essential performance properties and mixture proportions [1][2]. The primary slab is required to be Portland‑cement concrete conforming to Publication 408 Section 704, using an optimized aggregate gradation and a minimum 28‑day compressive strength as set out in Section 501, while the specifications also impose a traffic‑opening strength of at least 1,000 psi [1][2]. High‑early‑strength mixes are prohibited because of their tendency to shrink‑crack, and all uniform‑depth pavements constructed since 1945 must contain air entrainment [1]. The concrete shall be placed with a reduced water‑to‑cement ratio, often as low as 0.30 and typically around 0.40, to obtain the required strength and durability, and slump is limited to five inches; when the target slump exceeds three inches vibratory screeds are not permitted [1][2]. Aggregate gradation must be controlled so that the combined percent retained remains within the limits of PTM No. 529, with daily production monitored by sieve analysis and any adjustment in aggregate proportions accompanied by corresponding changes in cementitious material or water content documented through a new trial batch [2]. For jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) and roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) the mix is produced at a central plant, with RCC employing a dense aggregate gradation whose maximum size is less than one inch and achieving a compressive strength between 4,000 psi and 10,000 psi [1]. Reinforcement must be clean of rust, dirt, oil, grease or any foreign substance; dowel bars shall be Type A or Type B coated, with Type B bars receiving a bond‑breaker lubricant on at least half their length one hour before placement, and tie bars are installed as shown on standard drawings with fifteen tie bars from each day’s pour tested for pull‑out resistance after the concrete reaches 3,500 psi [2]. All expansion joints must be filled with approved premolded material such as cork, sponge rubber, fiber joint filler or closed‑cell polypropylene foam that meets AASHTO M 153, AASHTO M 213 or ASTM D8139, the filler extending through the full slab depth and having a minimum thickness of one inch for transverse joints and a length of at least eight feet for longitudinal base‑course joints [2]. Curing materials approved by the agency are applied promptly after finishing—within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation—using methods such as white polyethylene sheets, monomolecular film or liquid membrane‑forming compounds, followed by a penetrating sealer containing at least 25 percent solids [2]. When voids develop beneath the slab the specification calls for one of three grout types: a cement‑based grout composed of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and admixtures (approximately one cubic foot per void), hot asphalt pumped as liquid asphalt (30 to 50 gallons per hole), or a two‑component high‑density polyurethane placed at roughly 0.4 lb per square foot, each required to flow into the voids and achieve its prescribed cure time [1]. The supporting base must be an asphalt‑treated or cement‑treated permeable base (ATPB or CTPB) placed over a 2A aggregate subbase; open‑graded subbase is prohibited, and the elastic modulus of the base used in effective k‑value calculations varies with season—15,000 psi for spring thaw, 30,000 psi for normal summer/fall, and 50,000 psi for frozen winter—with a loss‑of‑support factor applied to reflect possible erosion or pumping [1]. Overlay work also requires precise material definitions: thin‑bonded PCC overlays are limited to 2.5–4 inches thick, standard bonded overlays to 4–8 inches, and unbonded overlays must include a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt separation layer or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer; the overlay surface typically consists of a Superpave wearing course of nominal one‑inch thickness made with a 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix, with additional options expressed in terms of aggregate size and thickness such as 1½‑inch Superpave 9.5 mm, 2‑inch Superpave 12.5 mm or 2½‑inch Superpave 19 mm courses [1]. For partial‑depth repairs the mix must be a Class AA Cement Concrete Modified for normal cure periods, an accelerated concrete when rapid reopening is required, or a rapid‑set concrete when the volume makes conventional delivery uneconomical, and repair joints shall contain a rigid polystyrene board or premolded expansion‑joint material matching the existing joint width [1]. The design record must capture key concrete properties such as the 28‑day mean modulus of rupture, the 28‑day mean elastic modulus of the slab, and the effective coefficient of subgrade reaction (k), together with coefficients governing load transfer at joints and drainage, to ensure that every component is defined by clear performance criteria [1].

For projects that need rapid placement together with long‑term durability, a precast system employing a high‑strength Class AA mix exceeding normal PCC requirements is appropriate, and high‑performance dowel bars are specified for service lives beyond twenty years. The mix design must employ a low water‑to‑cement ratio, typically about 0.40 and as low as 0.30, to achieve compressive strengths from four thousand to ten thousand psi while maintaining workability comparable to damp gravel. A dense aggregate gradation with a maximum size under one inch is required, and admixtures or air‑entrainment are used only when necessary; the mixture must be sufficiently dry for roller compaction without forms or reinforcement. Structural thickness of the slab is governed by anticipated traffic loads and design procedures such as those of PennDOT, with a minimum of eight inches for long‑life concrete pavement but allowable thickness ranging from four to eighteen inches depending on load conditions. Immediate curing is achieved by applying a white‑pigmented liquid membrane followed later by sealing, satisfying durability requirements. For overlay situations, if an existing slab can serve as a uniform subbase, an unbonded PCC overlay is selected with thickness set by standard design tables; the interlayer material depends on joint faulting—geotextile when faults exceed one‑quarter inch, otherwise asphalt concrete. Structural details and interfaces

Rigid pavement construction incorporates a systematic arrangement of transverse contraction joints, longitudinal joints, shoulder connections and expansion joints to control cracking and provide load transfer. Transverse contraction joints are cut through the full slab depth to at least one‑third of the slab thickness and a width of approximately one‑eighth inch, and they are spaced according to pavement type—30 ft for jointed reinforced concrete (RC‑21M) sections and 15 ft for jointed plain concrete (RC‑27M) sections, while concrete shoulders may not have joint spacing greater than 20 ft. Longitudinal joints are also full‑depth, cut at least one‑quarter inch wide and one inch deep, receive tie bars, and when they intersect a curb, manhole, valve box or other rigid structure a premolded expansion‑joint filler of at least one‑half inch thickness (three‑quarters inch adjacent to the structure) is placed through the entire slab depth. The filler material used in transverse joints must be supplied in lengths equal to the lane width, have a minimum overall thickness of one inch, present a smooth top surface and contain pre‑punched holes that receive dowel bars without reducing the filler thickness; pieces for longitudinal or structural joints shall be at least eight feet long. Reinforcement consists of clean steel mesh or rebar placed on approved supports before concrete placement, with tie bars installed throughout the slab except within 24 inches of any dowelled contraction joint and in corners, Type C patches and other indicated locations; longitudinal cracks are additionally treated by cross‑stitching with epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars placed at an angle across the crack. When a concrete patch exceeds 65 ft in length, longitudinal tie bars or bolts are required to provide additional restraint. Load transfer across transverse joints and mid‑panel cracks is achieved primarily by dowel‑bar retrofits, typically six epoxy‑coated smooth dowels installed in each wheel path and anchored with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders; the dowel bars are placed in drilled holes of approximately five‑eighths inch diameter and must be aligned so that vertical tilt or horizontal skew does not exceed 0.25 inch at either end and longitudinal translation is limited to one inch. For Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (LLCP) the dowel bars are high‑performance types, and LLCP applications are limited to slabs eight inches thick or greater and to bonded pavement constructions. The load‑transfer coefficient (J) is selected based on observed joint performance, with higher values used for softer subgrades or higher truck traffic, and at least 25 % of transverse joints should be stabilized when no prior testing has been performed. Joint rehabilitation includes spall repair, reconstruction of a sealant reservoir sized 0.75 in × 0.75 in and sealing with rubberized or asphalt‑based material; if minor spalling is caused by poor joint performance, slab stabilization using cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing or high‑density polyurethane may be applied. Partial‑depth repairs limited to one‑half the slab thickness are permitted for transverse or longitudinal joints, and rigid polystyrene boards are employed along longitudinal joints while premolded expansion‑joint material is used along transverse joints to preserve original joint width and prevent intrusion of repair material. When widening an existing pavement or adding a lane, the new portion must be tied to the existing slab; if the original transverse joint spacing is 20 ft or less the extension uses slabs of the same length, whereas spacings greater than 20 ft require a Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement (JPCP) with joint lengths limited to a minimum of 10 ft and a maximum of 17 ft, inserting intermediate joints as needed. Transition zones are provided where wheel‑rut patches terminate; Type C patches require the wheel ruts to be tapered over a minimum length of twenty feet to re‑establish the regular cross‑slope. Connections to special structures such as railway crossings employ mortar‑filled rail webs with expansion fillers ranging from one‑quarter to three‑quarters inch, and all expansion joint fillers must be approved by a listed manufacturer and may be made of cork, sponge rubber, fiber‑filled material or closed‑cell polypropylene foam. A continuous base drain is provided beneath the pavement, and shoulders that match the mainline material supply lateral support for edge loads; full‑depth PCC shoulders with dowelled transverse joints are required on high‑traffic urban interstates, while rural routes with average daily traffic under 10 000 vehicles may use undowelled JPCP shoulder joints. For roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements control joints are spaced 15 ft to 20 ft for slabs thinner than eight inches and at a spacing equal to 2.5 times the slab thickness in feet for thicker slabs, with shoulder joints aligned with adjacent mainline joints; RCC does not require forms, steel reinforcing or dowel bars. Edge forms are finished with a quarter‑inch radius edging tool to provide a smooth transition at the pavement perimeter. [1][2]

In rigid‑pavement design every contact between concrete slabs, the supporting base, adjacent structures, shoulders and other features is treated as a controlled interface that must provide load transfer, drainage and structural continuity. Load‑transfer units such as dowel bars are staked in their final positions before concrete placement, then embedded with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders that fully bond the bar to the slab and are coated for durability. Transverse joints receive dowel‑bar retrofits at mid‑panel cracks and at each joint, while longitudinal joints are cross‑stitched with epoxy‑coated tie bars to achieve near‑100 % load transfer. Joint spacing is governed by pavement type: reinforced concrete sections use 30‑ft perpendicular joints, plain concrete sections 15‑ft spacing, and for roller‑compacted concrete the control joint spacing ranges from 15 ft to 20 ft for slabs less than 8 in thick and equals 2.5 times slab thickness for thicker sections; concrete shoulders may not exceed 20 ft between joints. When a lane is reconstructed for 500 ft or more, new joints are aligned with the dominant existing joints and an intermediate joint is added if the original spacing exceeds the allowable limit; if alignment cannot be achieved a minimum ten‑foot separation from any existing joint is maintained. Pavement‑shoulder interfaces are sealed after cleaning by constructing a 0.75 in × 0.75 in sealant reservoir and applying rubberized or asphalt joint sealant; shoulders on interstates are full‑depth Portland cement concrete, while Type 2 concrete shoulders serve arterials, collectors and local roads, with joint spacing limited to 20 ft. Drainage at every interface is provided by a continuous base drain beneath the slab together with highly permeable granular base and sub‑base that are sloped for gravity flow, supplemented by edge drains, lateral underdrains and protected outlets along perimeters, low sides of superelevations and cut‑to‑fill transitions to prevent water entrapment. The subgrade is prepared by excavating to the required depth, forming a ten‑inch deep foundation slab, placing a six‑inch compacted aggregate bed that extends at least six inches beyond each side of the slab, and installing dummy transverse joints every five feet that are approximately one‑eighth inch wide and at least one inch deep to control cracking. Expansion‑joint filler is placed at all full‑depth joints, with a minimum thickness of three‑quarters of an inch for general joints, half‑inch for manholes and valve boxes, and three‑quarters of an inch against bridges, culverts or other rigid structures; railway track interfaces receive filler ranging from one‑quarter to three‑quarters of an inch after the rail web and flange are packed with mortar. When a concrete patch is installed, a rigid polystyrene board or premolded expansion‑joint material equal in width to the existing joint isolates the new concrete from adjacent slabs, providing space for thermal movement and preventing point‑loading failures; longitudinal tie bars or bolts are required only for patches longer than 65 ft. Overlay design treats the interface as either bonded or unbonded: a bonded Portland cement concrete overlay is placed directly on a sound slab after repairing all significant cracking, while an unbonded overlay inserts at least one inch of fine‑graded asphalt sheet or equivalent geotextile to separate the layers and allows joint spacing to follow new‑pavement criteria. For unbonded overlays transverse joints are sawed and sealed over the underlying concrete joints, dowel‑bar retrofits or other restoration techniques are applied as needed, and drainage of any interlayer is achieved by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain that extends through reconstructed shoulders and ties into the mainline. The load‑transfer coefficient J used in design is selected based on the effective modulus of subgrade reaction k, lateral support conditions, traffic levels and subgrade strength; lower J values are chosen when deflection testing indicates good load transfer, while higher values are applied for poorer conditions, with adjustments for lower k or higher truck percentages. When widening the pavement, full‑lane additions of ten feet or more are designed to the same thickness as the original slab and extend into the shoulder by two feet if average daily traffic exceeds 5 000 vehicles or truck‑adjusted volume exceeds 500; narrower widenings extend one foot into the shoulder. All joint cleaning, sealing and rehabilitation are performed after diamond grinding to obtain the proper sealant‑reservoir shape factor, and slab stabilization fills voids at the slab/subbase interface to reduce water infiltration that can cause pumping and loss of support. Drainage, environment, and accessibility

In rigid‑pavement design drainage is treated as a primary performance factor and is integrated throughout planning, subgrade preparation, construction, and maintenance [1]. The existing subgrade must be stable, well‑compacted, and its drainage system evaluated; deficiencies are corrected by installing continuous pavement‑base drains, edge drains, lateral underdrains at cut‑to‑fill transitions, and additional subgrade drains where subsurface water problems exist [1]. A high‑permeability granular material is placed on a properly sloped subgrade to promote rapid removal of surface runoff, and any permeable base layer must have a positive outlet; when a dense asphalt or treated permeable base is used together with an underdrain, the design adopts a drainage coefficient Cd of 1.1, whereas unbound aggregate alone uses Cd = 1.0 [1]. Moisture control also influences subgrade reaction by assigning seasonal elastic moduli to the subbase—15 psi·10³ for spring‑thaw periods and other values for summer/fall and frozen winter—and applying a loss‑of‑support factor that limits the ratio of subbase modulus to native soil modulus to four [1]. Prior to concrete placement the base course is required to be uniformly moist without standing water; this is achieved by sprinkling the base while avoiding puddles and, when necessary, cooling treated permeable bases so their surface temperature stays below 115 °F [2][1]. Concrete may not be placed if the ambient evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·h unless windbreaks or fog‑misting systems are used, and any misting equipment must atomize droplets without depositing water on the slab [2]. Temperature limits are imposed both on the environment and on the concrete itself—placement is halted when air temperature falls below 40 °F (45 °F for accelerated‑strength mixes), concrete temperature must remain between 50 °F and 90 °F, and plastic concrete above 90 °F triggers cooling measures such as chilled water or aggregates [2]. During curing high‑low thermometers are installed to monitor slab temperature; if forecasts predict air temperatures below 50 °F the cure period is extended, readings between 40–35 °F add an extra day, and any reading below 35 °F renders the slab defective [2]. The pavement surface and its edges are protected to maintain a minimum temperature of 40 °F until sufficient strength is achieved for traffic opening, and imminent rain requires immediate cessation of paving, side shielding, and covering of the fresh surface with protective material [2]. Curing must begin within 30 minutes after bleed water dissipates using approved curing compounds or covers that remain in place to prevent moisture loss from joints, thereby supporting the drainage strategy [2][1]. All longitudinal, transverse, and shoulder joints are cleaned and sealed with rubberized joint‑sealing material, and sealant reservoirs of 0.75 in × 0.75 in are constructed at pavement‑shoulder joints to prevent infiltration of water, chlorides, and incompressible materials; when particles in a deteriorated joint impede gravity drainage, sealing combined with slab stabilization eliminates trapped water [1]. Slab stabilization fills voids at the slab/subbase interface using cement‑based grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane, and at least 25 % of transverse joints and all patch joints are stabilized when no preliminary testing is available, limiting moisture‑induced pumping, frost‑heave joint tenting, and related climate distress [1]. For overlays water beneath the concrete interlayer is managed by daylighting the interlayer or installing a vertical subdrain that extends through reconstructed shoulders and ties into the mainline drainage system, ensuring continuous flow [1]. By combining comprehensive surface and subsurface drainage, moisture‑controlled base preparation, temperature‑regulated placement and curing, joint sealing, and slab stabilization, rigid pavements are designed to resist freeze‑thaw cycles, saturated subgrade conditions, and other environmental stresses, thereby extending service life [1][2].

Rigid‑pavement projects must incorporate a comprehensive set of accessibility, safety, geometric and user performance criteria [1][2]. Drainage is treated as a primary safety element, requiring a continuous base drain together with subsurface water removal systems such as daylighted interlayers or vertical subdrains, and guide‑rail and other drainage structures are adjusted or replaced as needed to protect both the pavement and its users [1]. Structural integrity is maintained by correcting sunken areas through slabjacking to restore original grade and by providing load transfer across working transverse cracks with dowel‑bar retrofits that employ coated dowels and proper sealing [1]. Joint spacing follows pavement‑type specifications: jointed reinforced concrete slabs are limited to 30 ft, jointed plain concrete to 15 ft, concrete shoulders may not exceed 20 ft, and roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) control joints are spaced 15–20 ft for sections thinner than eight inches or at a distance equal to thickness multiplied by 2.5 for thicker sections; additional transverse dummy joints may be placed at five‑foot intervals as a preservation measure [1][2]. Joint orientation is required to be perpendicular, while longitudinal joints must run parallel to the final lane markings and lie within twelve inches of those markings without intersecting a travel lane, limiting edge slippage to one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and one‑quarter inch where a lane meets a shoulder [2]. When widening or adding lanes, the pavement structure extends into the shoulder by two feet when average daily traffic exceeds 5,000 vehicles (or truck‑adjusted volume exceeds 500) and by one foot otherwise; full out‑to‑out paving is strongly recommended at high‑turning locations such as driveway entries, exits and intersections [1]. Shoulder design follows functional‑class criteria: full‑depth Portland cement concrete shoulders are mandatory on interstates and other limited‑access freeways, while Type 2 concrete shoulders serve arterials, collectors and local roads, with joint spacing not exceeding 20 ft; when full‑depth concrete shoulders are used, all material layers from subgrade through base, intermediate and surface must match those of the mainline pavement to reduce edge stresses, differential heave and settlement [1]. Urban interstates require tied concrete shoulders, whereas rural interstates without anticipated incident‑management use may employ tied shoulders with undowelled joints using jointed plain concrete pavement; for corridors with two‑way ADT below 10,000 vehicles, widened slabs may be paired with asphalt or RCC shoulders [1]. Surface macrotexture and skid resistance are addressed through a variety of treatments: longitudinal grooves about one‑eighth inch wide (± 1/32 inch), depth ranging from one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths inch and spaced half to three‑quarters inch centre‑to‑centre, together with comparable transverse grooves on shoulders and sidewalks, provide the required macrotexture, while diamond grinding, high‑friction surfacing, micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses or thin asphalt overlays may be applied to improve skid resistance and reduce hydroplaning risk; a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 in is required on curves tighter than fifty‑foot radius [2][1]. Bicycle traffic is accommodated by providing smooth‑riding shoulder types such as 1‑F, 1‑S or 1‑SP, and ride quality enhancements are further supported by diamond grinding when the pavement is structurally sound and wheel ruts are less than 0.5 in deep [1]. Accessibility features include curb‑ramp treatment with a slip‑resistant texture created by a coarse stiff‑toothed broom applied perpendicular to the slope, rounded edges rather than sharp changes of grade, and an embedded detectable warning surface placed before the concrete sets; proper joint sealing, curing within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates, and removal of forms after twelve hours further ensure durability and safe use [2]. Construction safety requires protective equipment for crews handling hot asphalt, adherence to temporary traffic‑control guidelines, and keeping the pavement closed to traffic until stabilizing materials have set—approximately twelve hours for cement grout or at least thirty minutes for hot asphalt; accelerated concrete mixes may achieve the required compressive strength of 1,000 psi within four to seven hours, allowing earlier reopening where appropriate [1][2]. During construction, excavated patch areas must not remain open longer than forty‑eight hours, and after saw‑cutting an existing slab traffic may use the patched area for at most seventy‑two hours; saw cuts opened to traffic must be full depth, no wider than one‑half inch, with longitudinal joint cuts limited to one‑quarter inch wide and one inch deep placed not more than one foot from an existing joint, and diamond‑tipped saws with water cooling are required to prevent slurry in the travel lane [2]. Expansion joints around manholes, inlets, valve boxes, bridges, culverts or other rigid structures must use premolded fillers at least one‑half inch thick (three‑quarters inch for larger structures), while transverse joint material must extend the full width of the traffic lane, ramp or taper and be at least one inch thick; longitudinal filler pieces shall be no shorter than eight feet and provided with a smooth top surface pierced for dowel bars to assure a snug fit without loss of thickness [2]. Concrete patching is limited to ten percent of the total pavement area unless a cost‑effective justification demonstrates a larger need, ensuring that preservation projects remain focused on maintaining existing performance levels [1].

Verification and expected performance

The completed rigid pavement is verified through a systematic inspection that incorporates the lot‑acceptance criteria of Publication 408 (Sections 506 and 530) together with a comprehensive quality‑control program that requires submission of a detailed paving‑operation QC plan at least two weeks before concrete placement, allowing the owner’s representative to review and approve procedures for aggregate gradation control, form alignment, grade and depth control, temperature monitoring, curing, joint sawing and sealing, and final joint wipe testing [1][2]. Compliance with construction methods, materials, mix‑plant configuration and slip‑form paver clearances is confirmed against the specifications in Publication 408 Section 101.03, while any deviation from these prescribed practices is recorded as non‑compliant [1][2]. The design traffic capacity is checked by applying the average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 to the ESAL calculations to ensure that the pavement can accommodate the anticipated loads [1]. Thickness and uniformity are verified by providing cross‑sections every 50 feet, modifying core‑sampling procedures where slab depth varies, and confirming through core drilling or nondestructive testing that the as‑built thickness meets the minimum required for the selected Superpave mix—no more than 4½ inches when a 9.5 mm wearing course is used or 5 inches with a 12.5 mm wearing course—and any measurement short of one‑quarter inch is corrected, while a deficiency greater than one‑half inch renders the slab defective [1][2]. Edge slump is measured with a 12‑foot straightedge before loss of plasticity and again after hardening, with acceptable limits of one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and one‑quarter inch between lane (or ramp) and shoulder [2]. Flatness of the finished slab is verified using the same straightedge, requiring no more than three‑sixteenths inch deviation when measured parallel or perpendicular to the centreline; any high or low spots exceeding that amount are ground smooth [2]. Surface texture is evaluated by an ASTM E965 mean‑texture‑depth test, which must show at least 0.040 inches of macro‑texture for low‑radius traffic [2]. Tie‑bars are pull‑out tested after the concrete reaches a compressive strength of 3,500 psi, and the average resistance divided by bar spacing must meet the values in Table B; dowel‑bar anchorage is sampled on the first day of dowel work with three random cores, accepting only those where peripheral voids are less than 0.50 inch [2]. Concrete cylinders from each batch are cured and tested for compressive strength, with minimum requirements of 1,000 psi for normal mixes and at least 1,200 psi for accelerated mixes, and traffic is not permitted until these strengths are achieved as determined by the representative [2]. Drainage of any interlayer is inspected to confirm that it has been daylighted or that a vertical subdrain has been installed, extending through reconstructed shoulders to maintain structural continuity [1]. Constructability items are examined in detail, including verification of surface and subsurface water removal, patching of obvious failures according to current specifications, correction of faulted or sunken sections by slabjacking per Publication 408 Section 681, stabilization of voids beneath the base, installation of dowel bars where transverse cracks occur, cross‑stitching of longitudinal cracks or joints, inspection of joint condition and sealing, cleaning and sealing of cracks, evaluation of shoulder support and surface friction characteristics, and verification that any concrete full‑depth patching and joint rehabilitation are limited to ten percent of the pavement area unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided [1]. Joint repairs are verified with Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, and any repaired areas must meet the performance criteria established for ride quality; the Evaluation of Concrete Pavement Ride Quality per Section 507 is performed, and diamond grinding or leveling courses are confirmed to achieve the intended smooth ride and cross‑slope requirements [1]. When all of these compliance, constructability, performance, and consistency checks are satisfied, the pavement is deemed compliant with design specifications, constructability standards, and overall project requirements [1][2].

The rigid pavement system is expected to provide a service life on the order of two decades when designed according to departmental assumptions for material quality, environmental exposure and traffic loading [1]. This longevity is attributed to the high compressive strength that roller‑compacted concrete can achieve—generally between four thousand and ten thousand pounds per square inch—and to slab thicknesses selected in accordance with established concrete pavement design procedures [1]. When tied plain‑cement concrete shoulders are incorporated, the increased structural stiffness further limits slab deflection, postponing the first maintenance cycle and contributing to a service life that exceeds that of conventional concrete pavements [1]. Distress resistance is achieved through the high compressive strength reducing susceptibility to cracking and the tied shoulder configuration minimizing differential heaving, settlement and horizontal separation at the pavement‑shoulder interface, thereby lowering overall maintenance requirements and improving motorist safety [1]. Edge slumping must be limited to no more than one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes, measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge before the concrete loses plasticity, to ensure that excessive edge deformation does not compromise structural integrity [2]. The finished slab surface is required to deviate no more than three‑sixteenths inch when checked with the same straightedge in both longitudinal and transverse directions, providing a tight surface flatness criterion for performance verification [2]. Ride quality is monitored through different metrics; for roller‑compacted concrete projects that rely on payment incentives based on the International Roughness Index, use of RCC is discouraged because its rougher texture can produce variable IRI results, whereas Long‑Life Concrete Pavement mandates a ride‑quality incentive evaluated according to Section 507 of the specifications [1]. In addition, the pavement must achieve a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 in, verified by ASTM E965 testing at four locations per lane, to provide adequate macrotexture for driver comfort and safety [2]. Tie‑bar pull‑out resistance is confirmed by center‑pull hydraulic jack tests that either reach twelve thousand pounds or produce a slippage of one‑thirty‑second inch after the concrete attains a compressive strength of three‑five hundred psi, ensuring sufficient structural continuity across joints [2]. The pavement may be opened to traffic only after cylinder compressive strength reaches the specified minimum—1 200 psi for accelerated mixes or the value required by design tables for normal mixes—as determined by standard cylinder testing or maturity methods, providing a quantitative threshold for early‑age performance [2]. Slab thickness must be within a quarter‑inch of the designed value, as measured by core drilling or approved non‑destructive techniques, to verify that the structural capacity meets design expectations [2]. Performance verification is carried out by recording the elapsed time before any maintenance action is required, documenting observed heave, settlement or separation at the pavement‑shoulder junction, tracking safety‑related outcomes, and applying the appropriate roughness metrics—IRI for conventional RCC assessments or the Section 507 evaluation for Long‑Life Concrete Pavement—as well as the edge slump, surface flatness, texture depth and tie‑bar pull‑out results [1][2].
