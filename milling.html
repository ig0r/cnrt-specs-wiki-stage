Milling

Part of: Asphalt Overlay is-subtopic-of
Milling is a topic under Asphalt Overlay.
millingmillingsmilledmilled-surfacemill-wearing-course
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Milling is a surface‑preparation technique that removes a controlled thin layer of existing asphalt or concrete to correct the transverse and longitudinal profile of a pavement, providing a level and stable platform for an overlay or interlayer [1]. It is employed only when the underlying base is stable, allowing removal of typically 1½–2 inches (or deeper if required) to eliminate bumps, corrugations, wheel ruts, gouges, ridges, and other irregularities so that a smooth surface not exceeding 1/8‑inch in 12 feet and the proper cross‑slope are achieved [2]. By restoring the designed elevation and cross‑slope, milling repairs existing distress and functions as a preservation treatment that retards further deterioration while ensuring adequate bond of subsequent pavement layers [1].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Milling is employed when the existing asphalt concrete surface requires correction of its profile so that a level and stable platform can be provided for an overlay; it addresses the need to correct the existing surface before construction of the new wearing course (section 2.1 GENERAL) [1]. As an alternative method for improving the transverse or longitudinal profile, milling essentially corrects surface unevenness and restores a proper cross‑section, but may be used only when the underlying pavement base is in stable condition so that removal of material will not compromise pavement performance (section 5.12 OVERLAYS) [1]. In the surface‑preparation step before placing an interlayer or overlay, milling is employed to correct rutting in the pavement surface, thereby restoring the intended profile and ensuring proper adhesion of subsequent layers (section 5.12 > a. Surface Preparation) [1]. When performed prior to a Hot‑In‑Place Recycled overlay, milling is intended to treat deeper surface cracks that exceed the shallow distress limits for HIR; by removing a thin layer it also helps retain vertical clearance for overhead structures, curbs, gutters and guide rails, preserving the existing vertical profile (section 5.19 C.) [1]. The manual notes that milling used together with an overlay on flexible pavements is intended to eliminate rutting and other identifiable material problems; the milling depth may be increased beyond the usual 1 ½‑inch or 2‑inch limits when needed to correct those distresses (section 12.3 DESIGN GUIDELINES) [1]. The Pennsylvania specifications state that milling is applied when the existing pavement surface exhibits profile defects such as bumps, corrugations, wheel ruts, gouges, grooves, ridges, or other irregularities that would create an unsafe ride or impair placement of a new wearing or binder course; by removing these imperfections the process re‑establishes the designed elevation and cross‑slope and produces a surface that meets the tolerance of 1/8 inch in 12 feet and complies with the surface‑tolerance requirements of Section 413.3(l) for asphalt and Section 501.3(o) for cement concrete (sections 491, 492) [2]. In Section 404—Evaluation of Asphalt Pavement Ride Quality and Payment of Incentive, milling is required when any individual bump exceeds 3/16 inch measured with a 12‑foot straightedge or when the surface adjacent to another lot contains a ridge or valley larger than 1/8 inch, because such irregularities render the lot defective under the ride‑quality criteria; in those cases the entire surface course is milled and at least the minimum specified layer depth is replaced to restore uniform texture, proper cross‑slope, and compliance with smoothness tolerances (section 404) [2]. Section 545—Bonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfaced Pavements requires milling (scarifying) when the existing pavement elevation must be lowered, removing the top layer to the indicated depth; after scarifying any deteriorated concrete or asphalt that extends below the scarified level, as well as loose reinforcement and full‑depth asphalt patches, are removed so that surface defects are corrected prior to placement of a bonded concrete overlay (section 545) [2]. Section 525—Concrete Pavement Partial‑Depth Repair specifies that milling removes all unsound concrete along the affected joint or crack to a minimum depth of 2 inches and a maximum depth of one‑half of the slab thickness, creating a clean, roughened surface that restores the pavement profile for the repair (section 525) [2]. For Sections 491, 591, 540, and 541, milling eliminates unsound or deteriorated pavement material—whether asphalt that would otherwise require replacement or cement‑concrete that has become delaminated or separated along joints or cracks—so that a proper bond can be achieved for subsequent overlays such as Class AA cement concrete, thin bonded concrete, or other overlay systems (sections 491, 591, 540, 541) [2].

In the Pavement Policy Manual, milling is employed when a scratch‑and‑leveling course or milling is required to correct the existing pavement surface so that it becomes level and stable enough for an overlay; by removing the deficient top layer the treatment repairs damage and restores the functional platform needed for subsequent construction (section 2.1) [1]. The manual further requires milling in the surface‑preparation step of an overlay to remove a rutted layer, thereby correcting distress before the interlayer is placed; this cutting away of deformed material directly repairs damage and restores the functional profile required for proper bonding (section 5.12) [1]. For hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt, milling performed prior to the HIR process addresses deeper cracks and helps retain vertical clearance for overhead structures, curbs, gutters, and guide rails, again showing that the objective is to repair damage and preserve the functional geometry of the pavement (section 5.19) [1]. The preservation design guidelines also allow milling depths of 1 ½ inches or 2 inches to be exceeded so that the removed layer can correct a rutting problem or other identifiable material problems, effectively restoring the pavement’s functional profile (section 12.3) [1]. Finally, the manual characterizes milling as a preservation treatment intended to retard future deterioration and thereby slow the progression of distress while maintaining or improving the functional condition of the roadway (section 13.7) [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that profile‑milling eliminates bumps, corrugations and wheel ruts and re‑establishes the specified elevation or cross‑slope to provide a smooth, safe riding surface, thereby restoring functional performance (section 492) [2]. Section 491 requires the milled surface to be free of gouges, grooves and ridges and to meet tolerance requirements, which restores the functional condition before an overlay is placed [2]. Similarly, Section 591 for cement‑concrete pavements mandates a defect‑free surface that meets profile tolerances and clears all loose material and dust prior to rehabilitation work, restoring functionality [2]. Section 541 clarifies that profile‑milling removes unsound pavement material so that a new cement‑concrete overlay can be placed, indicating that milling repairs damage by eliminating deteriorated layers [2]. Section 404 describes milling as a corrective action that removes the surface course and replaces at least the minimum specified layer depth, correcting the surface profile, providing uniform texture and positive cross‑slope for drainage, thus restoring functionality that has exceeded ride‑quality tolerances [2]. Finally, Section 545 specifies that scarifying (milling) is used to lower pavement elevation and eliminate loose reinforcement or deteriorated concrete or asphalt below the scarified surface, directly addressing existing damage rather than merely slowing deterioration and thereby restoring the functional profile [2]. Together, these sources indicate that the chief objective of milling is to repair damaged pavement and restore its functional condition, with an additional preservation intent to slow further deterioration.

Distress characterization and causes

The Pavement Policy Manual explains that milling is prescribed when distress originates from inadequate drainage that permits surface and subsurface water infiltration and from base‑layer failures that leave insufficient structural capacity or weak support, because these conditions prevent a level, stable platform for an overlay. [1] Water infiltration can cause loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, localized failures and crack propagation, so correcting the profile without compromising the underlying base helps mitigate moisture‑induced weakening, inadequate support and surface water accumulation that would otherwise accelerate deterioration. [1] The same manual notes that surface‑level cracking, rutting, raveling or minor corrugation are driven by load‑induced damage and base deficiencies that reflect insufficient structural capacity or weak support, and that inadequate drainage can accelerate the surface failure. [1] Environmental exposure such as high ambient temperatures or overspray of tack causing bleed‑through is identified as a cause of debonding and loss of waterproofing, which can further weaken the pavement structure. [1] The manual also requires that at least a minimum of 1 ½ inches of existing asphalt be left in place when milling a portion of an asphalt layer over a cement concrete or brick base to retain structural integrity and adequate bonding; removal of all asphalt is required only when debonding is detected. [1] Premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements may be caused by inadequate subbase material, and aggregate‑related issues such as Vanport Limestone concerns or Alkali Silica Reaction can affect pavement integrity and skid resistance, requiring field inspection before a milling‑preservation treatment is applied. [1] The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that any standing water left on the milled asphalt must be removed because it can infiltrate the substrate and accelerate moisture‑related distress; therefore no standing water should remain on the surface after the milling operation and measures acceptable to the Department must be provided to drain or remove it. [2] The specifications further identify exposed or loose reinforcement as an indication of weakened support that would increase distress under traffic loads, requiring that such reinforcement be cut off and removed. [2] Cracks wider than 3/4 inches are considered weak points; they must be filled with non‑shrink grout to eliminate the weak support before overlaying. [2] After uniformly scarifying the old surface to the indicated depth, all deteriorated concrete or asphalt materials that extend below the level of the scarification must be removed and all deleterious materials cleared before overlaying, addressing material aging and loss of structural integrity. [2] The cleaned and prepared surface must also be protected from oil or grease drippings because such contaminants degrade bond strength and can lead to premature failure. [2] Final cleaning of the surface in front of the paver shall occur no more than 30 minutes prior to placement, and concrete is placed only when its temperature is between 65 °F and 90 °F to prevent moisture‑related distress and ensure adequate bond strength and service life. [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–133), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 458–469)

Applicability and treatment selection

Milling may be used as an alternative to heater planing for correcting transverse or longitudinal profile when the pavement base is in stable condition and removal of material would not affect performance, or when curb reveal, shoulders, guide rail, or drainage adjustments are major considerations. For asphalt pavements, profile milling is appropriate when surface irregularities such as bumps, corrugations, or wheel ruts must be removed to re‑establish the designed elevation and cross‑slope, and it may be performed in small or confined areas and around utility facilities provided that supplemental equipment approved by the Representative is used and utilities are adjusted beforehand. When milling over a cement concrete or brick base, the preferred practice is to leave at least 1 ½ inches of existing asphalt in place so that structural integrity and bonding are retained; coring during design must verify an adequate bond, and if debonding is found the entire asphalt overlay may need removal. Traffic shall not be allowed on any milled surface for more than 6 calendar days, and the first overlay course must be placed within 7 calendar days of the start of milling, with weather‑related stoppages excluded. For rigid concrete pavements that will receive a bonded concrete overlay, scarifying (milling) is appropriate when the elevation must be lowered as part of the overlay, and it should be done only under weather conditions that permit concrete placement temperatures between 65 °F and 90 °F while ensuring the prepared surface remains below 120 °F; fog‑misting or protective covers may be used if needed. Site preparation for milling over rigid concrete must include removal of loose concrete, rusted reinforcement, full‑depth asphalt patches, and standing water, and the cleaned surface must be protected from oil, grease, and other contaminants until the overlay is placed; final cleaning of the scarified surface shall occur no more than 30 minutes before overlay placement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 349–350), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 466–469)

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, milling may be employed on overlay projects only when the pavement base is stable and removal of material will not compromise performance, or when major adjustments such as curb reveal, shoulder, guide‑rail, or drainage modifications are required; if the base is unstable, removal would affect performance, and no such structural adjustments are needed, milling is deemed unsuitable and an alternative treatment such as an overlay with a scratch or leveling course should be selected. The same manual further states that milling shall not be used when the pavement exhibits potholes, cracks defined in Section 469.3(d), or any local distresses originating from structural or subgrade failures, because those conditions require repair rather than surface removal. When addressing rutting, milling may be employed, yet a leveling course may be placed before an interlayer if milling is not chosen. In the design guidelines, milling is considered inappropriate when the depth required for removal exceeds the preservation limits; specifically, for flexible pavements any overlay project that would require more than 2 inches of mill‑and‑overlay is ineligible for pavement preservation and deeper corrections must be addressed with alternative rehabilitation methods. The guidelines also indicate that once advanced distresses have progressed beyond the early maintenance stage, treatments such as micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, cold in‑place recycling, or concrete‑specific restoration techniques are prescribed instead of milling. Moreover, when patching requirements exceed ten percent of the pavement area, or condition data reveal severe distress that surpasses preservation thresholds, a more extensive rehabilitation—such as larger base repairs, full overlays, or reconstruction—should be selected rather than milling. Specific problems identified in the Pavement Project Criteria, including premature mid‑slab cracking due to inadequate subbase, aggregate‑related skid‑resistance concerns with Vanport Limestone, or ramp pavements not captured by the STAMPP survey, also render milling unsuitable and call for alternative maintenance or rehabilitation approaches. In the specifications for bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavement, milling the asphalt surface is required only where a milling machine can physically reach; if any portion of the pavement is inaccessible to the equipment, milling is unsuitable and saw‑cutting together with pneumatic‑hammer chipping may be employed to remove those areas before placing the concrete overlay. For bonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements, scarifying (milling) is prescribed when lowering the pavement elevation or removing deteriorated concrete, but when only minimal minor surface defects are present, milling is not required and a thin overlay should be used instead of scarification. [1][2]

Condition assessment and timing

Determination begins with an annual Automated Pavement Condition Survey (videologging) that records distresses such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and faulting; the resulting condition data are entered into the Pavement Condition/Treatment Matrices which flag preservation treatments—including milling—when observed distresses reach the matrix thresholds. When structural capacity is in question, a Falling Weight Deflectometer test may be performed to supply pavement and subgrade characteristics that help confirm whether a preservation action such as milling is appropriate rather than more extensive rehabilitation. If skid‑resistance problems are suspected, skid friction testing by BOMO can also trigger corrective actions that may include milling as part of an asphalt resurfacing strategy. A visual inspection of the transverse and longitudinal surface profile is then made to identify wheel ruts, depressions or other irregularities, and a further visual assessment must identify rutting, cracking, raveling, corrugation or shoving. For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) candidate pavements the visual criteria require rutting severity less than 1 ½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous crack depth less than 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding ≤ ½ inch, and minor corrugation, shoving or slippage depth less than 1 ½ inches. The existing asphalt pavement thickness must be at least 1 ⅕ inches more than the proposed milling depth for HIR candidate pavements, while a separate specification requires asphalt pavement thickness to be at least 1 ½ inches more than the milling depth. The pavement base condition is assessed to confirm stability and to verify that removal of material will not compromise performance; adjustments to curb reveal, shoulders, guide rail or drainage structures are evaluated as part of the decision. Coring during the design phase must be performed to test whether an adequate bond exists between any underlying concrete or brick base and the existing asphalt layer, because debonding would dictate full‑depth removal; cores taken from the existing surface are inspected to confirm the depth and integrity of the pavement layers, thereby establishing the required milling depth. For concrete pavements subject to partial‑depth repair, the first inspection is to sound the concrete surface in the presence of the Representative using a steel bar, drag chains or a hammer to locate delamination. Once delamination is identified, rectangular repair limits are laid out extending approximately 3 inches beyond the edges of the delaminated area; these limits define the zones that may be milled, and for Type 1 and Type 2 partial‑depth repairs the milling machine is applied along the joint or crack. For asphalt pavements evaluated under Section 404, the Representative must first measure the pavement’s Mean Roughness Index (MRI) and compare it to the limits in Table A of Section 404.4; any lot whose MRI exceeds that limit becomes a candidate for corrective action. A 12‑foot straightedge test is then used to locate individual bumps, and if an irregularity exceeds 3/16 inch the bump must be ground. The transverse profile is also measured, and any ridge or valley greater than 1/8 inch relative to adjacent pavement signals that removal and replacement by milling may be required to restore a uniform surface. Additional smoothness verification testing with an inertial lightweight profiler may be performed as specified for federally funded projects. [1][2]

[1] Milling may be used as an alternative method to improve the transverse or longitudinal profile only when the underlying pavement base is in a stable condition such that removal will not compromise performance, or when major adjustments to curb reveal, shoulders, guide rail, or drainage structures are required. [1] Milling is selected as a preservation treatment prior to extensive deterioration for sections that exhibit early‑stage distress such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling, or joint faulting. [1] Within concrete pavement partial‑depth repair, milling is employed when delamination—unsound concrete identified by sounding with a steel bar, drag chains, or a hammer—is present and a Type 1 or Type 2 partial‑depth repair is required; the machine mills along the joint or crack, removing all unsound material to at least a minimum depth of 2 inches and not exceeding half of the slab thickness. [2] For bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavement, milling is indicated when the existing surface includes unsound material that must be removed to achieve the proper profile for the overlay; the unsound pavement is removed as part of the profile‑milling operation before placing the new cement concrete. [2] For bonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements, milling (scarifying) is used when the existing pavement elevation must be lowered for grade correction or when deteriorated material must be removed; the surface is scarified to the indicated depth, any exposed loose reinforcement is cut off and removed, and all deteriorated concrete or underlying asphalt extending below the scarification level is taken away. Materials and repair details

Milling for pavement correction requires that the contractor retain at least fifteen percent of the estimated total milled material, while any remainder is retained by the department; if the estimate is less than two hundred tons the entire quantity must be provided to the contractor, and these quantities are recorded in the bid package using ECMS standard items and calculated with the RAP Retainage Calculation spreadsheet. When a Mill Wearing Course is specified in a ten‑year maintenance plan, the milling operation must be followed by an asphalt wearing‑course inlay that is either one‑point‑five inches or two point zero inches thick and a saw‑and‑seal treatment applied to all transverse joints, with seal coat or microsurface also placed on shoulders as required for the pavement type. The milling equipment for profile work on asphalt must be a self‑propelled machine capable of cutting a full lane up to twelve feet wide in one pass and equipped with a built‑in automatic grade control system that controls both longitudinal profile and transverse cross‑slope, with profiler cutters mounted on the carrier and adjustable for depth of cut and cross‑slope. The same type of machine must be designed and built for existing asphalt pavements and also be capable of milling concrete, possessing an automatic grade and slope control system. For cement‑concrete pavement milling a machine specifically designed and built for existing cement‑concrete surfaces is required. In cold‑in‑place recycling the milling machine must be self‑propelled, fitted with a down‑cutting drum, an attached screening/crushing plant and a pugmill mixer that includes a liquid‑additive system and spray bar capable of volumetrically controlling the additive based on milling depth, width and speed. If foamed asphalt is to be incorporated, separate computerized controls and injection bars for foamed asphalt and water, a test nozzle for field samples, and hot‑asphalt tankers equipped with temperature gauges are required. The milled material must be removed immediately; it may be retained by the contractor or delivered to a department‑designated stockpile, with delivery costs considered incidental. Milling depth must be verified at least once per three thousand square yards and any area that is short by one‑half inch or more must be corrected at no additional cost. Typical removal depths for flexible pavements are one‑point‑five inches or two inches, although greater depths may be employed when required to correct rutting or other material problems; before any milling the existing pavement must be cored and the cores inspected to confirm both the needed depth and the integrity of underlying layers. When milling a portion of an asphalt layer over a cement‑concrete or brick base, the entire asphalt layer may be removed but it is preferred to leave at least one‑point‑five inches of existing asphalt so that structural integrity and bond are retained, and coring must be performed during the design phase to verify an adequate bond; full‑depth removal is required if debonding is found. If the milled surface is not smooth, a leveling course may be required before placing an interlayer, and when the pavement has been rough‑milled a similar leveling layer may also be needed; clean blotting sand or broadcast asphalt pavement material is spread over any area where fabric pickup could occur, with excess sand removed prior to asphalt placement. For hot‑in‑place recycled projects that include a preceding milling step, the existing asphalt pavement must be at least one‑point‑five inches thicker than the planned milling depth, any paving fabric on site must be a minimum of one‑point‑five inches below the cut depth, all mastic patches larger than five square feet must be removed, and raised pavement markers or durable markings must be removed before HIR placement. In cold‑recycled base courses the milling step uses the existing asphalt as the material to be removed, typically involving milling to a specified depth, then adding and mixing emulsified asphalt, and finally placing the mixture to a specified grade before compacting; the milled layer thus serves as the restored base course that can later receive a Superpave overlay or an alternative cold‑mix surface treatment. All repair areas are required to be rectangular with minimum dimensions of fifteen inches in length by ten inches in width by two inches in depth, and unsound concrete must be removed to a minimum depth of two inches but not deeper than one half the slab thickness. The head of the milling machine must be a “V” head, flat head, or rounded head; corner squares are cut with a saw to at least two‑inch depth before chipping with a hammer weighing no more than thirty‑five pounds operated at an angle of thirty to sixty degrees. After profile milling the completed surface is required to be profiled to a smoothness of one‑eighth inch in twelve feet and a cross‑slope within one‑eighth inch in twelve feet. Vertical surfaces such as curbs, structures, gutters and sides that will contact the new asphalt are painted with a uniform coating of PennDOT‑specified emulsified asphalt (Material Class TACK) or an appropriate hot asphalt binder applied in two or more coats, and a tack coat is also placed on the existing surface unless otherwise indicated. Where wheel ruts or small depressions exist, a scratch course may be used to fill them and a leveling course may be provided to create a relatively uniform working platform for the subsequent binder or wearing layer. The milled surface is then shot‑blasted or water‑blasted to achieve a roughened condition, standing water is drained, and the pavement is cleaned of debris, oil and grease with a compressed‑air stream at a minimum pressure of one hundred pounds per square inch for no more than thirty minutes before overlay placement. Joint backing or an approved joint‑filling material is installed in all existing transverse joints to within plus‑or‑minus one‑quarter inch of the prepared surface. Cracks greater than three‑quarters inch are filled with non‑shrink grout or other Department‑approved material before final cleaning. For thin bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements Portland cement concrete containing fibers is placed, and the quantity is computed from the indicated depth and placement area; an additional quantity not to exceed two percent of the computed overlay quantity may be paid as compensation for material needed to replace unsound pavement removed during milling. Class AA cement concrete measured in cubic yards is used for bonded concrete overlays, with up to an extra two percent of that volume compensated when unsound pavement material is removed, provided a written request with delivery tickets is submitted. Traffic may not be allowed on any freshly milled surface for more than six calendar days, and the first overlaying asphalt course must be placed within seven calendar days of the start of milling; if the overlay is not placed the same day, appropriate warning signs are required at specified intervals. The asphalt surface temperature must be less than one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit prior to overlay placement. [1][2]

For milling to be effective the existing asphalt concrete surface must be stable—exhibiting no movement under a roller—and it should be properly leveled, sealed and patched so that a level, stable platform is available for any overlay [1]. Milling may be employed to correct pavement profile only when the underlying base is stable and removal of material will not compromise performance, or when major adjustments to curb reveal, shoulders, guide rail, or drainage are required [1]. When an asphalt layer overlies a cement concrete or brick base, it is preferred that at least 1 ½ inches of existing asphalt be retained so the material retains structural integrity and remains adequately bonded; coring during design must verify such bond, because debonding will require removal of the entire asphalt depth [1]. Existing pavement conditions such as rutting, potholes and cracks must be repaired, and any rutting must be corrected through milling or a leveling course before an interlayer is placed; the surface also must be free of dirt, water, oil and other foreign materials because contaminants will prevent interlayer adhesion and may cause delamination [1]. The pavement must be moisture‑free, interlayers shall not be installed during precipitation, and for placement of fiberglass mesh grid with self‑adhesive glue the pavement temperature shall be between 40 °F and 140 °F, while newly placed asphalt surfaces must cool below 140 °F before the grid is applied [1]. The condition of the existing pavement dictates how much tack coat or asphalt material must be applied and whether a leveling course is needed; a rough milled surface typically requires an additional leveling layer before the interlayer is placed [1]. Compatibility with the chosen interlayer shall follow the manufacturer’s recommendations for type and rate of tack coat, especially on grades steeper than 5 % where the mix should be proportioned to aid compaction and shear‑rut stability [1]. For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) treatment milling is performed before the HIR process to remove surface layers, deepen cracks and retain vertical clearance for overhead structures, curbs, gutters and guide rails; performance relies on an underlying asphalt thickness that must be at least 1 ½ inches more than the milling depth, and any paving fabric must lie a minimum of 1 ½ inches below the specified HIR cut depth [1]. Pavements that contain rubber or fiber materials in the top 2 inches, have large mastic patches, raised markers or other prohibited surface treatments are considered incompatible with the HIR milling process [1]. The suitability of a milling treatment on flexible pavements is governed primarily by the condition of the existing surface; rutting or other identifiable material problems dictate how deep the mill must go, and cores must be taken and inspected to verify depth and integrity before milling, with guideline limits of 1 ½ inches or 2 inches that may be exceeded when required to correct a rutting problem provided coring confirms the condition; overlay projects on existing flexible pavements that exceed 2 inches are not eligible for consideration as Pavement Preservation unless an exception applies [1]. To obtain the intended profile, any existing surface irregularities such as bumps, corrugations or wheel ruts must be removed because they directly affect the ability of milling to meet the specified profile [2]. The milled surface shall be produced free from grooves, ridges, gouges or other defects and shall conform in smoothness and durability to the adjacent pavement; sound pavement damaged during milling shall be patched with an emulsified asphalt tack coat and an asphalt mixture of the same type so that the repaired portion matches the surrounding material [2]. The equipment used shall be a machine designed and built for milling existing asphalt pavements, equipped with an automatic grade‑and‑slope control system and capable of milling concrete; the rate of milling must be controlled to avoid tearing of the mat, which would create chunky or non‑uniformly milled material [2]. For asphalt surfaces the finished surface shall meet the surface‑tolerance limits of Section 413.3(l) and for concrete surfaces it shall meet the tolerance limits of Section 501.3(o); in all cases the milled pavement must be kept free of loose material and dust until the first overlay is placed [2]. When milling a cold‑recycled asphalt base course, the depth shall be maintained constant in a single pass so that reclaimed asphalt pavement does not exceed 1 ¼ inch in thickness; the pug‑mix mixer and spray bar must meter liquid additive volumetrically based on milling depth, width and machine speed with a weight variation of no more than ± 2.0 %, and depth measurements shall be made at least once per 3,000 sq yd with any section deficient by ½ inch or more corrected [2]. For partial‑depth concrete repairs classified as Type 1 or Type 2 the milling head must follow the existing joint or crack and be a “V” head, flat head or rounded head; removal depth shall be at least 2 inches but shall not exceed one‑half of the slab thickness, providing sufficient roughness for bond, and reinforcement may be cut only in jointed reinforced concrete pavement while it shall not be cut or removed in continuously reinforced concrete pavement [2]. Prior to a bonded concrete overlay on an asphalt‑surfaced pavement all reinforcement, loose or deteriorated concrete or asphalt that extends below the scarification depth must be removed, steel plates in transverse joints must be taken out, and any oil, grease, standing water or other deleterious material must be cleaned from the surface; the surface shall then be roughened by shot‑blasting or water‑blasting and kept dry with a compressed‑air stream that is free of oil and moisture and provides at least 100 psi [2]. The pavement temperature must be below 120°F before concrete placement, and the milled surface must be completely free of loose debris [2]. Ride‑quality requirements dictate that any surface irregularity measuring more than 3⁄16 inch on a 12‑foot straightedge must be corrected, and milling must not create a ridge or valley exceeding 1⁄8 inch in transverse profile; after removal the surface shall be replaced with at least the minimum layer depth of the specified surface‑course material to achieve a uniform texture and a positive cross‑slope that permits drainage [2].

Treatment procedures

Before any milling the existing surface must be conditioned by removing loose material, foreign debris, excess joint sealer and crack filler; a broom may be used if needed [2]. All vertical surfaces that will contact new asphalt shall be painted with a uniform coat of emulsified asphalt classified as PennDOT Material Class TACK or with hot binder, and a tack coat shall be applied in accordance with Section 460 before any overlay [2]. For profile milling of asphalt pavement surfaces the contractor shall provide a self‑propelled milling machine capable of milling a full‑lane width up to 12 feet in one pass, equipped with automatic grade control and cutter adjustments for the required depth and cross‑slope [2]. For generic asphalt pavement milling the machine must be designed and built for existing asphalt, include an automatic grade‑and‑slope control system and also be capable of milling concrete [2]. When cement‑concrete pavements are to be milled a machine specifically designed and built for existing concrete surfaces is required [2]. For cold‑recycled asphalt base courses the mill shall be self‑propelled with a down‑cutting drum, and when foamed asphalt is used water injection shall be provided; equipment that meters liquids automatically shall maintain variation within ± 2.0 % by weight [2]. Cold‑recycled base milling shall achieve the specified depth in a single pass while maintaining constant depth, width, cross‑slope and grade; depth is measured at least once per 3,000 sq yd and recorded [2]. Immediately after each pass the milled material is removed; the surface must be kept free of all loose material and dust before any subsequent overlay [2]. The milling operation itself must produce a surface free of gouges, grooves, ridges and other irregularities; for asphalt profile milling the machine removes bumps, corrugations and wheel ruts in automated mode, establishing the specified elevation or cross‑slope with one or more passes and shall meet a smoothness tolerance of 1/8 inch in 12 feet and an edge match within the same magnitude [2]. For cold‑recycled base courses the treatment typically involves milling to a specified depth, adding and mixing emulsified asphalt, placing to a specified grade, and compacting [1][2]. The reclaimed material is mixed with emulsified or foamed asphalt in a pugmill mixer, placed with an asphalt paver, uniformly spread, shaped, graded and compacted to at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density; re‑compaction may be required after scarifying or moisture adjustment [2]. Curing for cold‑recycled base courses requires either at least two weeks or until the average moisture content falls below 2 % for emulsified asphalt, and until moisture falls below 2 % for foamed asphalt before any wearing or binder course is placed [2]. Traffic may not be permitted on any milled surface for more than six calendar days, and the first overlay must be placed within seven calendar days from the start of milling; rough‑road or bump signs shall be installed if the overlay is delayed [2]. After scarifying for a bonded concrete overlay the surface shall be shot‑blasted or water‑blasted to produce a roughened profile and to drain standing water, and all loose debris must be completely removed while the surface is protected from oil or grease [2]. A final cleaning with compressed air at a minimum pressure of 100 psi must be performed in front of the paver no more than thirty minutes before concrete placement, and the pavement temperature must be kept below 120°F at that time [2]. The milled material is either retained by the contractor and disposed of immediately or delivered to a designated Department stockpile as specified in the contract documents [2].

Milling operations require a self‑propelled milling machine that can cut a full lane up to twelve feet wide in a single pass and is equipped with an automatic grade control system together with longitudinal controls such as a string line, ski of at least thirty feet, mobile string line of at least thirty feet or a matching shoe (Section 492) [2]. Quality and workmanship

The milling inspection must verify that the existing asphalt layer retains at least a minimum of 1 ½ inches thickness so that structural integrity is preserved [1]. In addition, coring performed during the design phase shall be used to confirm that an adequate bond exists between the overlying asphalt and any underlying concrete or brick base, and those cores are inspected for both depth and material integrity before milling commences [1]. The inspection also must ensure that the pavement base is stable so that removal of material will not compromise performance, and any debonded areas identified by coring shall be completely removed and addressed with appropriate surface replacement after milling [1]. While typical milling depths are 1 ½ inches or 2 inches, the actual removal depth may be exceeded when necessary to correct rutting or other identifiable material problems, but the final depth must still be confirmed against the inspected core data [1]. The contractor shall provide a self‑propelled milling machine capable of cutting a full lane up to twelve feet wide in one pass and equipped with an automatic grade and slope control system that maintains longitudinal reference and cross‑slope [2]. After milling, the milled surface must be inspected for the absence of grooves, ridges, gouges or other irregularities and for complete removal of all milled material, loose debris, dust, oil, grease drippings, standing water, loose concrete, rust, or exposed reinforcement before any overlay is placed [2]. The surface must also conform to the tolerance requirements of Section 413.3(l) for asphalt and to the limits specified in Section 501.3(o) for cement concrete [2]. For cold‑recycled asphalt base milling, the down‑cutting drum shall produce reclaimed material that does not exceed one ¼ inch as required by AASHTO MP 38, the liquid additive system must meter liquids within ±2.0 % by weight, water injection must be controlled volumetrically with a calibrated meter, and the actual milling depth shall be measured at least once per 3,000 sq yd with any section short by one‑half inch or more corrected [2]. Performance and service life

Milling can be used to correct transverse or longitudinal profile only when the pavement base is stable and material removal will not affect performance; under those conditions it preserves pavement condition and functionality. When milling an asphalt layer over a cement concrete or brick base, the entire asphalt layer may be removed, but it is preferred that a minimum of 1 1/2 inches of existing asphalt remain so that structural integrity and bonding to the underlying base are retained, which supports durability and extends the expected service life. Milling is listed among preservation treatments, which are defined as a planned, cost‑effective strategy to retard future deterioration and maintain or improve the functional condition of a highway section. By removing a surface layer before extensive distress develops, milling helps keep the pavement in better condition, supports its functionality, contributes to durability, and extends the expected service life enough that reconstruction—required only when functional pavement life has been exceeded—is avoided. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Milling may fail when the underlying base is not stable because removal of material can compromise performance; therefore it should be applied only where the base condition is stable and the amount removed will not affect the pavement structure. When milling an asphalt overlay that rests on a concrete or brick base, at least 1 1/2 inches of existing asphalt must be retained to preserve structural integrity and adequate bond, and coring during design is required to verify the bond; any detected debonding obliges removal of the entire existing asphalt layer before proceeding. If the milled surface is rough, a leveling course may be needed before placing an interlayer, and insufficient smoothing can lead to adhesion problems that appear as slippage at higher temperatures when the tack coat or asphalt material is applied too heavily; similarly, an interlayer may fail to bond when the asphalt is too cold or the application rate is inadequate. The most common recurring distresses observed after milling include excessive rutting greater than 1½ in, deep transverse or miscellaneous cracking with depth greater than 1 in or crack width exceeding ½ in, raveling or weathering beyond a medium rating, and noticeable corrugation, shoving or slippage deeper than 1½ in. These distresses are often triggered when the milling depth leaves an insufficient residual asphalt layer; the remaining pavement must be at least 1½ in thicker than the milled surface, with composite pavements requiring 1–2 in and full‑depth pavements 2–3 in of asphalt below the cut. Additional factors that increase the likelihood of such distresses are the presence of rubber or fiber material in the top two inches, a weak base/subbase condition identified during project selection, and cutting less than 1½ in which creates thin layers. For asphalt pavement milling, failures are declared when grooves, ridges, gouges, bumps, corrugations, wheel ruts, spalling or dislodged pavement cause the profile to exceed the smoothness tolerance of 1/8 inch in 12 feet or the cross‑slope tolerance of 1/8 inch in 12 feet. Individual bumps greater than 3/16 inch measured with a 12‑foot straightedge and ridges or valleys greater than 1/8 inch on the transverse profile also render the lot defective. These distresses are generally linked to inadequate equipment control such as an improperly calibrated automatic grade control system, worn cutters, incorrect depth‑of‑cut settings, or an uncontrolled milling rate that can tear the asphalt mat and produce chunky, non‑uniform material, as well as insufficient removal of milled debris. Allowing traffic on the freshly milled surface for more than six calendar days or delaying placement of the first overlay beyond seven calendar days also contributes to failure. For cold‑recycled asphalt base milling, a failure is identified when the cut does not reach the required profile by one‑half inch or more, and such sections must be corrected or replaced. Damage caused by construction traffic, manifested as raveling, distortion or marring, arises from the machine’s inability to maintain constant cutting depth and width, lack of speed control, or inadequate protection of the newly milled surface. Concrete pavement milling exhibits similar failures—gouges, grooves, ridges, tearing of the concrete mat, or chunky non‑uniform material—and these are typically caused by an uncontrolled milling rate, inappropriate equipment, or failure to remove oversized pieces. Leaving loose material or dust on the slab before an overlay can also compromise subsequent treatment. In partial‑depth concrete repair, failures occur when removal exceeds the limits of a minimum depth of 2 inches and a maximum depth of one‑half of the slab thickness, or when reinforcement is damaged; excessive removal or dowel‑bar damage converts the repair into a full‑depth patch that must be rebuilt as such. These problems are driven by not adhering to depth control, using a chipping hammer weighing more than 35 pounds, operating it at an angle outside 30–60 degrees, or applying excessive force, and by milling along joints without protecting dowel bars. For bonded concrete overlay over asphalt‑surfaced pavement, bond failure can result from standing water, oil or grease contamination, or residual loose debris on the milled surface; specifications require that no standing water remain after milling, that cleaning be completed within 30 minutes with a compressed‑air stream free of contaminants at a minimum pressure of 100 psi, and that the asphalt substrate be cooled to less than 120°F before overlay placement. Failure to meet any of these thresholds may cause premature delamination or cracking. For bonded concrete overlay over concrete‑surfaced pavement, common failures include bond strength below an average of 175 psi or any individual test under 160 psi, honeycombing, rain‑induced damage, and excessive edge slump limited to 1/4 inch next to shoulders and 1/8 inch next to adjacent lanes. These failures are influenced by inadequate removal of loose material, oil or grease contamination, concrete temperature not between 65°F and 90°F during placement, surface temperature exceeding 120°F before overlay, insufficient curing protection when air temperature is forecasted at 45°F or lower, and improper final finishing. Monitoring and follow-up

After milling, the treated area must be inspected by measuring the milling depth; at least one measurement is required for each 3,000 square‑yard (or less) of work and the values are recorded to confirm that the specified depth was achieved, and any section deficient by 1/2‑inch or more must be corrected or replaced. If directed, smoothness and grade are also checked with a 12‑foot straight edge over three locations in each 3,000‑square‑yard area, and the average of the maximum space at the three locations may not exceed 1/2 inch; any excess requires correction, and density is verified for each 3,000‑square‑yard segment to ensure it meets the minimum target. The milled pavement must be inspected to verify that no standing water remains, that all loose debris and any oil or grease contaminants have been removed, and that the surface temperature is less than 120 °F before concrete placement; a final cleaning must be performed no more than 30 minutes prior to the overlay using only approved methods such as compressed air that provides at least 100 psi measured at the source. After milling and replacement, the Representative must retest the treated lot (and any excluded areas) to confirm that no ridge or valley exceeds 1/8 inch on the transverse profile and that no individual bump exceeds 3/16 inch when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge; the retesting follows the smoothness acceptance procedures, comparing the MRI to Table A, and may be followed by Department‑performed smoothness verification testing on at least 10 % of lane miles to ensure the verification MRI does not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches per mile. Bond‑strength testing must be performed in the presence of the Inspector seven days later using ASTM C1583; for each day’s work covering not more than 3,000 square yards, five random test locations are selected and three tests are run at each location, and the area is considered defective if the average of those three results is below 175 pounds per square inch while each individual result has a minimum strength of 160 pounds per square inch, which then requires additional testing to delineate the defect. The design‑phase coring must verify an adequate bond between the existing asphalt and a concrete or brick base; if debonding is discovered, the entire depth of the existing asphalt overlay must be removed and the area treated as a surface replacement item (section 5.12 OVERLAYS, b. Interlayer Installation Guidance) [1]. Even when milling over a cement‑concrete or brick base, at least 1½ inches of the original asphalt should remain in place; if this thickness cannot be preserved, structural integrity is compromised and additional maintenance must be provided (section 5.12 OVERLAYS, a. Any remaining potholes, cracks as defined in Section 469.3(d), or other local distresses tied to structural or subgrade failures must be repaired before an overlay can be placed (section 13.7 PAVEMENT PROJECT CRITERIA > E. If rutting has not been eliminated by the milling operation, a leveling course must be added; likewise, when the milled surface remains rough, a leveling course may be required before the interlayer can be placed (section 5.12 OVERLAYS, b. Interlayer Installation Guidance) [1]. The pavement must be completely free of dirt, water, oil and other foreign material because contamination that prevents proper interlayer adhesion can cause delamination; such conditions require extra cleaning or re‑milling (section 5.12 OVERLAYS, a. Surface Preparation) [1]. For fiberglass mesh grid placement with self‑adhesive glue, the surface temperature shall be between 40 °F and 140 °F; otherwise work should be postponed and the area retreated (section 5.12 OVERLAYS, a. Surface Preparation) [1]. When the distresses tracked by the STAMPP surveys—cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling or joint faulting—reach the levels that trigger the treatment matrices, additional work beyond preservation is indicated (section 5.12 OVERLAYS, a. Surface Preparation) [1]. If patching required after milling exceeds ten percent of the pavement area, the project is generally not cost‑effective for a preservation approach and should be moved to rehabilitation (section 5.12 OVERLAYS, a. Surface Preparation) [1]. Early appearance of mid‑slab cracking in jointed concrete pavements, which may stem from inadequate subbase material, as well as aggregate‑related problems such as Vanport Limestone or alkali silica reaction that can affect skid resistance, also signal the need for repair or more extensive treatment (section 5.12 OVERLAYS, a. Surface Preparation) [1].

If spalling or dislodged unsound pavement remains after milling, or if grooves, ridges, gouges, bumps, corrugations or wheel ruts are retained, the work is considered defective and must be corrected to match adjacent pavement (section 492.3 CONSTRUCTION— PROFILE MILLING OF ASPHALT PAVEMENT SURFACES) [2]. The milled surface must meet a smoothness of 1/8 inch in 12 ft and a cross‑slope within 1/8 inch in 12 ft; failure to achieve these tolerances requires additional repair (section 492.3 CONSTRUCTION—) [2]. Loose material that could become a hazard also necessitates correction (section 492.3 CONSTRUCTION—) [2]. Allowing traffic on the milled surface for more than six calendar days or failing to place the first overlay within seven calendar days signals non‑compliance and may require remedial action (section 492.3 CONSTRUCTION—) [2]. When post‑compaction measurements show a density below the required minimum of 96 % of the PTM 402 target and further rolling cannot raise it, the section must be scarified, moisture adjusted and re‑compacted (section 404.3 CONSTRUCTION—) [2]. Any smoothness test that records an average gap greater than ½ inch over three locations in a 3,000‑square‑yard sample requires correction (section 404.3 CONSTRUCTION—) [2]. Milling depth checks that reveal a deviation of ½ inch or more from the specified depth also trigger repair or replacement at no additional cost (section 404.3 CONSTRUCTION—) [2]. After milling, if the lot’s MRI exceeds the maximum acceptable MRI specified in Table A of Section 404.4, corrective action such as further grinding or replacement is required (section 404.3 CONSTRUCTION—) [2]. Any individual bump measured with a 12‑foot straightedge that exceeds 3/16 inch, or any ridge or valley at the edge of the treated area that exceeds 1/8 inch, must be ground or corrected (section 404.3 CONSTRUCTION—) [2]. If verification MRI exceeds acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile on at least 90 % of the 528‑foot lots, the tolerances are not met and correction is required (section 404.3 CONSTRUCTION—) [2]. Bond‑strength testing performed according to ASTM C1583 that yields an average of the three test results below 175 psi or any individual result under 160 psi deems the treated area defective and it must be repaired (section 545.3 CONSTRUCTION— BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY) [2]. Visible surface defects resulting from rain‑induced damage, improper final finish, honeycombing, or concrete air content that exceeds the allowable limit also indicate that additional maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation is required (section 545.3 CONSTRUCTION—) [2].
