Frost Penetration

Part of: Frost Heave is-subtopic-of
Frost Penetration is a topic under Frost Heave.
frost-penetration
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Frost Penetration is a pavement‑design strategy that addresses the engineering problem of frost‑induced damage—such as differential heaving, loss of bearing support during thaw, and freeze/thaw deterioration—by limiting the depth to which freezing temperatures reach frost‑susceptible subgrade soils[1]. The approach achieves this by removing highly frost‑susceptible material and replacing it with a sufficiently thick layer of non‑frost‑susceptible soil or aggregate that provides thermal insulation, supplemented by a stronger base course capable of bearing reduced support and by incorporating appropriate drainage to control moisture[1]. When applied to full‑depth flexible pavements in cold climates, these measures maintain high serviceability over extended periods, while for rigid pavements they are employed only when justified by significant frost‑heave risk[1].

Purpose and design objectives

Frost Penetration is addressed in the design to counteract the engineering problem of frost‑induced damage to pavement structures, namely differential heaving, loss of bearing support during spring‑thaw or warm winter periods caused by excess moisture, and freeze/thaw deterioration of materials; the goal is to maintain high serviceability over extended periods by limiting frost penetration into frost‑susceptible subgrade soils through insulation, removal of susceptible material, and appropriate drainage. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Frost penetration design must ensure that the pavement retains a high degree of serviceability for extended periods by reducing or eliminating the factors that cause frost heave, loss of bearing support during thaw, and freeze‑thaw damage to materials; it should remove soils highly susceptible to frost heave and replace them with a non‑frost‑susceptible layer thick enough to insulate the roadbed from frost penetration, and it should provide a stronger base course capable of withstanding reduced bearing capacity, thereby meeting functional, structural, durability, safety and serviceability objectives. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Scope and applicability

Frost penetration considerations are appropriate when designing full‑depth flexible pavements, especially where frost‑susceptible soils, freezing temperatures, and moisture are present; in such cases treatment for frost heave should always be included. It is recommended that soils highly susceptible to frost heave be removed and replaced with a layer of non‑frost‑susceptible material thick enough to insulate the roadbed soil from frost penetration, and additional measures such as base drains may be used. For rigid pavements, frost heave is typically not a problem when an adequate granular foundation course is provided, so treatment for frost heave in the pavement design of rigid pavements should only be included if there is significant justification. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

When the subgrade consists of soils that are highly frost‑susceptible, the preferred alternative to relying on frost‑penetration depth alone is to remove those soils and replace them with a sufficiently thick layer of non‑frost‑susceptible material; if removal is not feasible, the design may instead provide a stronger base course, appropriate drainage, or other subgrade‑strengthening measures, especially for rigid pavements where an adequate granular foundation usually eliminates frost heave and treatment should only be included if there is significant justification. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Design inputs and requirements

The design of pavements in frost‑prone areas is governed by the 1993 AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures (Appendix G), which provides the procedure to calculate loss of serviceability due to frost heave, and by NCHRP Synthesis 26 on roadway design for seasonal frost areas. Department policy recommends that soils highly susceptible to frost heave be removed and replaced with a layer of non‑frost‑susceptible material thick enough to insulate the roadbed from frost penetration, while the basic design approach calls for providing a stronger base course capable of withstanding reduced bearing support; additional measures such as pavement base drains may also be required. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Design methods and criteria

Frost design relies on the procedure described in the 1993 AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures, Appendix G to compute the loss of serviceability caused by frost heave, and the associated Frost Heave Calculator that can be accessed online through the ECMS File Cabinet. The approach also calls for removing highly frost‑susceptible soils and replacing them with a sufficiently thick non‑frost‑susceptible layer or providing a stronger base course, together with appropriate drainage, to limit frost penetration and thaw weakening. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Materials and mixture design

To mitigate frost penetration, the design calls for removing soils that are highly susceptible to frost heave and replacing them with a layer of non‑frost‑susceptible material that is thick enough to insulate the roadbed from freezing temperatures; alternatively, a stronger base course may be provided to sustain reduced bearing support, and pavement base drains or other appropriate drainage measures should be incorporated to improve performance and maintenance. For rigid pavements an adequate granular foundation course is normally sufficient, so treatment for frost heave is only included when justified. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Structural details and interfaces

For frost‑penetration design the subgrade should be protected by removing frost‑susceptible soils and installing a sufficiently thick layer of non‑frost‑susceptible material, and where abrupt changes in support occur gradual transitions made of embankment or granular non‑frost‑susceptible subbase are required. Reinforcement can be provided by placing geogrids at the subgrade‑to‑aggregate interface, while separation and additional stabilization are achieved with Class 4 Type A geotextile across the full lane width (8 feet or greater) and, where the subgrade CBR is less than three, Class 4 Type C geotextile to act as reinforcement. Installation of pavement base drains or other appropriate drains is also recommended to mitigate differential heave. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Frost‑penetration design recommends removing highly frost‑susceptible soils and replacing them with a layer of non-frostsusceptible material thick enough to insulate the roadbed soil from frost penetration, provides a stronger base course able to withstand lower bearing support, includes installation of pavement base drains, and calls for gradual subgrade transitions at discontinuities such as cut‑fill intersections or rock‑earth junctions; granular transitions should be used where a new pavement structure will abut an existing pavement of a different total depth. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Frost‑penetration design must reduce or eliminate the three factors that cause damage—frost‑susceptible soils, freezing temperatures, and a supply of water—by removing highly susceptible material and replacing it with a layer of non‑frost‑susceptible material thick enough to insulate the roadbed soil from frost penetration. The pavement design should also provide base drains or other appropriate drains and maintain positive slope for drainage so that moisture is controlled and the water source for frost heave and thaw weakening is limited. Although these actions do not affect the structural thickness, they are required to achieve acceptable serviceability in cold‑climate environments. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)
