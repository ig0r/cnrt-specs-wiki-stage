Slab Rocking

Part of: Void (loss of support) is-subtopic-of
Slab Rocking is a topic under Void (loss of support).
rockingunstable-slabsslab-movement
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Slab rocking is a distress in which concrete pavement slabs or fragments lose uniform support beneath the slab and become unstable, moving or rocking visibly under normal traffic loads; this instability appears on the surface as slabs that shift, wobble, or rock when vehicles drive over them [1][2]. It occurs in existing Portland‑cement concrete pavements—specifically jointed plain concrete (JPCP) or jointed reinforced concrete (JRCP)—that are slated for an unbonded concrete overlay, and is identified by visual inspection or vertical‑movement testing capable of detecting slab lift to 0.001 inch with an allowable upward movement limit of 0.05 inch at the pavement edge or two outside slab corners adjacent to a joint [1][2]. When this unstable condition is not corrected, it creates non‑uniform support for any overlay and can propagate into additional distress such as premature cracking or loss of structural integrity in the overlay [2].

Description and identification

Slab rocking is a distress in which concrete pavement slabs or fragments lose uniform support beneath the slab and become unstable, causing them to move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads; this instability is evident on the surface as slabs that shift, wobble, or appear to rock when vehicles drive over them [1][2]. The Pavement Policy Manual notes that such visibly rocking slabs are considered unstable and may require full‑depth repairs as part of an unbonded PCC overlay design intended to correct faulting [1]. The Pennsylvania DOT specifications state that any areas exhibiting these unstable slabs must be stabilized or repaired before the placement of the overlay interlayer material [2].

[1] — Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462)

Visual inspection reveals an unstable, visibly rocking concrete slab when it is loaded; the Pavement Policy Manual labels these as “unstable (visibly rocking) slabs” and treats them with unbonded PCC overlay and full‑depth repairs rather than cracking the pavement [1]. The manual distinguishes this condition from joint faulting, which is measured by offsets such as greater than 1/4‑inch faulting or exceeding 1/2 inch and is addressed with milling, a wearing course, or other specific actions [1]. In the Pennsylvania specifications slab rocking is recognized when slabs or slab fragments move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads, indicating unstable support that would otherwise cause non‑uniform support of an overlay; this visible motion separates it from other distresses such as cracking or faulting, which do not involve movement [2]. For cement‑grout stabilization the specifications require vertical‑movement testing with equipment capable of measuring slab lift at the pavement edge or any two outer corners adjacent to a joint and shoulder to within 0.001 inch, and any upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is deemed unacceptable, providing a precise lift measurement that distinguishes rocking from other distresses lacking measurable vertical displacement [2]. The same vertical‑movement testing criteria apply for asphalt‑grout stabilization, using equipment with the same 0.001‑inch detection capability and the same 0.05‑inch upward movement limit to identify unacceptable rocking [2].

[1] — Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 589–591)

Where it occurs

In the rehabilitation guidance of the Pavement Policy Manual (section 4.9) slab rocking is identified as a distress that occurs in existing Portland‑cement concrete pavements—specifically jointed plain concrete (JPCP) or jointed reinforced concrete (JRCP) slabs that are to receive an overlay [1]. The same manual’s unbonded PCC overlay module (section 10.7) reports slab rocking for existing Portland‑cement concrete pavements with unstable, visibly rocking slabs and notes that the separation interlayer must also extend through any shoulder that is constructed or reconstructed [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications describe slab rocking as a condition of concrete pavements in which slabs or slab fragments move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads, indicating it occurs on rigid (concrete) pavement surfaces that are to receive an unbonded concrete overlay [2]. Those specifications further require that any unstable concrete slabs be stabilized or repaired before the overlay interlayer material is placed and that vertical‑movement testing be performed at the pavement edge or at any two outside slab corners adjacent to a joint and the adjoining shoulder, limiting upward movement to not exceed 0.05 inch during grout injection [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 589–590)

Within the pavement, slab rocking is identified at joints and cracks when the loaded slab corner deflection exceeds 0.02 inch (Section 679—Slab Stabilization, 679.3 Construction). The same section calls for testing each transverse joint and crack and stabilizing any location that also shows a joint efficiency of 65% or greater; locations below this efficiency are patched if approved. The specification also identifies slab rocking as occurring in areas where whole concrete slabs or slab fragments lose uniform support and move visibly under normal traffic loads, so the distress is manifested within the slab body itself rather than at joints, cracks, corners, edges, wheel paths, layer interfaces, or the subgrade (Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay, 548.3 Construction). In the high‑density polyurethane grout stabilization procedure of Section 679.3(d), slab rocking is assessed by measuring vertical movement at the pavement edge or at any two outside slab corners adjacent to a joint, indicating that the distress occurs primarily along edges and corner locations near joints; the testing equipment must be capable of detecting slab lift to measurements of 0.001 inch. In the asphalt grout stabilization procedure of Section 679.3(c), vertical movement testing is used to detect slab lift at the pavement edge or at any two outside slab corners that are adjacent to a joint and the adjoining shoulder, indicating that rocking‑type uplift manifests at edges, corner‑joint locations, and shoulders; the specification limits upward movement of the pavement to not exceed 0.05 inch during asphalt grout injection. In the cement grout stabilization procedure of Section 679.3(b), slab rocking is evaluated by measuring the upward movement of the slab at its perimeter, with detection at the pavement edge or at any two outside slab corners adjacent to a joint and the adjoining shoulder; the vertical‑movement test must be able to record changes to within 0.001 inch, and any observed uplift greater than 0.05 inch will not be allowed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–592)

Severity levels and rating

The slab‑rocking control in Section 679.3(c) specifies that during polyurethane grout injection the pavement may not rise more than 0.05 inch; any upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is deemed unacceptable and pumping must be stopped. No additional severity categories or rating tables are provided for slab rocking in this section. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (p. 592)

Measurement, survey and extent

In SECTION 679—SLAB STABILIZATION > 679.3 CONSTRUCTION, slab rocking is quantified by measuring the loaded slab‑corner deflection with a deflection test performed in accordance with ASTM D 4695; the test is applied to each joint and crack (and repeated at every transverse joint and crack) under the prescribed temperature limits, and any location showing a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more triggers stabilization. In subsection (d) High Density Polyurethane Grout Stabilization, slab rocking is quantified by measuring the amount of slab lift (vertical movement) at the pavement edge or at any two outside slab corners adjacent to a joint and its shoulder using vertical‑movement testing equipment that can simultaneously detect lift at those points, and the equipment must be capable of detecting slab lift to measurements of 0.001 inch. In subsection (c) Asphalt Grout Stabilization, slab rocking is quantified by measuring slab lift, i.e., the vertical movement of the pavement, with satisfactory vertical‑movement testing equipment that can detect slab lift to a resolution of 0.001 inch; the measurement must be taken simultaneously at the pavement edge or at any two outside slab corners adjacent to a joint and the adjoining shoulder, and upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed. In subsection (b) Cement Grout Stabilization, slab rocking is quantified by measuring the vertical lift of the concrete slab using a vertical‑movement testing device that can simultaneously record the lift at the pavement edge or at two opposite slab corners adjacent to a joint and shoulder with a resolution of 0.001 inch; during grout injection the operator monitors this lift in real time and any upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited. In subsection 2.b Preparation of Polyurethane, slab rocking is measured by the amount of upward (vertical) movement of the pavement observed during polyurethane grout injection; a positive means of monitoring lift is employed to continuously record slab rise in real time as material is pumped, and any vertical movement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited with pumping stopped when movement is detected. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–592)

Effects on performance

When slabs or slab fragments move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads, the resulting unstable condition creates non‑uniform support for any concrete overlay; if this rocking is not stabilized or repaired as required in Section 548.3(e)1.a, the uneven support can propagate into additional distress of the overlay such as premature cracking or loss of structural integrity. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462)

Repairs and treatments

Slab stabilization or jacking is prescribed when slab movement or faulting is present, directly addressing rocking by stabilizing the slabs before any overlay work; the source also requires that slabs that are rocking, pumping, and/or faulted be stabilized prior to overlaying. The same section recommends cracking an existing jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) or breaking the bond between reinforcing steel and concrete on an existing jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) for pavements so distressed that patching is no longer economical, because this method reduces the size of free‑moving slabs and thus minimizes differential movement associated with slab rocking. An unbonded Portland cement concrete overlay applied together with full‑depth concrete repairs is recommended when slabs are identified as unstable or visibly rocking; this combination supplies uniform support and shallow repairs that specifically target faulting linked to the rocking condition, and it is appropriate when the existing pavement cannot be cost‑effectively repaired but can still serve as a subbase providing relatively uniform support. Section 679.3 of the Pennsylvania specification requires that any joint or crack showing a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more and having a joint efficiency of at least 65 % be stabilized by grout injection, which directly addresses the vertical movement that defines slab rocking. When the same deflection threshold is met but the joint efficiency is less than 65 %, a patch‑and‑stabilize repair is prescribed. If panel displacement exceeds the allowable tolerance of 0.05 inch, grinding of the raised pavement to the correct grade is mandated to correct slab tilt. Cement grout stabilization addresses slab rocking by injecting grout to fill lost support; upward movement during injection may not exceed 0.05 inch, grout pressure is limited to a maximum of 200 psi unless otherwise directed, and traffic must be prohibited for at least 12 hours after grouting. Asphalt grout stabilization mitigates slab rocking by filling voids beneath the concrete slab; upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed while asphalt is injected under a pressure of 25 to 45 psi. High‑density polyurethane grout stabilization treats slab rocking by injecting a high‑density polyurethane material beneath the slab; vertical lift detection capable of sensing movement as small as 0.001 inch is required, uplift may not exceed 0.05 inch, pumping must stop when any movement occurs, and traffic must be prohibited for a minimum of 30 minutes after completion. If transverse cracks develop between adjacent grout‑injection holes, the entire slab must be replaced at no additional cost to the Department. Any area where slabs or slab fragments move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads—conditions that would produce non‑uniform support of an overlay—must be stabilized or repaired before placement of the interlayer material; acceptable treatments include concrete pavement patching, slab stabilization, slab jacking, flexible‑base replacement, and shallow repairs using an approved asphalt, cementitious or similar material. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–592)

Whenever any slab movement such as rocking, pumping or faulting is observed on an existing PCC pavement, slab stabilization or jacking must be performed before an overlay is placed, and this requirement applies regardless of whether a bonded or unbonded concrete overlay is later selected. The Pavement Policy Manual further recommends that for visibly rocking slabs the appropriate treatment is to apply an Unbonded Concrete Overlay together with full‑depth concrete repairs, which provides uniform support and shallow faulting correction without cracking the slab; no further limits on severity, extent, depth, width or location are given for this option. Under Section 679—Slab Stabilization, a joint or crack is eligible for stabilization when the loaded slab‑corner deflection measured under traffic reaches at least 0.02 inch; if the associated joint efficiency is 65 % or greater the treatment may be applied as plain slab stabilization, whereas joints with an efficiency below 65 % may receive patch‑and‑stabilize work provided the representative approves. The asphalt grout stabilization described in Section 679.3(c) is allowed only while the upward movement of a rocking slab does not exceed 0.05 inch; any measured uplift greater than 0.05 inch excludes this treatment. Similarly, the cement grout stabilization procedure in Section 679.3(b) imposes the same upward‑movement limit of 0.05 inch for eligibility and additionally requires that injection pressure be kept at or below 200 psi unless a specific deviation is authorized; uplift beyond 0.05 inch rules out the method. Polyurethane grout injection, prescribed in Section 679.3 2.b, may be used for slab rocking only while the pavement’s upward movement remains at or below 0.05 inch, and pumping must be stopped if any vertical movement exceeds that threshold. For unbonded concrete overlays, Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfaced Pavements states that any slab or fragment that moves or rocks visibly under normal traffic loads – creating unstable slabs that would cause non‑uniform support of the overlay – must be stabilized or repaired before placement of the interlayer; acceptable remedial actions include concrete pavement patching, slab stabilization, slab jacking or flexible‑base replacement. When a geotextile interlayer is used for shallow repairs, surface defects larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch must be filled with an approved patching material prior to overlay placement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–592)

Prevention in design and construction

The Pavement Policy Manual advises that slab stabilization—referred to as jacking—be performed as a surface‑preparation step whenever slab movement or faulting is observed; this measure directly targets rocking slabs and is intended to reduce further slab movement during subsequent work (Section 4.9 B) [1]. The same manual adds that any slabs identified as rocking, pumping, or faulted should be stabilized before an overlay is placed, thereby reducing further slab movement during rehabilitation (Section 4.9 C) [1]. In Chapter 10, Subsection 10.7 the Department recommends using an unbonded concrete overlay together with full‑depth concrete repairs when existing slabs are identified as unstable (visibly rocking); the overlay supplies uniform support and shallow faulting repairs, which serves to reduce further slab rocking, and this recommendation applies only where the pavement cannot be repaired cost‑effectively to a good condition but can still function as a subbase that provides relatively uniform support (Section 10.7) [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require that areas with unstable slabs or slab fragments that move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads be stabilized or repaired before placement of the interlayer material, in order to avoid non‑uniform support of an unbonded concrete overlay; acceptable treatments listed include concrete pavement patching, slab stabilization, slab jacking, flexible‑base replacement and shallow repairs of surface defects, each measure targeting the loss of support that generates slab rocking (Section 548) [2]. Within the slab‑stabilization provisions, Asphalt Grout Stabilization is prescribed to mitigate slab rocking by filling voids beneath the concrete and limiting any upward movement; the lift during injection must not exceed 0.05 inch and the treatment is applied only after vertical grout holes have been drilled according to the standard drawing pattern and when the asphalt is heated to a temperature between 380 °F and 450 °F and pumped at a pressure of 25–45 psi (Section 679.3 c) [2]. The same section also permits high‑density polyurethane injection as an alternative slab‑stabilization method, with the same restriction that upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is not permitted and traffic may not be allowed onto the repaired area for at least 30 minutes after grouting operations have been completed, in accordance with the contractor’s approved field‑QC plan or manufacturer recommendations (Section 679) [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 590–592)

The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) mandates that slab stabilization or jacking be performed as a surface‑preparation step whenever slab movement or faulting is observed, directly restraining rocking slabs [1]. It further requires adequate subdrainage in locations where subsurface drainage problems exist, mitigating moisture‑related loss of support that can lead to pumping and subsequent slab rocking [1]. For pavements in which the existing concrete slab can serve as a subbase providing relatively uniform support, the manual recommends an unbonded concrete overlay combined with full‑depth concrete repairs to address visibly rocking slabs, thereby supplying uniform support and shallow repairs that target faulting rather than cracking the pavement [1]. When a geotextile interlayer is specified, joints exhibiting greater than ¼‑inch faulting shall be milled, and if joint faulting exceeds ½ inch a Superpave wearing course (9.5 mm mix) shall be placed at the joint; these measures are prescribed in Chapter 10, Section 10.7 of the manual [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications limit slab‑stabilization work to joints or cracks that show a loaded‑slab corner deflection of at least 0.02 in and a joint efficiency of 65 % or greater (or less than 65 % if approved), and the testing may be performed only when air temperature is between 40 °F and 70 °F and the subgrade is not frozen, targeting slabs already displaying rocking‑type movement [2]. During any grout‑injection operation the specification requires stopping all material placement as soon as any vertical movement is observed, thereby preventing further rocking while the Representative evaluates feasibility [2]. If a panel has risen more than the allowable tolerance of 0.05 in it must be ground back to grade in accordance with Section 514.3, eliminating uplift that contributes to slab rocking [2]. Upon completion of grout work the drilled holes shall be sealed with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete patching material, closing voids that could otherwise allow loss of support and subsequent rocking [2]. Any area where slabs or slab fragments move visibly under normal traffic loads shall be stabilized or repaired before placement of an interlayer; the required actions include concrete pavement patching, slab stabilization, slab‑jacking, or flexible‑base replacement, and when a geotextile interlayer is used surface defects larger than four square inches and one inch deep must be filled with an approved patching material because non‑uniform support would otherwise affect the overlay [2]. High‑density polyurethane grout stabilization prevents slab rocking by providing support beneath the pavement and controlling upward movement during injection; this practice applies only when grout‑injection holes are drilled vertically, are round, do not exceed 5/8 in in diameter, and extend to a depth that penetrates any stabilized base, and it requires a calibrated pump unit capable of controlling the rate of rise together with pressure and temperature devices and vertical‑movement testing equipment able to detect slab lift to 0.001 in [2]. Asphalt grout stabilization prevents slab rocking by filling voids beneath concrete slabs; it applies only while performing that specific work, requires drilling vertical holes, clearing them with compressed air, and injecting hot asphalt grout at 25‑45 psi while continuously monitoring uplift, and it forbids upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 in, a limit verified with vertical‑movement testing equipment capable of detecting lift to 0.001 in [2]. Cement‑grout stabilization is used on slabs that are losing support and prevents slab rocking by limiting any upward movement during injection to no more than 0.05 in, a limit monitored with the same vertical‑movement testing equipment; grout pressure may not exceed 200 psi unless otherwise directed, and traffic must be prohibited on the treated area for at least 12 hours after grouting [2]. The preparation of high‑density polyurethane grout also prevents upward movement greater than 0.05 in by requiring that pumping be stopped as soon as any vertical movement occurs, and it mandates a minimum traffic closure of 30 minutes after grouting, or longer according to the contractor’s approved field QC plan or the manufacturer’s recommendations [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–592)

Specification limits and acceptance

The acceptance criterion for slab rocking is that upward movement of the pavement may not exceed 0.05 inch. For asphalt grout stabilization, this limit is applied during the grout injection step described in Section 679.3(c)2.d; any lift greater than the 0.05‑inch threshold must be stopped. The vertical displacement is measured with the vertical‑movement testing equipment required in Section 679.3(c)1.h, which shall be capable of detecting slab lift to a resolution of 0.001 inch. For cement grout stabilization, Section 679.3(b) likewise specifies that upward movement greater than 0.05 inch will not be allowed, and it requires vertical‑movement testing equipment able to measure slab lift within 0.001 inch, including simultaneous measurement at the pavement edge or two outside slab corners adjacent to a joint and the adjoining shoulder. For polyurethane grout injection, Section 679.3(d) (referenced in subsection 2.c) states that upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is not permitted; a positive means of monitoring lift must be used as specified in Section 679.3(d), and pumping shall be stopped when any vertical movement occurs to remain within the acceptance criterion. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 589–592)

The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) states that when the slab‑rocking limit is reached, the slabs shall be stabilized—or jacked—before any overlay is placed; this stabilization appears as a required item in the surface‑preparation steps of Section 4.9 and the party constructing the overlay is responsible for performing it, although the manual does not prescribe a specific completion time [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications provide a more detailed requirement. In Section 548.3 of the Unbonded Concrete Overlay specification, any slab that moves or rocks visibly under normal traffic loads must be stabilized or repaired before the interlayer material is placed, and the contractor must select one of the prescribed treatments—concrete pavement patching, slab stabilization, slab‑jacking, flexible‑base replacement, or shallow repairs—and complete the work prior to installing the geotextile or asphalt interlayer [2]. If upward movement of a pavement slab exceeds 0.05 inch, Section 679‑3(d) of the Slab Stabilization requirements mandates injection of high‑density polyurethane grout beneath the slab as the stabilizing remedial action; the injection is performed during the slab‑stabilization construction activity and must be stopped as soon as any vertical movement occurs, with the Inspector‑in‑Charge authorized to direct cessation. After the grout has been placed, traffic may not be opened for a minimum of 30 minutes, or longer if required by the contractor’s approved field quality‑control plan or the manufacturer’s recommendations [2].
