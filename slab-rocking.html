Slab Rocking

Part of: Void (loss of support) is-subtopic-of
Slab Rocking is a topic under Void (loss of support).
rockingunstable-slabsslab-movement
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Slab rocking is a condition in which concrete pavement slabs become unstable and visibly rock under normal traffic loads, indicating loss of uniform support beneath the slab[1][2]. It occurs on existing Portland‑cement concrete pavements—specifically jointed plain concrete (JPCP) or jointed reinforced concrete (JRCP) slabs slated for an overlay—and is most evident at slab edges, any two outside slab corners adjacent to a joint, and the adjoining shoulder where vertical lift can be measured[1][2]. The distress originates from deterioration of the underlying support and is aggravated by joint faulting greater than 1/4‑inch, leading to non‑uniform bearing that can cause premature failure of any subsequent overlay[2].

Description and identification

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), section 10.7 UNBONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT, slab rocking is defined as a condition in which concrete pavement slabs become unstable and visibly rock, indicating loss of uniform support beneath the slab; this movement is evident as slabs that shift under traffic and can lead to faulting that the overlay design seeks to correct.[1] In the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS, section 548—UNBONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF CONCRETE‑SURFACED PAVEMENTS > 548.3 CONSTRUCTION, slab rocking is described as a distress where concrete slabs or slab fragments become unstable and move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads, signaling loss of uniform support beneath the slab that must be stabilized or repaired before an overlay can be placed.[2]

[1] — Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) slab rocking is identified by a visual inspection that shows an unstable, visibly rocking concrete slab when it is loaded; these are termed “unstable (visibly rocking) slabs.” [1] The manual distinguishes this condition from joint faulting, which is quantified by offsets greater than 1/4‑inch faulting or exceeding 1/2 inch and is addressed with milling, a wearing course, or other specific actions rather than an unbonded overlay. [1] In the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, Section 679—Slab Stabilization recognizes slab rocking through vertical‑movement testing equipment capable of detecting slab lift to a resolution of 0.001 inch at the pavement edge or on two opposite corners adjacent to a joint and shoulder. [2] The specification states that any upward movement measured during asphalt grout injection that exceeds 0.05 inch is prohibited and therefore classified as rocking rather than normal settlement or other minor distresses. [2] Additionally, Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay identifies slab rocking on the existing surface when slabs or slab fragments are observed to move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads, indicating an unstable slab that would otherwise produce non‑uniform support for a new overlay. [2] Because the condition is defined by its motion, it is distinguished from other distresses that do not exhibit such movement and does not trigger the pre‑overlay stabilization steps listed in Section 548.3, which are reserved for conditions lacking visible rocking. [2]

[1] — Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–604)

Where it occurs

According to the Pavement Policy Manual rehabilitation guidance (section 4.9), slab rocking is a distress that occurs in existing Portland‑cement concrete pavements, specifically jointed plain concrete (JPCP) or jointed reinforced concrete (JRCP) slabs slated for an overlay, and the manual requires that any slab found to be rocking, pumping, or faulted be stabilized before applying either a bonded or unbonded concrete overlay; no similar rocking condition is described for asphalt, composite, or shoulder structures. In the same manual’s unbonded PCC overlay module (section 10.7), slab rocking is reported for existing Portland‑cement concrete pavements that have unstable, visibly rocking slabs, indicating that the distress is associated with concrete pavement structures rather than asphalt surfaces, and the separation interlayer used in that treatment must also extend through any shoulder that is constructed or reconstructed. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications treat slab rocking as a condition affecting concrete pavement slabs, including the edges of the slab, any two outside slab corners adjacent to a joint, and the adjoining shoulder where lift is measured, and vertical‑movement testing must be capable of detecting slab lift at these locations. During high‑density polyurethane grout stabilization, upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed. In Section 548 of those specifications, slab rocking is identified on concrete‑surfaced (rigid) pavements where unstable slabs or slab fragments move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads, and any area showing this condition must be stabilized or repaired before an unbonded concrete overlay or other interlayer material is placed; the specification likewise does not reference asphalt, composite, or overlay structures as locations for slab rocking. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 604–605)

In the slab‑stabilization provisions of Section 679, rocking is identified by measuring vertical lift of the concrete slab during grout stabilization. The specification requires equipment that can detect lift at the pavement edge or at any two outside slab corners adjacent to a joint and the adjoining shoulder with a precision of 0.001 inch. For both asphalt grout (Section 679.3(c)) and high‑density polyurethane grout (Section 679.3(d)), upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed, indicating unacceptable rocking; consequently the distress manifests primarily at slab perimeters—edges and corner‑joint locations rather than within the interior or at layer interfaces. Section 548.3 states that slab rocking is observed in the concrete slabs themselves, meaning any slab or fragment that moves or rocks visibly under normal traffic loads because of loss of support beneath the slab; such unstable slabs and fragments must be stabilized or repaired before placement of the interlayer material. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–605)

Causes and mechanisms

Slab rocking originates when the support beneath a concrete slab deteriorates so that the slab or its fragments move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads, creating non‑uniform support for any subsequent overlay (Section 548.3). The condition is identified as “unstable slabs” and is aggravated by joint faulting greater than 1/4‑inch, which requires milling before a geotextile interlayer can be placed, and by faulting exceeding 1/2‑inch, which demands a scratch course or additional joint grinding to limit further movement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Severity levels and rating

The high‑density polyurethane grout stabilization procedure permits only minimal slab lift; any upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed, so the specification effectively uses a single severity threshold rather than multiple ratings or a table for slab rocking. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 604–605)

Measurement, survey and extent

For high‑density polyurethane grout stabilization (Section 679.3(d)), slab rocking is quantified by measuring slab lift, defined as the vertical movement of the pavement edge or any two outside slab corners adjacent to a joint and the adjoining shoulder. The measurement is made with vertical‑movement testing equipment that is capable of detecting slab lift to 0.001 inch, and the equipment must record the lift of the specified points simultaneously; uplift may not exceed 0.05 inch, and pumping shall be stopped if any upward movement occurs. For asphalt grout stabilization (Section 679.3(c)), slab rocking is likewise quantified as slab lift measured with vertical‑movement testing equipment that can detect lift to 0.001 inch. The equipment must be able to record the lift of the pavement edge or any two outside slab corners adjacent to a joint and the adjoining shoulder at the same time, providing a positive means of monitoring lift during grout injection; upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–605)

Effects on performance

When slabs rock—i.e., move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads—they become unstable and provide non‑uniform support for any overlay placed on them. If this condition is left untreated, the uneven bearing can cause premature distress or failure of the overlay structure. Accordingly, Section 548.3 (1.a) requires that such unstable slabs be stabilized or repaired before the interlayer material is installed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Repairs and treatments

The Pavement Policy Manual requires slab stabilization or jacking when slab movement or faulting is present, and specifically mandates that slabs that are rocking, pumping, and/or faulted be stabilized prior to overlaying (Section 4.9 Rehabilitation) [1]. For pavements so distressed that patching is no longer economical, the manual recommends cracking an existing jointed plain concrete pavement or breaking the bond between reinforcing steel and concrete on a jointed reinforced concrete pavement, a method intended to reduce the size of free‑moving slabs and thus minimize differential movement associated with slab rocking (Section 4.9 Rehabilitation) [1]. When unstable or visibly rocking slabs are identified, the manual advises applying an unbonded Portland cement concrete overlay together with full‑depth concrete repairs; this combination supplies uniform support and shallow repairs that specifically target faulting linked to the rocking condition (Section 10.7 Unbonded PCC Overlay) [1]. The same section notes that such unbonded overlays are appropriate when the existing pavement cannot be cost‑effectively repaired to good condition but can still serve as a subbase providing relatively uniform support (Section 10.7 Unbonded PCC Overlay) [1]. High Density Polyurethane Grout Stabilization is identified as a repair that targets slab rocking by stabilizing the slab and limiting its vertical movement, and it may only be used when upward movement of the pavement does not exceed 0.05 inch (Section 679.3(d)) [2]. Asphalt Grout Stabilization also addresses slab rocking by filling voids beneath the concrete with hot asphalt to restore support; the method requires drilling vertical holes no larger than 1½ inches, heating the asphalt to a temperature of 380 °F to 450 °F, pumping it at a pressure of 25 psi to 45 psi, and monitoring lift with equipment capable of measuring to 0.001 inch so that upward movement of the pavement does not exceed 0.05 inch, and traffic may not be reopened until at least 30 minutes after grouting has been completed (Section 679.3(c)) [2]. For projects that include an unbonded concrete overlay, any area where slabs are unstable or rock visibly under normal traffic must be stabilized or repaired before placement of the interlayer material (Section 548.3); acceptable treatments listed for those unstable slabs include Concrete Pavement Patching (Section 516), Slab Stabilization (Section 679), Slabjacking (Section 681), Flexible‑base Replacement (Section 316) and shallow repairs with an approved patching material, each intended to prevent non‑uniform support of the new overlay (Section 548.3) [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–605)

When any slab movement such as rocking, pumping or faulting is observed on an existing PCC pavement, slab stabilization or jacking must be performed before an overlay is placed, and this requirement applies regardless of whether a bonded or unbonded concrete overlay is later selected. For slabs that are visibly rocking, the recommended treatment in section 10.7 is to apply an Unbonded Concrete Overlay together with full‑depth concrete repairs, which provides uniform support and shallow faulting correction without cracking the slab; no further limits on severity, extent, depth, width or location are given for this option. High‑Density Polyurethane Grout Stabilization may be applied only if slab uplift during injection does not exceed 0.05 inch; upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited and pumping must be stopped, which rules out the grout stabilization for slabs rocking beyond that limit. Asphalt Grout Stabilization is permitted only when the slab’s upward movement during injection does not exceed 0.05 inch; any lift greater than that is ruled out and the procedure must be stopped before the limit is reached, and the method also requires drilling grout‑injection holes to a depth sufficient to penetrate any stabilized base, so it applies to slabs where such hole depth can be achieved. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–605)

Prevention in design and construction

Slab stabilization, also described as jacking, is prescribed as a surface‑preparation step whenever slab movement or faulting is observed; this measure directly targets rocking slabs and is intended to reduce further slab movement during subsequent work (Section 4.9 B) [1]. The concrete‑overlay guidance adds that any slabs identified as rocking, pumping, or faulted should be stabilized before an overlay is placed, thereby reducing further slab movement during rehabilitation (Section 4.9 C) [1]. For pavements that cannot be repaired cost‑effectively but can serve as a subbase providing relatively uniform support, the Department recommends using an unbonded concrete overlay together with full‑depth concrete repairs when existing slabs are identified as unstable (visibly rocking); the overlay supplies uniform support and shallow faulting repairs, which serves to reduce further slab rocking (Section 10.7) [1]. High‑Density Polyurethane Grout Stabilization is a slab‑stabilization design measure intended to prevent or reduce slab rocking by providing support beneath the concrete and limiting any upward movement; the procedure requires injection at a controlled rate while a lift‑monitoring system capable of detecting changes as small as 0.001 inch is used, and it explicitly prohibits vertical displacement greater than 0.05 inch during the injection operation (Section 679.3(d)) [2]. Asphalt Grout Stabilization is identified as a slab‑stabilization design measure that addresses slab rocking by stabilizing the pavement from below; vertical grout holes are drilled and asphalt is pumped at pressures between 25 psi and 45 psi while continuous lift monitoring with equipment able to detect 0.001 inch changes ensures that upward movement does not exceed 0.05 inch, and this uplift limit applies during the grout‑injection operation set out in Section 679.3(c) [2]. When an unbonded concrete overlay is planned, any area exhibiting unstable slabs or visible slab fragments that could cause non‑uniform support of the overlay must be stabilized before placement of the interlayer; stabilization may be achieved by concrete pavement patching (Section 516), slab stabilization (Section 679), slabjacking (Section 681) or flexible‑base replacement (Section 316), and shallow repairs using an approved patching material are required for surface defects larger than 4 in² and deeper than 1 in to provide a flat, tight surface (Section 548) [2]. Joints with faulting greater than ¼‑inch must receive a geotextile interlayer, a measure intended to limit differential movement that could lead to rocking (Section 548.3‑3.a) [2]. Joints with faulting exceeding ½‑inch must be provided with an asphalt interlayer with a scratch course or milling, also intended to limit differential movement that could lead to rocking (Section 548.3‑3.b) [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–605)

The Pavement Policy Manual requires slab stabilization or jacking as a surface‑preparation step whenever slab movement or faulting is present, directly restraining rocking slabs (Chapter 4, Subsection 4.9) and it also states that slabs that are rocking, pumping, or faulted should be stabilized prior to overlay placement (Chapter 4, Subsection 4.9). Adequate subdrainage is required where subsurface drainage problems exist, mitigating moisture‑related loss of support that can lead to pumping and subsequent slab rocking (Chapter 4, Subsection 4.9). For visibly rocking slabs the department recommends an unbonded concrete overlay combined with full‑depth concrete repairs, providing uniform support and shallow repairs that address faulting rather than cracking the pavement; this practice applies only to pavements where the existing slab can serve as a subbase offering relatively uniform support (Chapter 10, Section 10.7). When separating the concrete layers with a geotextile interlayer (or asphalt concrete), joints that exhibit greater than 1/4‑inch faulting should be milled, and if joint faulting exceeds 1/2 inch a Superpave wearing course (9.5 mm mix) should be placed at the joint (Chapter 10, Section 10.7). The high‑density polyurethane grout stabilization described in Section 679.3(d) prevents slab rocking by filling voids beneath the concrete and providing support to stop upward movement; vertical holes are drilled and polyurethane is injected while continuous monitoring of lift with equipment capable of detecting 0.001‑inch changes, and injection must be halted if any upward movement greater than 0.05 inch occurs, and traffic may not be opened until at least 30 minutes after the grouting operations have been completed (Section 679.3(d)). Asphalt grout stabilization required by Section 679.3(c) also prevents slab rocking by controlling upward movement of the pavement: vertical‑movement testing equipment must be provided to measure slab lift with a resolution of 0.001 inch, the procedure limits upward movement to no more than 0.05 inch, the asphalt used for grout must be heated to between 380°F and 450°F and injected at a pressure of 25 psi to 45 psi, and drilling holes no larger than 1½ inches in diameter, vertical and round, creates proper access for grout to support the slab uniformly (Section 679.3(c)). Section 548.3 mandates that any area where slabs or slab fragments move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads—unstable slabs that would cause non‑uniform support of an unbonded concrete overlay—must be stabilized or repaired before placement of the interlayer material; the required construction practices include concrete pavement patching (Section 516), slab stabilization (Section 679), slabjacking (Section 681), flexible‑base replacement (Section 316), and shallow repairs using an approved asphalt, cementitious, or similar pavement repair material that meets representative approval before placing the overlay (Section 548.3). [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–605)

Specification limits and acceptance

In the slab‑stabilization specifications, Section 679—SLAB STABILIZATION subsection 679.3(d) for high‑density polyurethane grout stabilization limits slab rocking by prohibiting any upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch; this limit must be verified with vertical‑movement testing equipment that is capable of detecting slab lift to measurements of 0.001 inch and able to record the lift at the pavement edge or any two outside slab corners adjacent to a joint and the adjoining shoulder. Likewise, Section 679—SLAB STABILIZATION subsection 679.3(c) for asphalt grout stabilization controls slab rocking by the same restriction that any uplift greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed and must be measured with vertical‑movement testing equipment capable of detecting lift to 0.001 inch as required in Section 679.3(c)1.h and applied in the procedure of Section 679.3(c)2.d. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–605)

The Pavement Policy Manual specifies that when the slab‑rocking limit is reached, the slabs must be stabilized or jacked before any overlay is placed; this stabilization appears as a required item in the surface‑preparation steps of Section 4.9 and the party constructing the overlay is responsible for performing it, although the manual does not prescribe a specific time frame for completion [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications address slab rocking in two related sections. In Section 679—Slab Stabilization, under 679.3 Construction, the document states that if upward pavement movement exceeds 0.05 inch during High‑Density Polyurethane Grout Stabilization (subsection d), grout injection shall be stopped and the high‑density polyurethane treatment must be completed beneath the slab; this work is to be carried out by the contractor using the prescribed equipment, with the Inspector‑in‑Charge authorized to direct cessation of pumping [2]. The same section requires that for Asphalt Grout Stabilization (subsection c) pumping be stopped immediately when the slab lift exceeds the allowable 0.05 inch, an action performed by the contractor/operator executing Section 679.3(c) [2]. After any grouting operation in either case, traffic may not be opened for a minimum of 30 minutes following completion of the work, as mandated by the specification [2]. Additionally, Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfaced Pavements, in its construction subsection, requires that any area exhibiting unstable slabs or slab fragments that move or rock under normal traffic loads be stabilized or repaired before placement of the interlayer material; the remedial options listed include concrete pavement patching, slab stabilization, slabjacking, flexible‑base replacement, or shallow repairs, and these actions are to be performed as part of construction prior to overlay placement [2].
