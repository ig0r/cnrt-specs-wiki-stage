Longitudinal Crack

Part of: Cracking is-subtopic-of
Longitudinal Crack is a topic under Cracking.
longitudinal-crackslongitudinal-cracklongitudinal-cracking
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

A longitudinal crack is a fracture that runs parallel to the pavement’s centerline and appears as a break within a traffic lane[2]. These cracks are identified in plain cement concrete and Portland cement concrete slabs, occurring either in the longitudinal joint between adjacent lanes or at the joint where a lane meets a concrete shoulder (including shoulders that are asphalt), and they are not mentioned for pure‑asphalt surfaces, composite structures, overlays, or other shoulder types[1]. When left untreated, longitudinal cracks reduce load transfer, causing slab migration and faulting that compromise structural capacity, ride quality, safety and durability[1].

Description and identification

A longitudinal crack is a fracture that runs parallel to the pavement’s centerline and appears as a break within a traffic lane. When such a crack is observed, the specification requires the concrete slabs containing the crack be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–367)

A distress is recognized as a longitudinal crack or longitudinal joint when it is identified for the cross‑stitching demonstration within the project limits, and it is distinguished from similar distresses because the specification explicitly permits cross‑stitching only on those longitudinal features while prohibiting its use on transverse joints or cracks. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 424–425)

Where it occurs

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, longitudinal cracks are identified in plain cement concrete pavements and in Portland cement concrete pavement slabs, where they appear in the longitudinal joint between traffic lanes and at the joint where a lane meets a concrete shoulder, including cases where that shoulder is asphalt [1]. The same manual notes that it does not mention longitudinal cracking on pure asphalt surfaces, composite structures, overlays, or other types of shoulders [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications document longitudinal‑crack distress for rigid concrete pavements, limiting the cross‑stitching procedure to longitudinal cracks or joints located within the project limits of plain cement concrete slabs and prohibiting its use on transverse joints or cracks (Section 528) [2]. Section 501 requires that when a longitudinal crack occurs within a lane, the affected concrete slabs must be removed and replaced at no additional cost, and it treats cracks at the interface between concrete pavement and shoulder as part of the concrete construction (Section 501) [2]. Section 525 classifies longitudinal cracking encountered on existing concrete pavements for partial‑depth repair, specifying a Type 1 spot repair for crack lengths between 15 inches and 6 feet and a Type 2 extended repair for lengths greater than 6 feet; these repairs are applicable to transverse or longitudinal joints, cracks, and interior slab spalling on concrete pavements (Section 525) [2]. Section 469 includes longitudinal cracking as a distress considered for flexible (asphalt) pavements, specifying cleaning and sealing of longitudinal cracks in existing pavement surfaces (Section 469) [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 314), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–425)

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, longitudinal cracking in Portland cement concrete pavement can manifest either as a longitudinal joint that separates adjacent traffic lanes or the lane from a concrete shoulder, or as a crack that runs through the slab interior parallel to the direction of travel. The same manual requires cleaning and sealing the longitudinal joint between traffic lanes and between the lane and a concrete shoulder, and calls for strengthening longitudinal cracks in slabs to prevent slab migration. The rigid‑pavement specifications in Section 528 treat a longitudinal crack as a longitudinal joint that exists within the concrete slab itself and runs parallel to the pavement’s centerline, and cross‑stitching procedures are applied only to these longitudinal cracks or joints, expressly excluding transverse joints or cracks, which indicates that the distress is located in the slab interior along its length rather than at edges, corners, wheel paths, layer interfaces, or the base/subgrade. Section 501 notes that a longitudinal crack is observed within the lane‑width of a concrete slab and, when such a crack appears, it is treated as a defect in the slab interior; the affected slabs must be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department. Section 525 describes longitudinal cracking as a distress that occurs along longitudinal joints or as cracks within the concrete slab, including spalling on the interior slab surface, and permits spot repairs for lengths between 15 inches and 6 feet and extended repairs for sections longer than 6 feet. The description in Section 528 further indicates that longitudinal cracking appears in plain cement concrete slabs and that cross‑stitching can be performed on both longitudinal cracks and longitudinal joints, confirming that these cracks may be located within the slab interior as well as along the longitudinal joint lines. For flexible pavements, Section 469 identifies longitudinal cracking as a distress that appears at longitudinal joints on the pavement surface and calls for cleaning and sealing of those joints together with transverse joints and random cracks. In addition, Section 489 requires sealing any longitudinal cracks that occur along longitudinal joints and other cracks that are at least 1/4‑inch wide, reinforcing that longitudinal cracking is considered a distress located at joint lines on the pavement surface. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 314–338), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–425)

Severity levels and rating

Within Section 525 of the specification, longitudinal cracks that can be treated by a partial‑depth repair are grouped into two severity categories. Type 1 repairs cover spot repairs of transverse or longitudinal joints, cracks, and interior slab spalling whose length falls between 15 inches and 6 feet, while Type 2 repairs apply to extended‑length repairs of the same distress when the crack exceeds 6 feet. The classification is based solely on the measured length of the crack and determines whether a spot or an extended partial‑depth repair is specified. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 416)

Effects on performance

Longitudinal cracks that are not promptly addressed can reduce load transfer, leading to slab migration and faulting that compromise structural capacity, ride quality, safety and durability; consequently cleaning and sealing the longitudinal joint should be performed as part of every CPR project, and cross‑stitching may provide nearly 100 % load transfer while strengthening the crack to maintain aggregate interlock and help preserve service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90)

If a longitudinal crack is left untreated, the joint can develop extensive spalling that progresses from the top half of the slab to beyond mid‑slab depth, eventually causing slab migration, loss of aggregate interlock and faulting of the centerline joint; this separation may also cause lanes or shoulders to pull apart, creating a maintenance problem. The recommended progression of treatment begins with cleaning and sealing per Section 512, moves to partial‑depth spall repair (Section 525) when spalls are limited to the top half, escalates to full‑depth longitudinal joint repair (Section 519) once spalling extends past mid‑slab, and finally may require cross‑stitching (Section 528) to restore near‑100 % load transfer and tie the separating lanes or joints. The 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir is specified for joint sealing, and a 4.75 mm asphalt wearing course material is used to fill badly spalled joints before overlay. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90)

Repairs and treatments

Cross‑stitching is specified as the repair method for longitudinal cracks or joints in plain cement concrete pavements, and it consists of placing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle across the crack (section 2.1 GENERAL) [1]. The technique is intended to provide nearly 100 % load transfer when the longitudinal crack or joint is in reasonably good condition (section 4.3 JOINTS) and it serves to strengthen the crack so that slab migration is prevented and aggregate interlock is maintained; it may also be employed where centerline longitudinal joints are beginning to fault [1]. Cross‑stitching may be used only when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at least 40 °F, and it must not be applied to transverse joints or cracks (section 528) [2]. After a full‑depth saw cut, the crack is routed to three‑quarters of an inch wide by one inch deep, cleaned, epoxy‑coated tie bars are installed, and the crack is filled with sealant to a level between 1/8 inch and 1/4 inch below the pavement surface; the sealant placement also requires temperatures of at least 40 °F (section 528.3) [2]. In addition to cross‑stitching, cleaning and sealing of concrete cracks must be performed whenever required by Publication 408, Specifications, Section 590 (section 2.1 GENERAL) [1]. For asphalt pavements the prescribed remedy for longitudinal cracks is cleaning and sealing with the rubberized sealant specified in Section 469, and this sealant must be applied to cracks that are at least ¼ inch wide at least 24 hours before any paving work on an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course (section 469) [2]. Partial‑depth repair of concrete longitudinal cracks is permitted provided the repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness (section 525) [2]. When the crack length lies between 15 inches and 6 feet, a Type 1 spot repair is prescribed; for cracks longer than 6 feet, a Type 2 extended‑length repair is required (section 525) [2]. If a longitudinal crack occurs within a lane, the slab containing the crack may be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department, while if surface damage is produced an approved repair method may be used instead, also at no additional cost (section 528) [2]. Any crack or spall detected before final inspection must be repaired in accordance with Table A, Repair Procedures for Deficiency in New Concrete Pavement, at no additional cost to the Department (section 525) [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 314–338), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–425)

According to the PCCP guidelines, a dowel‑bar retrofit may be used on longitudinal cracks that are hairline in width and where aggregate interlock still exists, indicating a very low‑severity crack [1]. Cross‑stitching is applicable for longitudinal cracks or joints that are in reasonably good condition and just beginning to fault, providing near‑full load transfer for such early‑stage distress [1]. The same specification further limits cross‑stitching to longitudinal cracks or joints that can be routed to a width of 3/4 inch and a depth of 1 inch, and it may only be performed when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at least 40 °F; work must be stopped if either temperature falls below this threshold, and the method is expressly prohibited on transverse joints or cracks [2]. Under the Slip‑Form Paving Method, when a longitudinal crack is observed within a lane the specification requires removal and replacement of the slab(s) containing the crack at no additional cost to the Department, and any accompanying surface damage must be repaired by an approved method also at no extra charge [2]. The Concrete Pavement Partial‑Depth Repair may be employed on longitudinal cracks only when the required repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness; if the needed depth is greater than half the slab thickness the partial‑depth approach is ruled out and full‑depth patches must be used instead [2]. Within the allowable depth, cracks measuring between 15 inches and 6 feet in length are treated as Type 1 spot repairs, whereas cracks longer than 6 feet are addressed with a Type 2 extended‑length repair [2]. The ultra‑thin bonded wearing course specifications require that any longitudinal crack 1/4‑inch or wider be sealed with the rubberized sealant referenced in Section 469 at least 24 hours before paving; sealing does not apply to narrower longitudinal cracks [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 337–338), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–425)

Prevention in design and construction

Cross‑stitching is identified as a design measure that addresses longitudinal cracks in plain cement concrete pavements by installing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle across the crack or joint; it is prescribed when such longitudinal cracks or joints are present in PCC surfaces (section 2.1 GENERAL, item k) and also when the crack is in reasonably good condition, where it serves to strengthen the crack, prevent slab migration and maintain aggregate interlock, especially where lanes or shoulders are separating (section 4.3 JOINTS). Cleaning and sealing longitudinal joints should be performed as part of every CPR project to preserve joint integrity and thus limit the development of longitudinal cracking (section 4.3 JOINTS). Dowel‑bar retrofits are primarily used on roadways that receive heavily channeled loadings, where transverse joints or cracks would benefit from improved load transfer; they are most effective when hairline cracks are observed and aggregate interlock still exists (section 4.3 JOINTS). The slip‑form paving specification limits longitudinal cracking by requiring that longitudinal joints be placed parallel to and within 12 inches of the final pavement markings and, unless the construction drawings indicate otherwise, not traverse a driving lane (Section 501.3(g)2). Tie bars must be installed as indicated, with no tie bar located within 24 inches of a dowelled contraction joint (Section 501.3(g)). To minimize shear cracking, one tie bar shall be eliminated in each slab adjacent to a joint where a crack of at least 1/8‑inch has opened (Section 501.3(i)2). When paving from October 1 to April 1 an additional tie bar shall be eliminated in each slab adjacent to dominant joints (Section 501.3(i)2). Tied longitudinal contraction joints must be sawed to prevent random cracking (Section 501.3(i)2). Cross‑stitching is prescribed as a design measure that reduces the effect of existing longitudinal cracks and joints by placing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle across the crack or joint in plain cement concrete pavement (Section 528.1). For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, longitudinal joints and cracks that are 1/4‑inch or wider shall be sealed with the rubberized sealant of Section 469 at least 24 hours before paving; this preparation step is intended to prevent or reduce longitudinal cracking (Section 489.3(b)). Specification limits and acceptance

The Rigid Pavement specifications define several acceptance and construction limits for longitudinal cracks. Under Section 528, Cross‑Stitching work may only be undertaken when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at least 40 °F; the drilled holes must not exceed the tie‑bar diameter by more than 3/8 in.; the crack must be routed to a width of 3/4 in. and a depth of 1 in.; and after cleaning and drying, sealant may be placed so that it fills the crack to a level between 1/8 in. and 1/4 in. below the pavement surface. Section 501, Slip‑Form Paving Method, states that any longitudinal crack with an opening of 1/8‑inch or greater requires elimination of one tie bar adjacent to the joint, and if such a crack occurs within a lane the slab containing the crack must be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department; the condition is judged by measuring the visible opening of the crack. Section 525, Partial‑Depth Repair, limits the repair depth for a longitudinal crack to no greater than half the slab thickness; when the cracked length lies between 15 inches and 6 feet it is treated as a Type 1 spot repair, while lengths greater than 6 feet are classified as Type 2 extended‑length repair. Finally, Section 489, Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course, requires that any longitudinal crack 1/4‑inch or wider be sealed with the rubberized sealant prescribed in Section 469, using crack width as the controlling measurement and ensuring that the over‑band thickness and width of the sealant do not exceed the tolerances set out in Section 469. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 337–338), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–425)

When a longitudinal crack is observed within a lane, the specification calls for the contractor to remove and replace the cracked slab(s) and to repair any surface damage by an approved method, both at no additional cost to the Department; the excerpt does not specify a time limit for completing this work.
