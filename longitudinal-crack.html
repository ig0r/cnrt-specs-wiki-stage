Longitudinal Crack

Part of: Cracking is-subtopic-of
Longitudinal Crack is a topic under Cracking.
longitudinal-crackslongitudinal-cracklongitudinal-cracking
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Longitudinal cracks are pavement distresses that appear as fractures aligned parallel to the roadway’s centerline, distinguishing them from transverse cracks that run across the surface; any crack of either orientation that is 1/4‑inch or wider must be sealed before paving[2]. They occur on rigid concrete pavement structures—plain cement‑concrete and reinforced (Portland) cement‑concrete slabs—either as longitudinal joints between adjacent traffic lanes, between a lane and a concrete shoulder, or as cracks running through the interior of the slab body[1][2]. When left untreated these parallel fractures diminish load transfer across the slab, promoting slab migration, faulting of the centerline joint, loss of aggregate interlock and consequent reductions in structural capacity, ride quality, safety and durability[1].

Description and identification

Longitudinal cracks are identified by their alignment parallel to the pavement’s centerline and are set apart from transverse cracks that run across the surface; any crack of either orientation that is 1/4‑inch or wider must be sealed before paving. The preparation step requires sealing these longitudinal (and transverse) joints and cracks at least 24 hours prior to overlay work, using the rubberized sealant prescribed in Section 469 while keeping the over‑band thickness and width within the tolerances of that section. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 345)

Where it occurs

Longitudinal cracking is reported to occur on rigid concrete pavement structures, specifically plain cement‑concrete roadways and reinforced (Portland) cement‑concrete slabs, where the distress manifests in longitudinal joints between traffic lanes and at the joint where a lane meets a concrete shoulder—including cases where that shoulder is asphalt [1][2]. The Pavement Policy Manual notes that the source does not mention longitudinal cracking on pure asphalt surfaces, composite structures, overlays, or other types of shoulders [1]. Likewise, the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that the cross‑stitching treatment for longitudinal cracks applies only to concrete (rigid) pavement structures and expressly excludes asphalt, composite decks, overlays, or shoulders [2]. Section 525—Concrete Pavement Partial‑Depth Repair further describes that longitudinal joints or cracks appear within the slab of rigid (concrete) pavements and may be repaired by a partial‑depth method provided the repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–435)

In Portland cement concrete pavement, longitudinal cracking may appear either as a longitudinal joint that separates adjacent traffic lanes—or the lane from a concrete shoulder—or as a crack that runs through the slab interior parallel to the direction of travel. The Pennsylvania specifications treat a longitudinal crack as either an actual crack within a concrete slab or as a longitudinal joint that runs between adjacent slabs, locating the distress in the slab interior when it is a crack and at the slab‑to‑slab interface when it is a joint. Cross‑stitching procedures require full‑depth saw cuts along the existing longitudinal joint, placing the repair action at the joint line for joints or within the slab body for cracks. For slip‑form paving, a longitudinal crack is described as appearing inside the concrete slab, i.e., within a traffic lane, and the affected slabs must be removed and replaced, confirming that the distress occurs in the interior of the slab rather than at edges, corners, wheel paths, layer interfaces, or the base/subgrade. Partial‑depth repair provisions also note that longitudinal cracking is addressed when it appears either along a longitudinal joint or as a crack on the interior surface of the slab, again indicating occurrence at the joint line and within the slab body. The plain cement concrete pavement section similarly identifies the distress as a longitudinal crack that appears as a longitudinal joint or as a crack within the slab, reinforcing its location inside the concrete element. In asphalt pavements, longitudinal cracks are located at the longitudinal joints on the pavement surface and are cleaned and sealed as part of joint‑and‑crack maintenance. The ultra‑thin bonded wearing course specifications require sealing any longitudinal cracks that are at least 1/4 inch wide before paving; these cracks are treated together with longitudinal joints on the existing surface, placing them at the pavement surface where joints and cracks intersect the slab. The Pavement Policy Manual requires cleaning and sealing the longitudinal joint between traffic lanes and between the lane and a concrete shoulder, and calls for strengthening longitudinal cracks in slabs to prevent slab migration. Strengthening measures may include cross‑stitching longitudinal joints or cracks across the distress. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 322–345), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–435)

Severity levels and rating

The source classifies longitudinal cracks for partial‑depth repair into two categories under Section 525—Concrete Pavement Partial‑Depth Repair: Type 1 spot repairs are used when a crack (or joint or spall) measures between 15 inches and 6 feet in length, while Type 2 extended‑length repairs apply to cracks longer than 6 feet. These two categories constitute the only severity‑related limits for longitudinal cracking in this specification; no additional rating table is provided. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 426)

Effects on performance

Longitudinal cracks that are not promptly addressed can reduce load transfer, leading to slab migration and faulting that compromise structural capacity, ride quality, safety and durability; consequently cleaning and sealing the longitudinal joint should be performed as part of every CPR project, and cross‑stitching may provide nearly 100 % load transfer while strengthening the crack to maintain aggregate interlock and help preserve service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90)

If a longitudinal crack is left untreated, the joint can develop extensive spalling that progresses from the top half of the slab to beyond mid‑slab depth, eventually causing slab migration, loss of aggregate interlock and faulting of the centerline joint; this separation may also cause lanes or shoulders to pull apart, creating a maintenance problem. The recommended progression of treatment begins with cleaning and sealing per Section 512, moves to partial‑depth spall repair (Section 525) when spalls are limited to the top half, escalates to full‑depth longitudinal joint repair (Section 519) once spalling extends past mid‑slab, and finally may require cross‑stitching (Section 528) to restore near‑100 % load transfer and tie the separating lanes or joints. The 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir is specified for joint sealing, and a 4.75 mm asphalt wearing course material is used to fill badly spalled joints before overlay. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90)

Repairs and treatments

Cross‑stitching is the repair method specified for longitudinal cracks or joints in plain cement concrete pavements. It involves placing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle across the crack, and it is called for whenever there is a need to cross‑stitch such cracks (section 2.1 GENERAL). The technique is intended to provide nearly 100 % load transfer when the longitudinal crack or joint is in reasonably good condition, and it is used to strengthen the crack so that slab migration is prevented and aggregate interlock is maintained; it may also be employed where centerline longitudinal joints are beginning to fault (section 4.3 JOINTS) [1]. The same specification further requires that cross‑stitching may only be performed when the ambient air temperature or pavement surface temperature is at least 40 °F, and it must not be used on transverse cracks (Section 528). After the tie bars are installed, the longitudinal crack or joint is routed to a width of 3/4 inch and a depth of 1 inch, cleaned, and sealed with material filled to between 1/8 and 1/4 inch below the surface, again only when temperature is not below 40 °F (Section 528) [2]. Cleaning and sealing of longitudinal cracks is required whenever there is a need for cleaning and sealing as required by Publication 408, Specifications, Section 590. This treatment addresses the presence of longitudinal cracks, along with transverse joints and random cracks, by removing debris and applying a sealant to the crack surface on existing pavement surfaces (Section 469) [1][2]. In ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, longitudinal cracks that are 1/4‑inch or wider are treated by sealing them with the rubberized sealant referenced in Section 469; the seal must be placed at least 24 hours prior to paving and its over‑band thickness and width must stay within the tolerances set out in Section 469 (Section 489) [2]. A concrete pavement partial‑depth repair may be used on longitudinal cracks when the required repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness. For cracks or joints that are between 15 inches and 6 feet long, a Type 1 spot repair is prescribed; for those longer than 6 feet, a Type 2 extended‑length repair is prescribed, and both treatments address longitudinal cracking as well as transverse joints and interior spalling under the same depth limitation (Section 525) [2]. When such a partial‑depth repair abuts an existing longitudinal joint, the specification requires installing a 1/4‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene filler in that joint and then sealing the longitudinal joint as required by Section 512.3 (Section 525) [2]. If a longitudinal crack occurs within a lane, the specification calls for removing and replacing the entire slab or slabs containing the crack at no additional cost to the Department; this action addresses the distress by full slab replacement (Section 501) [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 322–345), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–435)

Dowel‑bar retrofit may be used on longitudinal cracks that are hairline in width and where aggregate interlock still exists, indicating a very low‑severity crack (source [1] section 4.3 JOINTS). Cross‑stitching is limited to longitudinal cracks or joints that are in reasonably good condition and are just beginning to fault, providing near‑full load transfer for such early‑stage distress (source [1] section 4.3 JOINTS). For cross‑stitching under the specification, it may be applied only to longitudinal cracks or joints that are located within the project limits, have been routed to a width of 3/4 inch and a depth of 1 inch before sealing, and when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are not below 40°F; it is expressly ruled out for transverse cracks or joints and any situation where the temperature falls below that threshold (source [2] section 528.3). A concrete pavement partial‑depth repair may be used on a longitudinal crack when the required repair depth does not exceed one‑half of the slab thickness; cracks whose length is between 15 inches and 6 feet are classified as Type 1, and cracks longer than 6 feet are classified as Type 2, while if the needed repair depth exceeds half the slab thickness the source directs use of full‑depth concrete patches instead (source [2] section 525.1). Section 525.3 permits a partial‑depth repair to address a longitudinal joint only when the repair area directly abuts that joint, in which case the specified treatment is to place 1/4‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene in the joint; no limits on crack severity, width or depth are stated (source [2] section 525.3). The Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course specification permits a treatment for longitudinal cracking only when the cracks are at least 1/4‑inch wide, and those cracks must be sealed with the rubberized sealant called out in Section 469 before applying the UTFCEM or UTWC (source [2] section 489.3). If a longitudinal crack occurs within a lane, Section 501.3(j) prescribes removal and replacement of the concrete slabs containing the crack at no additional cost to the Department, and if the crack has also caused surface damage an approved repair method must be applied at no extra charge (source [2] section 501.3).

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 345), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–435)

Prevention in design and construction

Cross‑stitching is identified as a design measure that addresses longitudinal cracks in plain cement concrete pavements by installing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle across the crack or joint; it is prescribed when such longitudinal cracks or joints are present in PCC surfaces (section 2.1 GENERAL, item k) and also when the crack is in reasonably good condition, where it serves to strengthen the crack, prevent slab migration and maintain aggregate interlock, especially where lanes or shoulders are separating (section 4.3 JOINTS). Section 528.1 of the specifications likewise prescribes cross‑stitching for existing longitudinal cracks or joints in plain cement concrete pavement, describing it as reinforcement that can reduce further propagation. Cleaning and sealing longitudinal joints is required as part of every CPR project to preserve joint integrity and thus limit the development of longitudinal cracking (section 4.3 JOINTS). For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, Section 489.3(g) mandates that at least 24 hours before placement all longitudinal joints and cracks measuring 1/4‑inch or wider shall be sealed with the rubberized sealant prescribed in Section 469, using minimal over‑band thickness and width and not exceeding the tolerances of Section 469; this preparation step is aimed at preventing further deterioration of those longitudinal cracks. Dowel‑bar retrofits are primarily used on roadways that receive heavily channeled loadings, where transverse joints or cracks would benefit from improved load transfer; they are most effective when hairline cracks are observed and aggregate interlock still exists (section 4.3 JOINTS). Placement of tie bars in longitudinal joints is required by Section 501.3(i)2 and 501.3(j); to limit shear cracking one tie bar shall be eliminated in any slab adjacent to a joint where an existing crack exceeds 1/8‑inch, and when paving between October 1 and April 1 one tie bar shall be eliminated in each slab next to dominant joints, with the tied longitudinal contraction joints sawed to prevent random cracking (Section 501.3(i)2). Partial‑depth repairs that intersect an existing longitudinal joint must have the joint filled with preformed cellular polystyrene and sealed in accordance with Section 512.3, actions intended to lessen opening or delamination of the joint; if the repair material can be sawed without damage the joint should be re‑established by sawing through the full depth of the repair, thereby restoring continuity (Section 525.3). [1] Cleaning and sealing of longitudinal joints between traffic lanes or between a lane and a concrete shoulder shall be performed as part of every CPR project, per Specification Section 512 referenced in Section 4.3 JOINTS, to protect the joint from traffic‑induced damage. [1] When extensive spalls appear in the top half of a slab, partial‑depth spall repairs specified in Specification Section 525 shall be applied, addressing surface loss that could otherwise compromise the longitudinal joint. [1] If spalls extend beyond mid‑slab depth, full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs prescribed in Specification Section 519 shall be executed to restore the integrity of deeper damage within the joint. [1] The slip‑form specifications require omission of a tie bar in any slab adjacent to a joint where cracks have opened at least 1/8 inch, a measure intended to limit shear‑type longitudinal cracking (Section 501.3(j)). [2] When paving between October 1 and April 1, the same omission is required for slabs next to dominant joints, also under Section 501.3(j). [2] All tied longitudinal contraction joints must be sawed as described in Section 501.3(i)2 so that random longitudinal cracking is prevented. [2] For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, any longitudinal (or transverse) joint or crack 1/4 inch or wider must be sealed with the rubberized sealant specified in Section 469 at least 24 hours before paving, keeping the over‑band thickness and width to a minimum and not exceeding the tolerances of that section; this preparation is intended to prevent water or debris infiltration into existing longitudinal cracks (Section 489.3). Specification limits and acceptance

Under Section 528—Concrete Pavement Cross‑Stitching, a longitudinal crack must be routed to a width of three‑quarters inch and a depth of one inch; the fill level must lie between one‑eighth inch and one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface; drilling for tie bars may not exceed three‑eighths inch larger in diameter than the tie bar on each side of the crack; and all work is permitted only when either ambient air temperature or pavement surface temperature is at least forty degrees Fahrenheit. In Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, any longitudinal crack that appears within a lane triggers removal and replacement of the affected concrete slabs at no additional cost to the Department, with the presence of a crack serving as the sole judgment criterion; additionally, when assessing tie‑bar performance under Section 501.3(j), an opened crack measuring one‑eighth inch or greater requires elimination of one adjacent tie bar. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses covered by Section 489, any longitudinal crack that is one‑quarter inch wide or wider must be sealed at least twenty‑four hours before paving, the width threshold being the measurement used to determine the sealing requirement, and the sealant application follows the specifications in Section 469. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 345), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–435)

When the limit for a longitudinal crack is reached, the specification requires that the cracked slab(s) be removed and replaced and any surface damage be repaired in an approved method, all at no additional cost to the Department; the document does not prescribe a specific time frame for completing this remedial work. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)
