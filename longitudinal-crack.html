Longitudinal Crack

Part of: Cracking is-subtopic-of
Longitudinal Crack is a topic under Cracking.
longitudinal-crackslongitudinal-cracklongitudinal-cracking
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Longitudinal cracking refers to cracks or joints that run parallel to the lay‑down direction of a rigid concrete slab, typically appearing within traffic lanes or between a lane and its shoulder [1][2]. These distress features are characterized by widths from about one‑quarter to three‑quarters inch, depths near one inch, and lengths ranging roughly fifteen inches to six feet, and they jeopardize pavement performance by permitting slab migration, loss of aggregate interlock, faulting, and lane or shoulder separation that degrade load‑transfer capability and overall structural capacity [1][2]. Because the cracks compromise slab integrity and may be accompanied by surface spalling, treatment is required to restore continuity, re‑establish load transfer, and preserve or extend the pavement’s service life [1][2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Longitudinal cracking refers to cracks or joints that run parallel to the lay‑down direction of a rigid concrete slab, commonly occurring within traffic lanes or between a lane and a concrete shoulder. The distress is characterized by widths as small as one‑quarter inch up to three‑quarters inch, depths of about one inch, and lengths ranging from fifteen inches to six feet. The presence of such cracks threatens pavement performance by allowing slab migration, loss of aggregate interlock, faulting, and separation of lanes or shoulders, which together degrade load‑transfer capability and overall structural capacity. Because the cracks compromise slab integrity and may be accompanied by surface spalling, they represent a condition that must be remedied to restore continuity and stabilize the pavement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 313–338), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–425)

Across the guides, treatment of longitudinal cracking is directed chiefly toward repairing the existing damage so that load transfer and structural continuity are re‑established [1][2]. The repair process begins with a full‑depth saw cut along the entire length of the joint, after which the crack is routed to a width of three‑quarters inch and a depth of one inch; a partial‑depth patch not exceeding half the slab thickness is then placed as a spot or extended‑length repair, or, when the crack compromises an entire slab, removal and replacement of that slab is required [2]. In addition to cleaning and sealing the cracks, cross‑stitching with epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars is prescribed to strengthen the joint and maintain aggregate interlock, thereby preventing slab migration and preserving functionality [1]. These interventions are explicitly intended not only to return the pavement to functional service but also to preserve or lengthen its useful service life when overlay is not planned [1][2]. Consequently, the overarching objective of longitudinal‑crack treatment is to repair damage and restore functionality, with the ancillary benefit of extending service life and mitigating further deterioration of the concrete slab system [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–425)

Distress characterization and causes

Longitudinal cracking is primarily driven by a combination of moisture-related and structural deficiencies that reduce the pavement’s ability to resist traffic stresses. When surface or subsurface drainage is insufficient, water accumulates within the base and can infiltrate the slab, weakening the supporting layers and lowering overall structural capacity. This moisture problem is compounded when obvious failures in the base are not removed and replaced with material capable of both providing adequate strength and facilitating proper drainage. Traffic moving over intermediate pavement layers and cold longitudinal paving joints imposes repeated flexural stresses that accelerate crack initiation, while inadequate shoulder support increases edge stresses that further promote longitudinal crack growth. In concrete pavements, the situation is aggravated when joints cannot effectively transfer load because of faulting, poor load‑transfer mechanisms, or construction errors such as omitted tie bars in contraction joints. These joint deficiencies allow water, chlorides and other incompressible materials to infiltrate the pavement structure, undermining subgrade stability. To arrest progression, it is necessary to restore subgrade support and re‑establish proper load transfer through measures such as dowel bar retrofits, cross‑stitching, full‑depth joint repair, or early resealing of longitudinal joints, thereby preventing further water ingress and slab migration. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90)

Applicability and treatment selection

Cross‑stitching of longitudinal cracks is limited to rigid concrete pavements where the distress is a longitudinal crack or joint, after a full‑depth saw cut along the joint and an approved QC plan with a demonstration have been completed; it may be performed only when ambient air and pavement surface temperatures are at least 40 °F and must not be used on transverse cracks. Traffic may be reopened only after the anchoring material has fully cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 424–425)

Longitudinal joint cleaning and sealing is limited to minor cracks that show little spalling; when spalls are extensive in the upper half of the slab the sealant method must be abandoned in favor of a partial‑depth spall repair [1]. If spalling penetrates beyond the mid‑slab depth, the appropriate action shifts to a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair rather than simple sealing [1]. When the crack is associated with load‑transfer problems or slab migration, the guides recommend restoring transfer through cross‑stitching or dowel‑bar retrofits instead of routine sealant work [1]. Moreover, if an unbonded Portland cement concrete or asphalt overlay is planned, sealing of longitudinal joints is not advised and another preservation technique must be selected [1]. Cross‑stitching itself is only permissible when the crack truly follows the lay‑down direction, ambient air and surface temperatures are at least 40 °F, and full‑depth drilling can be performed without breaking through the slab bottom; failure of any of these conditions makes cross‑stitching unsuitable [2]. If the feature is actually a transverse joint or crack, if temperature falls below 40 °F, or if drill holes would penetrate the slab, a different maintenance or rehabilitation method must be used [2]. When a longitudinal crack occurs within an active travel lane, the specifications prohibit patching or minor preservation and require removal and replacement of the affected slabs [2]. Partial‑depth repairs are constrained to a maximum depth of one‑half the slab thickness; when the required repair depth exceeds this limit, partial‑depth methods become inappropriate and a full‑depth concrete patch or corner repair is prescribed [2]. The choice between spot (Type 1) and extended (Type 2) partial‑depth repairs depends on crack length—15 inches to 6 feet for spot repairs and greater than 6 feet for extended repairs—but both remain limited by the half‑thickness depth rule [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–425)

Condition assessment and timing

The determination of a required longitudinal‑crack repair begins with a visual inspection for any crack running parallel to the pavement centerline; when such a crack is observed, the surface at that longitudinal joint is measured using a 12‑foot straightedge placed across and perpendicular to the joint, and if the deviation exceeds the allowable one‑quarter‑inch tolerance the slab is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–367)

Longitudinal cracks should be addressed when they are still in reasonably good condition but have begun to fault, such as early hairline openings that cause slab migration, lane or shoulder separation, or loss of aggregate interlock. The guides also require treatment once measurable thresholds are reached, specifically when the crack opening widens to at least one‑quarter inch. At that point the crack must be sealed with a rubberized sealant no less than twenty‑four hours before any paving activity. If the crack depth does not exceed half of the slab thickness, a partial‑depth repair is prescribed, with short cracks between fifteen inches and six feet receiving a Type 1 spot repair and longer cracks over six feet receiving a Type 2 extended repair. When the crack depth exceeds half the slab thickness, a full‑depth concrete patch is required. For cracks that appear within a traffic lane, the affected slabs are to be removed and replaced, and any associated surface damage must be repaired by an approved method before the pavement can be placed back in service. Cross‑stitching may be employed as a repair technique intended to restore near‑full load transfer across a longitudinal crack that is still reasonably sound, thereby preventing further slab migration and maintaining aggregate interlock. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 337–338), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–416)

Materials and repair details

Before any repair the longitudinal fissure is thoroughly cleaned of debris, loose material and any contaminant such as grease, oil or fuel. Full‑depth cracks that exhibit minimal spalling are sealed with a rubberized joint‑sealing material in accordance with Publication 408 Section 590, while partial‑depth cracks are treated according to Publication 408 Section 1091. When the crack threatens load transfer, the guides prescribe a cross‑stitching technique that begins with a full‑depth saw cut along the existing joint or crack and proceeds by routing the fissure to a width of three‑quarters of an inch and a depth of one inch. After routing, holes are drilled on each side of the crack at the specified angle; each hole may be no larger than three‑eighths of an inch beyond the diameter of the epoxy‑coated deformed tie bar that will be placed. The tie bars, which are epoxy‑coated and deformed for mechanical interlock, are inserted into the drilled holes, rotated to purge air, and left with at least one inch of epoxy flush with the pavement surface before the slots are backfilled with a compatible concrete patching material. The resulting diagonal reinforcement configuration restores structural continuity and is intended to achieve near‑100 % load transfer across the repaired longitudinal crack. If ambient air temperature or pavement surface temperature is below 40 °F, cross‑stitching work shall not be performed, and sealing of the crack must wait until temperatures exceed that threshold and curing follows the manufacturer’s recommendations. In cases where the longitudinal crack lies within a traffic lane and compromises slab integrity, the affected slab may be removed and replaced; the new concrete is jointed to the existing pavement and any dowels intersecting the repair are field‑coated with bond‑breaker lubricant as required. An alternative repair method employs dowel assemblies in which a power‑driven self‑propelled saw creates parallel slots for each dowel bar, after which a quarter‑inch thick preformed cellular polystyrene or other expansion joint material is cut to the slot dimensions and placed in the joint. Type B coated dowels receive bond‑breaker lubricant, are positioned with no more than a quarter‑inch tilt and within one inch longitudinally and horizontally, and are capped with 1½‑inch non‑metallic expansion caps that maintain a quarter‑inch clearance from the dowel end; plastic or non‑metallic chairs provide at least a half‑inch clearance from all slot surfaces. The slots are then filled with compatible concrete patching material, vibrated for consolidation, finished to match the existing surface and sealed as required. Partial‑depth repairs may be used when repair depth does not exceed one‑half of the slab thickness; spot repairs cover cracks from fifteen inches up to six feet in length, while extended‑length repairs address cracks longer than six feet, both using a concrete mix compatible with the existing pavement. When a partial‑depth repair abuts an existing longitudinal joint, a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene is installed so that it extends at least half an inch below the bottom of the repair; the repair area is laid out as a rectangle extending approximately three inches beyond the cracked edges with minimum dimensions of fifteen inches in length, ten inches in width and two inches in depth. Some specifications allow covering the crack with a heavy‑duty membrane placed at the location identified by the representative, although no further dimensional details are provided for that method. Any longitudinal crack one‑quarter inch wide or larger must be sealed at least twenty‑four hours before paving using the rubberized sealant prescribed in Section 469; the sealant over‑band thickness and width are kept to a minimum and must not exceed the tolerances set out in that section, and any thermoplastic or tape markings thicker than two‑tenths of an inch are removed prior to sealant application. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 313–338), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–425)

The existing pavement must be stable—exhibiting no movement when compacted—and its surface should be level, sealed and patched prior to applying an overlay or repair layer. Adequate drainage at both the surface and within underlying layers is essential to keep water from compromising the repaired area, and any base sections showing obvious failure must be removed and replaced with material that restores sufficient structural capacity while facilitating drainage. The repair materials must be compatible with the cementitious substrate; epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars are selected because they bond well to concrete and resist corrosion, while rubberized joint sealants are used for their elasticity that allows movement without loss of adhesion. All crack faces and adjacent pavement surfaces must be free of moisture, oil, grease, fuel, dust or other foreign substances; they are to be swept clean, dried, and sealed with the specified rubberized sealant at least 24 hours before paving. Reinforcement steel, tie bars and dowel bars must be clean and rust‑free so that a sound bond can develop between steel and concrete, and holes drilled for tie bars may not exceed three‑eighths of an inch larger than the bar diameter; the drilled holes must remain dry and receive at least one inch of epoxy coverage over the bar at the surface. When a longitudinal crack widens to one‑eighth inch or more adjacent to a joint, the affected tie bar is removed to prevent shear cracking, and any slab containing a visible longitudinal crack may be required to be replaced. Hairline cracks that still retain aggregate interlock, or spalling confined to the top half of a slab, can be addressed with partial‑depth sealing or cross‑stitching techniques that aim for nearly 100 % load transfer when the surrounding concrete is in reasonably good condition. Deeper spalls or faulted joints require full‑depth joint repair and restoration of load‑transfer mechanisms through dowel bars or comparable measures, with dowel bars sized and positioned to provide adequate pull‑out resistance. Dowel bars coated with Type B require application of a bond‑breaker lubricant to at least half their length at least one hour before concrete placement, whereas Type A bars need no lubricant; the dowels are seated on non‑metallic chairs and capped with 1½‑inch expansion caps that leave a quarter‑inch clearance from the dowel tip to the cap bottom. The repair slot is cut at mid‑depth of the slab, parallel to the centerline, cleaned promptly before patch material placement, and any damage caused by excessive jackhammer force mandates removal and replacement of the affected concrete. When a partial‑depth repair abuts an existing longitudinal joint, a ¼‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene insert is installed in the joint and extended at least half an inch below the bottom of the new concrete to ensure continuity before the joint is sealed according to specified guidelines. Ambient temperature requirements must be observed for each step; epoxy injection cannot be performed when either air or surface temperature falls below 40 °F, and asphalt placement requires dry conditions and an air or surface temperature of at least 50 °F. Asphalt overlay placement is performed with a paver equipped with a built‑in spray bar so that the sealant and finishing material are applied within five seconds of each other, guaranteeing proper bonding. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 337–338), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–425)

Treatment procedures

Before any work begins a quality‑control plan prepared in accordance with the applicable specifications must be submitted for review at least fourteen days prior to starting operations [2]. Work may only proceed when ambient air and pavement temperatures are above forty degrees Fahrenheit, ensuring suitable conditions for material placement [2]. All traffic markings thicker than two‑tenths of an inch are removed, any grease, oil or fuel contamination is thoroughly cleaned, and the surface is swept to eliminate loose particles before sealant or concrete application [2]. Any longitudinal joint or crack that is a quarter‑inch wide or larger must be sealed with the prescribed rubberized joint sealing material at least twenty‑four hours prior to paving, taking care not to exceed the allowable over‑band thickness and width tolerances [2][1]. The joint is first cleaned and any spalling removed; when spalls are limited to the upper half of the slab a partial‑depth repair is performed, while deeper damage that extends beyond mid‑slab depth requires a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair [1]. If the crack occurs within a lane and warrants complete slab replacement, the affected slab is removed and replaced using standard concrete paving procedures including line‑and‑grade layout, placement of fresh concrete with reinforcement and tie bars, sawing of contraction joints, flushing of saw cuts, finishing, texturing and curing as specified for new pavement [2]. For repairs that do not require full slab replacement a full‑depth saw cut is made along the entire length of the existing longitudinal joint, or alternatively two parallel slots are cut at mid‑depth centered over the crack using a power‑driven self‑propelled saw [2]. The concrete between the cuts is removed with a jackhammer weighing no more than thirty pounds and the exposed area is backed to create a level bottom, after which all dust and loose material are cleaned from the slots within twenty‑four hours before concrete placement [2]. Holes are then drilled on each side of the crack, sized no larger than three‑eighths of an inch beyond the tie‑bar diameter and positioned at the required angle, offset distance and spacing, and the holes are cleaned of all dust prior to bonding material injection [2]. Epoxy bonding compound is injected into each hole using a caulking gun or approved method, leaving space for the tie bar, after which epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars are installed at an angle across the crack (cross‑stitching) and the slots are filled with concrete patching material [1][2]. When dowels are used instead of tie bars, Type B dowels receive a thorough coat of bond‑breaker lubricant at least one hour before concrete placement, are seated on non‑metallic chairs with required clearances, and epoxy bonding compound is applied around them [2]. The filled slots or cut area are vibrated to consolidate the patching material around the dowel or tie‑bar assembly, then finished and textured to match the existing surface profile, and standard curing provisions for new pavement are followed [2][1]. After the concrete has cured, the joint is sawed and a sealant is placed just below the pavement surface without spilling onto the deck, and traffic may not be reopened until the epoxy or other anchoring material has achieved its specified cure time [2]. Throughout the process the over‑band thickness and width of the rubberized sealant are monitored to remain within the tolerances set by the specifications, and all steps are performed in accordance with the relevant sections referenced in the guides [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 337–338), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–425)

Implementation begins with a quality‑control plan that records the required equipment, work sequence, and permissible environmental limits [2]. At least one day before any paving activity all longitudinal cracks that are a quarter inch or wider must be sealed with the specified rubberized sealant and the surface cleared of loose material [2]. The repair sequence starts with a full‑depth saw cut made along the existing joint, followed by two parallel cuts created with a power‑driven self‑propelled saw whose blades run parallel to the roadway centreline [1][2]. The parallel slots may be opened using a jackhammer that does not exceed thirty pounds in weight, and if pavement damage occurs the tool must be reduced to fifteen pounds; alternatively a chipping hammer no heavier than thirty‑five pounds or a milling machine equipped with a V, flat, or rounded head can be employed for concrete removal [2]. All cut and drilled surfaces are required to be cleaned within twenty‑four hours, and a final air‑blast cleaning using an oil‑ and moisture‑free compressed‑air stream at a minimum of one hundred pounds per square inch must be completed no more than thirty minutes before the placement of concrete or epoxy material [2]. At least one hour prior to placing the repair material a bond‑breaker lubricant is applied to the prepared surfaces [2]. Epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars or dowels are positioned across the crack at the proper angle, and when the repair abuts an existing longitudinal joint a quarter‑inch pre‑formed cellular polystyrene insert extending below the repair bottom is installed [2]. The anchoring material—whether concrete patching mix or epoxy—is placed using volumetric equipment or a power‑driven drum/paddle mixer sized for the batch, and the mixture is allowed to cure fully before traffic is restored in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations [2][1]. Traffic may not be reopened until the anchoring material has achieved full cure as specified by the manufacturer, providing an implicit traffic restriction during the curing period [2][1]. For asphalt overlays the work requires an asphalt paver equipped with a built‑in spray bar that applies the UTFCEM and UTWC within five seconds of each other, and placement is prohibited when surfaces are wet or when ambient or surface temperatures fall below fifty degrees Fahrenheit; polymer‑modified mixes containing PG 64S‑22 may not be placed from November 1 to March 31 and those with PG 64E‑22 from October 15 to March 31 unless a written exception is granted [2]. No work involving epoxy injection or concrete placement should be performed when either the ambient air temperature or the pavement surface temperature is below forty degrees Fahrenheit, establishing a lower temperature limit for these operations [2].

Quality and workmanship

Inspectors must first confirm that ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at least 40 °F before any work is begun, because lower temperatures can compromise material performance. The joint preparation requires a full‑depth saw cut that is precisely three‑quarters of an inch wide and one inch deep, and drill holes placed on each side of the crack must not exceed three‑eighths of an inch larger than the tie‑bar diameter while being positioned at the prescribed angle and spacing. All joint faces and surrounding areas are required to be thoroughly cleaned and dried so that the specified rubberized joint‑sealing material can be applied, and a sealant reservoir of 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch must be formed in accordance with the design specifications. The reinforcement to be embedded in the crack must be free of rust, dirt, oil, grease or any other contaminant that could impair bond, and a representative sample of typically fifteen tie bars taken from the first day’s placement is tested for pull‑out resistance after the concrete has attained a compressive strength of 3,500 psi, with results required to meet the prescribed values. During installation each epoxy‑coated tie bar is rotated several times to purge air and must receive at least one inch of epoxy coverage on its surface; when the crack is in reasonably good condition cross‑stitching is inspected for proper angle placement and near‑complete load transfer, confirming that aggregate interlock and slab migration are prevented. After the epoxy has cured, sealant is packed to a level that leaves the finished joint surface between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the surrounding pavement, and the straightedge test is performed on all longitudinal joints, including the junction with the shoulder, to verify that deviation does not exceed the tolerance limits set in the specifications. The extent of any spalling is measured so that repairs can be classified as partial‑depth (top half of slab) or full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs according to the depth of the defect, and the completed slab is examined for any new cracks; if a crack appears within a lane the affected slabs are removed and replaced at no additional cost. Traffic may be reopened only after the cured material has satisfied the required cure time indicated by the manufacturer. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–425)

Acceptance of a longitudinal crack is judged primarily by its visual condition; a hairline opening in which the original aggregate interlock remains intact is considered satisfactory, whereas any visible spalling triggers repair actions [1]. When spalling is confined to the upper half of the slab, a partial‑depth spall repair is required, while spalls extending beyond the mid‑slab depth mandate a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair [1]. Repairs may only be initiated if both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at least forty degrees Fahrenheit [2]. The preparatory drilling for tie‑bars must produce holes no larger than three‑eighths of an inch beyond the tie‑bar diameter, placed at the angles, offsets and spacings shown on drawing RC‑26M and without penetrating the slab bottom [2]. After epoxy injection the tie bar is required to be covered with a one‑inch layer of material at the surface [2]. The crack must be routed to a width of three‑quarters of an inch and a depth of one inch before sealant placement [2]. Sealant is placed so that its top lies between one‑eighth and one‑quarter of an inch below the pavement surface, with no excess material spilling onto adjacent areas, and traffic may not resume until the epoxy has fully cured per the manufacturer’s specifications [2]. Only joints or cracks that are a quarter‑inch wide or greater require sealing; narrower openings are acceptable as‑is [2]. Sealing must employ the rubberized sealant prescribed in Section 469 while keeping the over‑band thickness and width within the tolerances set out in that section [2]. For repaired cracks the depth of the patch may not exceed half the slab thickness [2]. Cracks up to six feet long qualify for a Type 1 spot repair, whereas any crack longer than six feet must be treated with a Type 2 extended‑length repair [2]. The acceptance policy tolerates no longitudinal cracking in serviceable pavement; if a crack appears within a lane the entire affected slab must be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department [2].

Performance and service life

Longitudinal cracks compromise the pavement’s load‑transfer ability and can allow slabs to migrate, which degrades surface condition, reduces functional performance and shortens the structure’s useful life [1]. The guides agree that timely treatment is required to restore condition and extend service life; routine cleaning and sealing of the longitudinal joint restores a watertight seal that limits water and chloride ingress, thereby preserving durability for less severe cracking [1]. When cracks are more pronounced, cross‑stitching with epoxy‑coated tie bars is employed to re‑establish aggregate interlock and prevent slab movement, effectively extending the pavement’s service life [1]. In addition, the specifications treat a longitudinal crack that appears within a lane as a defect that must be corrected by removing and replacing the affected slab(s) at no extra charge; this remedial action restores the pavement’s structural condition, functional performance and durability, returning the surface to the service life originally intended for new concrete pavement [2]. Thus, across sources the treatment of longitudinal cracking—whether through sealing, cross‑stitching or full slab replacement—directly improves condition, functionality, durability and expected service life, with the choice of method depending on crack severity.

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–367)

Longitudinal cracks often lead to spalling that may be confined to the upper half of a slab or extend beyond mid‑depth, producing faulting, slab migration, loss of aggregate interlock and lane or shoulder separation. The development of these distresses is accelerated when sealing is inadequate, tie bars are missing, subgrade conditions are unstable, or drainage is poor, allowing water and chlorides to infiltrate the joint. Treatment failures commonly arise during repair work, especially when surface spalling occurs while drilling or when drilled holes unintentionally penetrate through the slab bottom, which then requires removal of the affected area and a full‑depth patch. Inadequate sealing also recurs if sealant is placed while ambient or pavement temperature is below 40 °F or if crack faces are not thoroughly cleaned and dried before epoxy injection. These failures are driven primarily by low temperatures, improper drilling technique—including incorrect angle, oversized holes, or damage to the slab—and insufficient surface preparation. When cracks exceed one‑eighth inch in width, shear‑cracking concerns arise, prompting specifications to eliminate tie bars adjacent to such joints and, if a crack occurs within a lane, to replace the entire slab at no extra cost. Recommended remedial actions include cleaning and resealing the joint, applying partial‑depth repairs for shallow spalls and full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs for deeper damage, and employing cross‑stitching with epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars to restore near‑full load transfer and prevent further migration. Proper joint layout also influences crack development; longitudinal joints should be placed parallel to and within twelve inches of final pavement markings, and tie bars should be avoided near contraction joints. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–425)

Monitoring and follow-up

When a longitudinal crack or joint first shows any distress—such as spalling, faulting, or loss of aggregate interlock—the pavement should be resealed even if overall serviceability still appears acceptable. If the spalling is extensive in the upper half of the slab, a partial‑depth spall repair is required; when spalls penetrate beyond the mid‑slab depth, a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair is called for. Continued faulting or evidence that slabs are beginning to migrate signals the need for more robust treatment such as cross‑stitching or dowel bar retrofits to restore load transfer and prevent further deterioration. The appearance of a longitudinal crack within a lane is treated as a defect that must be repaired or replaced, and cracks that have opened to at least one‑eighth inch trigger the removal of adjacent tie bars and may require slab replacement. Surface depressions between adjacent lanes exceeding three‑sixteenths of an inch are likewise considered defective and call for corrective action. [1][2]
