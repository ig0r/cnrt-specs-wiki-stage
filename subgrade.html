Subgrade

Part of: Pavement Structure is-subtopic-of
Subgrade is a topic under Pavement Structure.
subgradesubgrades
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-30

The subgrade is the compacted, uniformly graded earth foundation that forms the top‑of‑subgrade elevation beneath all pavement structures, intended to eliminate the inadequate bearing capacity and non‑uniform support of native soils that would otherwise cause excessive settlement, differential heave, cracking or rutting[1][2]. Its performance is quantified by stiffness parameters—resilient modulus (typically 5–10) or California Bearing Ratio—required to meet seasonal limits (spring values 10‑20 % lower than summer, frozen conditions roughly twice as stiff) and by density criteria (100 % of maximum dry density within three feet of the design grade, at least 97 % beyond), ensuring that traffic loads are transferred uniformly without permanent deformation[1][2]. Continuous positive drainage is achieved through a minimum .25‑in/ft slope, subgrade drains and daily sealing with a static steel‑wheel roller, while surface tolerances of half an inch and permanent deflection limits of 0.5 in for existing cuts or 1 in for raised embankments govern the allowable settlement[2][1].

PennDOT publications covering “Subgrade” also include the following subtopics: AASHTO Soil Classification, California Bearing Ratio, Resilient Modulus, Rock Capping, Subgrade Evaluation, Subgrade Stabilization, and Undercutting.

Subgrade Evaluation determines the foundation’s stiffness through Resilient Modulus or California Bearing Ratio tests, which are interpreted using AASHTO Soil Classification to identify moisture sensitivity and frost-heave potential. [1] When these indicators reveal insufficient bearing capacity, Rock Capping provides a strengthened platform over weak soils while Subgrade Stabilization chemically or mechanically improves in-place material to meet density requirements. [1][2] Undercutting serves as the final remedial excavation for severely deficient layers, removing unstable soil to replace it with compacted aggregate and geotextile separators that ensure uniform load transfer. [1][2]

Purpose and design objectives

The subgrade is provided to address the fundamental engineering problem of inadequate bearing capacity and non‑uniform support in native soils that would otherwise cause excessive settlement, differential heave, cracking, rutting and other distress in overlying pavement structures. [1][2] By establishing a uniformly graded, well‑compacted and level earth foundation at the required top‑of‑subgrade elevation, the subgrade eliminates uneven or improperly positioned roadbeds that could lead to localized overloading of pavement layers. [2] The design requires the subgrade stiffness, expressed through resilient modulus (Mr) or California Bearing Ratio (CBR), to fall within typical ranges of 5–10 and to meet seasonal limits, ensuring that traffic loads are reliably transferred to the ground without loss of support. [1] Seasonal moisture conditions are accounted for by recognizing that spring moduli may be 10 %–20 % lower than summer values and that frozen subgrades can be roughly twice as stiff, which drives required base elastic modulus values of 15 000 psi for spring‑thaw, 30 000 psi for normal (summer/fall) and 50 000 psi for frozen conditions. [1] To prevent excessive deformation, the ratio of subbase modulus to soil resilient modulus is limited to four, and when in‑situ Mr or CBR fall outside acceptable limits, corrective actions such as undercutting, backfilling, stabilization, addition of a prepared subbase, or installation of subgrade drains are mandated. [1] The subgrade must be compacted to specified densities and proof‑rolled to provide a uniform firmness that limits permanent deformation and rutting to less than one‑half inch for existing or cut sections and less than one inch for raised embankments. [2] Continuous positive drainage is achieved by maintaining a minimum slope of one‑quarter inch per foot on trench bottoms, extending subgrade drains sufficiently, and sealing the surface each day with a static steel‑wheel roller to prevent water infiltration and saturation that would otherwise reduce bearing capacity. [2][1] Unsuitable soils are removed, replaced, or stabilized through compaction, undercutting, geotextile reinforcement, scarification to at least six inches depth, or other measures to ensure the subgrade remains dry, free of deleterious materials, foreign debris, frost and ponded water before placement of base courses or rigid concrete pavement. [2][1] By satisfying these stiffness, density, elevation, and drainage criteria, the subgrade provides a stable foundation that distributes loads uniformly, limits deflection, prevents differential settlement and frost heave, and thereby extends the service life of the overlying pavement system.

The subgrade must provide reliable load‑supporting capacity and stiffness, transferring traffic loads to the foundation without excessive deflection and thereby maintaining ride quality and safety. To satisfy this functional objective the guides require that the subgrade meet minimum roadbed resilient modulus values and California Bearing Ratio (CBR) values; CBR results above ten must be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and no value greater than twenty may be used in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation. Where isolated low‑CBR zones are identified, they must be improved so that subgrade performance aligns with overall project conditions. In addition, the subgrade functions as a uniformly graded, permeable foundation that reliably bears the imposed loads while providing continuous positive drainage. It shall be formed at the established top‑of‑subgrade elevation and compacted to 100 % of maximum dry density for material within three feet of the design grade and to at least 97 % for more distant embankment foundations, with moisture contents maintained between minus three percent and optimum. Elevations and slopes must be verified at transverse intervals not exceeding twenty‑five feet using stringlines or GPS/laser control, and any surface irregularities greater than one‑half inch must be corrected by loosening, adding or removing material and re‑rolling; acceptance testing rejects the subgrade if more than ten percent of checks exceed this tolerance. Permanent deflection or rutting may not exceed one‑half inch in existing cuts and may not exceed one inch in raised embankments, and when these limits are exceeded stabilization measures such as undercutting, geotextile placement, backfilling, rock capping, soil stabilization or geogrids are required. Durability is ensured by keeping the subgrade dry; effective drainage must be incorporated before construction so that moisture does not degrade material integrity or reduce structural capacity, and all wet locations must be identified and remedied. Infiltration rates in test sections shall average above two hundred inches per hour, and any zones where erosion has deposited fines or caused ponding are to be scarified at least six inches deep to restore permeability. The subgrade surface is sealed each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode to promote drainage and limit water infiltration, and daily proof rolling provides a uniform firm condition. Frost‑susceptible soils must be eliminated or treated with rock capping, stabilization, geogrids, or other measures to avoid differential heaving during freeze‑thaw cycles, and the subgrade must remain free of standing water, frost or frozen conditions prior to placement of concrete or RCC. Safety considerations prohibit heavy equipment such as concrete mixers or haul trucks from traversing the prepared area, and no vehicle traffic is allowed on exposed geosynthetics until a minimum of six inches of loose backfill has been placed; the subgrade must also be free of foreign material before pavement placement. When laboratory CBR results appear uncertain or unusually high or low, additional field testing such as falling weight deflectometer or dynamic cone penetrometer is required to validate the modulus before design use, thereby preventing unexpected settlement or failure. Together these functional, structural, durability, safety and serviceability requirements ensure that the subgrade retains its shape over time, supports the pavement system, resists erosion and freeze‑related damage, and provides a stable platform for long‑term roadway performance. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

The subgrade design approach is required for any pavement reconstruction effort, including rubblized projects, and it applies to both flexible and rigid pavement designs [1]. For flexible pavements the resilient modulus of the soil characterizes the roadbed, while for rigid pavements this modulus must be converted to an effective modulus of subgrade reaction; laboratory determination of the resilient modulus or CBR testing may be used for interstate or expressway facilities, with a geotechnical engineer consulted if values fall outside typical ranges [1]. Subgrade evaluation is especially appropriate for full‑depth asphalt sections, asphalt overlays placed on thin concrete slabs less than eight inches thick, pre‑1945 concrete pavements, concrete suffering severe alkali‑silica reaction, and AC overlays of fractured PCC, as well as the rare case where loads are applied directly to subgrade or subbase [1]. For collector and local roads the resilient modulus may be estimated from soil surveys, but higher‑classification projects must rely on laboratory or field test results such as CBR, falling weight deflectometer, or dynamic cone penetrometer [1]. When existing subgrade CBR is around five, strengthening with cement, lime, or rock capping becomes cost‑effective if the pavement quantity exceeds roughly 25 000 square yards and the traffic pattern group is 1 to 4 [1]. In frost‑susceptible areas the subgrade may be removed and replaced with non‑frost‑susceptible material or a stronger base course while still meeting design assumptions [1]. Rigid‑pavement structures such as jointed plain concrete pavement or roller‑compacted concrete require a uniform and stable subgrade before placing the surface layer, and interstate and expressway rigid designs typically incorporate an asphalt or cement‑treated permeable base over a 2A subbase; the effective k‑value is adjusted for subbase type, thickness, loss‑of‑support factors, depth to bedrock, and seasonal conditions—spring thaw (15 000 psi), normal summer/fall (30 000 psi), and frozen winter (50 000 psi) [1]. The subgrade preparation approach is also applicable to flexible pavement systems that incorporate pervious asphalt and to rigid concrete (RCC) pavements, as well as any project in which the roadbed elevation must be established through fill placement or excavation [2]. For pervious‑asphalt installations the existing soil is excavated to an undisturbed condition and left as permeable as possible; where fines have accumulated or ponding has occurred the material is removed and the underlying soil scarified at least six inches with light equipment before any geotextile or stone bed is placed, and compaction of the subgrade is avoided until those layers are installed [2]. The resulting base must achieve an infiltration capacity greater than 200 in/hr as verified by test sections [2]. For RCC pavements the subgrade (or subbase) must be prepared in accordance with project specifications, kept clean of foreign material, free of standing water and frost, uniformly moistened, and never allowed to be frozen at the time of concrete placement [2]. In all cases the subgrade serves as the foundation for the subsequent pavement layers, whether the work involves adding fill or removing material to set the top‑of‑subgrade elevation [2].

When the native subgrade consists of weak clays or silts that cannot support construction equipment, an overlay may initially appear attractive because it shields the deficient soil from traffic loads. However, if providing the overlay would require extensive shoulder widening, slope changes, guide‑rail relocation, or vertical adjustments at structures—changes that significantly increase overall project cost—the overlay ceases to be the most economical choice and another design strategy must be pursued. Likewise, when the existing pavement is severely deteriorated or grade restrictions prevent raising the surface with an overlay, designers should abandon reliance on the existing subgrade and opt for complete removal of the pavement down to a stable layer, followed by backfilling, stabilization, or full replacement; this approach yields a more uniform foundation while minimizing grade adjustments. In situations where distress is primarily surface‑related rather than due to inadequate support layers, improving the subgrade through full‑depth reclamation (FDR) is not preferred; instead, alternative rehabilitation methods should be selected. Excess moisture in the subgrade further discourages reliance on subgrade improvement, requiring effective drainage before any FDR can be considered, and the practicality of FDR diminishes when the existing pavement is too thin or the available subgrade material insufficient to form a reclaimed layer. Geotechnical criteria also dictate alternative approaches: CBR values exceeding ten must be reviewed and approved by the District and Chief Geotechnical Engineers before a subgrade‑based design proceeds, while CBR values below five trigger requirements for significant undercutting, stabilization, or replacement, indicating that another pavement design is preferable. If reliable CBR data cannot be obtained—often due to high fines content, elevated Atterberg limits, or a high Group Index—designers are directed to consult the Highway Design and Technology Section for viable options. Isolated low‑CBR zones or widespread low values likewise deem the native subgrade inadequate, necessitating targeted improvement or replacement; when large variations in soil type and CBR exist across an alignment, it may be appropriate to divide the project into limited subsections each with its own design. For projects of at least 25,000 square yards on Traffic Pattern Groups 1 to 4, a cost‑benefit analysis of strengthening techniques such as cement or lime stabilization, rock capping (modeled with CBR = 20), or geogrid/geotextile reinforcement is required when CBR falls below five. Finally, frost‑susceptible soils that could cause differential heaving demand either a stronger base course or removal and replacement with non‑frost‑susceptible material, as reliance on the subgrade alone would not ensure performance. Design inputs and requirements

The subgrade design must be based on a comprehensive set of site‑specific data, beginning with an estimate of future traffic loading to define the expected stresses, and a recent pavement condition survey that documents existing distress and drainage problems. Geotechnical information is obtained through in‑situ testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer or lightweight deflectometer to quantify subgrade stiffness, while material characteristics are captured by systematic sampling of the existing layers for laboratory evaluation. Environmental considerations focus on identifying wet subgrade areas and ensuring proper drainage to prevent moisture‑induced loss of strength. Together these traffic, existing‑condition, material, geotechnical and environmental inputs provide the basis for determining appropriate layer thicknesses and rehabilitation strategies. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126)

The subgrade that underlies any pavement on PennDOT right‑of‑way must be designed and constructed in accordance with a suite of departmental publications, specifications, test methods, and performance constraints that together define the governing standards for the element. Drainage provisions are set out in Publication 72M, which requires protection of drainage outlets located in mowable areas by headwalls, while Publication 13M prescribes the sizing and spacing of pavement‑base drains; lateral underdrains are encouraged in wet zones, continuous edge drains are mandatory on interstate facilities, and additional subgrade drains must be installed wherever water damage or sagging is observed [1]. Geotechnical evaluation follows the Geotechnical Engineering Manual (Publication 293) and requires consultation with the District Geotechnical Engineer to obtain a resilient modulus for the roadbed soil derived from AASHTO laboratory tests that include moisture‑density, unconfined compressive strength, soil classification, resilient modulus, and soaked CBR testing; for interstate and expressway projects a laboratory CBR may be used in place of a direct modulus measurement provided it does not exceed twenty, and any CBR greater than ten must be recommended by the District Geotechnical Engineer and approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer [1]. Subgrade soil evaluation is further defined in Publication 408 Section 2.2, which establishes acceptable soil type and thickness, while Specification 203 limits excavation to Class 1 quantities and Specification 316 requires density acceptance sampling and testing; the subgrade and any accompanying subbase are treated as separate construction items that must be capable of supporting the compaction of roller‑compacted concrete or RCC layers [1]. Rigid pavement designs on interstates and expressways must incorporate an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base over a 2A subbase, with open‑graded subbase prohibited, and effective seasonal elastic moduli are applied as 15 000 psi for spring thaw, 30 000 psi for normal conditions, and 50 000 psi for frozen conditions, while the ratio of subbase modulus to roadbed soil resilient modulus may not exceed four [1]. Performance thresholds require that, for projects other than collectors or local roads, the roadbed soil resilient modulus be based on laboratory or in‑situ testing and that pavement plans specify a minimum acceptable modulus for all borrow material; frost‑susceptible soils must be treated according to frost‑design guidance and any subgrade improvement such as stabilization, rock capping, geogrids, or geotextiles must comply with Publication 408 [1]. Prior to rehabilitation the in‑situ stiffness of the existing subgrade is evaluated using Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, optionally supplemented by dynamic cone penetrometer or lightweight deflectometer measurements, and sample collection follows Appendix J procedures [1]. The subgrade must be formed to the established top‑of‑subgrade elevation and compacted in accordance with Section 206.3(b), then proof‑rolled as required by Section 206.3(a) to achieve a uniform, firm surface; daily sealing of all constructed or disturbed surfaces is performed with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode to promote positive drainage and prevent water infiltration, and the surface is re‑sealed at the end of each workday [2]. Grade checks are made at intervals not exceeding 25 feet (or up to 500 feet when GPS/laser control is used), and any irregularities greater than one‑half inch are corrected before work proceeds; acceptance testing is conducted at random points every five hundred feet, and if more than ten percent of those tests exceed the tolerance, additional staking and stringline verification is required [2]. Compaction density requirements are explicit: material must reach at least ninety‑seven percent of maximum dry density for embankment foundations or existing subgrades deeper than three feet below the proposed elevation, and a full one hundred percent of maximum dry density for structure foundations, subgrades, or material within three feet of the proposed elevation, with moisture content no more than three percent below optimum [2]. Permanent deflection limits are set at half an inch for existing subgrade and one inch for raised embankments; exceeding these limits triggers stabilization measures [2]. Rolling equipment must meet defined criteria—pneumatic‑tire rollers capable of varying loads between twenty‑six and twenty‑eight tons, sheepsfoot or pad rollers with a minimum contact pressure of 160 psi, smooth steel‑drum vibratory rollers delivering at least fifty thousand pounds dynamic force, or loaded triaxle dump trucks of seventy‑two thousand pounds GVW—and all rolling is performed under the Representative’s supervision and kept at least ten feet from bridge abutments [2]. Excavation of the subgrade is required to an undisturbed condition without compaction so that permeability is retained; any fines or ponded water resulting from erosion are removed with light equipment, and the underlying soil is scarified a minimum of six inches deep using a spring‑tooth rake or equivalent, while heavy machinery such as concrete mixers or haul trucks is prohibited from traveling over the installation area and no compaction of the existing subgrade is allowed before placement of the geotextile separator and stone bed [2]. Damaged or unintentionally compacted zones must be repaired to the Representative’s satisfaction, with notification for final inspection prior to advancing to subsequent pavement layers; when a replacement area is required, the perimeter is marked, saw‑cut or milled, all material within that cut is removed to the indicated depth, and the existing subgrade or subbase is then compacted, with re‑compaction using vibratory equipment if disturbance occurs [2]. Unsuitable subgrade or subbase material is removed in accordance with Sections 203.1(b)–(c), vertical faces are cleaned and coated with PG 64S‑22, and final compaction must meet the density prescribed for approved equipment in Section 108.05(c); the foundation must be prepared to the horizontal and vertical tolerances of Section 206.3, remain free of deleterious soils, and any unstable areas identified during proof rolling are repaired per that section, while elevation adjustments due to bedrock encounters may be made only with approval from the District Geotechnical Engineer [2]. Prior to placement of fill, geosynthetics, or reinforced concrete pavement, the subgrade must be inspected and formally approved by the Representative; it must also be clean, free of foreign material, standing water, and frost, and uniformly moistened at the time of concrete overlay [2].

Design methods and criteria

The evaluation of subgrade begins with in‑situ strength testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements to obtain stiffness data, complemented by dynamic cone penetrometer and light weight deflectometer tests, all performed according to the sampling protocol described in the project appendices. Collected soil samples are handled per the detailed sampling procedures outlined in Appendix J to ensure representative laboratory testing. Laboratory determination of the resilient modulus M_r follows AASHTO T 307, while an empirical estimate can be derived from a California Bearing Ratio test using the Heukelom‑Klomp relationship M_r = 1500 × CBR, with the CBR value limited to a maximum of 20 and reduced to a multiplier of 1000 when in‑situ density is low. Values of CBR greater than 10 require chief engineer approval, and DCP resistance may be converted to CBR via the prescribed chart before applying the same Heukelom‑Klomp equation. When field data are available, M_r can be back‑calculated from FWD measurements by halving the dynamic stiffness and then applying a correction factor of 0.25 to align with laboratory values; any back‑calculated modulus exceeding roughly 15 000 psi must be confirmed through additional testing. The resilient modulus is used directly in flexible pavement design, whereas for rigid pavements it is transformed into an effective modulus of subgrade reaction k that is proportional to M_r and adjusted for seasonal conditions using base elastic moduli of 15 000 psi for spring‑thaw, 30 000 psi for normal periods, and 50 000 psi for frozen conditions. Loss‑of‑support factors for each supporting layer are combined with a weighted average formula, and the resulting k value is constrained so that the subbase‑to‑soil modulus ratio does not exceed four; these calculations are performed by the DARWin software package. When subgrade strengthening is required, rock capping is modeled with a CBR of 20, while lime or cement stabilization employs a structural coefficient of 0.12 together with the project‑specific CBR, and reinforced concrete pavement design uses MRSG = 1500 × CBR entered into the ACPA StreetPave12 program to evaluate combined support. Construction of the subgrade starts by forming the roadbed to the established top‑of‑subgrade elevation and compacting it in accordance with Section 206.3(b). Existing soil is excavated to an undisturbed condition without compaction to preserve permeability, fines are removed with light equipment, and the underlying layer is scarified at least six inches deep using a spring‑tooth rake or equivalent while heavy machinery such as concrete mixers or haul trucks is kept off the area. After excavation, the surface is proof rolled and sealed each day with a smooth‑drum steel‑wheel roller operating in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage and prevent infiltration of surface water. Grade and slope are verified by stretching a taut string line transversely across the subgrade at intervals not exceeding 25 feet, or by GPS/laser control when available, and deviations greater than one‑half inch are corrected by loosening the surface and adding or removing material as required. Special rolling for fine or cohesive soils employs sheepsfoot, pad foot, or pneumatic‑tire rollers in static mode at speeds not exceeding two miles per hour, while granular materials are compacted with a smooth steel‑drum vibratory roller under the same speed limit. Compaction must achieve 97 percent of maximum dry density for embankment foundations or existing embankments more than three feet below the proposed subgrade elevation, and 100 percent for structure foundations, subgrades, or material within three feet of the proposed elevation, with moisture content between minus three percent and the optimum value. Permanent deflection limits are set at one‑half inch for existing subgrade and one inch for raised embankments; when these limits are exceeded the Representative may require stabilization by undercutting and excavating to a depth of one foot for deflections between 0.5 and 3 inches, or up to three feet for larger settlements, followed by placement of Class 4 geotextile and backfill as specified. Before placing geotextile and stone bedding, any damaged or compacted spots are repaired to the Representative’s satisfaction, and compaction of the native subgrade is avoided until after those layers are installed. Subgrade protection during reconstruction includes full‑depth saw cutting of existing pavement along designated lines, grading the excavation to promote drainage, sealing with smooth‑wheeled compaction equipment to keep the subgrade from becoming saturated, and installing temporary drainage structures or channels as needed. Upon completion of all subgrade work, the contractor notifies the Representative for final inspection and acceptance before proceeding to subsequent pavement courses. [1][2]

The subgrade must be identified and sized during the design phase by applying Subgrade Soil Evaluation procedures, which require selection of an acceptable soil type and a suitable subbase thickness for each project location [1]. It is treated as a distinct construction item, and when existing conditions are judged deficient the design incorporates an “as directed” undercut quantity to remediate poor zones [1]. Structural capacity is expressed through a resilient modulus derived from soaked California Bearing Ratio (CBR) tests using the standard conversion M_r = 1,500 × CBR, and this value serves as input for slab‑thickness calculations [1]. All CBR testing must reflect seasonal moisture extremes and be reviewed with the District Geotechnical Engineer; values greater than ten require a recommendation from that engineer and approval by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, while values below five trigger mandatory documentation of improvement measures such as significant undercutting or other subgrade strengthening techniques [1]. In no case may a CBR value exceed twenty in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation, thereby capping the allowable resilient modulus at thirty thousand psi [1]. For projects that are not collectors or local roads the resilient modulus must be confirmed by laboratory or field testing, and if a back‑calculated M_r exceeds fifteen thousand psi independent verification through methods such as falling weight deflectometer or dynamic cone penetrometer testing is required before it can be used in pavement analysis [1]. The effective modulus of reaction (k) for the subgrade is derived from the confirmed resilient modulus and must be adjusted for subbase type, thickness, loss‑of‑support factors, and depth to any rigid foundation, with the ratio of subbase modulus to soil resilient modulus limited to four [1]. Seasonal design values are prescribed at fifteen thousand psi for spring‑thaw conditions, thirty thousand psi for normal summer/fall conditions, and fifty thousand psi for frozen winter conditions [1]. Geometric tolerances require the subgrade surface be formed to the established top‑of‑subgrade elevation, and any surface irregularities exceeding one‑half inch are corrected by adding or removing material and recompacting [2]. Acceptance testing must check the finished surface at all hinge points, centerlines, lane edges, shoulder edges, and at random locations every five hundred feet, with no more than ten percent of these checks allowed to exceed the half‑inch tolerance; otherwise additional staking and string‑line verification is mandated [2]. After placement the subgrade must remain in place for at least sixty days or through one winter season before a final compaction and proof‑rolling are performed prior to installing a Class 4, Type A geotextile separator [2]. Compaction criteria depend on location: embankment foundations situated more than three feet below the proposed grade must be compacted to at least ninety‑seven percent of maximum dry density, whereas subgrades supporting structures or lying within three feet of design elevation require a full one hundred percent of maximum dry density [2]. Moisture content during compaction shall not be lower than three percent below optimum moisture content, and testing follows AASHTO methods with equipment operating at no more than two miles per hour in static mode for fine soils [2]. Permanent deflection limits are imposed such that existing or cut subgrades may not exhibit rutting greater than one‑half inch, and raised embankment foundations may not exceed one inch; exceeding these thresholds triggers stabilization measures including undercutting, geotextile placement, and back‑filling [2]. Drainage provisions require subgrade drains of sufficient length to remove water from wet zones, installed at right angles (or as directed) and continued to an appropriate outlet, with trench bottoms beneath pavement and shoulders sloped no less than one‑quarter inch per foot [2]. The subgrade material must be free of deleterious or unsuitable soils, proof‑rolled to achieve a uniform firm surface, and where the native material consists of AASHTO No. 1 or No. 3 coarse aggregate, R‑rock, or shot rock fill, a sufficient thickness of No. 2A aggregate shall be placed and compacted over the proof‑rolled surface to cap voids identified by the Representative [2]. Reliability targets are embedded in the design criteria: collector roads require a ninety percent reliability level and a terminal serviceability index of 2.5, while local roads target an eighty‑five percent reliability and a serviceability index of 2.0, ensuring that subgrade performance meets prescribed durability and functional standards for the intended traffic class [1].

Structural section and dimensions

The only dimensional requirement tied to the subgrade in this section is that, when erosion has caused fine material buildup, the existing soil must be scarified to a minimum depth of six inches; otherwise the subgrade is to remain undisturbed and as permeable as possible. No specific thicknesses or dimensions are prescribed for pavement, base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab, shoulder, edge‑support, or reinforcement. [2]

Materials and mixture design

The subgrade specification must first identify whether the material is native in‑situ soil or borrow fill and require that each meet a minimum acceptable resilient modulus, with laboratory CBR values permitted as an equivalent for certain facilities [1]. Laboratory testing of representative samples taken under seasonal stress‑moisture conditions is required, and the designer coordinates with the district geotechnical engineer to determine the appropriate value [1]. For every soil type the specification must list the AASHTO classification together with gradation indices Cu and Cc, the percent passing the #200 sieve, Atterberg limits (liquid limit, plastic limit, plasticity index) and the calculated AASHTO Group Index, as well as maximum dry density and optimum moisture content [1][2]. The subgrade material must be free of deleterious or unsuitable soils before preparation [2]. When subgrade strengthening is required, the specification prescribes a stabilization method—cement, lime, or rock capping—and mandates laboratory determination of additive rates; required tests include hydrometer gradation analysis, liquid and plastic limits, shrinkage limit, unconfined compressive strength, dry density, sulfate/chloride content, organic content, and pH for lime stabilization [1]. The specification also identifies the type of subbase material placed beneath the pavement—such as asphalt‑treated base, cement‑treated base or a 2A subbase—and states its thickness in inches, assigning a loss‑of‑support factor to each layer to adjust the effective k‑value for potential erosion or pumping; the ratio of subbase modulus to the underlying roadbed resilient modulus shall not exceed four [1]. Seasonal design values for the base elastic modulus are set at 15,000 psi for spring thaw, 30,000 psi for normal summer/fall, and 50,000 psi for frozen winter, and if bedrock lies within ten feet of the surface its depth is incorporated into the k‑value calculation through weighted averages of loss‑of‑support factors and layer depths [1]. Existing native soil must be left undisturbed and as‑permeable; any zones where erosion has deposited fines or caused ponding are cleared and the underlying material scarified to at least six inches using a spring‑tooth rake [2]. Soil type shall be identified, with fine‑grained cohesive soils compacted by static roller and granular materials compacted with vibratory equipment [2]. Compaction requirements depend on location relative to design grade: deep embankment foundations must achieve at least 97 % of the maximum dry density, whereas subgrades within three feet of the proposed elevation must reach 100 % of the maximum dry density [2]. Moisture content during compaction shall not be lower than three percent below the optimum moisture content and may not exceed the optimum value [2]. Permanent deflection limits verify stability: existing or cut subgrade may not settle more than one‑half inch, and raised embankments may not settle more than one inch; when these limits are exceeded the distressed portion must be undercut—one foot for moderate distress and up to three feet for severe—and backfilled with an approved fill placed over a Class 4 Type C geotextile, optionally including a No. 57 aggregate drainage layer [2]. At least eight inches of fill must be placed before compaction, followed by a minimum six‑inch subbase over a Class 4 Type A geotextile [2]. When the subgrade material consists of AASHTO No. 1 or No. 3 coarse aggregate, R‑rock, or shot rock fill, a cap layer of PennDOT No. 2A coarse aggregate shall be placed and compacted on the proof‑rolled surface to fill any voids or irregularities; no specific mixture proportions are prescribed beyond using these designated aggregate grades [2]. Heavy equipment traffic is prohibited before placement of the geotextile and stone bedding, the subgrade shall not be compacted prior to installation of the geotextile, and any damaged or overly compacted areas must be repaired to the Representative’s satisfaction [2].

Selection of subgrade materials begins with a thorough characterization of the existing ground through representative sampling and in‑situ stiffness testing such as falling weight deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer and lightweight deflectometer [1]. The test results are expressed primarily by California Bearing Ratio values, and the lowest CBR within the project controls the pavement design, with isolated low‑CBR zones improved to achieve uniformity across the site [1]. When the in‑place CBR is five or less, a cost‑benefit analysis for projects larger than 25 000 square yards on Traffic Pattern Groups 1‑4 typically justifies strengthening measures [1]. Strengthening may be achieved mechanically by pulverizing the existing material and recompacting it with added aggregate or reclaimed asphalt pavement to improve gradation, or chemically by adding cement, lime or fly ash in proportions determined from laboratory mix‑design tests that target the required resilient modulus while avoiding excessive brittleness [1]. For very weak soils a rock cap modeled with CBR = 20 can be used, and the stabilized layer is represented in design calculations with a structural coefficient of 0.12 while retaining the native CBR beneath it [1]. Geosynthetic reinforcement such as geogrids or Class‑4 geotextiles may be placed at the subgrade‑base interface to increase durability and improve load distribution for weak soils [1]. All borrow material incorporated into the subgrade must satisfy minimum resilient modulus values established from laboratory or field testing to ensure structural capacity [1]. The mixture proportions for reclaimed or stabilized subgrade are defined by a mix design that specifies quantities of native material, optional aggregates or RAP, stabilizing additives and water needed to meet target density and strength [1][2]. Placement is required at the optimum moisture content determined by the mix design but not exceeding three percent above that value, and compaction must achieve at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum dry density as verified on a control strip and confirmed by random nuclear density tests [2]. Chemically stabilized bases must develop a seven‑day unconfined compressive strength between 300 and 500 psi (or a minimum of 280 psi for emulsion mixes), providing the durability needed before traffic is allowed [2]. For rigid‑pavement systems the subgrade is built by selecting an appropriate subbase type—aggregate treated base or cement treated base—and a thickness that yields the required effective modulus of subgrade reaction, with elastic modulus values of 15 000 psi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30 000 psi for normal summer/fall periods, and 50 000 psi when frozen [1]. A loss‑of‑support factor is applied to correct the effective k‑value for potential erosion or pumping, and the ratio of subbase resilient modulus to underlying soil modulus is limited to four to protect against excessive deformation [1]. By integrating these characterization, stabilization, mix‑design, moisture‑control, compaction, strength, reinforcement, and subbase criteria, the selected subgrade materials satisfy structural demands, provide long‑term durability, remain constructible with practical layer sizes and equipment, and deliver the performance required over the pavement’s design life [1][2].

Structural details and interfaces

The subgrade foundation is achieved without the use of traditional joints or conventional load‑transfer devices, relying instead on an integrated drainage network, reinforcement fabrics and carefully designed transitions. Continuous base drains are installed along the outer edge of the pavement, on the low side of any superelevated sections, and on the median wherever subsurface water problems have been identified, while lateral underdrains are placed at every cut‑to‑fill transition and at other critical locations flagged by maintenance personnel. Additional subgrade drains are provided in sag areas or wherever existing pavement shows signs of moisture damage to maintain positive drainage conditions. Prior to any reinforcement, the subgrade must be compacted and proof‑rolled after remaining in place for at least sixty days or one winter season to ensure adequate strength and stability. Elevation control is achieved by installing reference grade lines with ample supports or grade stakes along each side of the subgrade, establishing a precise longitudinal profile before further work proceeds. At the close of each workday, all constructed or disturbed surfaces are sealed using a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage and limit water infiltration. Reinforcement is provided primarily with geogrids positioned at the interface between the subgrade and the overlying aggregate to stabilize weak soils, complemented by full‑width Class 4 Type A layer‑separation geotextile placed longitudinally across a lane width of no less than eight feet before the subbase is installed. When the California Bearing Ratio of the native material falls below three, a Class 4 Type C stabilization geotextile is added to supply lateral restraint and reduce load stresses on the subgrade, and this same type of fabric is also placed at the bottom of undercut zones before backfill. In areas where abrupt changes in soil conditions occur, a gradual transition is achieved using granular fill or a rock‑capping layer one to three feet thick composed of R‑3 rock, AASHTO No. 1, or the best available rock, with the separation fabric incorporated as part of the construction detail. Where an undercut exists, transition slopes are required at both the beginning and end of the feature, limited to a maximum gradient of eight horizontal to one vertical, and reinforcement in these zones includes the aforementioned Class 4 Type C geotextile beneath the backfill followed by a Class 4 Type A layer over the selected borrow material before placing the subbase. Although routine subgrade construction does not employ joints, repair sections are treated as joints by marking the perimeter of the area to be replaced, creating a saw‑cut or milled edge, cleaning the exposed vertical faces and applying a uniform coat of PG 64S‑22 before the asphalt base course is laid. All strengthening treatments, whether involving geogrids, geotextiles, drainage installations, or transition layers, are integrated to maintain the functional integrity of each material layer and to provide the necessary load distribution across the subgrade system. The design process starts by evaluating the native subgrade soil to determine whether it is weak, moisture‑sensitive, or otherwise unsuitable for supporting the intended pavement structure [1][2]. When such soils are found, an overlay may be placed to protect them from construction traffic and to raise the grade line above high‑moisture zones [1]. Prior to any overlay, the subgrade is excavated to an undisturbed, minimally compacted condition so that its permeability is retained [2]. Areas where erosion has deposited fines or ponded water are cleared with light equipment and the underlying soil is scarified at least six inches deep using a spring‑tooth rake or equivalent tool [2]. Damaged or overly compacted spots are repaired to the satisfaction of the overseeing authority before work proceeds, and final acceptance is obtained after inspection [2]. The prepared surface is then proof‑rolled to achieve a uniform firm base, and all vertical faces that will contact asphalt mixtures—such as curbs, structures, gutters, and existing pavement edges—are coated with a thin, uniform layer of an approved tack coat (PG 64S‑22 or emulsified asphalt applied in two passes) to ensure sound bonding [2]. All engineered interfaces with adjacent features—including shoulder widths, embankment slopes, guide‑rail locations, and vertical alignments at structures—are coordinated so that the geometry of each interface promotes uniform load distribution while considering project cost impacts [1]. A continuous Class 4 Type A separation geosynthetic is placed over the entire prepared subgrade across the full lane width, extending longitudinally with its transverse edges positioned between six and twelve inches from the interior edge of the pavement base drain, and adjacent sheets are overlapped a minimum of twelve inches [2]. Where dissimilar materials such as subbase and subgrade must remain distinct—particularly when the California Bearing Ratio is below three—a Class 4 geotextile is used, employing Type A fabric for pure separation or Type C when combined separation and reinforcement are required [1][2]. At locations where an undercut is required, the transition at its beginning and end is formed with a slope no steeper than 8 H:1 V; a Class 4 Type C geotextile is placed at the bottom of the undercut, followed by at least eight inches of approved fill material before compaction [2]. After the primary geosynthetic layer, a minimum six‑inch subbase is placed and compacted to the specified density, providing a stable interface between the subgrade, the geosynthetics, and the overlying aggregate backfills [2]. Where weak soil‑aggregate bonds are anticipated, a geogrid is installed at the interface between the subgrade and the aggregate base in accordance with Publication 408 to reinforce the connection [1]. Abrupt changes in soil type, cut‑fill boundaries, or junctions where a new pavement abuts an existing section of different total depth are smoothed with gradual transitions composed of granular, non‑frost‑susceptible material to maintain drainage, prevent differential heave, and achieve uniform subgrade performance across all engineered interfaces [1]. For reinforced concrete pavement sections, dowel bars are installed across all transverse patch joints, anchored with suitable binders such as epoxy, polyester, or vinylester, and joint spacing is limited to thirty feet for perpendicular joints in reinforced concrete and fifteen feet for plain‑concrete sections; each joint receives appropriate seals and coated dowels [1]. Precast units are placed on a prepared bedding course that rests on the defined subgrade and subbase thicknesses, and when the existing subgrade is deficient an “as directed” undercut quantity is added to improve support [1]. Throughout construction, each day’s exposed subgrade surface is sealed with a static smooth‑drum steel‑wheel roller to promote continuous positive drainage and limit water infiltration [2]. Special rolling operations are prohibited within ten feet of bridge abutments or approach slabs, and existing underground utilities must be protected during undercut work [2]. Subsurface drainage systems are installed as shown on standard drawings to manage water at abutments and fill ends, thereby creating a stable interface among subgrade, geosynthetics, aggregate backfills, and the pavement structure [2]. Prior to placing the asphalt base course, any disturbed subgrade or subbase surface is re‑compacted with vibratory equipment, grade loss is restored with additional subbase material, and the final compacted layer provides a uniform platform for the overlying asphalt placed without segregation and compacted to the specified density [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

The design process starts with a systematic survey to locate zones of excess moisture or intersecting water tables, and those areas are treated before the reclaimed layer is placed because uncontrolled water degrades material integrity and reduces structural capacity [1]. Subsurface drainage is provided by installing lateral underdrains in wet subgrade sections and at every cut‑to‑fill transition, and by lowering water tables with appropriately sized drains that discharge to a positive outlet [1]. High‑permeability base and subbase materials are placed on adequately sloped layers so that free water can be removed by gravity, while open‑graded subbase is prohibited; where a reliable outlet exists an asphalt‑ or cement‑treated permeable base may be used, otherwise a dense base with longitudinal edge drains is required [1]. Prior to any drainage installation the subgrade is excavated to the natural undisturbed soil and graded so that water can flow away from the future pavement, and heavy equipment such as concrete mixers is kept off the area to preserve permeability [2]. Any fine material or ponded water produced by erosion is removed with light equipment and the underlying layer is scarified at least six inches deep using a spring‑tooth rake or equivalent, after which damaged or compacted zones are repaired to the satisfaction of the project representative [2]. Moisture content is verified against the mix design and compaction is performed only when the water content lies between optimum moisture and three percent below optimum; material that exceeds this range must be dried by disking, scarifying, or other approved means before compaction [2][1]. Soils with high fines content or elevated liquid limits are identified as moisture‑sensitive and more prone to frost heave, influencing the selection of optimum moisture targets and compaction criteria [1]. For chemically stabilized subgrades the surface is kept continuously damp through daily water applications or an emulsified‑asphalt primer to support curing, which proceeds more rapidly under dry, sunny, low‑humidity, high‑temperature conditions and slows in rain or cool, humid weather [2]. After the subgrade has remained in place for at least sixty days—or one winter season—it is re‑compacted and proof‑rolled to achieve a uniform, firm surface before placement of the geotextile separator and subbase [2]. At the time of concrete placement the subgrade must be uniformly moistened; any drying that occurs during construction is corrected by evenly applying water without creating mud or standing pools, and placement on frozen subgrade, subbase, or base drain is prohibited [2]. To maintain continuous positive drainage the finished subgrade surface is sealed at the end of each workday with a static smooth‑drum steel roller, producing a water‑shedding skin that limits infiltration [2]. Following recompaction a Class 4 Type A geotextile separator is installed continuously in the longitudinal direction, with its transverse edges positioned six to twelve inches from the interior edge of the pavement base drain, and surface irregularities greater than one‑half inch are corrected before placement [2]. Frost susceptibility is addressed either by removing frost‑prone soils or by covering them with a sufficiently thick layer of non‑frost‑susceptible granular material that insulates the roadbed from frost penetration, thereby preserving strength through freeze‑thaw cycles [1]. For pervious pavement installations infiltration testing of test sections must demonstrate an average rate exceeding two hundred inches per hour in accordance with ASTM C1701, and placement is prohibited when air or surface temperatures are below fifty degrees Fahrenheit [2]. Subsurface drainage systems are also provided at bridge abutments and at the far end of the GSBA fill as shown on standard drawings, ensuring that water does not accumulate beneath the pavement structure [2].

Verification and expected performance

The completed subgrade is verified through a coordinated program of field measurements and laboratory analyses that together demonstrate that the foundation meets the stiffness, strength, compaction, and overall project specifications [1][2]. After construction, a Falling Weight Deflectometer test is performed on the rebuilt layer to obtain an in‑situ modulus that must satisfy the design stiffness values, while dynamic cone penetrometer and lightweight deflectometer tests are run concurrently to provide complementary strength and deformation data [1]. Representative soil samples are collected according to the sampling plan defined by the project specifications so that laboratory testing reflects the material actually placed in the field [1][2]. In the laboratory the samples undergo moisture‑density determination, unconfined compressive strength, classification, and either resilient modulus or California Bearing Ratio testing, with the CBR result being cross‑checked against gradation, percent passing #200, Atterberg limits, group index, maximum dry density, and optimum moisture content to confirm that the soil can achieve the assumed resilient modulus [1]. When a CBR value falls outside the typical range for the identified soil type—specifically above ten or below five—the result must be reviewed and approved by the District Geotechnical Engineer before it can be accepted as compliant [1]. Seasonal moisture variations are addressed by obtaining modulus values under differing moisture conditions to ensure performance throughout the design life [1]. Prior to testing, the subgrade is excavated to an undisturbed condition that retains natural permeability; any eroded or fine‑filled zones are removed with light equipment and the underlying soil is scarified at least six inches deep, while overly compacted or damaged areas are repaired to the satisfaction of the project Representative [2]. Once these preparatory actions are finished, the contractor notifies the Representative, who conducts a final visual inspection and issues formal acceptance before any pavement layers are placed [2]. Grade and slope compliance is verified by establishing reference grade lines and measuring the surface with a taut string line run transversely at intervals no greater than twenty‑five feet (or up to fifty feet when automatic grading equipment is used, or five hundred feet for GPS/laser‑controlled work) [2]. A template and a ten‑foot straightedge are employed to detect irregularities, and any deviation exceeding one‑half inch is corrected by loosening the surface and adding or removing material followed by recompaction [2]. The Representative performs quality‑control checks at hinge points, lane edges and shoulders on a random basis every five hundred feet, rejecting the subgrade if more than ten percent of those checks exceed the half‑inch tolerance [2]. Compaction control requires that material within three feet of the final grade or supporting structures reach one hundred percent of maximum dry density, while deeper embankment fill must achieve at least ninety‑seven percent, with moisture content maintained between optimum and three percent below optimum [2]. In‑place moisture‑density testing in accordance with AASHTO T191 or T310 is carried out on random test strips after each compaction pass, and compaction continues until successive passes increase density by no more than one percent, indicating a stable mass [2]. After the required densities are attained, special rolling with an approved roller is performed in the presence of the Representative, who measures permanent deflection and requires that it not exceed one‑half inch for existing subgrade or cut sections and not exceed one inch for raised embankments; exceeding these limits triggers stabilization actions such as undercutting, geotextile placement, or back‑filling [2]. Proof rolling the foundation provides a uniform, firm surface, and any unstable zones identified during this step are repaired before final acceptance [2]. The Representative’s final inspection confirms that grade tolerances of plus or minus one‑half inch, surface condition, moisture‑density compliance, and deflection limits have been satisfied, thereby ensuring that the completed subgrade is constructible, performs as intended, and is consistent with all project requirements before pavement placement proceeds [2][1].

Subgrade performance is judged by its ability to support a reinforced concrete pavement for a design service life of twenty years while limiting slab cracking to no more than fifteen percent at the end of that period and delivering ride quality expressed as terminal serviceability values of 2.5 on collector roads and 2.0 on local roads. [1] To achieve these outcomes the subgrade must provide sufficient bearing capacity, which is quantified primarily by the California Bearing Ratio (CBR) test; CBR values must exceed five to avoid mandatory improvement, values above ten require approval by the chief geotechnical engineer, and any value greater than twenty is excluded from use in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation. [1] The resilient modulus (Mr) of the subgrade is calculated from CBR using Mr = 1,500 × CBR and entered into pavement design software to verify that reliability targets of ninety percent for collector roads and eighty‑five percent for local roads are met. [1] Independent verification of Mr may be obtained by back‑calculating from Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements or by converting Dynamic Cone Penetration results, with any back‑calculated modulus exceeding fifteen thousand psi requiring confirmation by an independent test. [1] In‑situ density of the subgrade is also measured and compared to its maximum dry density to confirm proper compaction. [1][2] Stability over the design life is further required, with permanent deflection limited to a half‑inch for existing or cut subgrades and to one inch for raised embankments. [2] These deflection limits are monitored through post‑rolling permanent deflection measurements after each compaction pass. [2] Compaction quality is assessed by moisture‑density testing performed in accordance with AASHTO T191 or T310, and the subgrade must achieve at least one hundred percent of its maximum dry density, while most embankment material must reach a minimum of ninety‑seven percent. [2] By satisfying the CBR, resilient modulus, density, permanent deflection, and moisture‑density criteria, the pavement is expected to maintain the prescribed serviceability, distress resistance, and ride quality throughout its intended twenty‑year life.
