Subgrade

Part of: Pavement Structure is-subtopic-of
Subgrade is a topic under Pavement Structure.
subgradesubgrades
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-28

The subgrade is the prepared earth foundation beneath a pavement that supplies the essential bearing capacity and reliably transfers traffic loads to the underlying strata without excessive settlement or heave [1]. It must meet prescribed stiffness—expressed by a resilient modulus or California Bearing Ratio typically in the 5‑10 range—and seasonal elastic‑modulus values of about 15 000 psi for spring‑thaw, 30 000 psi for normal conditions and 50 000 psi when frozen, while being compacted to at least 97 % (100 % in the top three feet) of maximum dry density and kept within ±3 % of optimum moisture content [1][2]. Uniformity is achieved by grading to a flat, smooth bearing plane free of protrusions, ponded water or frost, installing properly sloped subgrade drains, and applying stabilization measures such as geotextiles, biaxial grids or chemical treatment so that permanent deformation remains below one‑half inch, thereby protecting the pavement’s service life [1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Subgrade” also include the following subtopics: Subgrade Stabilization and Undercutting.

Subgrade Stabilization supplies the necessary stiffness and water resistance by treating weak masses with chemical or geosynthetic agents when inherent capacity is insufficient. [1][2] Undercutting removes deficient zones identified by low California Bearing Ratio values and replaces them with compacted aggregate over geotextile fabric to restore structural integrity. [1][2]

Purpose and design objectives

The subgrade is incorporated to remedy the fundamental problem of insufficient bearing capacity and non‑uniform support in native soils that would otherwise cause excessive settlement, differential heave, cracking, rutting and other distress in overlying pavements. By preparing a uniform, stable earth foundation whose stiffness—expressed through resilient modulus (M_r) or California Bearing Ratio (CBR) typically in the range of 5 to 10—and effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k) meet prescribed seasonal limits, the design ensures reliable transfer of traffic loads to the ground without loss of support. Seasonal moisture conditions are accounted for by recognizing that spring moduli are 10–20 % lower than summer values and frozen subgrades can be roughly twice as stiff, leading to required base elastic modulus values of 15 000 psi for spring‑thaw, 30 000 psi for normal (summer/fall) and 50 000 psi for frozen conditions. The design also limits the ratio of subbase modulus to soil resilient modulus to four in order to prevent excessive deformation of the pavement structure. To achieve the needed uniformity, the native material is graded to a flat, smooth and level bearing plane free of sharp protrusions, debris, ponded water, frost or deleterious soils, with surface irregularities exceeding one‑half inch removed by scarifying at least six inches deep using light equipment and then proof‑rolling to verify firmness. Any zones identified as unstable are corrected through additional compaction, undercutting, excavation and replacement, backfilling, or stabilization measures such as geotextile placement (Class 4 Type A) and appropriate biaxial grids—polypropylene for confinement of weak soils or slopes and polyester for reinforcement in load‑distribution pads. When the in‑situ M_r or CBR values fall outside the acceptable ranges, corrective actions such as undercutting, backfilling, soil stabilization, addition of a prepared subbase, or installation of subgrade drains are mandated to restore uniform support and proper drainage. Subgrade drains are installed with a minimum slope of ¼ in./ft, extended sufficiently to convey water away, and their outlet ends are kept open until final acceptance, thereby preventing excess moisture that would otherwise reduce bearing capacity and promote frost heave or pumping. For pervious concrete systems the subgrade must remain permeable, requiring excavation to undisturbed soil without compaction and achieving a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour, which further supports the performance need for effective water management while maintaining structural stability. Satisfying these stiffness, uniformity, deformation‑limit, and drainage criteria prevents differential settlement, frost heave, erosion or pumping, limits permanent deflection to less than one‑half inch, and thereby extends the service life of both rigid and pervious pavement structures under anticipated traffic loads. [1][2]

The subgrade is required to function as a reliable load‑supporting foundation that transfers traffic loads to the underlying strata without excessive deflection, thereby preserving ride quality and safety [1][2]. To satisfy this functional role the design must achieve at least the minimum roadbed resilient modulus specified for the pavement structure and must meet the prescribed California Bearing Ratio limits, with values above ten requiring engineering approval and no value greater than twenty permitted in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation [1]. Where isolated zones exhibit CBR values below the target, those areas are to be improved so that the subgrade performance is consistent with overall project conditions [1]. The subgrade must be constructed to the established top‑of‑subgrade elevation and shaped to the required lines and grades, using uniform material from a single borrow source placed in lifts that are compacted to the density criteria [2]. Compaction shall reach at least ninety‑seven percent of the laboratory maximum dry density for embankment foundations and the upper three feet must be compacted to one hundred percent of that maximum, while moisture content is controlled within three percent below optimum (or ± 2 % for granular Type 1) and not more than three percent drier than optimum during compaction [2]. Structural performance is demonstrated by limiting permanent deformation: existing subgrades and cuts may not exhibit rutting or deflection greater than one‑half inch, and embankment foundations are limited to one inch of permanent deformation [2]. The compacted mass must be free of rutting, displacement or shear wave under compaction equipment; any zones that exceed these limits are to be repaired or stabilized before further work [2]. Surface irregularities exceeding a half‑inch over a ten‑foot template must be corrected, and if more than ten percent of acceptance tests show such excess, additional staking and stringline control is required [2]. Durability objectives require the subgrade to remain dry and permeable; effective drainage systems are installed before construction so that moisture does not degrade material integrity or reduce structural capacity [1][2]. All wet locations are identified and remedied, ponded water is removed, and the subgrade surface is proof‑rolled at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller in static mode to seal the surface and promote positive drainage [1][2]. Frost‑susceptible soils must be eliminated or treated with rock capping, stabilization, geogrids or similar measures to prevent differential heave during freeze‑thaw cycles [1]. The subgrade must be free of deleterious materials, erosion‑produced fines and frozen conditions; any accumulated fines are removed and the underlying soil scarified at least six inches with a spring‑tooth rake [2]. Safety considerations include prohibiting heavy equipment such as concrete mixers from traveling over the prepared area, limiting drop heights of subsequent subbase layers to three feet, ensuring construction traffic does not contact geocells until the compacted subbase is at least one inch above cell tops, and restricting vehicle traffic on stabilized backfill until a minimum six‑inch layer of loose fill is placed [2]. Serviceability is achieved by maintaining profile tolerances of no more than half an inch deviation over ten feet, providing a minimum cross‑slope of a quarter inch per foot for drainage, and proof‑rolling to obtain a uniform firm surface before pavement placement [1][2]. When the subgrade is chemically or asphalt‑stabilized it must develop the specified strength within seven days, attaining at least 50 psi indirect tensile strength and an unconfined compressive strength between 300 and 500 psi before traffic may be allowed [2]. Additional field testing such as falling weight deflectometer or dynamic cone penetrometer is required when laboratory CBR results appear uncertain, to validate the modulus before design use and prevent unexpected settlement or failure [1].

Scope and applicability

The subgrade design and preparation approach is applicable to both flexible and rigid pavement systems, including full‑depth asphalt sections, asphalt overlays on thin concrete slabs, pre‑1945 concrete pavements, and reinforced cement or pervious concrete slabs that require a prepared base. [1][2] It is required for any pavement reconstruction effort—such as rubblized projects, overlays of fractured Portland‑cement concrete, or rehabilitation of jointed plain concrete and roller‑compacted concrete—where the existing subgrade must be evaluated or strengthened. [1][2] For high‑volume highways, interstates and expressways, the guides recommend laboratory determination of the resilient modulus or CBR testing to characterize the subgrade, with conversion to an effective modulus of subgrade reaction for rigid pavements. [1] On lower‑classification collector and local roads, the resilient modulus may be estimated from soil surveys, while still requiring that the subgrade meet uniformity and stability criteria before placement of the surface layer. [1] When existing CBR values are around five, strengthening the subgrade with cement, lime, or rock capping becomes cost‑effective for projects exceeding roughly 25 000 square yards and traffic groups 1 to 4. [1] In frost‑susceptible regions the approach calls for removal of frost‑affected material and replacement with non‑frost‑susceptible fill or a stronger base course, ensuring that design assumptions regarding seasonal k‑values are satisfied. [1] For rigid‑pavement designs such as jointed plain concrete pavement, roller‑compacted concrete, or pervious concrete, the subgrade must be uniformly moist and free of debris, ponded water, frost, or compacted zones before concrete placement, with any damaged areas repaired to the satisfaction of the project representative. [2] The preparation includes excavating to an undisturbed condition without compaction, scarifying the soil at least six inches where fines have accumulated, and uniformly moistening the surface immediately prior to slab placement. [2] Overall, the subgrade design approach is appropriate wherever flexible or rigid pavements are being designed—particularly on reconstruction projects, thin‑slab overlays, frost‑prone sites, high‑traffic highways, and any situation where subgrade strengthening or precise modulus characterization is required. [1][2]

When the native subgrade consists of weak clays or silts that cannot support construction equipment, an overlay may seem attractive but if implementing it would require extensive shoulder widening, slope changes, guide‑rail relocation, or vertical adjustments at structures—changes that markedly increase overall project cost—the overlay is no longer the most economical option and another design approach must be adopted [1]. Similarly, when the existing pavement is severely deteriorated or grade restrictions prevent raising the surface with an overlay, designers are directed to remove the pavement down to a stable layer, then backfill, stabilize, or fully replace it in order to obtain a uniform foundation while minimizing grade adjustments [1]. If distress is primarily surface‑related rather than caused by inadequate support layers, full‑depth reclamation (FDR) is not preferred and alternative rehabilitation methods should be selected [1]. Excess moisture within the subgrade further discourages reliance on subgrade improvement; wet locations must be identified and effective drainage installed before any FDR can be considered, and the practicality of FDR diminishes when the existing pavement is thin or the available subgrade material is insufficient to form a reclaimed layer [1]. Geotechnical criteria based on California Bearing Ratio (CBR) also dictate alternative strategies: values greater than ten require review and approval before a subgrade‑based design proceeds, while values below five trigger requirements for significant undercutting, stabilization, or replacement, indicating that another pavement design is preferable [1]. When reliable CBR data cannot be obtained because of high fines content, elevated Atterberg limits, or a high Group Index, designers are instructed to consult the Highway Design and Technology Section for viable options rather than rely on the native subgrade alone [1]. Isolated low‑CBR zones or widespread low values deem the native material inadequate; in such cases targeted improvement or replacement is required, and when large variations in soil type and CBR exist across an alignment it may be appropriate to divide the project into limited subsections each with its own design [1]. For projects of at least 25 000 square yards on Traffic Pattern Groups 1‑4, a cost‑benefit analysis of strengthening techniques such as cement or lime stabilization, rock capping modeled with CBR = 20, or geogrid/geotextile reinforcement is required when CBR falls below five, because the subgrade alone would not ensure performance [1]. Frost‑susceptible soils that could cause differential heaving also demand either a stronger base course or removal and replacement with non‑frost‑susceptible material, making reliance on the subgrade alone unacceptable [1]. When permanent deflection or rutting in the existing subgrade exceeds one‑half inch, the native material is no longer acceptable; if the deformation lies between a half‑inch and three inches, the subgrade must be undercut to one foot, a geotextile placed over the excavation, and the void filled with approved backfill before compaction, whereas deflection greater than three inches requires a deeper cut of up to three feet using the same treatment sequence [2]. In soft soils that contain buried obstructions, shallow utilities, or other features that prevent driving anchors, the conventional anchored geocell method cannot be used and an installation frame capable of fully expanding the cells and retaining them until backfill placement must be employed [2]. Where the embankment or fill foundation lies in water, swampy ground, or otherwise saturated or unstable conditions, specifications require constructing the structure with rock rather than a conventional subgrade, and frozen material may not be placed or compacted and must be removed before construction proceeds [2]. When only small hand‑operated compaction equipment is feasible, fill must be placed in uniform horizontal layers no thicker than four inches to achieve adequate compaction [2]. If the native material is deleterious or unsuitable, or if the subgrade consists of coarse AASHTO No. 1 or No. 3 aggregate that leaves voids, or when bedrock is encountered during excavation, a standard subgrade alone cannot meet performance criteria; instead a geosynthetic‑stabilized bridge approach is required, beginning with proof rolling the foundation, capping it with a sufficient thickness of No. 2A aggregate to fill irregularities, and then installing a Class 4, Type A separation geosynthetic for stabilization [2].

Design inputs and requirements

The subgrade design must be based on a comprehensive set of site‑specific data, beginning with an estimate of future traffic loading to define the expected stresses, and a recent pavement condition survey that documents existing distress and drainage problems. Geotechnical information is obtained through in‑situ testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer or lightweight deflectometer to quantify subgrade stiffness, while material characteristics are captured by systematic sampling of the existing layers for laboratory evaluation. Environmental considerations focus on identifying wet subgrade areas and ensuring proper drainage to prevent moisture‑induced loss of strength. Together these traffic, existing‑condition, material, geotechnical and environmental inputs provide the basis for determining appropriate layer thicknesses and rehabilitation strategies. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126)

The subgrade underlying any pavement on state right‑of‑way must be designed and constructed in accordance with a suite of departmental publications, specifications, test methods, and performance constraints that address drainage, geotechnical evaluation, material properties, and construction practices [1][2]. Drainage requirements are set out in the roadway construction standards, which call for protection of drainage outlets in mowable areas by headwalls and prescribe sizing and spacing of pavement‑base drains; lateral underdrains are encouraged in wet zones, continuous edge drains are required on interstate facilities, and additional subgrade drains must be provided wherever water damage or sagging is observed [1]. Geotechnical evaluation follows the geotechnical engineering manual, requiring a resilient modulus for the roadbed soil to be obtained from the district geotechnical engineer based on AASHTO laboratory tests that include moisture‑density, unconfined compressive strength, soil classification, resilient modulus, and soaked CBR testing; for interstate and expressway projects a laboratory CBR may substitute for a direct modulus measurement provided it does not exceed twenty, and any CBR greater than ten must be recommended by the district geotechnical engineer and approved by the chief geotechnical engineer [1]. Subgrade soil evaluation is defined in the design manual, which establishes acceptable soil types and thicknesses during the design phase; the subgrade is treated as a separate construction item, excavation is limited to Class 1 quantities, and density acceptance sampling and testing are required by the specifications [1]. Rigid‑pavement designs on interstates and expressways must incorporate an asphalt or cement‑treated permeable base over a 2A subbase, prohibit open‑graded subbase, and limit the ratio of subbase modulus to roadbed soil resilient modulus to four, while seasonal elastic moduli are prescribed as fifteen thousand psi for spring thaw, thirty thousand psi for normal conditions, and fifty thousand psi for frozen conditions [1]. Performance thresholds require that, for all projects other than collectors or local roads, the roadbed soil resilient modulus be based on laboratory or in‑situ testing and that plans specify a minimum acceptable modulus for borrow material; frost‑susceptible soils must follow frost‑design guidance, and any subgrade improvement such as stabilization, rock capping, geogrids, or geotextiles must comply with the applicable publication [1]. Prior to rehabilitation, existing subgrade stiffness is evaluated using falling weight deflectometer testing, optionally supplemented by dynamic cone penetrometer or lightweight deflectometer measurements, and sample collection follows prescribed appendix procedures [1]. Construction of the subgrade begins with forming it to the established top‑of‑subgrade elevation and compacting it as specified in the construction section, after which it is proof‑rolled and sealed each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller to promote drainage; grade control lines are installed and the finished surface is checked at intervals not exceeding twenty‑five feet (or up to five hundred feet when GPS or laser equipment is used), and any irregularities greater than one‑half inch must be corrected before work proceeds, with acceptance testing allowing no more than ten percent of random checks to exceed this tolerance [2]. Compaction density requirements call for a minimum of ninety‑seven percent of maximum dry density for embankment foundations or existing embankments deeper than three feet, while the uppermost three feet and any subgrade within three feet of the final elevation must reach one hundred percent of maximum dry density; moisture content shall be maintained within minus three percent of optimum, with a plus‑or‑minus two percent allowance for Type 1 granular material or surface‑damp conditions for Type 2 [2]. General compaction is performed with a smooth‑drum roller in static mode, whereas fine‑grained and cohesive soils require a sheepsfoot or pad roller having at least one hundred sixty pounds per square inch contact pressure; equipment specifications include pneumatic rollers of twenty‑six to twenty‑eight ton capacity, vibratory steel‑drum rollers delivering at least fifty thousand pounds of dynamic force, and loaded triaxle dump trucks limited to seventy‑two thousand pounds gross vehicle weight, all operated under supervision with a travel speed not exceeding one point five feet per second and a ten‑foot clearance from bridge abutments [2]. Permanent deflection or rutting greater than one‑half inch in existing subgrade, or more than one inch in embankment foundations, is deemed unsatisfactory and must be remedied by recompaction or undercutting [2]. Excavation of the subgrade shall reach an undisturbed, permeable condition without initial compaction; any erosion‑induced fines or ponded water are removed with light equipment and the underlying soil scarified a minimum of six inches using a spring‑tooth rake or equivalent, while heavy equipment such as haul trucks is prohibited from traversing the area before placement of geotextile and stone bedding, and excessive traffic that could compact the subgrade is not allowed [2]. After the subgrade has remained in place for at least sixty days or one winter season it is re‑compacted, proof‑rolled and covered with a Class 4 Type A geotextile prior to subbase placement; trench work, when required, must be excavated, backfilled and adequately tamped while maintaining a minimum slope of one quarter inch per foot on the trench bottom, and subgrade drains remain open until final shoulder acceptance [2]. For projects employing geocell systems, the subgrade must be uniformly compacted to the specified lines and grades before cell placement; adjacent geotextile sheets shall overlap by at least twelve inches and extend six inches beyond the geocell perimeter, anchors must penetrate a minimum of twelve inches below the cell bottom, and the loose subbase is overfilled so that the compacted surface sits at least one inch above the geocell, with drop heights not exceeding three feet and lift‑thickness allowances applied when cell height exceeds six inches [2]. Prior to placement of reinforced concrete pavement, the subgrade or subbase must be clean, free of foreign material, standing water, frost or frozen conditions and uniformly moistened to provide proper support; RCC pavement shall not be placed on a frozen base [2]. Preparation also requires removal of deleterious soils, repair of unstable areas, and provision of a flat, smooth, level surface free of sharp protrusions or debris before reinforcement, with proof‑rolling providing a uniform firm surface as specified, and the completed subgrade is inspected and approved before any fill or geosynthetics are placed; encounters with bedrock require field adjustment approved by the district geotechnical engineer [2]. When the native material consists of AASHTO No 1 or No 3 coarse aggregate, R‑rock or shot rock fill, a choke layer of sufficient thickness of No 2A aggregate is placed and compacted on the proof‑rolled surface to eliminate voids, after which a Class 4 Type A geotextile is laid over the prepared subgrade before any geogrid or other reinforcement; backfill may not be dropped from a height greater than three feet and must be compacted in accordance with the construction section [2]. Subgrade stabilization using biaxial geogrids follows the specified procedure, requiring polypropylene grids for confinement and polyester grids for reinforcement that meet minimum tensile‑strength values of two thousand pounds per foot for Type C grids, with sampling according to ASTM D4354 and handling and storage in compliance with ASTM D4873 [2].

Design methods and criteria

The subgrade evaluation begins with in‑situ strength testing that includes Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements to obtain stiffness data and systematic sampling of the material for laboratory analysis, complemented by dynamic cone penetrometer and light weight deflectometer tests as detailed in the project appendices [1][2]. The principal property used in design is the resilient modulus (M_r), which is determined in the laboratory according to AASHTO T 307 or estimated empirically from a California Bearing Ratio test using the Heukelom‑Klomp relationship M_r = 1,500 × CBR, with the multiplier reduced to 1,000 when the in‑situ density is low and the CBR value limited to a maximum of 20; values above 10 require chief engineer approval [1]. When field data are available, M_r may be back‑calculated from FWD measurements by halving the dynamic stiffness and applying a correction factor of 0.25, and any back‑calculated modulus exceeding roughly 15,000 psi must be confirmed through additional testing; DCP resistance can also be converted to CBR using a prescribed chart before applying the same equation [1]. For flexible pavements the resilient modulus is used directly, whereas for rigid pavements it is transformed into an effective subgrade reaction modulus (k) that is proportional to M_r and adjusted for seasonal conditions with base elastic moduli of 15,000 psi for spring‑thaw, 30,000 psi for normal periods, and 50,000 psi for frozen conditions; loss‑of‑support factors for supporting layers are combined using a weighted average formula and the resulting k is constrained so that the subbase‑to‑soil modulus ratio does not exceed four, with these calculations performed by the DARWin software package [1]. When subgrade strengthening is required, rock capping is modeled with a CBR of 20, while lime or cement stabilization employs a structural coefficient of 0.12 together with the project‑specific CBR; reinforced concrete pavement design uses a subgrade resilient modulus expressed as MRSG = 1,500 × CBR and entered into the ACPA StreetPave12 program to evaluate combined support and determine slab thickness [1]. Subgrade preparation starts by excavating the existing soil to an undisturbed condition while preserving permeability, removing eroded fines or ponded water with light equipment, and scarifying at least six inches deep using a spring‑tooth rake attached to a light tractor; the foundation is then shaped to the required horizontal lines and grades, forming the roadbed to the established top‑of‑subgrade elevation and compacting it in accordance with specified dry‑density targets of 97 % of maximum dry density for embankment foundations and 100 % for structure foundations and subgrades, while maintaining moisture content within ±3 % of the optimum determined by PTM No. 106 and verified with AASHTO T191 or T310 test strips and the direct‑transmission method of PTM No. 402 [2]. Compaction equipment is selected based on soil classification, with rollers operating at no more than 1.5 ft/s for a minimum six‑second pass, and special rolling is performed in the presence of the Representative who approves stability; permanent deflection limits of one‑half inch for existing subgrade and one inch for raised embankments are used to judge stability, and if these limits are exceeded the subgrade must be stabilized by additional compaction or by undercutting/excavating to a depth of one to three feet and backfilling with approved material and geotextile layers [2]. Proof rolling is continued until a uniform and firm surface is obtained, and any unstable spots identified during proof rolling are repaired to the Representative’s satisfaction before proceeding; grade and slope are verified using a taut string line stretched transversely across the subgrade or, when required, GPS or laser‑controlled grading equipment with a machine‑control quality‑control plan submitted at least one week prior to the preconstruction conference, and grading tolerances are checked at prescribed intervals of up to 500 ft for high‑accuracy systems with additional spot checks every 500 ft [2]. After achieving the required density and moisture criteria, the surface is sealed each day with a static smooth‑drum steel‑wheel roller to promote drainage, after which a geotextile is laid over the prepared subgrade with a minimum twelve‑inch overlap between sheets and extending six inches beyond any installed geocell; geocell panels are expanded and anchored, loose subbase material is placed in the cells and overfilled so that the compacted surface sits at least one inch above the geocell, and compaction follows the specified section, allowing a slightly greater lift thickness when cell height exceeds six inches [2]. For additional reinforcement, a Class 4 Type A geotextile is installed prior to geogrid placement, with uniaxial grids spaced up to two feet vertically and biaxial grids at twelve‑inch vertical spacings, overlapped by at least three inches (uniaxial) or one foot (biaxial); the grid must be covered with an eight‑inch loose backfill lift before any traffic or equipment can cross, the backfill drop height is limited to three feet, and subsequent compaction complies with the prescribed standards, while handling and storage of geogrid conform to ASTM D4873 [2].

The subgrade is identified and sized during the design phase by applying Subgrade Soil Evaluation procedures that require selection of an acceptable soil type and appropriate subbase thickness for each project location [1]. It is treated as a separate construction item, and when existing conditions are deficient the design incorporates an “as‑directed” undercut quantity to remediate poor zones [1][2]. The structural capacity of the subgrade is expressed through a resilient modulus (M_r) obtained from California Bearing Ratio (CBR) tests on soaked samples, using the standard conversion M_r = 1,500 × CBR [1]. All CBR testing must reflect seasonal moisture extremes and be reviewed with the District Geotechnical Engineer; values greater than ten require a recommendation and chief‑engineer approval, while values below five trigger mandatory documentation of improvement measures such as significant undercutting or other strengthening techniques [1]. Moreover, any CBR value exceeding twenty is prohibited in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation, thereby capping the allowable resilient modulus at thirty thousand psi [1]. For projects that are not collector or local roads, the resilient modulus must be confirmed by laboratory or field testing, and if a back‑calculated M_r exceeds fifteen thousand psi independent verification through methods such as falling weight deflectometer or dynamic cone penetrometer testing is required before it may be used in pavement analysis [1]. The effective modulus of reaction (k) for the subgrade is derived from the confirmed resilient modulus and must be adjusted for subbase type and thickness, loss‑of‑support factors, and depth to any rigid foundation, with the ratio of subbase modulus to soil resilient modulus limited to four [1]. Seasonal design values for the subgrade resilient modulus are prescribed at fifteen thousand psi for spring‑thaw conditions, thirty thousand psi for normal summer/fall conditions, and fifty thousand psi for frozen winter conditions [1]. For Interstate and Expressway rigid pavements the base layer shall consist of an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base over a 2A subbase; open‑graded subbases are expressly prohibited [1]. Reliability targets are embedded in the design criteria, with collector roads required to achieve a ninety percent reliability level and a terminal serviceability index of 2.5, while local roads target an eighty‑five percent reliability and a serviceability index of 2.0 [1]. The constructed subgrade must be placed exactly to the top‑of‑subgrade elevation shown on the plans and must remain flat, smooth and level, free of sharp protrusions, debris, ponded water, frost or any deleterious material [2]. Zones where erosion has deposited fines or caused standing water are required to be scarified to a minimum depth of six inches before compaction [2]. Compaction is performed in uniform horizontal lifts at speeds not exceeding 1.5 ft s⁻¹, with moisture content held within three percent below optimum for cohesive soils (or ±2 % of optimum for Type 1 granular material) and surface‑damp for Type 2 material [2]. All layers except the upper three feet must achieve at least ninety‑seven percent of the maximum dry density, while the top three feet—or any material located within three feet of the proposed grade, as well as structural foundations—must be compacted to one hundred percent of that density; embankment foundations situated more than three feet below design grade are also subject to the ninety‑seven percent minimum [2]. After each lift the surface is proof‑rolled, and permanent settlement greater than one‑half inch in existing or cut subgrade, spread footings, or rutting beyond this limit renders the area unsatisfactory; settlement exceeding one inch in embankment foundations is also unacceptable [2]. When these limits are exceeded the subgrade must be stabilized by undercutting—at least one foot for deflections of 0.5–3 inches and up to three feet for larger movements—followed by installation of a Class 4 Type A geotextile and appropriate backfill [2]. The finished subgrade surface is inspected for grade and slope at longitudinal intervals not exceeding twenty‑five feet (or up to fifty feet with automatic grading equipment and up to five hundred feet when GPS/laser‑controlled), and any irregularity greater than a half‑inch must be corrected by loosening the surface, adding or removing material as required, and recompacting [2]. After the subgrade has remained in place for more than sixty days or at least one winter season it is compacted and proof‑rolled again before the geotextile separator is placed over the entire lane width; no heavy‑equipment traffic or additional compaction is permitted after preparation until the geotextile and stone bed are installed [2]. Damaged areas must be repaired to the satisfaction of the Representative, who also inspects and approves the subgrade prior to placement of fill or any geosynthetic layers; field adjustments involving bedrock require authorization from the District Geotechnical Engineer [2]. When reinforcement is required a Class 4 Type A geotextile layer is placed on the prepared surface before any geogrid, with vertical spacing limits of two feet for uniaxial applications and twelve inches for biaxial systems; stabilization grids must be Class 2 biaxial geogrids having a longitudinal or transverse tensile capacity not less than two thousand pounds per foot, and no reduction factors are permitted [2].

Materials and mixture design

The subgrade specification begins by identifying the material that will form the foundation, which may be native in‑situ soil, borrow fill, Type 1 or Type 2 granular fill, rock, shale, random material, or a reclaimed base composed of existing road material, aggregates and any recycled asphalt pavement [1][2]. For each material class the required properties are enumerated: soils must be classified according to the AASHTO system with gradation indices Cu and Cc, percent passing the #200 sieve, Atterberg limits (liquid limit, plastic limit, plasticity index), calculated Group Index, maximum dry density and optimum moisture content, while granular fills are described by their type and aggregate size specifications [1][2]. The subgrade must also meet a minimum acceptable resilient modulus, which can be expressed directly or through laboratory CBR values as an equivalent for certain facilities; the appropriate value is obtained from testing representative samples under seasonal stress‑moisture conditions in coordination with the district geotechnical engineer [1]. Moisture control requirements are prescribed as follows: soils must be placed within three percent below their optimum moisture content, Type 1 granular fill within plus or minus two percent of its optimum, Type 2 fills must remain surface‑damp, and stabilized mixes may not exceed the optimum by more than three percent [2]. Compaction criteria are expressed as a percentage of laboratory maximum dry density: all material except that in the upper three feet must achieve at least ninety‑seven percent, while the top three feet—whether soil, Type 1 fill or other structure‑foundation subgrade—must be compacted to one hundred percent; reclaimed base mixtures are required to reach a minimum of ninety‑five percent [2]. Lift thickness limits are set at six inches for soils and Type 1 fills and eight inches for Type 2 fills, with coarse aggregate fills requiring a sufficient layer of No. 2A aggregate placed over the proof‑rolled surface to cap voids before further construction [2]. Prior to compaction the existing native soil must be excavated without disturbance, eroded fines removed, and the underlying material scarified at least six inches with a spring‑tooth rake; heavy equipment traffic is prohibited until geotextile and stone bedding are placed, and any damaged or overly compacted zones must be repaired to the representative’s satisfaction [2]. When subgrade strengthening is required the specification prescribes a stabilization method—cement, lime, calcium chloride, emulsified asphalt, or rock capping—and mandates laboratory determination of additive rates using tests that include gradation (hydrometer analysis), liquid and plastic limits, shrinkage limit, unconfined compressive strength, dry density, sulfate/chloride content, organic content and pH for lime stabilization [1]. The mix design for reclaimed base or stabilized subgrade must enumerate the constituents—FDR material, any added aggregates, RAP if used, stabilizing additives and water—in the proportions dictated by the project mix, with placement and compaction to achieve at least ninety‑five percent of the laboratory‑determined maximum dry density while maintaining moisture within three percent of optimum [2]. Geotextile requirements call for a Class 4 Type C fabric placed at the bottom of an undercut with a minimum eight inches of approved fill over it before compaction, and optional geogrids may be specified, consisting of polypropylene biaxial grids for confinement or polyester biaxial grids for reinforcement, each fabricated with integrally welded ribs, apertures large enough to interlock with surrounding material, and a tensile strength not less than two thousand pounds per foot in both principal directions, conforming to ASTM D4354 and stored according to ASTM D4873 [2]. The specification also identifies the type of subbase material placed beneath the pavement—such as asphalt‑treated base, cement‑treated base or a 2A subbase—and states its thickness in inches, assigning a loss‑of‑support factor to each layer to adjust the effective k‑value for potential erosion or pumping; the ratio of subbase modulus to the underlying roadbed soil resilient modulus shall not exceed four [1]. Seasonal design values for the base elastic modulus are provided as fifteen thousand psi for spring thaw conditions, thirty thousand psi for normal summer and fall, and fifty thousand psi for frozen winter, with any bedrock within ten feet of the surface incorporated into k‑value calculations through weighted averages of loss‑of‑support factors and layer depths [1].

Selection of subgrade materials begins with a thorough characterization of the existing ground through representative sampling and in‑situ stiffness testing such as falling weight deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer, and lightweight deflectometer, with results expressed primarily by California Bearing Ratio values that guide the pavement design [1]. The lowest CBR value identified across the project controls the design, and any isolated low‑CBR zones are improved so that the subgrade achieves a uniform CBR representative of overall conditions [1][2]. When the in‑place CBR is five or less, a cost‑benefit analysis for projects larger than 25 000 square yards on Traffic Pattern Groups 1‑4 typically justifies strengthening measures, which may be mechanical, chemical, or a combination thereof [1]. Mechanical strengthening can involve pulverizing the existing material and recompacting it with added aggregate or reclaimed asphalt pavement to improve gradation, while chemical stabilization employs cement, lime, or fly ash proportioned according to laboratory mix‑design tests that target the required resilient modulus without inducing excessive brittleness [1][2]. For very weak soils a rock cap modeled with CBR = 20 may be placed, and the stabilized layer is represented in design calculations with a structural coefficient of 0.12 while retaining the native CBR beneath it [1]. Geosynthetic reinforcement such as geogrids or Class‑4 geotextiles may be positioned at the subgrade‑base interface to increase durability and load distribution for weak soils, and any borrow material incorporated into the subgrade must satisfy minimum resilient modulus values established from laboratory or field testing to ensure structural capacity [1]. Prior to mix placement, deleterious or unsuitable soils are removed and zones that appear unstable are repaired; the surface is then proof‑rolled to obtain a uniform, firm platform before further treatment [2]. When the in‑situ material consists of coarse aggregate such as AASHTO No. 1 or No. 3, R‑rock, or shot rock, a cap layer of No. 2A aggregate is placed and compacted over the proof‑rolled surface to fill voids and irregularities before installing geosynthetics [2]. The subgrade mix is proportioned according to an approved design that may incorporate reclaimed native material together with additional aggregates, RAP, and any required stabilizing additives; water is added to achieve the optimum moisture content specified by the mix design but not exceeding it by more than three percent [2]. The blended material is mixed thoroughly, placed in lifts, and compacted to at least ninety‑five percent of the laboratory maximum dry density determined at the optimum moisture condition, thereby satisfying density criteria essential for constructability and performance [2]. Chemically stabilized mixes are required to develop an unconfined compressive strength between 300 and 500 psi within seven days, while asphalt‑stabilized mixes must attain a minimum indirect tensile strength of about 50 psi; meeting these strength thresholds ensures the subgrade provides the necessary structural capacity and durability under traffic and environmental loads [2]. For rigid‑pavement systems the subgrade is built by selecting an appropriate subbase type—either aggregate treated base or cement treated base—and a thickness that yields the required effective modulus of subgrade reaction, with elastic modulus values varying seasonally (15 000 psi for spring‑thaw, 30 000 psi for normal summer/fall, and 50 000 psi when frozen) [1]. A loss‑of‑support factor is applied to correct the effective k‑value for potential erosion or pumping, and the resulting subbase‑to‑soil resilient‑modulus ratio is limited to four to protect against excessive deformation, thereby integrating thickness, modulus, reinforcement, and proportioning criteria that satisfy structural demands, long‑term durability, constructability with practical layer sizes and equipment, and overall performance over the pavement’s design life [1].

Structural details and interfaces

Subgrade design does not rely on conventional joints or traditional load‑transfer devices; its structural integrity is achieved through an integrated drainage network, geosynthetic reinforcement and carefully detailed transitions [1][2]. Continuous base drains are installed along the pavement’s outer edge, on the low side of any superelevated sections, and on the median wherever subsurface water problems have been identified, while lateral underdrains are placed at every cut‑to‑fill transition and at other critical locations flagged by maintenance personnel; additional subgrade drains are provided in sag areas or where existing pavement shows signs of moisture damage [1]. Reinforcement is supplied primarily with geogrids positioned at the interface between the subgrade and overlying aggregate to stabilize weak soils, and may be supplemented by geocell panels that are overlapped a minimum of twelve inches and anchored at least twelve inches into the subgrade; uniaxial geogrid is installed in the primary reinforcement direction with vertical spacing not exceeding two feet and sheet overlaps of at least three inches, whereas biaxial grids—polypropylene for confinement and polyester for load distribution—are spaced as close as twelve inches vertically with edges overlapped a minimum of one foot [1][2]. A full‑width Class 4 Type A layer‑separation geotextile is laid longitudinally across the entire lane width before any subbase material, seams are prohibited and adjacent sheets overlap at least twelve inches (four feet when installed perpendicular to traffic), and when the California Bearing Ratio falls below three a Class 4 Type C stabilization geotextile is added to provide lateral restraint and reduce load stresses; a second Class 4 Type A geotextile may be placed over the compacted fill prior to placing at least six inches of subbase, with a minimum eight‑inch layer of approved material resting on the first geotextile before compaction [1][2]. Transition slopes at the beginning and end of any undercut are limited to an eight‑horizontal‑to‑one‑vertical ratio, and where abrupt changes in soil conditions occur a gradual transition is achieved using granular fill or a rock‑capping layer one to three feet thick composed of R‑3 rock, AASHTO No. 1 or the best available rock; when the subgrade consists of coarse aggregate or rock fill, a cap layer of No. 2A aggregate is placed and compacted over the proof‑rolled surface to fill voids before geosynthetic placement [1][2]. Overlapping joints of geotextiles and geogrids provide continuity, while anchors driven twelve inches into the subgrade serve as load‑transfer devices for geocell panels; adjacent geocell sections are joined by overlapping welded cell ends and secured with mechanical fasteners, and drainage trenches filled with No. 57 aggregate function both as moisture control and additional load‑transfer elements at undercut locations [2]. The preparation sequence begins with establishing the designed elevation using reference grade lines or stakes, followed by compaction and proof‑rolling to achieve a uniform firm surface; each day the finished subgrade surface is sealed with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller to promote drainage and protect the prepared layer [2].

Subgrade design begins with a comprehensive assessment of the existing soil condition; when weak or moisture‑sensitive soils are identified, an overlay is used to protect the unsuitable material and raise the grade line away from high‑moisture zones while coordinating geometry at adjacent features to promote uniform load distribution and control project cost impacts [1]. Preparation then proceeds by excavating to an undisturbed condition, removing eroded fines and ponded water, scarifying the soil a minimum of six inches with light equipment such as a spring‑tooth rake, repairing damaged or compacted zones, and proof‑rolling to produce a uniform, firm, permeable plane before any geosynthetic is placed [2]. All contacts between the subgrade and adjacent elements—including shoulder widths, embankment slopes, guide‑rail locations, vertical alignments at structures, curbs, gutters, structural walls, and adjoining pavements—are treated as engineered interfaces, with geometry coordinated to support uniform loading and to mitigate cost implications [1][2]. After subgrade preparation, drainage provisions are installed prior to the subbase; trench drains in pavement and shoulder zones are laid with a minimum slope of one‑quarter inch per foot and extend within six to twelve inches of the interior edge of the pavement base drain, with outlet ends kept open through construction [2]. A continuous Class 4 Type A geotextile separator is then placed longitudinally over the entire lane width (minimum eight feet), extending transversely to within six to twelve inches of the interior edge of the pavement base drain; adjacent sheets overlap at least twelve inches, the fabric is positioned within ±½ inch of design elevation, anchored and protected during subsequent work [2]. Where abrupt changes in soil type, cut‑fill boundaries, or new pavement abuts an existing section of different total depth occur, gradual transitions are provided using granular, non‑frost‑susceptible material to maintain drainage, prevent differential heave, and achieve uniform subgrade performance across all engineered interfaces [1]. At the interface between subgrade and aggregate base, a geogrid is installed in accordance with Publication 408 to strengthen the weak soil‑aggregate bond; the geogrid is laid directly over the Class 4 Type A geotextile, with edges overlapping at least one foot (minimum three inches for uniaxial grids) and vertical spacing between successive grid layers not exceeding twelve inches [1][2]. When dissimilar materials such as subbase and subgrade must remain distinct—particularly when the California Bearing Ratio falls below three—a Class 4 geotextile is used, employing Type A fabric for pure separation or Type C when combined separation and reinforcement are required; at the bottom of undercut transitions a Type C geotextile is placed followed by at least eight inches of approved fill before compaction [1][2]. Undercut transitions themselves are limited to an 8H:1V slope ratio, with the transition constructed accordingly, a second layer of Class 4 Type A geotextile laid over the fill, and a minimum six‑inch subbase placed on that layer; special rolling equipment is kept at least ten feet from bridge abutments or approach slabs [2][1]. Load transfer across transverse joints in overlying concrete pavement sections is achieved by dowelling all patch joints and anchoring the dowels with epoxy, polyester, or vinylester binders; joint spacing follows prescribed limits of 30 ft for reinforced‑concrete sections and 15 ft for plain‑concrete sections, each joint being provided with appropriate seals and coated dowel bars [1]. Prior to asphalt placement, all adjacent vertical features that will contact the pavement—curbs, gutters, structural walls, and adjoining pavements—receive a thin, uniform coat of emulsified asphalt applied in multiple passes to ensure proper bonding at those interfaces [2]. Subgrade material for each lift is sourced from a single borrow location, placed in uniform horizontal layers and compacted to specified moisture‑density targets; each layer is shaped to provide positive drainage, fine‑graded to eliminate low spots, and sealed daily with a smooth drum roller, prohibiting furrows that would impede drainage [2]. Compaction adjacent to structures follows the details shown on standard drawings, and the final surface is finished with a static‑mode smooth drum roller after proof‑rolling [2]. The overall design of subgrade interfaces thus integrates soil assessment, controlled geometry at structural contacts, geosynthetic separation and reinforcement, drainage provisions, undercut transition slopes, load‑transfer detailing, and surface preparation to achieve uniform support and durability for the pavement system [1][2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Subgrade design begins with a systematic survey to locate zones of excess moisture or intersecting water tables and treats those areas before any reclaimed layer is placed because uncontrolled water degrades material integrity and reduces structural capacity.[1] Wet subgrade locations are identified and effective drainage installed prior to final preparation, with lateral underdrains placed in wet sections and at every cut‑to‑fill transition, sized to lower water tables to a positive outlet.[1][2] Subgrade drains are constructed beneath the subbase after the subgrade is completed, extending sufficiently to remove water from the wet area, maintaining a minimum trench‑bottom slope of one‑quarter inch per foot across pavement and shoulder zones, and keeping outlet ends open through construction until final acceptance.[2] Where a reliable positive outlet exists an asphalt‑ or cement‑treated permeable base may be used; otherwise a dense base with longitudinal edge drains is required and the use of open‑graded subbase is prohibited for concrete projects.[1] High‑permeability base and subbase materials are placed on adequately sloped layers so that free water can be removed by gravity, and drainage capacity is verified by infiltration testing in accordance with ASTM C1701 requiring an average rate greater than 200 inches per hour.[1][2] Frost susceptibility is addressed either by removing frost‑prone soils or covering them with a sufficiently thick layer of non‑frost‑susceptible granular material that insulates the roadbed, and rock fill replaces soil wherever the foundation is saturated, waterlogged, or otherwise unstable; frozen material may not be placed.[1][2] Seasonal variations in moisture and temperature are reflected in resilient‑modulus values used for design, with spring values typically ten to twenty percent lower than summer values and frozen conditions roughly twice the warm‑season value, a base elastic modulus of 15 000 psi adopted for spring‑thaw conditions, and a loss‑of‑support factor applied to account for potential erosion or pumping of the subbase.[1] Laboratory testing on representative samples must simulate stress‑moisture states of different seasons, identifying soils with high fines content or elevated liquid limits as moisture‑sensitive and more prone to frost heave, which influences compaction targets and optimum moisture contents.[1] Compaction is performed within a moisture range from minus three percent to the optimum moisture content, never exceeding optimum by more than three percent; if the soil is too wet it is dried by disking or scarifying before compaction, while if it is too dry water is added to reach optimum.[2] Each lift of subgrade is fine‑graded and shaped to eliminate low spots and furrows that would impede runoff, and the surface of each day’s work is sealed with a static‑mode smooth‑drum steel wheel roller to promote continuous positive drainage and limit infiltration.[2] After the subgrade has remained in place for more than sixty days or through one winter season it is re‑compacted and proof‑rolled, then a full‑width Class 4 Type A geotextile separator is laid to protect the subgrade from moisture and environmental effects while providing a stable base for the subbase.[2] When chemical stabilization is used the reclaimed layer is kept continuously damp either by daily watering or by applying an emulsified asphalt primer, allowing curing rates to respond to ambient weather; dry sunny conditions accelerate strength gain while rain retards it, and pervious concrete or pavement mixtures are prohibited when air temperatures are projected below forty (or fifty) degrees Fahrenheit or when rain is forecast before final finishing and curing.[2] Reinforced concrete pavement is not placed on a frozen base, subbase, or subgrade to ensure temperature conditions do not compromise the foundation.[2] All geosynthetic materials are stored elevated and protected from temperatures above one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit and direct sunlight to prevent degradation prior to installation.[2]

Verification and expected performance

Verification of a completed subgrade is achieved through coordinated field and laboratory examinations that together confirm compliance with design stiffness, strength, compaction, grade, slope, and overall project requirements. After construction the subgrade is subjected to a post‑construction Falling Weight Deflectometer test to obtain the in‑place modulus and verify that it meets the stiffness values prescribed in the design; complementary Dynamic Cone Penetrometer and Lightweight Deflectometer tests are also run to provide additional strength and deformation data. Simultaneously, grade and slope are checked against the reference lines by stretching a taut transverse string line across the work at intervals not greater than 25 ft (or longer where GPS/laser control is documented), and any deviation exceeding one‑half inch is identified with the string, a ten‑foot straightedge or the approved machine‑control QC plan and corrected by reshaping and recompacting. When GPS or laser grading is employed, stakes are set at prescribed intervals and checks are made at hinge points, centerline, lane edges, shoulders and random locations every 500 ft; if more than ten percent of those checks exceed the half‑inch tolerance, additional verification with string line and stakes is required. Compaction quality is established in the presence of the Representative through special rolling, with moisture‑density tests taken at random locations after each pass according to AASHTO T191 or 310, and compaction continues until successive passes produce less than a one‑percent increase in density. The target moisture content must be within –3 % of optimum for soils or ±2 % for Type 1 granular material, and the achieved dry density must reach at least 97 % of maximum dry density, with the upper three feet required to attain 100 % of maximum dry density. For reclaimed base material a control strip is compacted to a minimum of 95 % of laboratory‑maximum dry density and verified by ten random nuclear density readings per 3 000 sq yd. At the end of each workday the surface is fine‑graded with a smooth drum roller operating in static mode at no more than 1.5 ft/s to eliminate low areas, prevent rutting and provide continuous positive drainage. Stability of the subgrade is then evaluated by special‑rolling deflection measurements, and permanent deflection or rutting greater than one‑half inch for existing cuts or more than one inch for embankment foundations is deemed unsatisfactory, triggering additional compaction, undercutting, excavation, replacement, or installation of specified geotextiles until the required performance is achieved. Representative soil samples are taken from locations defined by the sampling plan and sent to a laboratory where moisture‑density, unconfined compressive strength, classification, and either resilient modulus or California Bearing Ratio tests are performed. The CBR result is cross‑checked against accompanying data on gradation, percent passing #200, Atterberg limits, group index, maximum dry density and optimum moisture content to confirm that the soil can achieve the assumed resilient modulus, and values falling outside the typical range for the identified soil type—specifically those above ten or below five—must be reviewed and approved by the District Geotechnical Engineer (and ultimately the Chief Geotechnical Engineer). Seasonal moisture variations are addressed by obtaining modulus values under differing moisture conditions to ensure that the subgrade will perform throughout the design period. Constructability is demonstrated through two test sections of at least 225 sq ft each constructed on the finished subgrade using the approved mix and placement procedures; infiltration testing per ASTM C‑1701 must yield an average rate exceeding 200 in/hr, and the compacted thickness may not be more than one‑quarter inch below the design thickness. The subgrade is proof‑rolled to verify a uniform, firm surface and to confirm that grade and level conform to the plan lines before final acceptance. Any deleterious or unsuitable soils are removed, the underlying material scarified to a minimum depth of six inches with a spring‑tooth rake, and repaired to the Representative’s satisfaction. After a seven‑day cure period specimens from each lot are tested for strength—minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi for asphalt‑stabilized material or compressive strength of 300–500 psi for chemically stabilized material—and any lot failing density, surface irregularity, or strength criteria is reworked. When all grade, slope, moisture‑density, deflection, infiltration, thickness, strength and laboratory test requirements are satisfied, the Representative issues final acceptance, confirming that the completed subgrade complies with constructability, performance and project specifications. The subgrade must provide a stable foundation that supports the intended design life of the pavement while limiting distress and maintaining ride quality [1][2]. A primary performance target is a twenty‑year service life for reinforced concrete pavements, with slab cracking limited to no more than fifteen percent at the end of that period and terminal serviceability values of 2.5 for collector roads and 2.0 for local roads serving as ride‑quality indicators [1]. To achieve these outcomes the subgrade is required to exhibit sufficient bearing capacity, measured primarily by the California Bearing Ratio, which must exceed five; values above ten require engineering approval and any value greater than twenty is excluded from use in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation [1]. The resilient modulus of the subgrade is calculated from the CBR using Mr = 1,500 × CBR and entered into pavement design software to verify reliability levels of ninety percent for collector roads and eighty‑five percent for local roads, with back‑calculation from Falling Weight Deflectometer data or conversion of Dynamic Cone Penetration results used as independent checks; any back‑calculated modulus exceeding fifteen thousand psi must be confirmed by an additional test [1]. In addition to bearing capacity, the subgrade must resist excessive permanent deformation, with allowable permanent deflection after special rolling limited to one‑half inch for existing cuts or spread footings and up to one inch for embankment foundations, a limit that is demonstrated by measuring the residual settlement under a Representative during compaction [2]. Compaction quality is verified through moisture‑density testing in accordance with AASHTO T191 or T310, requiring a dry density of one hundred percent of the laboratory maximum within three feet of the design elevation and at least ninety‑seven percent for deeper layers; the top three feet of fill must reach one hundred percent of its maximum dry density while maintaining moisture content within plus or minus three percent of optimum, and reclaimed material may be accepted at a minimum of ninety‑five percent [2]. In‑place density for these zones is confirmed using the direct transmission nuclear gauge method prescribed in PTM No. 402 on designated control strips [2]. Where chemically or asphalt‑stabilized fill is employed, the stabilized layer must develop an indirect tensile strength of at least fifty pounds per square inch within seven days for asphalt‑stabilized material and an unconfined compressive strength between three hundred and five hundred psi for chemically stabilized mixes, as measured by the respective laboratory procedures [2]. Surface smoothness is also a performance criterion; irregularities are checked over a ten‑foot straightedge template and any three‑thousand‑square‑yard area may not exceed an average deviation of one‑half inch, with cross‑slope required to meet design minima [2]. If any of the specified limits for CBR, resilient modulus, permanent deflection, density, strength, or surface tolerance are exceeded, corrective actions such as re‑compaction, undercutting, or additional stabilization must be undertaken before the pavement can be considered ready for service [1][2].
