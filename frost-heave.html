Frost Heave

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Frost Heave is a topic under Pavement Design.
frost-heave
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-23

Frost heave is the upward movement of pavement that occurs when moisture‑laden, frost‑susceptible soils beneath a subgrade freeze and expand, temporarily reducing bearing support and producing differential uplift that can exceed the structure’s capacity [1]. This phenomenon leads to loss of serviceability—manifested as cracking, reduced ride quality, and weakened support during subsequent thaw periods—and therefore must be anticipated in pavement design through serviceability‑loss criteria and dedicated frost‑design provisions [1].

Purpose and design objectives

Frost heave represents the fundamental engineering problem of loss of pavement serviceability caused by upward movement and temporary reduction in subgrade support when moisture‑laden soils freeze and later thaw. The expansion of water in frost‑susceptible subgrades generates differential uplift that can exceed bearing capacity, produce cracking, and degrade ride quality; during spring thaws the weakened subgrade further reduces support, especially for rigid pavements where added slab thickness provides only limited mitigation. Consequently pavement designs must anticipate this serviceability loss—incorporating it into terminal serviceability criteria for flexible systems and applying specific frost‑design guidance for rigid structures—and employ measures such as effective subsurface drainage to remove water, removal or replacement of frost‑sensitive soils with insulating layers, and provision of a stronger base course rather than relying solely on increased structural thickness. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 8 (p. 164), Ch. 9 (pp. 168–169)

Frost‑heave design must ensure that the pavement retains a high level of serviceability over its intended life by preventing differential heaving, preserving bearing support during freeze–thaw cycles, and protecting the structural materials from frost‑induced damage; this is achieved through removal or insulation of frost‑susceptible soils, provision of an adequately thick non‑frost‑susceptible layer, a stronger base course capable of supporting reduced bearing capacity, and appropriate drainage and gradual subgrade transitions to maintain safety and functional performance. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Scope and applicability

Frost‑heave design considerations are appropriate for any pavement whose subgrade or base can become saturated with water that subsequently freezes. This includes both concrete pavements—whether plain or receiving an asphalt overlay—and full‑depth flexible (asphalt) pavements, particularly where fine, low‑permeability soils, poor subsurface drainage, trapped water under joints, cut‑to‑fill transitions, or frozen layers beneath a thawing subgrade are present. Whenever the subgrade contains frost‑susceptible soils and freezing temperatures are expected, the approach must be applied to flexible pavements; for rigid (concrete) pavements it is invoked only when differential frost heave is anticipated or when an adequate granular foundation is lacking.

The design procedure for flexible pavements requires determining a frost‑heave rate from the appropriate tables based on subgrade soil type, estimating the depth of frost penetration using the local Design Freezing Index, and applying a probability of occurrence typically between 25 % and 75 %. These inputs are used with AASHTO serviceability‑loss charts to adjust the terminal serviceability index for the project. For rigid pavements, standard design provides sufficient reserve capacity unless differential frost heave is expected; in that case the dedicated Frost Design section should be consulted rather than relying on increased slab thickness, which has limited effectiveness.

Mitigation measures prescribed for both pavement types involve removing highly frost‑susceptible soils and replacing them with a non‑frost‑susceptible layer of sufficient thickness to insulate the roadbed from frost penetration, providing a stronger base course, and installing adequate drainage to prevent moisture accumulation. When an adequate granular foundation is already present under a rigid pavement, additional frost‑heave treatment is generally unnecessary. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 8 (p. 164), Ch. 9 (pp. 168–169)

When an adequate granular foundation can be provided—especially beneath rigid pavements—or when the existing subgrade is not highly susceptible to frost, designers should favor alternatives such as a stronger base course, improved drainage, or selective grading rather than relying on increased pavement thickness for frost‑heave mitigation. If frost‑susceptible material is present, the preferred first action is its removal and replacement with a sufficiently thick layer of non‑frost‑susceptible fill that insulates the roadbed from freezing temperatures; increasing slab depth alone is justified only when removal is impractical or when full‑depth flexible designs demand additional treatment. For rigid pavements, simply adding thickness offers only marginal benefit against significant differential frost heave, so the limited success of a thicker structural design makes dedicated frost‑design strategies—such as those outlined in the Frost Design section—more appropriate. In cases where wholesale replacement is not feasible, other subgrade improvement methods are chosen when they can achieve required performance without excessive structural increase. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 8 (p. 164)

Design inputs and requirements

Control of frost heave in PennDOT projects is dictated by drainage specifications that require subsurface drainage systems to be sized and spaced according to Publication 13M, Design Manual Part 2, Highway Design Chapter 10 Section 10.3.D, with each base‑drain outlet protected by a headwall as prescribed in Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards. The standards further call for lateral underdrains at wet subgrade zones and cut‑to‑fill transitions, and continuous edge drains on both Interstate and non‑Interstate alignments wherever subsurface water poses a problem. Frost‑heave performance requirements are established by the 1993 AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures, Appendix G (Section G.2). Designers must obtain a frost‑heave rate from Table 9.1 or Table 9.2 based on subgrade soil type, determine the maximum serviceability loss using Figure G.7 together with the frost‑penetration depth derived from the Design Freezing Index in Appendix D, and then apply a probability range of 25 % to 75 % as directed by the District Geotechnical Engineer when employing Figure G.8. The calculated loss is added to the terminal serviceability value required in Table 6.3 to meet the pavement’s performance criteria. When soils are identified as highly frost‑susceptible, the guidance recommends their removal and replacement with a sufficiently thick layer of non‑frost‑susceptible material that insulates the subgrade; if removal is not feasible, a stronger base course combined with appropriate drainage may be used. For flexible pavements this frost‑heave treatment is mandatory, whereas rigid pavement designs rely on saturated subgrade strength and invoke additional measures only when differential frost heave is anticipated. In such cases for rigid pavements, designers are instructed to consult Section 6.2.B (Frost Design), recognizing that increasing slab thickness provides limited mitigation. The overall requirements are supplemented by the recommendations in NCHRP Synthesis 26 for seasonal‑frost roadway design. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 8 (p. 164), Ch. 9 (pp. 168–169)

Design methods and criteria

In frost‑prone regions, the design methodology begins with removal of frost‑susceptible soils and replacement by a sufficiently thick layer of non‑frost‑susceptible material to act as thermal insulation. After that, a stronger base course is provided to compensate for the reduced bearing capacity of the underlying subgrade, and appropriate drainage or gradual transitions in subgrade stiffness are incorporated to limit differential heave. Because AASHTO does not endorse increasing overall pavement thickness as a remedy, the loss of serviceability caused by frost heave must be quantified using the procedure set out in Appendix G of the 1993 AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures. The quantitative component is obtained by first selecting an appropriate frost‑heave rate from the tables that correspond to the identified subgrade type, then calculating the depth of frost penetration from the Design Freezing Index using the prescribed figure and reading the associated maximum potential serviceability loss for the prevailing drainage condition. A probability of occurrence—typically ranging between 25 % and 75 %—is assigned by the geotechnical engineer; the rate, depth‑derived loss, and probability are plotted on the serviceability‑loss chart to determine the estimated reduction, which is added to the terminal serviceability index. Since the department’s design software (DARWin) lacks a frost‑heave module, these calculations are performed with external tools: an online Frost Heave Calculator hosted in the ECMS file cabinet and a spreadsheet tool supplied on PennDOT’s PDAU web page. [1]

Design for frost heave must first address any frost‑susceptible soils by either excavating them and backfilling with a non‑frost‑susceptible material of sufficient thickness to prevent frost penetration, or by providing an insulating layer that meets the same protective function. The pavement structure is required to incorporate a stronger base course capable of sustaining reduced bearing capacity during freeze‑thaw cycles, and this requirement applies universally to full‑depth flexible pavements; rigid pavements are treated only when justified on a case‑by‑case basis. Adequate drainage and gradual transitions in the subgrade are also mandated to limit differential heaving. In the performance prediction for flexible pavements, the terminal serviceability index is increased by the loss of serviceability attributable to frost heave. That loss is quantified from three inputs: a frost‑heave rate selected from the appropriate subgrade‑soil tables (Table 9.1 or 9.2), the maximum potential serviceability loss derived from the calculated depth of frost penetration using the Design Freezing Index together with the assessed drainage quality, and an assumed probability of frost heave that is constrained to a range of 25 % to 75 % as directed by the district geotechnical engineer. The design life in years provides the time axis on the AASHTO chart for evaluating this loss, and when markedly different soil types occur within the project area separate calculations and designs must be performed. Serviceability loss is evaluated using the AASHTO Appendix G procedure. [1]

Materials and mixture design

To mitigate frost‑heave effects the design must first eliminate frost‑susceptible soils by excavating them and backfilling with a sufficiently thick layer of non‑frost‑susceptible granular material that insulates the subgrade from freezing. The pavement structure should then incorporate a stronger base course capable of bearing reduced support, and appropriate drainage (e.g., base drains) should be provided to remove excess water. Uniformity of the subgrade is achieved through selective grading, blending or mixing of soils and by using gradual granular transitions at changes in soil type or fill‑cut interfaces. These measures together satisfy structural integrity, durability, constructability and long‑term performance requirements for frost‑prone locations. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Structural details and interfaces

To mitigate frost heave the pavement design must replace frost‑susceptible soils with a sufficiently thick layer of non‑frost material, provide a stronger base course, install pavement base drains, and incorporate gradual subgrade transitions—typically granular or embankment fill—at any change in soil type, groundwater condition, or cut‑fill interface; reinforcement can be achieved by placing geogrids at the subgrade‑aggregate interface and using class 4 geotextiles to separate or stabilize the subgrade and subbase layers. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

To mitigate frost‑heave effects, the interface between the subgrade and overlying layers should be treated by removing any frost‑susceptible soil and replacing it with a sufficiently thick layer of non‑frost‑susceptible granular material that insulates the roadbed from freezing. A stronger base course is then provided to carry reduced bearing capacity, and drainage (e.g., pavement‑base drains) is installed at the base interface to remove excess water. Where subgrade conditions change abruptly, designers should create gradual transitions using blended or graded fill or a granular non‑frost‑susceptible subbase, and may employ geogrids or Class 4 geotextiles at the subgrade‑aggregate interface for reinforcement and separation. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

In frost‑heave design the engineer first screens the subgrade for soils that are highly susceptible to heaving; those soils are removed and replaced with a sufficiently thick layer of non‑frost‑susceptible material that insulates the roadbed from freezing temperatures. A stronger base course is then provided to sustain the reduced bearing capacity that can develop under frozen conditions, and drainage is enhanced by installing pavement‑base drains or other appropriate subsurface drains. High‑permeability granular materials are specified for the base and subbase so that gravity‑drainable water is removed rapidly, while closely spaced lateral underdrains are placed in wet subgrade areas and at cut‑to‑fill transitions. Drain outlets are protected against crushing or plugging to ensure prompt removal of surface runoff and to prevent ponded water from infiltrating and freezing. Positive longitudinal slopes are maintained through selective grading, blending, boulder removal, and gradual granular transitions, which keep groundwater below the pavement and limit upward migration of moisture during freeze‑thaw cycles. The depth of frost penetration is determined from the local Design Freezing Index and entered into the AASHTO chart assuming a drainage quality classified as FAIR to estimate the maximum potential serviceability loss. An estimated probability that frost heave will occur, typically taken between 25 % and 75 % for the district, is applied together with the design life to compute total serviceability loss, which is then incorporated into the terminal serviceability index for pavement design. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 9 (pp. 168–169)

Verification and expected performance

Frost‑heave design is directed toward preserving the pavement’s serviceability over its intended life by preventing differential heaving, loss of bearing support during thaw periods, and freeze‑thaw damage to the structural layers; these objectives are reflected in reduced cracking, rutting and other distress and in a ride quality that remains within acceptable limits. The expected performance is quantified through the loss of serviceability due to frost heave, which is calculated according to the procedure set out in the 1993 AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures (Appendix G) or by using the online Frost Heave Calculator. For full‑depth flexible pavements the terminal serviceability index from Table 6.3 is adjusted upward by the estimated loss, ΔPSIFH, which is derived from three inputs: the frost‑heave rate obtained from subgrade tables, the maximum potential serviceability loss based on frost penetration depth and drainage quality, and the probability that frost heave will occur. The resulting ΔPSIFH value is added to the base terminal serviceability index, providing a measurable indicator of the expected reduction in serviceability and thereby confirming whether the design meets the targeted service life, distress resistance, and ride‑quality criteria.
