Frost Heave

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Frost Heave is a topic under Pavement Design.
frost-heave
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-28

Frost heave is a frost‑action phenomenon that occurs when moisture in frost‑susceptible soils beneath a pavement freezes and expands, causing the ground to swell upward; the resulting uplift often appears as localized tenting of concrete joints or uneven surface irregularities within the pavement structure[1]. It develops wherever wet subgrade or base material is present—most notably in concrete pavements, asphalt overlays on concrete, and flexible (asphalt) pavements built over moisture‑prone subgrades—and is aggravated by inadequate subsurface drainage that traps water, producing non‑uniform heaving[1]. The differential uplift reduces bearing support during spring‑thaw or warm winter periods, degrades ride quality, and can lead to transverse cracking, surface rutting, and a measurable loss of serviceability[1].

Description and identification

In section 2.3 DRAINAGE, frost heave occurs when moisture in the soil freezes and expands, causing the ground to swell; the swelling is often nonuniform in location and magnitude, making it especially detrimental to pavement performance. When water becomes trapped beneath a deteriorated joint, freezing can produce tenting—an upward movement of the joint that appears as localized heave at the surface or within the pavement structure. In section 6.2 SUBGRADE SOILS > INTENTIONALLY BLANK, frost heave is described as a frost‑action phenomenon that causes differential heaving of the pavement, loss of bearing support during spring‑thaw or warm winter periods, and freeze/thaw damage to the materials themselves; it manifests as uneven uplift or surface irregularities within the pavement structure when moisture in frost‑susceptible soils freezes and expands. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Frost heave is identified by a swelling of the pavement where wet soil expands as it freezes, often appearing as localized upward movement or tenting of concrete joints and is especially problematic when the heaving occurs non‑uniformly across the surface. It differs from transverse cracking that develops when a saturated subgrade loses support under traffic loads, from surface rutting that mirrors deformation of a softened subgrade, and from shrinkage cracks that are purely thermal and not moisture related. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59)

Where it occurs

In section 2.3 DRAINAGE the source notes that frost heave occurs when wet soil beneath a pavement expands upon freezing, producing an upward swelling that can damage the structure; the phenomenon is specifically mentioned for concrete pavements and also for asphalt overlays placed on concrete, where trapped moisture in joints may freeze and cause tenting of the joint surfaces, and it states that any pavement type built over moisture‑prone subgrade is susceptible, with concrete and its asphalt overlays explicitly called out. In section 6.2 SUBGRADE SOILS > INTENTIONALLY BLANK frost heave is described as a concern for flexible (asphalt) pavements and, when justified, for rigid (concrete) pavements; the design guidance requires that treatment for frost heave be included in full‑depth flexible pavement designs, while it is generally not needed for rigid pavements unless significant justification exists. Overlays that are part of a full‑depth flexible system inherit this requirement, whereas shoulders are not specifically addressed. In section 8.11 CALCULATED DESIGN THICKNESS the guidance for rigid‑pavement design states that frost heave is considered only for rigid (concrete) pavements; there are no specific frost design requirements for them, and any expected differential frost heave should be addressed per Section 6.2.B, with the note that increasing slab thickness offers limited success in mitigating differential frost heave on rigid pavements. In section 9.3 TERMINAL SERVICEABILITY frost heave is accounted for in the serviceability loss calculations of full‑depth flexible pavements, indicating it is a distress considered for asphalt (flexible) pavement structures; concrete, composite, overlays and shoulders are not mentioned in this context. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 8 (p. 164), Ch. 9 (pp. 168–169)

In the 2.3 DRAINAGE context, frost heave develops where moisture in the base or subgrade soils freezes and expands, so the phenomenon is rooted in the wet soil beneath the pavement structure; consequently frost heave can be observed both in the underlying base/subgrade layers and locally at compromised joints, with joint tenting occurring when a deteriorated joint traps water that later freezes and pushes the joint upward. In the 6.2 SUBGRADE SOILS context, frost heave originates in frost‑susceptible soils of the roadbed subgrade and may produce differential heaving that stresses the overlying base course and its interfaces with other layers; it is not described as occurring at joints, cracks, corners, edges, wheel paths, or within slab interiors. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Causes and mechanisms

Frost heave occurs because wet soil expands when it freezes, so the combination of freezing temperatures and a supply of water in the subgrade is identified as the primary cause. Inadequate subsurface drainage that traps moisture intensifies the phenomenon, producing non‑uniform heaving and joint tenting. The presence of frost‑susceptible soils further contributes, with differential heaving and loss of bearing support during spring‑thaw or warm winter periods arising from excess moisture. Design and construction practices that fail to remove highly frost‑susceptible material, provide a non‑frost‑susceptible layer, install stronger base courses, or incorporate pavement‑base drains can exacerbate the problem. Traffic loading is not cited as a cause in any of the excerpts. The design procedure described in Section 9.3 requires determining the frost‑heave rate from Table 9.1 or Table 9.2 according to subgrade soil type, using a recommended frost‑heave probability range of 25 %–75 % for flexible designs, and accounting for the depth of frost penetration based on climatic conditions and a FAIR drainage quality. The distress accelerates whenever frost‑susceptible soils, freezing temperatures, and a supply of water coexist, especially under varying groundwater conditions or abrupt subgrade changes such as different soil types, cross‑trenches, shallow bedrock, or large stones. Severity levels and rating

The rating procedure for frost‑heave severity is taken from the AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures (1993) Appendix G, Section G.2, which supplies a serviceability‑loss rating that is added to the terminal serviceability value in full‑depth flexible pavement design as described in Chapter 9, Subsection 9.3 of Publication 242 [1]. In the example presented in Publication 242, a frost‑heave loss of 0.75 serviceability units is applied, producing a terminal serviceability index of 3.75 (3.0 + 0.75) [1]. The incidental construction specifications for high‑tension cable barrier systems refer to the severity rating called the Maximum Potential Serviceability Loss Due to Frost Heave that appears in Publication 242, Section 9.3, Item 2, and this rating represents the highest level of serviceability loss that frost heave can cause [2]. Accordingly, the required depth for line‑post foundations is tied to the frost‑depth assessment defined by that Maximum Potential Serviceability Loss criterion (i.e., construction of the bottom elevation 6 inches below the minimum frost‑depth penetration determined according to Publication 242, Section 9.3, Item 2) [2].

Effects on performance

According to section 2.3 Drainage, frost heave occurs when moisture in a poorly drained subgrade expands upon freezing, which reduces the support provided by the subgrade and therefore diminishes the pavement’s structural capacity; the resulting non‑uniform upward movement of joints (tenting) and surface undulations degrade ride quality and can compromise safety, while the accelerated loss of aggregate strength and base stability shortens durability and overall service life. In section 6.2 Subgrade Soils, frost action damages pavements by causing differential heaving, reducing bearing support during spring‑thaw or warm winter periods, and producing freeze/thaw damage to the materials themselves, which together lower structural capacity and compromise the intended long‑term serviceability; because these mechanisms arise only when frost‑susceptible soils, freezing temperatures, and water are present, they can create uneven surfaces that degrade ride quality and safety. The design response recommended in the Frost Design section is to remove highly susceptible soils and replace them with a sufficiently thick non‑frost‑susceptible layer or to provide a stronger base course and appropriate drainage, thereby mitigating loss of support and extending durability and service life. Section 8.11 Calculated Design Thickness notes that rigid pavements designed using saturated subgrade strength tests already possess ample reserve structural capacity to accommodate periods of reduced subgrade support during spring thaws, so the phenomenon does not typically compromise the pavement’s load‑bearing ability; however, attempting to counteract differential frost heave by simply increasing slab thickness is generally of limited effectiveness for rigid pavements, indicating that additional mitigation measures may be required if significant heave is anticipated. Section 9.3 Terminal Serviceability states that frost heave introduces a measurable loss of serviceability that must be added to the terminal serviceability value; an estimated frost‑heave loss of 0.75 raises the required terminal serviceability index from 3.0 to 3.75, indicating lower ride quality, safety and durability over the design life. Because this loss is derived from frost‑heave rate, depth of frost penetration and a probability typically taken as 25 %–75 %, designers must treat the reduction explicitly in flexible‑pavement designs to preserve structural capacity and service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 8 (p. 164), Ch. 9 (pp. 168–169)

In the drainage context (section 2.3), when frost heave is left untreated the expansion of wet soil upon freezing lifts pavement layers, producing localized upward movement—often described as tenting—of concrete joints where trapped water freezes under a deteriorated joint; this tenting reduces subgrade support and leads to transverse cracks under traffic loads, especially during spring thaw. For asphalt pavements the same non‑uniform heave softens the base so that vehicle loads can rut the softened subgrade, creating surface ruts, roughness and a noticeable loss of ride quality. In the subgrade soils context (section 6.2), untreated frost heave also results in differential heaving of the pavement, a loss of bearing support capacity during spring‑thaw or warm winter periods caused by excess moisture, and freeze‑thaw damage to the pavement materials themselves; these mechanisms together cause uneven surface movement, reduced structural support and material degradation. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Repairs and treatments

Subsurface drainage systems—including lateral underdrains, continuous pavement base or edge drains, and subgrade drains—are prescribed to mitigate frost heave by removing the water whose expansion when frozen causes the soil to swell; they are applied wherever subsurface water is a problem, such as wet subgrade areas, transitions from cut to fill, sag zones, or locations identified by the local maintenance representative (section 2.3) [1]. When soils are identified as highly susceptible to frost heave, those soils should be removed and replaced with a non‑frostsusceptible insulating layer (section 6.2) [1]. As an alternative basic design approach for a frost‑susceptible subgrade, a stronger base course capable of bearing reduced support is provided (section 6.2) [1]. Installation of pavement base drains is advised as an extra measure to lessen differential heaving even after other frost‑heave treatments are applied (section 6.2) [1]. Selective grading, blending, mixing of subgrade soils and boulder removal are all effective methods where abrupt changes in subgrade occur (section 6.2) [1]. Gradual transitions constructed with embankment material or granular non‑frostsusceptible fill such as a subbase are called for at subgrade discontinuities (section 6.2) [1]. For rigid pavements, increasing structural strength by using a thicker slab is suggested when differential frost heave is expected, although its success is limited; designers should also refer to Section 6.2.B Frost Design for additional measures (section 8.11) [1]. The high‑tension cable barrier system treats frost heave by imposing depth criteria on its foundation elements. For end‑anchor terminal foundations the bottom elevation shall be placed six inches below the minimum frost depth, a depth that must first be determined according to Publication 242’s maximum potential serviceability loss due to frost heave; this design provision applies to all end‑anchor terminal foundations (section 629.3(a)2) [2]. For line‑post foundations the specification calls for constructing the bottom elevation to the greater of the manufacturer’s minimum depth or six inches below the minimum frost‑depth penetration determined according to Publication 242, then adding six inches and rounding up the resulting value to the nearest six‑inch increment; this requirement applies whenever line‑post foundations are placed (section 629.3(b)6) [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 8 (p. 164); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 540–543)

In the Frost Design subsection of Publication 242, soils that are described as highly susceptible to frost heave shall be removed and replaced with a non‑frost‑susceptible layer thick enough to insulate the roadbed from frost penetration; this treatment is applicable wherever such frost‑susceptible material occurs. For rigid pavement designs, the manual notes that frost heave is typically not a problem when an adequate granular foundation course is used, and therefore any frost‑heave mitigation treatment shall be limited to cases with significant justification. Under the high‑tension cable barrier specifications of Publication 408/2020, placement of Class A cement concrete and reinforcement for line‑post foundations is required when the bottom elevation of those foundations must be at least six inches below the minimum frost‑depth penetration determined according to Publication 242, with an additional six inches added and the total rounded up to the nearest six‑inch increment. Prevention in design and construction

Providing subsurface drainage reduces frost heave by removing water that would otherwise expand when frozen; this measure is applied wherever subsurface water is a problem and mitigates the expansion of wet soil that causes non‑uniform heaving (Section 2.3 Drainage) [1]. The use of lateral underdrains is strongly encouraged in wet subgrade areas and at all cut‑to‑fill transitions, and continuous pavement base drains must be provided along the outside edge of the pavement, the low side of superelevations, and on median sections where subsurface water is a particular problem; where evidence of water damage or sagging exists, subgrade drains may be installed to further reduce moisture that could produce frost heave (Section 2.3 Drainage) [1]. Soils highly susceptible to frost heave should be removed and replaced with a layer of non‑frost‑susceptible material thick enough to insulate the roadbed from frost penetration; this measure applies when such highly susceptible soils are identified (Section 6.2 Frost Design) [1]. The basic approach used in the design procedure to accommodate a frost‑susceptible subgrade is to provide a stronger base course able to withstand the effects of lower bearing support, and installation of pavement base drains or any other appropriate drains is recommended to lessen differential heaving caused by excess moisture whenever frost action is being addressed (Section 6.2 Frost Design) [1]. Uniform subgrade conditions are achieved through selective grading, blending and mixing of subgrade soils, boulder removal, or gradual transitions using granular non‑frost‑susceptible material at discontinuities; these methods apply wherever abrupt changes in subgrade type, groundwater, cut‑fill interfaces, or rock/earth boundaries exist (Section 6.2 Frost Design) [1]. There are no specific frost‑design requirements for rigid pavements because slabs based on saturated subgrade strength already have sufficient reserve capacity to survive spring‑thaw loss of support; however, if differential frost heave is expected the designer should refer to Section 6.2.B Frost Design (Section 8.11 Calculated Design Thickness) [1]. Attempting to mitigate differential frost heave by increasing structural strength (i.e., thicker pavement) generally has only limited success for rigid pavements (Section 8.11 Calculated Design Thickness) [1]. The bottom elevation of end‑anchor terminal foundations must be placed at least six inches below the minimum frost depth calculated according to Publication 242 Section 9.3 Item 2, thereby keeping the foundation out of the zone that can swell from freezing moisture and preventing or reducing frost‑heave‑related movement (Section 629.3(a)2) [2]. For line‑post foundations (and end‑anchor terminals where applicable) the bottom must be positioned at the greater of the manufacturer’s minimum depth or six inches below the minimum frost‑depth penetration identified by Publication 242 Section 9.3 Item 2, with the resulting depth rounded up to the next 6‑inch increment; this provision is intended to prevent serviceability loss caused by frost heave and applies during construction as described in Section 629.3(b) step 6 (Section 629.3(b)) [2].

In cold regions, frost heave is mitigated by installing subsurface drainage systems—including continuous pavement‑base drains, lateral underdrains at wet subgrade areas and cut‑to‑fill transitions, and optional subgrade drains where water damage is evident—because these measures remove the moisture that expands when it freezes; this practice applies wherever wet subgrades or transition zones are identified. The same drainage guidance also requires base and subbase materials to be highly permeable so that gravity‑drainable water is removed rapidly, limiting capillary wicking that would otherwise supply water for freezing expansion, and this requirement applies to all base and subbase layers in the drainage design. Maximizing the slope of subbase layers is prescribed to increase the rate of gravity drainage, thereby reducing moisture available for frost heave, and it applies wherever subbase material is placed. Adequate surface‑runoff removal—by cutting or cleaning ditches, providing positive outlet low points, and clearing inlets and pipe buildup—is required to prevent ponded water from infiltrating the pavement structure, a condition that can lead to localized frost heave. Soils identified as highly susceptible to frost heave should be removed and replaced with a non‑frost‑susceptible layer thick enough to insulate the roadbed from frost penetration; this measure applies when such soils are present in the subgrade. When a frost‑susceptible subgrade must remain, the guidance advises providing a stronger base course capable of withstanding lower bearing support and incorporating pavement‑base drains or other appropriate drainage to lessen differential heaving caused by moisture, and this approach applies wherever a susceptible subgrade cannot be eliminated. In areas where abrupt changes in subgrade conditions exist, selective grading, blending, mixing, boulder removal, or gradual transitions using embankment material or granular non‑frost‑susceptible subbase are prescribed to achieve uniform support and prevent differential heave. An adequate granular foundation course is noted as generally preventing frost heave for rigid pavements, although the manual also states that there are no specific frost‑heave construction measures for rigid pavements and that reliance on increased structural thickness has limited success; this observation applies to rigid pavement designs that use saturated subgrade strength tests. For high‑tension cable barrier systems, the specification requires the bottom elevation of each end‑anchor terminal foundation to be placed at least six inches below the calculated minimum frost depth, with the depth determined according to Publication 242 Section 9.3 Item 2 and then rounded up to the nearest six‑inch increment, a condition intended to limit the maximum potential serviceability loss caused by frost heave and applicable only to end‑anchor terminal foundations as described in Section 629.3(b). The same specification mandates that line post foundations be constructed of Class A cement concrete with reinforcement (Class AA for end anchor terminals) and that their bottom elevation be set at the greater of the manufacturer’s minimum depth or six inches below the minimum frost‑depth penetration identified in Publication 242 Section 9.3 Item 2, after which an additional six inches is added and the result rounded up to the nearest six‑inch increment; this requirement applies whenever line post foundations are installed under Section 629.3(b)(6) and also serves to limit potential serviceability loss due to frost heave. Specification limits and acceptance

The Pavement Policy Manual specifies that for all flexible pavement designs the estimated frost‑heave probability (Pf) must be taken as a value between 25 % and 75 %; this probability is judged by the district geotechnical engineer’s estimate of the percent area of the project expected to experience frost heave based on the soils report, and it is used together with the frost‑heave rate and depth of frost penetration to calculate the loss of serviceability that is added to the terminal serviceability value in Table 6.3 [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation construction specification for high‑tension cable barrier systems requires that the bottom elevation of line post foundations be set to the greater of the manufacturer’s minimum depth or six inches below the minimum frost‑depth penetration determined according to Publication 242 Section 9.3 Item 2; after this base depth an additional six inches is added and the total is rounded up to the nearest six‑inch increment [2].
