Composite Pavement

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Composite Pavement is a topic under Rigid Pavement.

Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Composite pavement is an overlay system that combines asphalt and concrete layers to provide a continuous load‑bearing surface while correcting existing structural deficiencies and low‑friction conditions, thereby delivering a smooth ride and enhanced safety[1]. The design requires a stable, joint‑free underlying concrete slab (or an AC/PCC composite) and mandates minimum binder thicknesses—at least 2½ inches of asphalt over cementitious bases and one to two inches beneath any Hot‑In‑Place Recycled layer—to ensure adequate bonding and load transfer[1]. By limiting joint or crack openings to roughly half‑to‑one millimetre, employing fiber‑reinforced concrete where appropriate, and using mechanistic‑empirical procedures such as the BCOA‑ME method for bonded overlays, the composite system is intended to achieve a service life of eight to twenty years with sustained ride quality and safety[1].

Purpose and design objectives

The design of a composite pavement overlay must first satisfy the functional requirement of delivering a smooth ride while eliminating any existing structural deficiencies and addressing low‑friction conditions that could compromise safety. Structurally it is required to provide additional capacity over the existing concrete or brick base, which entails using at least 2½ inches of asphalt binder when placed on such substrates and incorporating sufficient thicknesses dictated by traffic inputs—current average daily traffic, projected growth, and truck percentages—to sustain anticipated loads throughout the design period. The pavement must also achieve a minimum service life of eight years and not exceed twenty years, with durability ensured by limiting joint or crack openings to roughly half‑to‑one millimetre; this tight control of opening widths, together with the use of fiber‑reinforced concrete, reduces slab movement, misalignment, plastic shrinkage cracking and overall crack widening. By maintaining effective load‑transfer efficiency at joints and cracks, the composite system extends its serviceable life, preserving ride quality and safety for the intended design horizon. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188)

Scope and applicability

Composite pavement is appropriate when the existing structure consists either of a sound Portland‑cement concrete slab whose joints are stable, or of an asphalt‑concrete (AC/PCC) composite that already contains both materials. In the former case a thin high‑performance hot‑mix overlay can be placed over the concrete provided that at least one to two inches of new asphalt are installed beneath the HIR layer to achieve proper bonding, and the pavement exhibits only surface‑level distress with rutting and cracking within acceptable limits while the top two inches contain no rubber or fiber. In the latter situation an overlay is required—either an additional asphalt layer (an AC overlay) or a bonded concrete layer—so that the project can be treated as one of the seven DARWin‑supported overlay types for AC/PCC composites. Because conventional AASHTO procedures do not consider bonding between new concrete and underlying asphalt, any bonded‑concrete overlay on an AC/PCC composite must be designed with the specialized BCOA‑ME mechanistic‑empirical method. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177)

If a pavement improvement calls for a concrete overlay that must be bonded to an existing asphalt or AC/PCC composite surface, the usual composite‑pavement design is not suitable because the standard AASHTO procedures do not consider the effects of bonding; in this situation a different mechanistic‑empirical method (the BCOA‑ME procedure) must be used instead. Consequently, whenever bonding between the new concrete and the underlying composite layers is required, designers should prefer the bonded‑overlay design approach over a conventional composite pavement overlay. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177)

Design inputs and requirements

The design and construction of composite pavements are controlled by a suite of federal specifications, mechanistic‑empirical design procedures, and performance thresholds. The baseline design methodology follows the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures as implemented in the DARWin software; a structural design life must be at least eight years and may not exceed twenty years, and the analysis must be based on current average daily traffic, projected growth rates, and truck‑percentage forecasts that are recorded on FHWA Form D‑4332. When a bonded concrete overlay is placed over an existing asphalt‑concrete (AC/PCC) composite deck, the BCOA‑ME mechanistic‑empirical procedure is required, while other accepted programs such as ACPA Pavement Designer or AASHTOWare Pavement ME may be used for thin bonded overlays that incorporate macrofibers.

Material thickness requirements are explicit. For any asphalt layer placed over cement‑concrete or brick surfaces a minimum of 2½ inches of binder material must be provided, and the overall pavement thickness must exceed the milling depth by at least 1½ inches; when a Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) overlay is used, the recycled asphalt must be backed by one to two inches of new asphalt. In addition, any paving fabric must be positioned no closer than 1½ inches below the cut surface.

Specific performance limits are imposed through Publication 336 criteria. Rutting severity on the existing pavement must be less than 1½ inches, cracking depth must be under one inch with an average crack width not greater than ½ inch, and raveling may only receive a medium rating. Pavements that contain rubber or fiber in the top two inches, have mastic patches larger than five square feet, or retain durable markings that have not been removed are excluded from composite overlay projects.

When macrofibers are used in thin bonded concrete overlays, Publication 408 Section 541 mandates their inclusion, and ASTM C1609 is employed to verify the residual strength (f150), which must fall within the typical range of 100 to 225 psi. Fiber dosage is selected to achieve the required residual strength, with macrofiber content generally limited to between 0.2 % and 0.5 % by volume in order to maintain workability and control air‑content. The condition of the existing pavement determines whether a bonded or unbonded overlay is appropriate, forming an additional project constraint. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188)

Design methods and criteria

Composite pavement overlays are designed using the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures, which define seven overlay configurations including asphalt‑over‑asphalt or concrete‑over‑asphalt/composite systems. When a concrete overlay is bonded to an existing asphalt or composite surface, the standard AASHTO method does not capture the bond effects; therefore the thickness of such bonded overlays must be obtained from the bonded concrete overlay of asphalt mechanistic‑empirical design procedure (BCOA‑ME). This model was created at the University of Pittsburgh under FHWA Pooled Fund Study TPF 5‑165. For composite designs that incorporate a fiber‑reinforced concrete overlay, the contribution of macrofibers is quantified by measuring residual flexural strength according to ASTM C1609. The measured residual strength (f150) is added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to form an effective flexural strength, expressed as Feff = MOR + f150. This effective strength is then entered into mechanistic‑empirical design tools such as BCOA‑ME, ACPA Pavement Designer, or AASHTOWare Pavement ME. To aid engineers in selecting the appropriate f150 value for a given overlay thickness and traffic condition, a Residual Strength Estimator spreadsheet has been provided. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188)

The composite pavement design must satisfy a set of durability, structural, and reliability criteria. First, the existing concrete portion must remain stable with no joint movement; any overlay that incorporates Hot‑In‑Place Recycled treatment requires that condition. The asphalt layer placed over such a surface must have at least one to two inches of material beneath the HIR depth. When an asphalt binder is applied for the first time over a concrete or brick base, the binder thickness must be no less than two and a half inches, and the wearing course must meet the material‑specific minimum thickness, with any deviation such as tapering requiring special approval. All overlay designs are required to achieve a structural design life of at least eight years but not exceed twenty years. To ensure that the predicted performance meets this lifespan, designers must employ the most accurate traffic information available—including current average daily traffic, projected growth rates, and truck‑percentage data—to compute the daily 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) in accordance with Chapter 7. For asphalt concrete overlays on existing AC/PCC composite structures, conventional AASHTO procedures are appropriate; however, bonded concrete overlays on these composites must be designed using the BCOA‑ME mechanistic‑empirical method because standard 1993 AASHTO methods do not account for the bonding effects between the new concrete and the underlying asphalt. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177)

Structural section and dimensions

The guidance for composite pavements states that the hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) layer is typically about one‑and‑a‑half inches thick, with a possible range from one to two inches, and that at least one to two inches of asphalt must be placed beneath this HIR depth; no additional dimensions are given for base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab, shoulder, edge‑support, or reinforcement layers. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133)

Materials and mixture design

A composite pavement design must specify that the asphalt overlay be at least one to two inches thick beneath the hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) layer, and it must require the underlying concrete slab to be stable with no joint movement. These thickness and stability criteria are the essential material specifications for a composite pavement according to the guidelines. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133)

When using Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) mixtures in a composite pavement, the design must provide an asphalt layer at least as thick as the HIR depth—typically one to two inches—and should not be thinner than about one‑and‑a‑half inches to avoid weak spots. The underlying concrete slab must be structurally sound with joints that do not move, ensuring durability and consistent performance, while the specified minimum thickness also supports constructability by giving a sufficient base for placement and traffic opening. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133)

Structural details and interfaces

In composite pavement systems that incorporate an HIR overlay, the underlying concrete slab must be provided with joints that remain stable and exhibit no movement so that structural continuity is maintained across the interface. The design also requires a minimum asphalt bedding of one to two inches beneath the HIR layer, which serves as a transition medium supporting the combined structure. When a fiber‑reinforced concrete (FRC) overlay is used, the joint layout shifts to properly placed contraction joints that are saw‑cut at the prescribed depth and timing; this early activation limits crack propagation. Reinforcement within the FRC layer consists of synthetic or steel macrofibers mixed into the concrete, which add flexural capacity and enhance load‑transfer efficiency across the contraction joints, behaving in a manner comparable to traditional tie bars under repeated loading. Nevertheless, where fault control is required, conventional dowel bars must still be installed because the fibers are not a substitute for standard joint reinforcement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133), Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

In composite pavement design, the interface between the high‑intensity‑traffic (HIR) wearing surface and the underlying layer must contain at least one to two inches of asphalt beneath the HIR depth; thinner layers risk inadequate bonding. When an asphalt binder is placed over a cement‑concrete or brick substrate for the first time, a minimum of 2½ inches of binder material should be provided before the wearing course. The concrete portion of the composite system must be stable, with joints that exhibit no movement so that the overlay can act as a continuous load‑bearing surface. The selection of a bonded versus an unbonded interface is governed primarily by the condition of the existing asphalt layer. Engineers should core the pavement to assess stripping or deterioration; if the underlying asphalt is in fair to good condition, a bonded overlay may be specified, whereas a poor or deteriorated base calls for an unbonded design. The presence of macro‑fibers in the concrete slab does not alter this decision. For bonded interfaces, conventional 1993 AASHTO procedures are insufficient because they do not account for bonding; thicknesses must therefore be determined using the Bonded Concrete Overlay of Asphalt mechanistic‑empirical (BCOA‑ME) design procedure. Unbonded overlays follow the standard AASHTO design methodology. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188)

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

When designing a composite pavement that incorporates an HIR surface, the underlying concrete must be structurally sound and exhibit no joint movement, while the base and subgrade should be well‑drained to support the overlay; the HIR asphalt layer is required to rest on at least one to two inches of existing asphalt (or equivalent) to provide adequate support. No specific guidance on moisture or climate effects is given beyond the need for a stable, well‑drained foundation. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133)

The composite pavement design must provide at least one to two inches of new asphalt over the existing concrete layer, and the underlying concrete must be joint‑stable with no movement. Construction should retain the existing vertical clearance for overhead structures, curbs, gutters and guide rails, satisfying geometric and user‑clearance requirements. Minor bleeding and low friction values are permitted, meeting safety criteria for surface texture. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133)

Verification and expected performance

Composite pavements that use a fiber‑reinforced concrete overlay are expected to provide greater structural capacity and extended service life by limiting crack growth and preserving joint load transfer; laboratory slab tests have shown higher flexural and ultimate load capacities and up to a 30 % increase in load‑transfer efficiency versus plain concrete, which translates into improved distress resistance and smoother ride quality. Performance is quantified by measuring the residual strength (f150) according to ASTM C1609—typically between 100 and 225 psi—and adding it to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength (feff) for use in mechanistic‑empirical design programs, while field monitoring of crack widths and joint LTE serves as additional indicators. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)
