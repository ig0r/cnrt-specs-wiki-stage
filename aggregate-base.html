Aggregate Base

Part of: Base Course is-subtopic-of
Aggregate Base is a topic under Base Course.
aggregate-basecrushed-aggregate-base-coursestone-basestone-or-aggregate-base
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Aggregate Base is a highly permeable, load‑bearing layer placed beneath pavement to eliminate excess water from the subgrade, thereby preserving structural support and extending pavement life [1]. It is built from well‑graded aggregates (or reclaimed asphalt or cement‑stabilized mixes) that are uniformly blended, kept at proper moisture, laid in lifts typically 3–6 inches thick (or as required), and compacted to at least 100 % of the maximum density (96 % for recycled mixes), with surface irregularities limited to one‑half inch [2]. This construction provides rapid gravity drainage, maximized subbase slopes and protected drain outlets, satisfying functional, structural, durability, safety and serviceability objectives across crushed‑aggregate, cold‑recycled asphalt, asphalt‑treated permeable and cement‑treated permeable applications [1].

Purpose and design objectives

Aggregate Base is used to address excessive water within or beneath pavement layers, which reduces subgrade support and leads to traffic‑induced deflections that shorten pavement life; by providing a highly permeable, well‑drained layer it keeps the underlying soil dry, preserving structural support, increasing resistance to deflection, and extending durability. [1] In Section 312—Crushed Aggregate Base Course, Type DG, the aggregate base is required to be uniformly blended, kept at proper moisture, placed in layers no thicker than six inches (with each layer at least three inches) and compacted to not less than 100 % of maximum density, with surface irregularities limited to one‑half inch, thereby providing a stable, load‑bearing foundation that supports overlying pavement layers. [2] In Section 310—Crushed Aggregate Base Course, the aggregate base must be uniformly compacted and meet strict depth, layer‑thickness, and surface‑tolerance requirements (layers of approximately equal depth, no less than 4 inches nor more than 8 inches, or for vibratory methods 5–10 inches; surface irregularities exceeding one‑half inch corrected) so that it can support overlying layers and distribute traffic loads without settlement. [2] In Section 654—Paved Shoulders, Type 4, the aggregate base is used to fill low spots down to the bottom elevation of the proposed base course when existing graded material is insufficient, creating a continuous foundation that provides a stable, load‑bearing substrate for subsequent pavement layers. [2] In Section 341—Cold Recycled Asphalt Base Course, Cold‑In‑Place, the treatment requires compaction to at least 96 % of PTM 402 target density and curing until moisture content is less than 2 %, ensuring a stable base layer that can reliably carry an asphalt overlay without settlement. [2] In Section 360—Asphalt Treated Permeable Base Course, the aggregate base is designed to provide structural support while preserving permeability; it must be compacted but not so much as to lose free‑draining capability or crush the aggregate, and placed in maximum four‑inch lifts. [2] In Section 303—Cement Treated Permeable Base Course, the aggregate base supplies structural support while maintaining drainage, with compaction using steel‑wheel rollers of 8–10 tons completed within one hour (or up to 1½ hours with retarding admixture) after water addition, followed by immediate curing for at least 24 hours and traffic restriction for three days. [2] In Section 344—Full Depth Reclamation, aggregate base created through full‑depth reclamation converts existing roadway layers together with stabilizing additives and imported aggregate into a new compacted base that solves the problem of insufficient structural support in deteriorated pavements, allowing reliable carriage of an asphalt overlay or surface treatment. [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 207–236), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (p. 574)

The aggregate base must satisfy functional objectives related to effective subsurface drainage that removes water from beneath the pavement, thereby reducing loss of subgrade support and increasing resistance to traffic‑induced deflections; keeping the base dry also limits deterioration caused by freezing, thawing, or fine‑material pumping and promotes uniform performance and ride quality [1]. For asphalt‑treated permeable base course (Section 360), placement is required only when the surface is stable and the air temperature is at least 35 °F, and the mixture must be placed within eight hours of production to achieve its functional purpose [2]. Structural objectives for crushed‑aggregate base course Type DG (Section 312) specify that each layer shall be between 3 inches and 6 inches thick and compacted to not less than 100 % of the maximum density as determined by PTM No. 106 Method B, with moisture retained until the next layer is laid to obtain a positive bond [2]. Structural objectives for crushed‑aggregate base course (Section 310) require compacted layers that meet thickness limits of at least 4 inches and no more than 8 inches when using Method 1, or at least 5 inches and no more than 10 inches when using Method 2 [2]. Structural objectives for paved shoulders (Section 654) demand that the material be compacted with rollers as prescribed and is considered adequately compacted only when it does not move under the compaction equipment [2]. Structural objectives for cold‑recycled asphalt base course (Section 341) call for a minimum density of 96 % of the PTM 402 target density, and any area that cannot achieve this after additional compaction shall be scarified, re‑moistened and recompacted; emulsified or foamed asphalt‑stabilized CRABC must cure for at least two weeks before the wearing or binder course—or until moisture content is less than 2 %—with fog seal applied to control dust or raveling [2]. Durability objectives for cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303) require placement and compaction so that the base remains free draining and aggregate is not crushed, with compaction completed within one hour after water addition (or up to 1½ hours if a retarding admixture is used); curing material shall be maintained for 24 hours, extending an additional 24 hours if temperature falls below 40 °F [2]. Durability objectives for asphalt‑treated permeable base course also mandate that compaction not eliminate free drainage of the base and that aggregate crushing be avoided; construction traffic is prohibited on the course for at least three days to protect durability and safety [2]. Safety objectives across the crushed‑aggregate base courses (Sections 312, 310) limit traffic on the newly placed base to necessary local traffic and essential equipment, require prompt repair of any marred or distorted areas, and prohibit placement in frozen material or temperatures below 40 °F to preserve durability [2]. Additional safety measures for asphalt‑treated permeable base course prohibit placement in rain or frozen conditions and require that construction traffic speed be controlled with a pilot car when raveling is likely [2]. Serviceability objectives common to the aggregate bases require surface irregularities not to exceed ½ inch; any depth deficiency of ½ inch or more shall be removed and replaced, and depth corrections must be made within ½ inch of the design grade to maintain a smooth, load‑bearing platform and acceptable ride quality [2][1]. Serviceability for the asphalt‑treated permeable base course also limits surface irregularities to less than ½ inch and requires correction of any area where the average maximum space under a straight edge exceeds ½ inch within each 3,000‑square‑yard segment [2]. The functional drainage objective described in the policy manual further contributes to serviceability by preventing fluid‑base conditions that cause surface roughness and noticeable loss of ride quality [1].

Scope and applicability

Aggregate base is appropriate for brick pavements that are constructed on a stone or other flexible base rather than on a concrete (rigid) base, and the design of any overlay must treat the underlying material as a separate structural layer applying the structural coefficient designated for “Brick with Flexible Base” during analysis. In such brick pavement applications the base material—whether stone or aggregate—receives its own structural coefficient applied only to the depth of that base. For paved shoulders classified as Type 4, aggregate base is appropriate when the shoulder must be built to the bottom elevation of the proposed base course, particularly where existing graded material is lacking or where the shoulder has been excavated below that level; in those situations the specification requires providing aggregate‑base course material and compacting it as required by Section 350.3 and per Section 108.05 to achieve the indicated depth. Aggregate base is also appropriate for paved shoulders classified as Type 3 when the construction specification calls for an aggregate base in accordance with Section 350.3, and if granulated steel slag is selected as the base material the top three inches of that aggregate base must be placed using stone, gravel, or air‑cooled blast furnace slag aggregate. The aggregate‑base design described in Section 342 for a central‑plant mixed cold‑recycled asphalt base course is suitable where reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) and/or reclaimed asphalt millings (RAM) are combined with virgin aggregates or cement, the mixture being stabilized with an emulsified or foamed asphalt agent, and this approach applies to projects that call for a cold‑recycled asphalt base constructed in a continuous or batch mixer. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (p. 62); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 223), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 573–574)

When the seasonal or temperature constraints for a Cold Recycled Asphalt Base Course cannot be satisfied—specifically, placement is prohibited from September 1 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0, 2‑0, 3‑0, 4‑0, 9‑0, and 10‑0, and from October 1 to April 30 in all other districts, or when freezing temperatures have occurred within the prior 24 hours or ambient air temperature is below 45°F—the project would need to adopt an alternative design such as a conventional aggregate base rather than using CRABC. In addition, if granulated steel slag is selected as the base material, the specification requires that the uppermost three inches be constructed with stone, gravel, or air‑cooled blast furnace slag aggregate instead of the standard aggregate base referenced in Section 350.3, making this alternative approach preferable under that condition. [2]

Design inputs and requirements

The aggregate‑base layer must be designed to satisfy the drainage requirements set out in Chapter 2‑3 of the project considerations, which call for highly permeable base material, maximized subbase slopes, and properly protected drain outlets; sizing and spacing of pavement‑base drains are required to follow the method described in Publication 13M Design Manual Part 2 Highway Design Chapter 10 Section 10.3.D, while continuous edge and lateral drains must be installed as prescribed in Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards (RC‑30M) for both Interstate and non‑Interstate projects, including protection of outlets with headwalls[1].

The crushed‑aggregate base course identified as Type DG is constructed in accordance with the requirements of Section 312.3, which call for acceptance of the mixing plant and installation of automatic cutoff devices; aggregates are measured by volume or weight and blended in proper proportions while water is added by weight, volume or metering device and distributed uniformly[2]. When the compacted depth of a Type DG base exceeds six inches it shall be placed in two or more lifts of approximately equal thickness, each lift being no less than three inches and no more than six inches, and every lift must be compacted to not less than 100 % of the maximum density as determined by PTM No. 106 Method B using field‑density measurements per AASHTO T 191[2]. Surface irregularities greater than one‑half inch shall be corrected immediately, and a test hole to full depth is required for each three thousand square yards; any deficiency of one‑half inch or more must be removed and replaced[2]. Placement of crushed aggregate base course under Section 310.3 shall be performed with adjustable, self‑propelled mechanical spreaders capable of placing and screeding the material without segregation, and compaction shall use rollers specified in Section 108.05(c)3.a or vibratory equipment when Method 2 is selected[2]. If the compacted depth exceeds eight inches the base shall be constructed in two or more lifts of approximately equal thickness, each lift being no less than four inches and no more than eight inches; for Method 2 when the compacted depth exceeds ten inches the lifts are limited to a minimum of five inches and a maximum of ten inches[2]. All surface irregularities greater than one‑half inch shall be corrected by loosening the surface and removing or adding material as required, and a test hole to full depth is to be cut for each three thousand square yards; any area deficient by one‑half inch or more shall be removed and replaced[2]. Until the surface course is placed the completed base may be maintained and protected, allowing only necessary local traffic and essential construction equipment as directed[2].

For paved shoulders of Type 4 the aggregate‑base layer must be constructed to the depth shown on the plans in accordance with Section 350.3, and after placement it shall be compacted as directed in Section 654.3(f); rollers identified in Section 108.05(c)3.a, (c)3.b, (c)3.e or (c)4 are to be used, and compaction is considered satisfactory only when the base does not move under the equipment[2]. The aggregate‑base layer for paved shoulders of Type 3 shall also be constructed according to Section 350.3; however, if granulated steel slag is used as a base material the top three inches of the aggregate base must be placed with stone, gravel or air‑cooled blast furnace slag aggregate[2].

The cold‑recycled asphalt base course specified in Section 341 shall be placed with equipment capable of metering liquids within ± 2.0 % by weight and using an asphalt paver as called for in Section 413.3(e)1, and placement may not occur during the seasonal windows listed for each district unless freezing conditions are avoided and ambient temperature is at least 45 °F and rising[2]. Each compacted layer shall be between two inches and five inches thick and shall be compacted to a minimum of 96 % of the PTM No. 402 target density, with in‑place density verified for every three thousand square yard area by AASHTO T 310; RAP size shall not exceed 1¼ inch as required by AASHTO MP 38[2]. After placement the material must be cured until its average moisture content is less than 2 %, which for emulsified asphalt requires at least two weeks before any wearing or binder course is installed[2].

Placement of asphalt‑treated permeable base course is prohibited on surfaces that are unstable, frozen, or when the air temperature is below 35 °F, and also during rain; the mixture shall be produced at a temperature below 320 °F to obtain adequate coating of aggregate particles without segregation, and it must be placed within eight hours of production with no stockpiling[2]. Placement shall use steel‑wheel power rollers having a certified metal weight of eight tons to ten tons, and the material is to be spread in compacted lifts not exceeding four inches; exactly four roller passes are required, with additional passes permitted only to eliminate surface irregularities, and over‑compaction that would crush aggregate or impede drainage shall be avoided[2]. Depth control is verified by one six‑inch diameter test hole per three thousand square yards, and any section deficient in depth by one‑half inch or more shall be removed and replaced[2].

The cement‑treated permeable base course must be placed and compacted in accordance with Section 303.3, which requires steel‑wheel power rollers of certified metal weight between eight tons and ten tons and at least one roller pass; the interval from water addition to completion of compaction may not exceed one hour (or one‑and‑a‑half hours if a retarding admixture is used)[2]. Surface tolerances are checked with a ten‑foot straightedge and any irregularities greater than one‑half inch shall be corrected without grinding or milling[2]. Curing begins immediately after compaction by applying a white membrane forming compound at a rate of one gallon per one hundred square feet ± 10 % (or alternatively covering with white polyethylene sheeting) for twenty‑four hours, extending the period if temperature falls below 40 °F[2]. Construction traffic is prohibited on the cement‑treated permeable base for at least three days after placement and an overlying pavement may not be placed during that time[2].

Design methods and criteria

The drainage design of an aggregate‑base layer follows the sizing and outlet‑spacing methodology set out in Publication 13M, Design Manual Part 2, Highway Design, Chapter 10, Section 10.3.D [1]. Permeability of the base material is assessed by using gradation (particle‑size distribution) as an indicator test, with the understanding that similar gradations may still exhibit different permeabilities because particle shape influences flow [1]. Designers are instructed to maximize the slope of subbase layers in order to increase the rate of gravity drainage within granular materials and to select pipe spacing that enhances gravity drainage [1]. When a flexible aggregate base supports a brick pavement, the structural analysis employs the structural coefficient identified for “Brick with Flexible Base” in Table 9.3, and a separate structural coefficient is applied only to the depth of the base material [1]. For crushed‑aggregate base courses classified as Type DG (Section 312), the mix is proportioned by volume or weight, with coarse and fine aggregates fed to a mixing plant equipped with automatic cutoff devices that stop feed if any component delivery ceases [2]. Calcium chloride may be added before water at a rate of 7 lb per ton for Type 1 mixtures or 5.7 lb per ton for Type 2 mixtures to achieve the required moisture condition [2]. The homogeneous mixture is placed in layers whose individual thickness shall not be less than 3 inches and shall not exceed 6 inches; when the total required depth exceeds 6 inches, placement is divided into two or more layers of approximately equal depth [2]. Each layer must be compacted to at least 100 % of the maximum density determined by PTM No. 106, Method B, with field density verified in accordance with AASHTO T 191 [2]. When a crushed‑aggregate base is placed according to Section 310, an initial fine‑material bed of 2 inches on the subgrade (or 1 inch on a subbase or existing pavement) is first spread [2]. The coarse material is then laid in layers whose thickness shall not be less than 4 inches nor more than 8 inches when the total compacted depth exceeds 8 inches, and shall not be less than 5 inches nor more than 10 inches when it exceeds 10 inches [2]. Adjustable self‑propelled spreaders and rollers specified in Section 108.05(c)3.a are used for uniform placement, and vibratory equipment is required for Method 2 [2]. The material is uniformly spread, corrected for surface irregularities at intervals of no more than 25 feet, compacted with the prescribed rollers (and vibration where required), finished by applying fine material, sprinkling water and rolling until a smooth monolithic surface is obtained, and depth is verified by test holes for each 3,000 square yard area [2]. For paved shoulders of Type 4, surplus shoulder material is removed to the bottom of the proposed base course, the area is scarified to a maximum depth of 4 inches below existing grade, low spots are filled and shaped with graded material or aggregate‑base course constructed in accordance with Section 350.3, and the fill is compacted with rollers as prescribed in Section 108.05(c)3.a, (c)3.b, (c)3.e, or (c)4 until the material does not move under the compaction equipment [2]. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses require equipment that meters liquids within ± 2.0 % by weight; compacted layers are placed between 2 inches and 5 inches thick, and each layer is compacted to a minimum of 96 % of the PTM No. 402 target density [2]. In‑place density for each 3,000 square yard area is determined according to AASHTO T 310, and milling depth is measured at least once per 3,000 square yards and recorded [2]. Asphalt‑treated permeable base courses are produced at a temperature below 320 °F with sufficient viscosity for coating, must be placed within eight hours of production on stable, unfrozen surfaces above 35 °F and not during rain [2]. Compaction is performed with steel‑wheel rollers weighing eight to ten tons, applying four roller passes while avoiding over‑compaction that would crush aggregate or impede drainage; the mat may be compacted only after it has cooled enough to prevent shoving [2]. Joints are formed by overlapping longitudinal edges 1–2 inches and full‑depth transverse bulkheads (or saw cuts) are installed when work stops for more than thirty minutes, surface irregularities greater than one‑half inch are corrected by loosening or adding material rather than grinding, depth is verified by drilling a six‑inch diameter test hole to the full base depth for each 3,000 square yards, and any deficiency of half an inch or more must be removed and replaced [2]. Cement‑treated permeable base courses require that any rubblized pavement or subbase be thoroughly wetted until moist before placement with an approved asphalt paver or mechanical spreader equipped with a screed, plate vibrator and automated sensors to control profile and grade [2]. Compaction uses steel‑wheel power rollers weighing from eight to ten tons; at least one pass is required but compaction shall not be so great as to eliminate drainage or crush the aggregate, and it must be completed within one hour of adding water (or up to one‑and‑a‑half hours if a retarding admixture is used) [2]. Surface tolerance is checked with a 10‑foot straightedge moved no more than five feet at a time, and irregularities greater than one‑half inch are corrected by adjusting the course without grinding or milling [2]. Curing begins immediately after compaction using a white‑membrane curing compound applied at a rate of 1 gallon per 100 square feet ± 10 % (or covering with white polyethylene sheeting) for twenty‑four hours, extending the period if temperature falls below 40 °F and requiring replacement if temperature drops to 32 °F within seventy‑two hours [2]. Construction traffic is prohibited on the cured surface for at least three days [2].

The aggregate base layer must be highly permeable so that water readily flows through and gravity‑drainable water is removed rapidly, with larger voids also preventing capillary wicking; particle shape may influence drainage performance even though gradation is used as an indicator of permeability [1]. The slope of subbase layers should be maximized to increase the rate of gravity drainage within the granular materials, and the spacing between pavement base drains and their outlets must be designed according to the method shown in Publication 13M, Design Manual Part 2, Highway Design, Chapter 10, Section 10.3.D [1]. The crushed aggregate base course must be placed in layers no thinner than three inches and no thicker than six inches whenever the total compacted depth exceeds six inches, and each layer shall be compacted to not less than one hundred percent of the maximum density as determined by PTM No. 106 Method B with field verification per AASHTO T 191; surface irregularities are limited to a maximum of one‑half inch, any deviation exceeding that must be corrected immediately, and depth measurements showing a deficiency of one‑half inch or more require removal and replacement of the affected area [2]. Materials and mixture design

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, the aggregate‑base specification must call for a highly permeable granular material and must include its particle‑size distribution (gradation) as the primary indicator of that permeability, thereby ensuring rapid gravity drainage through the base layer. [1] Section 312—Crushed Aggregate Base Course, Type DG requires that coarse and fine aggregate be measured separately by volume or weight and fed into the mixer in proper proportions. [2] Water shall be measured by weight, volume, or a metering device and uniformly distributed throughout the mix. [2] When directed, calcium chloride must be proportioned and blended at exactly 7 pounds per ton for Type 1 or 5.7 pounds per ton for Type 2 before water is added. [2] Each compacted layer of the base course shall achieve not less than 100 % of maximum density as determined by PTM No. 106 Method B. [2] The aggregate base shall be built from a coarse crushed‑aggregate base material topped with a fine filler, beginning with an initial uniform layer of fine material placed to a depth of 2 inches on subgrade or 1 inch on subbase or existing pavement. [2] If the compacted depth exceeds 8 inches (Method 1), the course shall be constructed in two or more layers of approximately equal thickness, with no layer less than 4 inches nor more than 8 inches; if the compacted depth exceeds 10 inches (Method 2) the same requirement applies with each layer not less than 5 inches nor more than 10 inches. [2] For Method 2 the fine material applied after vibration shall be about one‑half of the amount required to fill the voids in the coarse aggregate, and the fines are then compacted and bonded with water and rolling. [2] The recycled base must consist of reclaimed pavement material mixed with an emulsified or foamed asphalt stabilizing agent and sufficient liquid (including water) metered to within ± 2.0 % by weight; any additional water shall be added at the mill head using a calibrated meter to achieve thorough mixing. [2] The placed layer must be between 2 inches and 5 inches thick and compacted to at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density, with in‑place density verified for each 3,000‑square‑yard area according to AASHTO T 310. [2] Before a wearing or binder course is placed, the cured base must have an average moisture content of less than 2 %, which shall be achieved after at least two weeks of curing for emulsified asphalt or until the moisture criterion is met for foamed asphalt. [2] The dry aggregate and asphalt binder shall be identified and heated so that they combine into a mixture in which the asphalt coats the aggregate with a film of adequate thickness to provide the required binding properties; the mixed ATPBC must be produced at a temperature below 320 °F with a viscosity suitable for coating the particles and without causing segregation, and it shall be placed within eight hours of production. [2] Compaction shall be performed with steel‑wheel power rollers weighing between 8 tons and 10 tons, applying four roller passes to achieve the required density while avoiding over‑compaction that would crush aggregate or impede drainage. [2] The Aggregate Base shall follow the requirements set out in Section 350.3, and if granulated steel slag is used as a base material the top three inches must be constructed with stone, gravel, or air‑cooled blast furnace slag aggregate. [2] The aggregate base must consist of a combination of reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP), reclaimed asphalt material (RAM), virgin aggregates, or cement, and it must be blended with an emulsified or foamed asphalt stabilizing agent in either a continuous or batch type mixer. [2] The Aggregate Base is defined as a blend of existing roadway material layers together with any required stabilizing additives and imported aggregate or RAP material, mixed to achieve the prescribed depth and grading; the exact proportions of these constituents shall be set out in an approved FDR mix design, and the work calls for pulverizing and mixing these components in‑place before compacting them into a new pavement base layer. [2]

To achieve the required structural, durability, constructability and performance objectives, the aggregate‑base layer is first specified to be highly permeable so that gravity‑drainable water can be removed rapidly, preserving subgrade support and limiting moisture‑related damage; permeability is evaluated through gradation because a particle‑size distribution that creates larger voids reduces capillary rise and promotes drainage [1]. At the same time the mix must retain sufficient stability for construction equipment, therefore some reduction in drainability may be accepted to provide a workable platform during placement, and the design should also incorporate adequate subbase slope and appropriately spaced drains to sustain the required drainage capacity [1]. Aggregate‑base mixtures are proportioned by first measuring coarse and fine aggregates separately—by volume or weight—and feeding them into a mass‑proportioning plant that provides separate bins for each size and automatic cutoff devices to stop feed if any component stops [2]. After the dry ingredients are uniformly blended, water is added, measured by weight, volume or a metering device and sprayed to achieve uniform distribution; calcium chloride is then proportioned at 7 pounds per ton of mixture for Type 1 or 5.7 pounds per ton for Type 2 before water addition, and the accuracy of all proportioning and measuring devices shall be checked and verified while stockpiled aggregates are protected from segregation to ensure a homogeneous mix [2]. For crushed aggregate base, an initial fine‑material bed is spread uniformly over the subgrade to a depth of 2 inches (or 1 inch on a subbase), followed by placement of well‑graded coarse material; if the required compacted depth exceeds the limits, the course must be built in two or more layers each kept within the prescribed thickness range—no less than 4 inches and no more than 8 inches for Method 1 (or no less than 5 inches and no more than 10 inches for Method 2) [2]. After compacting each coarse layer, fine material is applied to fill voids—about one‑half of the volume needed to fill the voids when using vibratory equipment, or uniformly until all voids are filled in non‑vibratory construction—and the surface is water‑sprinkled and rolled to achieve a smooth, monolithic base [2]. For a cold‑recycled asphalt base, the contractor must use equipment that can automatically meter liquids with a variation of not more than ± 2.0 % by weight, maintain enough total liquid in the mix to assure thorough blending of reclaimed material, aggregate and emulsified asphalt, compact the placed mixture to at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density with density checks performed on each 3,000‑square‑yard area, and cure the stabilized base for at least 2 weeks—or until its average moisture content drops below 2 %—before any wearing or binder course is installed [2].

Structural details and interfaces

For a crushed aggregate base course, the specification limits treatment to construction joints; when placement on a new day meets material placed the previous day, the existing edge shall be scarified and moistened, blended with the fresh mix, and compacted so that a continuous section is formed without creating a joint, and no other longitudinal or transverse joints, interlayers, membranes, edge or widening details, notches, transitions, connections, or similar structural details are prescribed in this section. For an asphalt‑treated permeable base course, longitudinal joints shall be created by spreading the material to overlap the edge of the previously placed lane by 1 inch to 2 inches while maintaining a uniform uncompacted depth adjacent to the compacted lane so that a smooth joint results after compaction. Transverse joints shall be required at the end of each day’s work or whenever an interruption longer than 30 minutes occurs; they must be formed with a temporary vertical bulkhead that extends the full depth and width of the ATPBC, although the contractor may alternatively saw the construction joint. For a cement‑treated permeable base course, the base may be placed either as a half‑pavement or full‑pavement width construction. Surface tolerance checks shall be performed at any transverse joints and paving notches using a 10‑foot straightedge. The freshly compacted layer shall be cured by applying a white membrane‑forming curing compound at a rate of 1 gallon per 100 square feet ± 10 % of the surface area, or, as an alternative, the contractor may cover the CTPBC with white polyethylene sheeting for the required period; no additional longitudinal joint, interlayer, edge widening, transition, or connection details are stipulated beyond these requirements. When an aggregate or stone base underlies a brick pavement the interface is treated by selecting the appropriate structural coefficient for the brick‑with‑base condition, using the “Brick with Rigid Base” coefficient when the underlying layer is concrete and the “Brick with Flexible Base” coefficient when it is stone or another flexible material. The base course shall be constructed in two or more lifts when its compacted depth is more than 6 inches, with each lift no less than 3 inches nor more than 6 inches, and each lift must be compacted to not less than 100 % of the maximum density specified. [1] The subbase should be given a maximum slope to accelerate gravity drainage within the granular materials. [1] Lateral underdrains are strongly encouraged at wet subgrade areas and cut‑to‑fill transitions to intercept seepage from excavations. [1] Drain outlets for the base must be protected from crushing or plugging to ensure that the entire drainage system will work properly over the pavement’s life. [1] The crushed aggregate base course shall be kept at the proper moisture content throughout construction; water is added uniformly by spray and frequent moisture‑control tests are performed at delivery. [2] When directed, calcium chloride may be blended at a rate of 7 lb per ton for Type 1 or 5.7 lb per ton for Type 2 to maintain the required moisture. [2] During transport the mix should be covered as needed to prevent loss of moisture, and after placement each layer is kept moist until the next layer is laid. Surface tolerance is then checked by moving a 10‑ft straightedge along the centreline in stages no more than five feet apart and any deviation greater than one‑half inch must be corrected before acceptance. Curing compliance is confirmed by applying the specified white membrane curing compound at the rate of one gallon per 100 square feet (±10 %) or by covering with white polyethylene sheeting for a continuous 24‑hour period, extending to an additional 24 hours if temperature falls below 40 °F. Traffic protection is ensured by prohibiting construction traffic for at least three days and limiting post‑cure traffic to only equipment needed for the next lift, with any damaged or contaminated areas removed and replaced at no cost to the Department. [2]

In the context of subsurface drainage, the aggregate base is expected to increase pavement service life and deliver more uniform performance by reducing deflection of the base, limiting aggregate deterioration, and preventing frost‑heave related distress; resistance to moisture‑induced rutting, surface roughness, and loss of ride quality is evaluated through field observation of those specific distresses—rut depth, undulations, and cracking adjacent to thermal cracks. Drainage effectiveness, which underpins these outcomes, is not measured directly in the field; instead PennDOT uses gradation (particle‑size distribution) as an indicator test for base material permeability. For a cold‑recycled asphalt base course, performance expectations are expressed through specific construction criteria: the base must be compacted to at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density, with in‑place density verified on each 3,000‑square‑yard area according to AASHTO T 310. Surface smoothness is required such that the average gap measured under a 12‑foot straight edge across three test locations does not exceed ½ inch for any 3,000‑square‑yard segment; this tolerance is checked with straight‑edge measurements. After placement the material must be cured until the average moisture content falls below 2 % (and for emulsified asphalt the cure period shall be at least 2 weeks before overlay); moisture determinations are performed per PTM 749. Milling depth must be measured and any deficiency of ½ inch or more is corrected, with depth surveys providing verification. [1][2]
