Void (loss of support)

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Void (loss of support) is a topic under Distress.
voidsvoids-beneath-the-slabuniform-support
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-28

Void (loss of support) in concrete pavements is defined as small voids or empty spaces that develop beneath a slab as a result of pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or settlement[1]. These underlying voids reduce the slab’s bearing capacity and produce excessive deflections and stresses that manifest on the surface as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes) in shoulders at a transverse joint or crack, corner breaks, and overall slab settlement[1]. The condition is limited to Portland cement concrete slabs—plain or reinforced—and is most commonly observed near transverse joints, cracks, and the outside corners of the slab, where loss of support can lead to progressive faulting, diagonal cracking and eventual slab breakup[1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Void (loss of support)” also include the following subtopics: Slab Rocking and Subgrade Consolidation.

Slab Rocking represents a specific instability where unsupported concrete fragments move visibly under traffic loads, creating non-uniform support conditions that exacerbate the loss of bearing capacity inherent in Void. [1][2] Subgrade Consolidation provides the primary mechanism for this condition by generating small voids beneath the slab through pumping action and settlement, which directly leads to the excessive deflections and surface distresses characteristic of the general concept. [1]

Description and identification

Void (loss of support) in concrete pavements is defined as small voids or empty spaces that develop beneath a slab as a result of pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or settlement. Under Section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION these voids reduce the slab’s bearing capacity and cause excessive deflections and stresses, which appear on the surface as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, corner breaks, and overall slab settlement. Restoration of support is achieved by slab stabilization, which requires filling the voids with cement grout (approximately 1 cubic foot of grout per hole, 0.25 bag of cement per hole), hot asphalt (30‑50 gallons per hole) or high‑density polyurethane (about 0.4 lb per square foot); the work shall be performed in accordance with Publication 72M, Roadway Construction Standards, RC‑26M, and Publication 408, Specifications, Section 679, and at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized when no preliminary testing has been done. In Section 4.6 SLABJACKING the same void condition is recognized as a depression or profile deficiency where the slab falls below the desired grade and may be accompanied by faulting; it is remedied by slabjacking, which fills those voids with cement grout or high‑density polyurethane to raise the slab back to an acceptable level. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Void‑related loss of slab support is identified by a set of field signs that differ from ordinary surface cracking: transverse joint faulting, the presence of fines accumulating at joints or cracks on either the traffic lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder directly over a transverse joint or crack, corner breaks, and overall slab settlement. These symptoms typically appear where pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or settlement have created small voids beneath the concrete, most often near transverse joints and cracks at the outer corners of the slab. Because these indicators are linked to loss of bearing beneath the slab, they distinguish the condition from surface‑only distresses that do not involve underlying voids. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Where it occurs

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242 Change 10) void loss of support is addressed only for Portland cement concrete pavements and is not listed as a distress for asphalt concrete surfaces, so it does not apply to asphalt overlays or other asphalt structures; shoulder sections are discussed in terms of providing lateral support rather than describing voids. In the slab‑stabilization portion of that manual, void‑related loss of support is described for Portland cement concrete slabs where pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement may create small voids beneath the slab, especially near transverse joints, cracks and outside slab corners, and such voids can also appear as small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack. The same section calls for filling those voids with cement grout estimated at 1 cubic foot per hole, hot‑asphalt undersealing between 30 and 50 gallons per hole, or high‑density polyurethane applied at 0.4 pounds per square foot of slab; when no preliminary testing has been performed, at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints shall be stabilized according to Publication 72M, RC‑26M and Publication 408 Section 679. The slabjacking portion likewise treats void loss of support for Portland cement concrete pavements, stating that air gaps or settled areas under concrete slabs create a lack of bearing that can lead to faulting, and the recommended remedy is to fill the underlying voids with either a cement grout mixture estimated at 0.40 bags of cement per hole or high‑density polyurethane about 0.7 lb per square foot, often followed by dowel‑bar retrofits as specified in Publication 72M and Publication 408 Section 527. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification (Publication 408/2020) also limits the discussion of loss of support to rigid pavements—plain or reinforced cement‑concrete slabs and their associated structures such as shoulders—and its only directive concerning voids is to “Patch voids or spalls, in excess of the specified width” as required by Section 525. [1] [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–367)

According to the General provisions (section 2.1), void loss of support is found in the space beneath rigid concrete slabs, that is, between the slab and its underlying base or pavement course. The slab‑stabilization discussion (section 4.5) notes that these sub‑slab voids most often develop near transverse joints, along cracks, and especially at outside slab corners; their presence leads to excessive deflections that can produce joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and eventual slab breakup. For estimating purposes in the same section, when no preliminary testing has been performed, at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized. The slab‑jacking guidance (section 4.6) observes that void loss of support becomes most apparent when grout used for slabjacking begins to extrude from joints, cracks or the pavement edge, which signals voids at those locations. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Causes and mechanisms

Void loss of support in PCC pavements is attributed to pumping action, subgrade consolidation, and settlement, which generate small voids that tend to form near transverse joints, cracks, and especially at outside slab corners. The resulting lack of support produces excessive slab deflection and stresses leading to joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and eventual breakup. Slab stabilization—performed per Publication 72M (RC‑26M) and Publication 408 Section 679—restores support by filling the voids with cement grout, hot asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane, and the guidance calls for stabilizing at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints when no preliminary testing is done. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Void loss of support in PCC slabs originates from pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement that generate small voids beneath the slab, especially at transverse joints, cracks and outside corners; these voids cause excessive deflection and stress leading to joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and eventual slab breakup. The process is accelerated when water infiltrates the pavement—as shown by fines near joints or cracks, blow‑holes in shoulders, and ongoing settlement—and when joint and crack sealing are not performed or subsurface drains are poorly maintained, allowing continued pumping. To restore support, slab stabilization fills the voids with cement grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane as specified in Publication 72M, RC‑26M and Publication 408 Section 679, and at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized if no preliminary testing has been performed. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Effects on performance

In section 2.1 GENERAL, voids beneath a rigid pavement remove lateral support, which increases edge stresses and can cause damage that reduces the pavement’s structural capacity and overall durability; the manual therefore calls for slab stabilization to fill those voids (Section 2.1.B.2.d) and notes that shoulders provide lateral support that significantly reduces edge stresses, thereby reducing resulting damage consistent with the applied stress level (supported vs. unsupported). In section 3.3 GUIDELINES FOR DEVELOPING INITIAL COSTS > B. Concrete Rehabilitation Strategies, when voids or loss of support occur in a concrete pavement slab stabilization is prescribed to restore full support around patches and other affected areas, indicating that adequate support is essential for maintaining the pavement’s structural performance; although diamond grinding may be used to improve ride quality, the source indicates that grinding removes the effective mortar layer and can cause a rapid decline in skid resistance on Vanport Limestone aggregate, which may compromise safety. In section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION, void‑induced loss of support beneath a Portland cement concrete slab—often created by pumping action, subgrade consolidation or settlement near transverse joints and outside corners—generates excessive slab deflections and stresses that appear as transverse joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately complete slab breakup; these distresses degrade the pavement’s structural capacity, produce rough ride quality and safety hazards, and accelerate deterioration. While slab stabilization fills the voids and restores structural integrity by reducing deflections, it does not increase the design structural capacity or eliminate existing faulting, but it lessens the likelihood of further pumping, faulting and cracking, thereby extending durability and service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Void formation beneath a Portland cement concrete slab removes the sub‑base support and produces excessive slab deflections and stresses. The loss of support allows the slab to settle and creates faulting in the ride surface; as these stresses build, transverse joint faulting appears first, followed by corner breaks and diagonal cracking, and if this condition is ignored the settlement can progress to cracking and tilting of the slab, further degrading structural integrity and rideability, and if the voids remain untreated the slab eventually breaks up completely. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Repairs and treatments

Slab stabilization is prescribed as a treatment that fills voids beneath existing rigid base or pavement courses, thereby addressing loss of support caused by those voids; it applies when such underlying voids are present and must be performed in accordance with Publication 408 Specifications Section 679 (section 2.1) [1]. The same manual also lists slab stabilization “as required” within the concrete rehabilitation strategies to restore full support around patches and at other locations whenever a design field view indicates insufficient support after patching or similar repairs (section 3.3) [1]. Stabilization is carried out by pumping cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane into drilled holes to fill slab/subbase voids, which restores full support and reduces deflections that lead to transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks, blow‑hole depressions, corner breaks, or slab settlement; it shall be done according to Publication 72M (RC‑26M) and Specification Section 679 whenever those loss‑of‑support indicators are present, and at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized if no preliminary testing has been performed (section 4.5) [1]. Slabjacking is described as a technique that restores structural integrity and rideability by filling voids beneath the pavement and raising slabs to an acceptable profile; it should be used only when conditions permit, being usually impractical if the temperature is high because expanded slabs place joints in compression, although for extensive profile deficiencies such as embankment settlement slabjacking may be more successful if the slab is already in compression (section 4.6) [1]. Dowel‑bar retrofits, specified in Publication 72M and Publication 408 Section 527, are typically applied together with slabjacking to restore load transfer of settled slabs that have lost support and can reduce or eliminate faulting (section 4.6) [1]. In the Pennsylvania specifications, void‑related loss of support is also addressed by patching when a void or spall exceeds the width allowed by the specifications; such patches are performed in accordance with Section 525 and directly target the void condition (Section 501.3) [2]. When edge slumping is detected after concrete has hardened and cannot be corrected within the acceptable limits of 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes and 1/4 inch between lane‑shoulder, the specifications require removal and replacement of the defective pavement as called for in Section 501.3(t), with the action triggered only after field testing confirms the defect (Section 501.3) [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–367)

Slab stabilization is required when any loss‑of‑support indicators such as transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes), corner breaks, or slab settlement are observed in a Portland cement concrete pavement; the treatment restores support by filling voids but does not correct surface depressions or increase structural capacity [1]. If no preliminary testing has been performed, at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized [1]. Patching of voids is permitted only when the voids exceed the width defined in Section 525; any void smaller than that specified width is not treated by this provision [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–367)

Prevention in design and construction

The source identifies several design measures that are intended to prevent or reduce void‑related loss of support. Base Repair is specified for asphalt surfaces when areas of obvious failure are present; the measure calls for removal of those failed portions and replacement with material that will provide adequate structural capacity and facilitate drainage, thereby addressing loss of support caused by underlying voids (section 2.1 GENERAL). Slab Stabilization is required whenever voids exist beneath an existing rigid base or pavement course (Publication 408 Section 679) and also when loss‑of‑support indicators such as transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks, small depressions, corner breaks or slab settlement appear; filling the slab/subbase interface with a grout material restores full support and reduces deflections, pumping, faulting and cracking, and the guidance states that at least 25 % of transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized if no preliminary testing has been performed (section 4.5). In addition, slab stabilization is prescribed as required to restore full support around patches and at other locations (section 3.3). Slabjacking is applied when voids cause an extensive profile deficiency such as embankment settlement; by filling the voids beneath the concrete slab and raising it to an acceptable profile, the technique restores structural integrity and rideability, but it is more likely to succeed if the slab is in compression rather than when temperature is high (section 4.6). Dowel bar retrofits are mentioned for use together with slabjacking to restore load transfer of settled slabs that have lost support, which may reduce or eliminate faulting (section 4.6). Shoulders on both asphalt and PCC surfaces are prescribed when the shoulder structure needs improvement—on asphalt to improve condition or performance and on concrete to supply lateral support under edge loads—thereby reducing unsupported edge stresses and associated damage (section 2.1 GENERAL). [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Slab stabilization—filling voids beneath existing rigid base or pavement courses according to Publication 408, Specifications, Section 679—is required to prevent loss of support caused by underlying voids whenever such voids are present under a Portland Cement Concrete surface (Section 2.1 GENERAL). In the concrete rehabilitation strategies of Chapter 3, slab stabilization is prescribed as required to restore full support to the pavement around patches and at other locations, thereby preventing loss of support; slabjacking is likewise allowed as required when needed to re‑establish support (Section 3.3 B). Chapter 4, subsection 4.5 states that loss of slab support caused by voids beneath the concrete—identified by transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks, small depressions, corner breaks, or settlement—shall be prevented by stabilizing the slab with grout materials such as cement‑based grout, hot asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane; joint and crack sealing must be performed together with stabilization and subsurface drains must be kept in good condition because these actions limit water entry that contributes to pumping and void formation, and stabilization shall be applied to at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints when no preliminary testing has been performed (Section 4.5). Slabjacking prevents loss of support by filling the voids beneath a concrete slab and raising it to an acceptable profile, but it should be performed in accordance with this Manual and Publication 408, Specifications Section 681 and is considered impractical when pavement temperature is high because the slabs expand and joints are in compression (Section 4.6); dowel bar retrofits, referenced in Publication 72M and Publication 408 Section 527, are prescribed to be used together with slabjacking to restore load transfer of settled slabs that have lost support; the grout material for the void‑filling operation may be a cement‑based grout, which requires curing before traffic, or high‑density polyurethane, which expands and hardens after at least thirty minutes (Section 4.6). [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Specification limits and acceptance

In Section 4.5 Slab Stabilization, loss of slab support – the void condition – is identified by visual signs such as transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, corner breaks and slab settlement, which typically develop near transverse joints, cracks and outside slab corners due to pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement; corrective stabilization must be performed according to Publication 408, Specifications, Section 679 (and Publication 72M, RC‑26M), and for estimating purposes at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized if no preliminary testing has been performed. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)
