Void (loss of support)

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Void (loss of support) is a topic under Distress.
voidsvoids-beneath-the-slabuniform-support
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Void (loss of support) is a condition in Portland cement concrete pavements where empty spaces develop beneath the rigid base or slab—typically at transverse joints, cracks, or outer corners—as a result of water infiltration, pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or settlement, thereby reducing the structural capacity and stability of the pavement [1]. These sub‑slab cavities produce excessive slab deflection and stress that appear as joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking, surface depressions (blow‑holes), and a loss of rideability [1]. The treatment objective is to restore full support by filling the voids with cement grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane foam and incrementally lifting the slab to a preset profile until it contacts the reference line, after which the material cures and load transfer may be reestablished [1].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Void (loss of support) describes the condition where empty spaces exist beneath an existing rigid base or concrete pavement slab, reducing structural capacity and stability. These sub‑slab cavities—often created by pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or settlement—cause excessive slab deflection and stress that appear as joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking, surface depressions and a loss of rideability. The intended remedy is to restore full support for the pavement by stabilizing the slab: the voids are filled with an appropriate material such as cement grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane foam, and the slab may be incrementally lifted until it meets a preset profile defined by string lines and gauge blocks. Acceptance is achieved when the lifted slab contacts the reference line after the fill has cured—approximately 12 hours for cement grout or at least 30 minutes for polyurethane—and dowel‑bar retrofits are often added thereafter to reestablish load transfer. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

When voids develop beneath a rigid base or concrete slab, the treatment is directed at re‑establishing full support for the pavement structure. By drilling through joints or cracks and injecting cement grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane foam, the gaps are filled and the slab is lifted to its proper profile. This refilling restores the structural integrity of the slab, eliminates excessive deflection and joint faulting, and thereby returns rideability. Because full support is regained, the pavement’s deterioration is halted and its remaining service life is prolonged. The work therefore seeks primarily to restore functionality of the affected section, which simultaneously extends the overall service life of the pavement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Distress characterization and causes

The void‑related loss of support in Portland cement concrete pavements is observed beneath the slab, most frequently at transverse joints, existing cracks and especially at the outer corners of slabs. The voids produce depressions whose dimensions are governed by the size of the underlying gap; even small blow‑hole depressions in the shoulder indicate the presence of a loss‑of‑support condition. As the unsupported area expands, the slab experiences excessive deflection and stress, which first appear as joint faulting or localized depressions, then advance to corner breaks and diagonal cracking, and ultimately can culminate in complete slab breakup if left untreated. This progression degrades ride quality and creates hazardous surface irregularities that may endanger motorists. Because the structural integrity of the pavement is compromised, traffic operations are often affected: lanes may require temporary closure for remedial actions such as slab‑jacking or dowel‑bar retrofits, and an improper repair can exacerbate damage by inducing additional cracking, tilting or faulting. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Void‑related distress in concrete pavements is driven primarily by the ingress of water that cannot be removed because surface or subsurface drainage is inadequate. When water penetrates through joints, cracks or other openings it generates a pumping action; together with consolidation and settlement of the subgrade—often triggered by embankment settlement—the moisture creates and enlarges voids beneath the rigid slab. The presence of these voids reduces the effective structural capacity of the underlying base, producing loss of support that manifests as excessive deflection, joint faulting and cracking. Consequently, insufficient drainage, weak or undersized base material, subgrade consolidation, and continuous water infiltration act in concert to accelerate the development and expansion of voids. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Applicability and treatment selection

Void‑related loss of support applies to Portland cement concrete slab pavements in which voids have developed beneath the slab, typically near transverse joints, cracks or outside corners as a result of pumping action, subgrade consolidation or settlement. The condition is deemed appropriate for treatment when field signs such as transverse‑joint faulting, accumulation of fines at joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes) in shoulders, corner breaks, or measurable slab settlement are observed, indicating that water infiltration and insufficient drainage are compromising support. Repair can be undertaken only if traffic can be confined to a single lane under temporary traffic control; high ambient temperatures make the method impractical because thermal expansion places joints in compression. The void‑filling operation must be sized according to the length of the depressed area, the required correction magnitude, subgrade and drainage characteristics, and the location of any joints or cracks, with drilling and grout placement completed within the same lane before traffic is restored. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Void‑related slab stabilization or slabjacking is only appropriate when the deficiency consists solely of loss of support beneath a concrete slab. The treatment becomes unsuitable as soon as surface depressions that need correction are present, when additional structural capacity is required, or when faulting must be eliminated; in those cases a more extensive repair such as full‑depth patching, reconstruction, or another rehabilitation technique should be chosen because simply filling voids does not address these deficiencies. Likewise, slabjacking is impractical at high pavement temperatures, where the slabs have expanded and the joints are already in compression, making lifting ineffective. It should also be avoided whenever the work cannot be performed with sufficient care, since improper jacking can introduce new cracking, tilting or faulting that outweigh any benefit, prompting selection of full‑depth replacement or an alternative preservation method. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Condition assessment and timing

The determination of whether void stabilization is required begins with a systematic visual field inspection that looks for the distress indicators that signal loss of slab support. Inspectors check transverse joints for faulting, observe any accumulation of fines near joints or cracks on either the traffic lane or shoulder, and look for small depressions—often described as blow‑holes—in the shoulder at transverse joints or cracks. The appearance of corner breaks or general slab settlement also flags a potential void, and when any of these conditions are observed they identify the locations where grout, asphalt, or polyurethane injections will be needed. After visual identification, the field measurement phase quantifies the profile deficiency. An arrangement of string lines is stretched across the slab at a height roughly one inch above the desired finished grade; gauge blocks are then placed on the pavement surface and lifted until they contact the string line, thereby establishing the target elevation. The gauge‑block readings reveal the magnitude of depressions or faulting and confirm when the required profile has been achieved during lifting operations. Concurrently, a detailed evaluation of each depressed area is performed, recording the size or length of the depression, the amount of correction needed, subgrade condition, drainage characteristics, and proximity to joints and cracks; these factors dictate the pattern and spacing of injection holes that will be drilled for slab‑jacking or void filling. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Void (loss of support) treatment is indicated when voids are detected beneath an existing rigid base or concrete pavement, manifested by loss‑of‑support symptoms such as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines at joints or cracks, small depressions or blow‑holes at joints, corner breaks, slab settlement, or a general profile deficiency. At this stage the slab no longer rests on adequate sub‑base material and must be stabilized before any overlay or reconstruction. The prescribed remedial action is to fill the voids—by injecting cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing, high‑density polyurethane, or by performing slabjacking—starting at the lowest point of the depression and working outward. Lifting is monitored with gauge blocks until they contact a string line positioned approximately one inch above the desired grade; pumping is then reduced and stopped. The material is allowed to cure (about 12 hours for cement‑based grout or a minimum of 30 minutes for polyurethane) before traffic is reopened. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Materials and repair details

Void‑related loss of support beneath a concrete or rigid pavement is corrected by slab stabilization, also described as slabjacking, which fills the sub‑base voids to restore bearing capacity. The repair must follow the procedures set out in Publication 408, Specifications Section 679, and involves drilling a pattern of holes under the affected transverse joints or cracks that is sized according to the depression depth, required correction, subgrade conditions and joint locations. The void can be filled with either a cement‑based grout mixture or a flowable polymeric material; the grout is typically placed at about one cubic foot per hole, which corresponds to roughly 0.25 – 0.40 bags of cement per hole, and must be allowed to cure for approximately twelve hours before traffic is permitted. Alternatively, hot asphalt undersealing may be pumped into each hole in quantities ranging from thirty to fifty gallons, with a cooling period of at least thirty minutes prior to reopening the slab. A high‑density polyurethane foam can also be used, requiring an application rate of roughly 0.4 lb per square foot (or up to 0.7 lb per square foot when employed for slabjacking) and hardening in about thirty minutes before traffic. During the fill operation the material is pumped through the drilled holes while joint and crack sealing are performed concurrently to limit water infiltration. After the void has been filled, the slab is lifted to the target profile using a string line set approximately one inch above the desired grade together with gauge blocks placed on the pavement; dowel bar retrofits are frequently installed afterward to re‑establish load transfer across the repaired area. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Treatment of void‑related loss of support in concrete pavements succeeds when the injected material possesses the flow and hardening characteristics needed to fill the narrow gaps that develop beneath slabs, and when those materials are compatible with the existing slab environment and site conditions. A fluid cement grout must remain pumpable long enough to reach the voids and then achieve sufficient strength within about twelve hours before traffic returns; hot liquid asphalt requires a minimum cure time of roughly thirty minutes, while a low‑viscosity two‑component high‑density polyurethane must also be pumpable but cures rapidly, expanding into a lightweight rigid closed‑cell foam that resists water and chemical ingress. The grout’s ability to penetrate the voids is enhanced by its low viscosity, and the polyurethane’s expansion provides long‑term durability. Successful placement further depends on proper preparation of the slab and surrounding structure: joints and cracks must be sealed, functional subsurface drains installed to limit water infiltration that could re‑pump voids, and the subgrade and drainage system must accommodate the hole‑pattern design. Ambient temperature must allow the slab to remain in compression; high temperatures that cause slab expansion make slabjacking impractical because resulting compressive stresses can induce cracking. When these material properties, compatibility steps, and existing pavement conditions are addressed, the void is filled, support is restored, deflections are reduced, and future faulting and cracking are mitigated. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Treatment procedures

To address void‑related loss of support in concrete pavements the crew first prepares the area by sealing all joints and cracks and confirming that any subsurface drainage is operating properly. Traffic control is installed so that work can be performed within a single lane while protecting the remaining traffic. For slabjacking the desired final elevation is established using string lines positioned about one inch above the target grade, with gauge blocks placed on the slab to monitor lift as material is injected. Immediately before grout placement holes are drilled in a pattern sized to the observed depression, subgrade conditions and joint locations; drilling is done just prior to pumping and confined to the same lane as the traffic control setup. The selected fill material—either a cement‑based grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane foam—is pumped into each hole according to the prescribed layout. As the slab is raised, the gauge blocks are watched until they contact the string line; if grout begins to extrude additional holes are added. Once the voids are filled the surface is left undisturbed to allow curing: cement grout requires roughly twelve hours before traffic can be restored, while asphalt or polyurethane foam hardens in at least thirty minutes. After the appropriate cure time the lane is reopened and normal traffic resumed. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

The repair of void‑related loss of support requires a drilling rig and a pump, together with string lines and gauge blocks that define the target profile. Holes are drilled immediately before any stabilizing material is placed—whether cement grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane—so that both crews can operate under a single Maintenance and Protection of Traffic (MPT) setup. The drilling and subsequent injection or slabjacking must be confined to one traffic lane at a time; the lane remains closed until the injected material has attained sufficient strength—cement grout may need up to twelve hours to cure, while hot asphalt must cool for at least thirty minutes and polyurethane must expand and harden for a minimum of thirty minutes. Pumping begins at the lowest point of the void and proceeds outward, and when slabjacking the slab is raised in incremental steps until the gauge blocks contact the string line, which limits stresses and prevents cracking. Ambient temperature is an important environmental constraint: operations are generally avoided when temperatures are high because thermal expansion places joints in compression, although a compressive condition can sometimes be used for large‑scale profile corrections. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Quality and workmanship

Inspection for void‑related loss of support should focus on identifying transverse joint faulting, the presence of fines at joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes) near joints, corner breaks, and overall slab settlement, which are symptoms of subgrade pumping and consolidation. Preparation requires proper joint and crack sealing and verification that subsurface drains are functional before any stabilization work. Installation must follow the prescribed drilling pattern and use an approved fill material—cement grout, hot asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane—while observing the specified cure times (up to 12 hours for cement, at least 30 minutes for asphalt or polyurethane) before reopening traffic. The completed job is accepted only after the voids are filled, deflections are reduced, and no surface depressions remain, confirming restored structural integrity. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

The slabjacking procedure is considered satisfactory when the lifted concrete reaches the profile defined by a reference string line that is set roughly one inch above the intended grade; pumping is reduced as the gauge blocks near this line and stopped once the blocks actually touch it. Any premature grout leakage from joints, cracks or edges before that elevation signals that additional holes and material are required. The repaired slab may only be returned to traffic after the selected grout has fully cured—about 12 hours for cement grout or a minimum of 30 minutes for polyurethane—ensuring the void beneath the slab is completely filled and stable. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 92–93)

Performance and service life

When voids develop beneath a concrete slab the loss of support produces excessive slab deflection and stress, which manifest as joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately slab breakup. These distress mechanisms degrade the pavement’s condition, reduce rideability and functional performance, and shorten its expected service life. Slab stabilization addresses this by drilling holes at joints or cracks and filling the voids with cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane foam; once the material cures full support is restored, deflections are reduced and the likelihood of further pumping, faulting and cracking diminishes, thereby improving durability and extending service life. In the absence of preliminary testing the practice calls for stabilizing at least twenty‑five percent of transverse joints and all patch joints. A complementary approach, slabjacking, also fills voids and lifts the slab to an acceptable profile, restoring structural integrity and rideability. When combined with dowel‑bar retrofits it can further reduce or eliminate faulting. The method may employ cement grout at roughly 0.40 bags per hole, requiring up to twelve hours to cure, or high‑density polyurethane applied at about 0.7 lb/ft² that hardens in as little as thirty minutes and forms a lightweight, rigid closed‑cell foam. Properly executed slabjacking is often more economical than full slab replacement and can be completed with minimal traffic disruption, contributing to longer‑lasting pavement performance. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Void formation beneath a concrete pavement slab—often generated by pumping action, subgrade consolidation or settlement—creates loss of support that is expressed through excessive slab deflections and stresses. The resulting distresses commonly include transverse joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and overall slab settlement, with the defects tending to concentrate near transverse joints, existing cracks and outside corners where water infiltration and inadequate drainage further accelerate deterioration. When remedial slab‑jacking is applied without proper control it can introduce additional failures such as cracking, tilting or faulting of the repaired slab. The severity of these treatment‑related distresses depends on field‑determined hole patterns that reflect the size or length of the depressed area, the magnitude of correction required, subgrade and drainage conditions, and the proximity of joints or cracks; high ambient temperatures also limit slab‑jacking because expanded slabs place joints in compression. Effective restoration therefore requires a slab‑stabilization procedure that first drills holes to access the voids and then fills them with an appropriate material—cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing or a two‑component high‑density polyurethane foam whose low viscosity promotes deep penetration. The fill must be allowed to cure before traffic is returned, and joint and crack sealing together with functional subsurface drains are essential to prevent recurrence of voids. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93)

Monitoring and follow-up

When field inspections reveal any sign that the pavement no longer has adequate support, immediate remedial work is required. Observable loss‑of‑support includes visible failure of the base course, ponding or other indications of inadequate drainage that suggest water infiltration, and any voids detected beneath a rigid surface. In concrete slabs the condition manifests as transverse joints beginning to fault, accumulation of fine material at joints or cracks on the lane or shoulder, small blow‑hole depressions in the shoulder adjacent to joints, corner breaks, overall slab settlement, or localized depressions in the riding surface. The appearance of these distress patterns signals that the underlying support has been compromised and calls for maintenance such as base repair with structurally adequate fill, slab stabilization to fill the voids, joint sealing, or slab‑jacking to raise the slab to an acceptable profile. During slab‑jacking, if grout begins to extrude from joints, cracks, or pavement edges before the target elevation is reached, it indicates that the void has not been fully stabilized and additional grouting or new drill holes are required. Improper execution of slab‑jacking that produces new cracking, tilting, or faulting also marks the need for further rehabilitation. [1]
