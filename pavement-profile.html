Pavement Profile

Part of: Ride Quality is-subtopic-of
Pavement Profile is a topic under Ride Quality.
profileprofile-deficiency
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Pavement Profile is a field‑based evaluation of the elevation, grade and surface geometry of concrete or asphalt roadways that determines whether the finished slab meets prescribed ride‑quality and drainage criteria[1]. It establishes a reference grade with a string line positioned about one inch above the desired finish and uses gauge blocks and calibrated PTM No. 428 profiling equipment to record longitudinal roughness (IRI), mean roughness (MRI) and transverse cross‑slope at regular intervals[2]. The investigation quantifies depressions, ridges, valleys or bumps—typically with a 12‑foot straightedge—and verifies that deviations stay within defined limits (e.g., ≤¼ inch in 12 ft for concrete, ≤3/16 inch for asphalt, transverse ridge/valley ≤⅛ inch) while IRI must not exceed 70 in/mile on roads posted above 45 mph or 90 in/mile at 45 mph or less, and MRI may not surpass the acceptance value by more than 6.1 inches per mile on at least 90 % of 528‑ft lots[2].

Purpose and investigation objectives

The Pavement Profile investigation for concrete slab work evaluates the slab’s elevation and grade, checking whether the surface meets the intended profile and identifying any depressions or settlements that could impair rideability. It should determine how far the existing slab deviates from the target line established by a string line positioned about 1 inch above the desired grade, quantify the size or length of any depressed area, estimate the amount of correction required, and assess subgrade and drainage conditions as well as joint and crack locations that will govern hole pattern and grout selection. [1] For concrete pavements covered under Section 514‑3, the investigation evaluates the geometric condition by measuring cross‑slope uniformity and longitudinal roughness that together define the profile. It must determine whether the transverse slope has been restored to a positive cross‑slope that drains, check uniformity at 100‑foot intervals, identify any deviation greater than 1/4 inch in 12 feet, and verify that ride quality measured with pavement surface profile equipment meets the International Roughness Index limit of 70 in/mile when posted speed exceeds 45 mph or 90 in/mile when posted speed is 45 mph or less. [2] When slabjacking is performed under Section 681‑3, the investigation focuses on elevation and longitudinal grade of concrete slabs, requiring a survey of existing roadway profiles to locate depressions and the proposed design grade line, and it must compare the measured profile with the intended grade to confirm that the post‑jacking profile falls within the allowable tolerance—within 0.02 foot for continuously reinforced concrete pavement or within 0.03 foot for jointed slabs—and decide whether additional injection or corrective grinding is required. [2] The corrective‑action provisions of Section 507‑3 require the investigation to check that the transverse profile does not develop ridges or valleys beyond the allowed limit and that bumps are within acceptable size; it must verify whether any deviation between adjacent lots exceeds 1/8 inch, whether any individual irregularity measured with a 12‑foot straightedge is greater than 1/4 inch, and whether the corrected surface provides a uniform texture and a positive cross‑slope to assure drainage, prompting carbide grinding, diamond grinding, or removal and replacement when those conditions are not met. [2] For asphalt pavements addressed in Section 404‑1, the investigation evaluates the surface profile of finished pavement—including overlaid bridge approach slabs and decks—to quantify ride quality; it must measure the pavement according to PTM No. 428, supply calibrated daily results, provide raw unfiltered data together with International Roughness Index and Mean Roughness Index values within 24 hours so that the Representative can determine incentive eligibility, and also identify which construction operations are present while confirming that excluded areas are not included in the incentive calculation. [2] The acceptance criteria of Section 404‑3(c) require the investigation to check smoothness and cross‑slope of an asphalt surface by looking for deviations such as bumps, ridges or valleys that exceed defined limits; it must determine whether the lot’s measured MRI stays within the maximum acceptable MRI, whether any individual bump exceeds 3/16 inch on a 12‑foot straightedge, whether any ridge or valley exceeds 1/8 inch on the transverse profile, and whether corrective actions have produced a uniform texture and positive cross‑slope to ensure proper drainage. [2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 92–93); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 237–239), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 394–405), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 611–612)

The measured cross‑sectional profile obtained with a string line positioned about 1 inch above the target grade serves as the reference elevation that guides slabjacking operations, ensuring the lifted slab meets an acceptable profile for rideability and drainage. [1] By comparing the existing slab surface to this desired profile, engineers can decide where voids must be filled, how many lift points are required, and whether dowel‑bar retrofits should accompany the lift, thereby informing maintenance and rehabilitation plans. [1] The profile data also help limit over‑lifting because pumping is slowed as gauge blocks approach the string line, reducing the risk of cracking or faulting. [1] In slabjacking projects, surveyed pavement profiles supplied before work acceptance document the existing grade, the proposed grade and the grade actually achieved after slabjacking; engineers compare results against required tolerances of within 0.02 foot (or 0.03 foot for jointed slabs) from the string line grade, and if the profile shows the pavement raised beyond those limits, additional injection may be ordered or over‑jacked sections exceeding 0.10 foot must be removed and replaced, guiding acceptance decisions and future maintenance planning. [2] For concrete diamond grinding, the measured transverse cross‑slope indicates when a section exceeds a deviation greater than 1/4 inch in 12 feet and therefore must be corrected to restore the original uniform positive cross slope for proper drainage, directing corrective grinding actions. [2] Longitudinal profile data collected before and after grinding are used to verify that the post‑grind International Roughness Index does not exceed the maximum IRI of 70 in/mile when posted speed limits are greater than 45 mph, or the maximum IRI of 90 in/mile when posted speed limits are less than or equal to 45 mph; this determination guides whether additional re‑grinding is required. [2] The same pavement surface profile measuring equipment is employed for all ride‑quality evaluations, providing a consistent basis for deciding when concrete repairs, slab stabilization, or further grinding should be scheduled. [2] In concrete ride‑quality corrective action, profile measurements that reveal a transverse ridge or valley exceeding the 1/8‑inch limit trigger corrective action before any depth testing, directing crews to apply carbide grinding for sections up to 15 feet long with passes limited to 1/8 inch, or to use diamond grinding or removal and replacement when larger areas are affected. [2] When a bump greater than 1/4 inch is detected, the lot is classified as defective and must be ground; verification MRI may not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile on at least 90 % of the 528‑foot lots, informing designers about acceptable tolerances and prompting planner scheduling of profiler recertification if those limits are breached. [2] In asphalt ride‑quality evaluation, measuring the pavement surface profile according to PTM No. 428 and delivering IRI and MRI data within 24 hours provides quantitative ride‑quality results that the Representative uses to determine incentive payments for each lot; these results directly inform planning, design, maintenance and rehabilitation choices by indicating how well construction operations such as milling or asphalt courses meet performance expectations. [2] The standardized full lot length of 528 feet per lane (with partial lots evaluated proportionally when less than 100 feet) ensures consistent comparison across projects, while exclusions—such as bridge decks, tapered pavements, and roadways with posted speed limits of 40 mph or lower—focus decision‑making on applicable full‑depth overlay sections. [2] Acceptance criteria define clear transverse profile limits—ridges or valleys may not exceed 1/8 inch and any bump greater than 3/16 inch on a 12‑foot straightedge triggers corrective grinding; these findings allow engineers to pinpoint where surface grinding, diamond grinding, or removal and replacement are required to restore ride quality, maintain proper cross‑slope for drainage, and meet incentive payment criteria. [2] The measured surface profile provides IRI and MRI values that quantify ride quality for each completed concrete lot; because incentive payment is based on the MRI, these results influence budgeting decisions and can highlight sections where roughness exceeds acceptable levels, prompting targeted maintenance or rehabilitation. [2]

Scope and applicability

Pavement Profile is appropriate for rigid Portland cement concrete slabs that exhibit depressions or extensive profile deficiencies such as embankment settlement, where slabjacking can be employed to fill voids beneath the slab and raise it to an acceptable grade; the desired elevation is set by a string line positioned about 1 inch above the target grade [1]. The method should not be attempted when pavement temperature is high because the slabs will have expanded and joints may be in compression, although if the slab is already in compression correcting a large profile deficiency may be more successful [1]. For slab‑jacking of continuously reinforced concrete pavement, jointed concrete slabs, and bridge end slabs, a pre‑treatment roadway profile must be prepared and submitted as a Roadway Profile Report; the injection is directed to raise continuously reinforced concrete pavement to within 0.02 foot plus or minus from the string line grade, while jointed slabs and bridge end slabs are raised to within 0.03 foot plus or minus, and any overjacking that exceeds 0.10 foot requires removal and replacement as specified [2]. When rigid concrete pavements are being diamond‑ground, Pavement Profile measurements are used to verify cross‑slope uniformity at 100‑foot longitudinal intervals, and any segment where the deviation exceeds 1/4 inch in a 12‑ft straightedge must be corrected to preserve the original cross‑slope for drainage [2]. In the ride‑quality acceptance criteria for reinforced cement‑concrete panels and plain cement‑concrete slabs, the transverse profile may not contain a ridge or valley greater than 1/8 inch relative to adjacent pavement, and any individual bump larger than 1/4 inch measured with a 12‑foot straightedge is deemed defective; compliance also requires that the verification MRI does not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches per mile on at least 90 % of the 528‑ft lots and that the average may not exceed the acceptance average by more than 6.1 inches per mile, with corrective actions such as carbide grinding, diamond grinding, or removal and replacement required when these limits are exceeded [2]. For flexible asphalt surfaces constructed as a full‑depth overlay—including overlaid bridge approach slabs or decks—Pavement Profile measurements are applicable where at least two of the listed construction operations (e.g., profile milling, base, scratch, leveling, binder, wearing course) are present; measurements must be taken on lanes that are at least 12 feet wide and not classified as excluded areas. Excluded conditions include vertical transition zones, bridge decks that are not overlaid, ramps shorter than 1,500 feet and narrower than 12 feet, tapered pavements, shoulders, medians, pavement within five feet of appurtenances, partial lots under 100 feet, and any roadway with a posted speed limit of 40 mph or lower [2]. For the surface‑course ride quality of such flexible asphalt pavements, any individual bump that exceeds 3/16 inch on a 12‑foot straightedge or any ridge or valley that exceeds 1/8 inch on the transverse profile requires corrective action such as grinding, diamond grinding, or removal and replacement of the surface course [2]. The Pavement Profile measurement for rigid concrete road surfaces—including approach slabs and relief joints—applies to lanes at least 12 feet wide that are not excluded; a full lot is defined as 528 feet of a single lane, with lot type determined by whether the posted speed limit is greater than 45 mph (Type 1) or less than or equal to 45 mph (Type 2), and excluded conditions include bridge decks, any approach slab or relief joint placed under another contract, ramps shorter than 1,500 feet, tapered pavements narrower than 12 feet, shoulders, medians, and partial lots shorter than 100 feet [2]. Pavement Profile must be taken with equipment verified according to PTM No. 428 and operated by a Department‑certified person in the presence of the Representative [2].

The surface‑profile evaluation defined in Section 404 is applicable only to ride‑quality lots that are full‑depth overlays and satisfy the inclusion criteria; any pavement surfaces that are listed as excluded—such as vertical transition areas at paving limits or bridge approaches, bridge decks unless overlaid, ramps shorter than 1,500 feet and narrower than 12 feet, tapered pavements, shoulders, medians, other indicated surfaces, pavement within 5 feet of appurtenances, partial lots less than 100 feet, and roadways with a posted speed limit of 40 mph or lower—must be investigated by another or complementary method because the profile measurement does not apply to them. The pavement‑profile assessment described in Section 404.3(c) is limited to detecting individual bumps larger than 3/16 inch when tested with a 12‑foot straightedge and to preventing ridge or valley deviations greater than 1/8 inch on the transverse profile, so broader smoothness deficiencies may be missed; consequently, when the acceptance MRI exceeds the maximum allowable or when the verification MRI differs from the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile for at least 90 percent of the lots, the Department requires a complementary investigation using its inertial lightweight profiler smoothness verification and will review testing procedures, equipment, and personnel. [2]

Existing information and records

A Pavement Profile review should examine the cross‑slope test documentation that records each test location and the maximum depth of depression or slope misalignment, the longitudinal roughness test records submitted before diamond grinding, and the ride‑quality (IRI) measurements taken both prior to and after diamond grinding, noting whether the IRI values meet the limits of 70 in/mile for speeds over 45 mph or 90 in/mile for speeds at or below 45 mph; it should also include records confirming that any required concrete rehabilitation was completed before grinding. The review should examine the existing roadway profile documents that depict current depressions and the planned grade line for each contract‑specified area, as well as the surveyed profile drawings prepared before work acceptance that compare the original profile grade, the design‑proposed grade, and the grade actually achieved after stabilization. When a pavement profile is evaluated, the reviewer should examine the daily calibration results recorded before any surface measurements, the raw unfiltered data files collected during the PTM No. 428 survey, and the summary printout that contains International Roughness Index (IRI) and Mean Roughness Index (MRI) values for each lot. [2]

Under SECTION 514—DIAMOND GRINDING OF CONCRETE PAVEMENT, field investigation must close the record gaps concerning the pavement profile by confirming that cross‑slope measurements have been taken at each 100‑foot interval and that any depression or slope misalignment exceeding 1/4 inch in 12 feet is documented; it also must verify that longitudinal roughness (pre‑grinding) has been tested and recorded as required, establish the pre‑and post‑grinding IRI values to ensure they meet the maximum IRI of 70 in/mile for speeds over 45 mph or 90 in/mile for speeds at or below 45 mph, and confirm that all concrete repairs were completed before grinding so that ride‑quality evaluations can be reliably compared. Under SECTION 681—SLABJACKING, the contract records require a field‑derived profile that documents the current depressions and the design grade line for each treatment area, but existing files often omit these measurements, creating uncertainty about the baseline condition; investigators must verify that the achieved profile meets the tolerance limits of within 0.02 foot (or 0.03 foot for jointed slabs) from a string line and must identify any overjacking exceeding 0.10 foot that would trigger pavement removal. [2]

Visual inspection and condition assessment

When evaluating a pavement profile, visible cracking, tilting, or faulting of concrete slabs that may develop from improper slabjacking must be inspected [1]. The inspector should also look for any grout that extrudes from joints, cracks, or the pavement edge before the target elevation is reached, as this indicates a problem with the slabjacking process [1]. Broader profile deficiencies such as settlement of an embankment that depresses the surface are another condition to be noted [1]. The transverse profile must be checked to ensure that no ridge or valley exceeds 1/8 inch when measured across adjacent pavement sections [2]. Additionally, any individual bump larger than 1/4 inch as determined with a 12‑foot straightedge is considered defective and requires corrective action [2]. The surface texture should provide a uniform cross‑slope so that drainage is maintained, and the inspection must confirm this condition [2]. Finally, the measured ride index (MRI) shall not exceed the maximum acceptable value specified for the lot; exceeding this limit triggers corrective measures [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 92–93); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 395–396)

In the evaluation of pavement profile, the first step is to classify each observed distress by its geometric form; ridges or valleys are identified on the transverse profile while individual bumps are detected with a 12‑foot straightedge. Under Section 507—Evaluation of Concrete Pavement Ride Quality and Payment of Incentive, any ridge or valley that exceeds 1/8 inch when measured on the transverse profile is considered defective and must be corrected, and any individual bump whose irregularity is more than 1/4 inch when tested with a 12‑foot straightedge likewise requires correction. The extent of each concrete defect is recorded by the length of pavement that requires treatment; use of carbide grinding is limited to areas 15 feet in length or less, and removal and replacement is applied to a minimum of 10 feet between transverse joints of reinforced cement concrete pavements. Documentation for Section 507 must note the lot number, the exact location of the defect relative to adjacent ride‑quality lots, and the measured deviation so that corrective actions can be verified. For asphalt pavements evaluated under Section 404—Evaluation of Asphalt Pavement Ride Quality and Payment of Incentive, a ridge or valley is unacceptable if it exceeds 1/8 inch on the transverse profile, and an individual bump is considered severe enough to require grinding when the irregularity is more than 3/16 inch as measured with a 12‑foot straightedge. The length of the affected area is recorded; use of carbide grinding is limited to areas 15 feet in length or less, while diamond grinding may be applied across the entire lane width. The precise location of each distress is documented within the lot or excluded‑area, and the overall smoothness is documented by comparing the lot’s MRI to the maximum acceptable MRI specified in Table A of Section 404.4 before and after corrective action. [2]

Field measurements and testing

In pavement profile work the field team first establishes a reference grade by laying a string line that is typically positioned about 1 inch above the desired finished elevation, and gauge blocks placed on the slab give a visual indication of the slab’s current height relative to that line so that pumping can be slowed or stopped when the blocks contact the line [1]. For slabjacking projects a roadway profile survey is performed before grout injection to record existing surface elevations, depressions, and the proposed grade line, and after stabilization a post‑stabilization drawing shows the existing, proposed, and achieved grades; the measured profile must be raised to within 0.02 foot of the string‑line grade for continuously reinforced concrete or within 0.03 foot for jointed slabs [2]. Transverse cross‑slope is measured at 100‑foot intervals along each lane, and any deviation greater than 1/4 inch over a 12‑ft length must be corrected [2]. Longitudinal roughness of the pavement surface is tested before and after diamond grinding with surface‑profile equipment that produces an International Roughness Index; the IRI may not exceed 70 in/mile on facilities whose posted speed limit exceeds 45 mph and may not exceed 90 in/mile where the posted speed limit is 45 mph or less [2]. Transverse profile measurements are used to locate ridges or valleys, and a ridge or valley exceeding 1/8 inch relative to adjacent pavement triggers corrective action [2]. Individual bumps are identified with a 12‑foot straightedge; an irregularity greater than 1/4 inch on concrete (or greater than 3/16 inch on asphalt) requires grinding of the affected lot [2]. An inertial lightweight profiler, calibrated according to PTM No. 428, records raw unfiltered surface data that are processed into IRI and Mean Roughness Index values; the MRI is used by the Representative to calculate incentive payments and must not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile on at least 90 percent of the 528‑foot lots, with average differences also limited to that amount [2]. The profiler operation requires daily sensor calibration, a valid PennDOT certification card for the operator, and calibration before each day’s testing; in the presence of the Representative the profiler records IRI and MRI values that indicate ride quality and compliance with the specified limits, and those data must be submitted immediately [2]. Where profiling cannot be performed, traffic cones with reflective tape are placed at lane ends and around the excluded area to trigger the profiler, or a 12‑foot straightedge is held parallel to the centerline and moved in stages of no more than six feet to manually assess the surface profile across the area [2]. Smoothness verification testing is performed on at least one day and on a minimum of ten percent of the project’s lane miles using the Department’s inertial lightweight profiler, and the verification MRI may not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 92–93); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 237–239), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 394–405), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 611–612)

Under Section 514, the governing testing requires a power‑driven, self‑propelled diamond‑grinding machine equipped with diamond blades, and the same pavement surface profile measuring equipment that is used for ride‑quality evaluations. Cross‑slope must be checked at 100‑foot intervals along the longitudinal direction, and longitudinal roughness tests must be performed before any grinding and again after grinding in accordance with Section 507.3; these results determine whether regrinding is required. Grinding shall not be performed within 2 feet of curbs, bridge barriers or roadway barriers, and slurry must be removed so that it does not enter gutters. In Section 404.3(c), smoothness verification testing employs a 12‑foot straightedge to detect any bump whose irregularity exceeds three‑sixteenths inch, and the Department’s inertial lightweight profiler is used to record the mean roughness index for overall ride‑quality assessment; the testing must be conducted on at least one day and must cover no less than ten percent of the project’s lane miles, with the profiler measurements taken concurrently with acceptance testing. Section 507.3 specifies that pavement surface profile measuring equipment shall be Department‑verified according to PTM No. 428, the distance sensor shall be calibrated and the system checked before each day’s testing, and a certified operator carrying a valid PennDOT certification card shall operate the profiler. Traffic control shall consist of cones with reflective tape and reflective tape placed on the pavement to mark start and stop points for the profiler, and excluded areas shall be profiled in stages using a 12‑foot straightedge held parallel to the roadway centerline; when moving from one stage to the next the straightedge may be advanced no more than 6 feet. No additional site or environmental conditions are prescribed. Section 404.3(b) reiterates that traffic cones fitted with reflective tape and matching pavement tape shall define start‑stop locations for the profiler, that a 12‑foot straightedge shall be used for excluded‑area measurements, and that the lot surface profile must be obtained in the presence of the Representative according to PTM No. 428; the straightedge may be moved no more than 6 feet between stages. Structural and functional performance

The pavement profile is assessed for functional condition by measuring the longitudinal surface geometry with a calibrated profiler that complies with PTM No. 428; the resulting International Roughness Index (IRI) and Mean Roughness Index (MRI) are reported for each lot and used to determine ride‑quality incentives. For concrete pavements subject to diamond grinding, cross‑slope is checked at 100‑foot intervals and any deviation greater than ¼ inch in a 12‑ft length must be corrected; IRI may not exceed 70 in/mile on roads posted above 45 mph or 90 in/mile on those at or below that speed, and the texture is defined by parallel grooves 0.09–0.13 inch wide with a land area of 0.060–0.110 inch and a depth of about 1/16 inch. Surface‑profile verification for concrete also limits transverse ridges or valleys to ⅛ inch, requires that any individual bump measured with a 12‑foot straightedge be less than ¼ inch, and mandates that the verification MRI not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches per mile on at least 90 percent of the 528‑ft lots and that the average verification MRI for all lots in a lane stay within the same margin. For asphalt pavements, the inertial lightweight profiler is used to obtain MRI values and a 12‑foot straightedge is employed to locate localized height irregularities; any bump greater than 3/16 inch must be ground and transverse cross‑sections may not show ridges or valleys larger than ⅛ inch. The same MRI acceptance criteria of not exceeding the acceptance value by more than 6.1 inches per mile on at least 90 percent of the 528‑ft lots and for the overall average apply, with length differences required to be under 0.2 percent. No measurements related to structural capacity—such as deflection, load‑transfer efficiency, layer thickness, friction or bearing strength—are prescribed in these sections. [2]

Data interpretation and diagnosis

The Pavement Policy Manual explains that the observed profile deficiency is most often caused by loss of support under the slab – voids that develop beneath the pavement structure or settlement of the underlying embankment – which create depressions that require slabjacking; weak subgrade and drainage conditions can also contribute to these voids, and construction defects such as cracked joints allow grout to escape before the target elevation is reached, indicating inadequate support [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that the profile problem is most likely due to construction defects when an individual bump exceeds 3/16 inch on a 12‑foot straightedge or when a ridge or valley with the adjacent pavement exceeds 1/8 inch on the transverse profile, because such deviations indicate that the surface was not built to the required uniform texture and positive cross slope needed for proper drainage [2].

Findings, confidence, and next actions

Using string lines positioned about 1 inch above the desired grade together with gauge blocks, slabjacking can reliably establish a target pavement profile while lifting; when the blocks contact the line pumping is stopped, allowing the corrected elevation to be achieved and providing a defined profile that can guide void‑filling and slab raising and may reduce or eliminate faulting when combined with dowel bar retrofits. However, this approach is limited by temperature sensitivity because high temperatures make slabjacking usually impractical as slabs expand and joints are in compression, and there is a risk of additional damage such as cracking, tilting, or faulting if the procedure is not performed correctly. Under Section 514 diamond grinding may be used to re‑establish the original pavement cross‑slope when any deviation exceeds 1/4 inch in 12 feet, and the longitudinal roughness after grinding must not exceed 70 in/mile on roads with posted speed limits greater than 45 mph or 90 in/mile when posted speed limits are less than or equal to 45 mph; the source provides no information on how the restored profile behaves over the service life or under varying traffic loads, leaving uncertainty about long‑term drainage performance and ride‑quality retention. Section 681.3(a) requires profile drawings that allow the engineer to verify that a stabilized slab meets grade tolerances of within 0.02 foot for continuously reinforced concrete pavements or within 0.03 foot for jointed slabs, and if the measured rise exceeds 0.10 foot the pavement must be removed and replaced per Section 516, so Pavement Profile supports acceptance decisions and corrective‑action triggers but its conclusions are limited to measured elevation compliance and do not address other performance factors or underlying conditions. The transverse profile measurement can flag a defect when a ridge or valley exceeds more than 1/8 inch, which then triggers corrective actions such as carbide grinding, diamond grinding, or removal and replacement, and it also underpins the ride‑quality acceptance criteria by requiring that the verification MRI not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile for at least 90 % of the 528‑foot lots in each lane; reliability of these conclusions depends on the accuracy of the profiling equipment and testing procedures, because if the verification tolerances are not met the Department will review the testing methods and may suspend the contractor’s profiler certification pending recertification. When the pavement surface profile is measured according to PTM No. 428 with daily calibration, the resulting International Roughness Index and Mean Roughness Index can be used by the Representative to award ride‑quality incentives for each eligible lot defined as a full lot of 528 feet of a single lane that includes at least two listed construction operations; however bridge decks unless overlaid, ramps shorter than 1,500 feet or narrower than 12 feet, tapered pavements, shoulders, medians, any pavement within five feet of appurtenances, and roadways with a posted speed limit of 40 mph or lower are excluded from the profile measurement, so the conclusions are limited to the remaining overlay sections. Any bump exceeding 3/16 inch on a 12‑foot straightedge triggers grinding, and any ridge or valley greater than 1/8 inch on the transverse profile must be corrected before depth testing, thereby supporting decisions on corrective action, uniform texture, cross‑slope for drainage, and eligibility for incentive payment when verification MRI stays within the 6.1 inches/mile limits specified for at least 90 % of the 528‑ft lots and for the lane average; nevertheless confidence in these conclusions relies on equipment verification, daily sensor calibration, and operator certification, and because the specification does not give quantitative tolerances or uncertainty limits some measurement uncertainty remains. Profile measurements are accepted only when the measuring equipment has been verified by the Department according to PTM No. 428, the distance sensor is calibrated and the profile system checked before each day’s testing, and a Department‑certified operator who carries a valid PennDOT certification card performs the profiling; the source does not discuss any additional limitations or uncertainties regarding the accuracy, interpretation, or applicability of the profile results beyond these requirements. When a pavement profile deficiency is identified, the corrective sequence may begin with slabjacking that is monitored by an arrangement of string lines positioned about 1 inch above the target grade together with gauge blocks placed on the slab, and pumping is reduced as the blocks approach the line and stopped when they touch it [1]. The hole pattern for grout injection must be sized to the depressed area, the amount of correction required, subgrade and drainage conditions, and the locations of joints and cracks, and any grout that extrudes from joints, cracks, or edges before reaching the target elevation triggers additional slabjacking and new drill holes [1]. After achieving the desired profile, a dowel‑bar retrofit is typically applied to restore load transfer for slabs that have lost support [1]. For slabjacking under the specifications, the contractor must submit a field quality‑control plan that includes existing roadway profiles showing depressions and the proposed grade line, and before acceptance provide a Roadway Profile Report with surveyed drawings of the existing, proposed, and achieved grades; the Representative may require additional injection if the profile grade report indicates deviation [2]. The pavement must be raised to within 0.02 foot plus or minus of the string‑line grade for continuously reinforced concrete (or within 0.03 foot for jointed slabs), any excess rise is corrected by grinding, and an overjacking greater than 0.10 foot triggers removal and replacement of the slab as specified in Section 516 [2]. When diamond grinding of concrete pavement is performed, the cross‑slope must be checked at 100‑foot intervals along the length and any segment showing a deviation greater than 1/4 inch in 12 feet must be corrected to restore a positive drainage slope, with each location documented [2]. Longitudinal roughness tests are required before grinding and repeated after grinding; if the post‑grind results exceed the allowable limits, re‑grinding of the affected segments is mandated [2]. Ride quality must also be evaluated both pre‑and post‑grind using the same profile‑measuring equipment, and the finished surface must achieve an IRI not exceeding 70 in/mile where posted speeds are over 45 mph or not exceeding 90 in/mile where speeds are 45 mph or less; any joints damaged by grinding are to be resealed in accordance with the specified sections [2]. For concrete pavement ride‑quality evaluation, when a transverse profile deviation exceeds 1/8 inch or an individual bump measured with a 12‑foot straightedge exceeds 1/4 inch, correction is required before any depth testing and may include carbide grinding of sections up to 15 feet long with a maximum single‑pass depth of 1/8 inch, diamond grinding of the full lane width, or removal and replacement of at least 10 feet between transverse joints [2]. After correction the affected lots are retested for surface profile and subsequently subjected to smoothness verification testing using an inertial lightweight profiler on at least one day and on a minimum of 10 % of lane miles, coordinated with the Representative at least 14 days in advance, so that the verification MRI does not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile for 90 % of the 528‑foot lots; if the verification tolerances are not met, the Department reviews the contractor’s testing procedures and may suspend the profiler certification until recertified [2]. For asphalt pavement, when a profile inspection identifies bumps exceeding 3/16 inch on a 12‑foot straightedge or ridges/valleys over 1/8 inch in the transverse profile, corrective work such as carbide grinding of sections up to 15 feet long with a maximum single‑pass depth of 1/8 inch, diamond grinding of the full lane width, or removal and replacement of the surface course must be performed until a uniform texture and positive cross‑slope are achieved, after which the profile is retested before any pavement depth testing [2]. Subsequent smoothness verification testing with the Department’s inertial lightweight profiler must be coordinated at least 14 days in advance, run concurrently with the contractor’s acceptance test, and the verification MRI may not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile on at least 90 % of the 528‑foot lots or on the lane average; otherwise testing procedures are reviewed and the contractor’s profiler certification may be suspended pending recertification [2]. Following pavement profile measurement for asphalt work, traffic control and station marking must be provided and all debris removed before any further testing; profiling limits and excluded areas are delineated on the day of verification using cones with reflective tape at lane ends and at excluded‑area boundaries, after which a Representative confirms the limits and the surface profile for each lot is determined in accordance with PTM No. 428 [2]. The resulting IRI data and MRI values are submitted immediately to the Representative, and for any excluded areas the entire surface must be tested in stages with a 12‑foot straightedge held parallel to the centerline, advancing the test location no more than 6 feet at each step [2].
