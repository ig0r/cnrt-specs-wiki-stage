Subsurface Drainage

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Subsurface Drainage is a topic under Pavement Design.
subsurface-drainage
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-29

Subsurface drainage is a pavement‑level system that provides a positive, gravity‑driven pathway for the removal of free water and capillary moisture from the base, sub‑base and subgrade, thereby preserving subgrade support, limiting deflection, and preventing distress mechanisms such as cracking, rutting, frost‑heave and potholes [1]. The system consists of perforated pipe underdrains and edge drains placed in smooth‑sided trenches, wrapped in Class 1 geotextile, backfilled with compacted high‑permeability aggregate and sealed to preclude soil intrusion so that continuous flow is established within 24 hours of trenching and maintained throughout the pavement’s design life [2]. By lowering intersecting water tables, intercepting seepage from cuts or superelevated sections, and providing adequately sloped, highly permeable layers, subsurface drainage sustains uniform structural performance and extends service life while maintaining ride quality [1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Subsurface Drainage” also include the following subtopics: Drainage Coefficient, Drainage Structure, Permeability, Permeable Base, Positive Drainage, Subgrade Drain, and Water Infiltration.

The system relies on Permeability and Positive Drainage to establish the necessary hydraulic conditions for removing moisture from pavement layers. [1][2] Subgrade Drains and Drainage Structures provide the physical infrastructure that conveys this water away through gravity-driven pathways. The effectiveness of these components is quantified by the Drainage Coefficient in rigid pavement design, while Water Infiltration controls ensure that unwanted entry does not compromise the integrity of the Permeable Base or other structural layers. [1][2] Subsurface Drainage employs Permeable Base and Positive Drainage as structural methods that establish continuous gravity flow paths while utilizing Drainage Structure components like sealed pipe underdrains to convey water away from the pavement foundation. [1][2] To preserve subgrade support capacity, Subgrade Drain installations excavate into the native soil and connect to these drainage networks, while the overall effectiveness is quantified by the Drainage Coefficient in rigid pavement design procedures. [1][2]

Purpose and design objectives

Subsurface drainage is employed to counteract the detrimental effects of excess moisture that accumulates within and beneath pavement layers, which otherwise leads to loss of subgrade support, reduced aggregate base strength, increased deflection under traffic loads, and a suite of distress mechanisms such as cracking, rutting, frost‑heave, stripping, potholes, localized failures, and crack propagation [1]. By providing a positive, gravity‑driven pathway for water removal, the system preserves structural performance and extends service life while maintaining more uniform ride quality, especially for thin asphalt overlays [1][2]. The design calls for pipe underdrains and base drains to be placed, connected, and backfilled in a manner that precludes soil or foreign material intrusion, thereby ensuring continuous flow and preventing blockage of the drainage core [2]. Installation specifications also require outlet piping to be installed promptly—typically within 24 hours of trenching—to establish positive drainage as soon as possible after construction begins [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 521–522)

Subsurface drainage must continuously remove free water and capillary moisture from the base, sub‑base and subgrade to preserve sufficient support and deflection resistance throughout the pavement’s design life. By maintaining high permeability of aggregate layers, providing adequate slope and outlet spacing, and lowering the groundwater table while intercepting seepage from cuts, the system sustains uniform performance and meets durability objectives. The drainage network is required to be either already in satisfactory condition or upgraded during construction so that structural integrity and long‑term serviceability are not compromised. Functionally, water removal must begin immediately after placement, establishing positive gravity flow within twenty‑four hours of trenching and remaining uninterrupted through sealed joints and fittings that preclude soil intrusion. The pipe is positioned on a uniformly firm, smooth‑sided trench bottom with at least two inches of properly tamped embankment bedding, and backfill is placed in four‑inch lifts of satisfactory aggregate that are thoroughly compacted to preserve the structural support beneath the pavement and shoulder. Connections are limited to a maximum 45‑degree bend, pipe ends and outlet openings are protected as shown on standard drawings, and all components are sealed with approved fittings to guard against deterioration from sunlight or other environmental factors. Geotextile must overlap adequately across the trench top and roll ends, be laid flat without stretching, and be covered with aggregate within two weeks so that exposure does not exceed that period; traffic and construction equipment are prohibited on any uncovered fabric to avoid puncture or degradation. Safety is enhanced by barricading trenches, never leaving them open during precipitation or overnight, limiting any open trench depth to two inches, and providing clear outlet identification with a red marker embedded at least eighteen inches below the surface facing oncoming traffic. The removal of water also reduces frost‑heave pressures, limits rutting and cracking caused by softened bases, and eliminates non‑uniform surface roughness, thereby contributing to a smooth, stable driving surface for the life of the pavement. Serviceability is achieved through smooth, non‑restricting transitions that sustain gravity flow, consistent compaction of backfill, maintenance of trench geometry, and ongoing performance of the drainage system throughout its design life. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 173–174), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 521–526)

Scope and applicability

Subsurface drainage is appropriate for any pavement type—concrete, asphalt, and asphalt‑over‑concrete overlays—and should be incorporated whenever moisture in the subgrade or subbase threatens support. Typical conditions that trigger its use include wet subgrades, high water tables intersecting the subbase, cut‑to‑fill transitions, superelevated sections, median zones with known moisture problems, sag zones, and any situation where frost heave, rutting, or loss of ride quality could develop. The design calls for highly permeable base and subbase materials combined with lateral underdrains placed in the identified problem areas and continuous edge drains along the pavement’s outer edge and on the low side of superelevated sections to provide reliable gravity drainage. Both new construction and rehabilitation projects benefit from this approach. In addition, subsurface drainage is considered acceptable for thin Superpave wearing‑course overlays less than 1½ in thick applied to non‑Interstate pavements whose existing surface is asphalt, provided the underlying structure is sound with less than about two percent of the pavement requiring patching and no structural upgrade is needed. In such cases the project must already have good surface drainage or include improvements that bring surface and subsurface drainage to a satisfactory condition. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

Subsurface drainage is not the preferred solution when the subgrade consists of fine, capillary‑active soils that retain water in the base layer, or when the groundwater table intersects the subbase so that gravity flow cannot be achieved. In those situations the drains cannot remove all moisture and designers must consider alternatives such as installing lateral underdrains at appropriate locations, providing surface grading to divert water, or selecting a less permeable but more stable base material; a hybrid system that combines limited drainage with stronger materials may also be favored when achieving high permeability would compromise construction‑equipment stability or raise costs beyond acceptable limits. The same constraints govern the use of a thin asphalt overlay: it is viable only when the existing surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than 2 % patching required, and both surface and subsurface drainage are already adequate or can be upgraded without needing a structural upgrade. If any of these conditions—especially inadequate subsurface drainage—are not met, designers must adopt another rehabilitation approach such as a thicker overlay, an interlayer system, or a different repair method. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

Design inputs and requirements

Design of subsurface drainage for pavement projects is required to follow PennDOT’s Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards together with the Design Manual Part 2 (Publication 13M) [1]. All outlets that lie within mowable areas must be protected by appropriate headwalls as specified in Publication 72M [1]. The sizing of base drains and the spacing of their outlets are governed by the procedure described in Chapter 10, Section 10.3.D of Publication 13M [1]. Continuous edge drains must be provided along the outer perimeter of the pavement; this requirement is explicit for interstate facilities in RC‑30M of Publication 72M and is applied similarly to non‑interstate routes [1]. At cut‑to‑fill transitions, lateral underdrains are strongly encouraged to promote rapid gravity drainage through highly permeable base and subbase materials placed on maximized slopes [1]. Thin‑asphalt overlays on non‑interstate segments may be placed only when the existing subsurface drainage system is already adequate or will be upgraded to meet the project’s “good” performance level, and such overlays must also conform to the pavement depth limits shown in Table 9.5 and observe the weather restrictions set forth in Publication 408, Specification Section 413.3(b) [1]. Subsurface drainage installations must also conform to the Standard Drawings and the specifications identified in Sections 600, 601.3(f) and Section 212.3(b) [2]. The design calls for Class 1 subsurface‑drainage provisions, requiring smooth side and bottom trench surfaces to prevent fabric bridging or damage from rock projections [2]. Trench dimensions follow the drawings; width is as shown and depth must extend at least two inches into the subgrade [2]. After fine grading, a Class 1 geotextile is placed flat in the trench without stretching, with adjacent rolls overlapped by an amount equal to the trench width, and exposure limited to two weeks before being covered with aggregate [2]. Backfill material shall be fine‑aggregate Type A for the main pipe section, placed in hydraulically compacted lifts not exceeding nine inches, each lift sprayed with water at a rate of one gallon per linear foot [2]. The outlet portion is backfilled in four‑inch layers of satisfactory embankment material that are thoroughly tamped, and the segment beneath pavement or shoulder is compacted as required by Section 601.3(f) [2]. Pipe must rest on a minimum of two inches of properly tamped embankment material; any bends in connections may not exceed forty‑five degrees, and connections are to be made with manufacturer‑approved bands, split couplings or special fittings sealed to prevent soil intrusion and inspected by the Inspector‑in‑Charge before final backfill [2]. Plastic pipe ends must be protected from sunlight using suitable endwalls and backfill, and each outlet is marked with a red marker embedded at least eighteen inches deep [2]. Open trenches may not remain open greater than two inches deep overnight or during precipitation, and barricading shall comply with 67 PA Code Chapter 203 [2]. Installation of prefabricated pavement‑base drains requires written submission of the installation method to the Representative at least two weeks before work begins, and any deviation from the approved procedure must receive written approval [2].

Design methods and criteria

Subsurface drainage design proceeds through a sequence that first evaluates the potential for gravity‑drainable water, then identifies moisture held by capillary action, seeks to lower intersecting water tables, and plans interception of seepage from cuts or slopes.[1] The design calls for highly permeable base and subbase materials, with permeability judged in the field by gradation rather than laboratory testing, and for maximizing subbase slopes to promote rapid drainage.[1] Lateral underdrains are placed in wet zones and at cut‑to‑fill transitions, while drain outlets located in mowable areas are protected with headwalls as specified in roadway construction standards.[1] The spacing and sizing of base drains and their outlets are determined by the method presented in Publication 13M Design Manual Part 2, Chapter 10, Section 10.3.D, which provides the empirical relationships and equations used for sizing.[1] Construction of a Class 1 subsurface drainage system begins with excavating a trench whose sides and bottom are smoothed to prevent fabric bridging or damage from rock projections.[2] A geotextile is then laid flat in the trench without stretching, folded over the sidewalls, and overlapped at roll ends by an amount equal to the trench width to ensure continuity.[2] Aggregate backfill is placed and compacted up to the pipe invert level before installing the drainage pipe, after which the remaining trench volume is filled with additional compacted aggregate while keeping the fabric protected.[2] Overlap of adjacent fabric rolls across the backfill at the trench top follows the standard drawing details, and the installed fabric must be covered with aggregate within two weeks to avoid exposure.[2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 173–174)

The subsurface drainage system must be capable of removing any free water that accumulates in the base and subbase layers by gravity while also providing for the removal or control of capillary moisture that does not drain naturally. When the groundwater table intersects the subbase, drains are required to lower it, and seepage originating from cuts or slopes must be intercepted to prevent upward migration into the pavement structure. Drain outlets are required to be protected against crushing or plugging so that the entire drainage network remains functional over its design life. Lateral underdrains are placed in zones of wet subgrade and at every cut‑to‑fill transition, and continuous edge drains are mandated along pavement edges on the low side of superelevations and in median sections where water problems have been identified, thereby creating a reliable long‑term drainage framework. The base and subbase materials must be highly permeable to allow rapid gravity flow, and the slope of the subbase should be maximized within geometric constraints to enhance drainage efficiency. Pipe and outlet spacing shall be kept as small as practicable, with pipe sizing performed in accordance with Publication 13M, and for thin asphalt overlays on non‑Interstate pavements the existing subsurface drainage must be classified as good or upgraded to a good condition before overlay acceptance. Installation of the drainage system begins only after fine grading is completed, at which point a Class 1 geotextile is placed flat in the trench without stretching and its edges are overlapped by an amount equal to the full trench width. No portion of the geotextile may remain uncovered for more than two weeks before aggregate backfill is applied, ensuring that the fabric remains protected from exposure. The trench must be excavated so that both sidewalls and bottom are uniformly firm and smooth, preventing the fabric from bridging depressions or being punctured by rock projections, and it must extend at least two inches below the subgrade to provide adequate cover for the pipe. Pipe underdrain or base‑drain pipe is then laid on a minimum of two inches of satisfactorily tamped embankment material, with perforations oriented downward to promote gravity flow toward a protected outlet that is marked with a red indicator positioned at least eighteen inches beneath the surface. All pipe connections and fittings are required to be sealed against soil intrusion and limited to deflection angles of forty‑five degrees or less; where plastic pipe is used, concrete endwalls or equivalent sunlight protection must be provided. Backfill is placed in compacted lifts that never exceed nine inches in height when hydraulically compacted, although the practice of installing four‑inch layers that are thoroughly tamped is prescribed to achieve uniform density throughout the trench. The aggregate employed for backfilling shall be fine Type A material, and each lift must be adequately moist to attain the specified compaction, while trenches deeper than two inches are prohibited from being left open overnight to prevent moisture infiltration before sealing. By adhering to these dimensional thresholds—minimum cover depths, smooth trench surfaces, controlled bend angles, prescribed geotextile handling, and strict backfill compaction limits—the subsurface drainage design attains the reliability required for long‑term pavement performance. Materials and mixture design

Subsurface drainage design calls for the base and subbase layers to be constructed from granular material that exhibits high permeability, allowing rapid gravity‑driven water removal and limiting capillary rise [1]. The required permeability is achieved through a particle‑size distribution that creates large intergranular voids, a condition verified by gradation testing as used by PennDOT, although the guides do not prescribe exact mixture ratios for these layers [1]. In addition to the granular base, the drainage system incorporates perforated pipe whose perforations are oriented downward and whose semicircular profile rests on the trench bottom with its smooth circular side in contact with the substrate [2]. Pipe joints must be fitted so that any change in direction does not exceed forty‑five degrees, ensuring hydraulic continuity [2]. A Class 1 geotextile fabric is mandated for every base drain and must be placed in a trench whose sides and bottom are smooth to prevent bridging of depressions or puncture by rock projections [2]. The fabric is laid flat without stretching, overlapped laterally across the top of the trench by at least one trench width, with end laps equal to the trench width, and must be covered with aggregate within two weeks of placement to protect it from exposure [2]. Backfill material consists of fine‑aggregate designated as Type A, which is placed in hydraulically compacted lifts not exceeding nine inches in height [2]. Prior to compaction each lift is sprayed with water at a rate of one gallon per linear foot of trench, and the aggregate is compacted up to the pipe invert level before pipe installation, after which the remaining trench is filled and similarly compacted [2]. No explicit mixture proportions for the fine‑aggregate are prescribed beyond these placement, moisture, and compaction specifications [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 173–174), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 521–522)

In subsurface drainage design the base and subbase layers are specified to be highly permeable aggregates so that gravity‑drainable water can be removed rapidly, while their gradation is used as an indicator of that permeability [1]. Because a completely open structure may not provide a stable platform for construction equipment, the guides allow some reduction in permeability to achieve sufficient workability and support during placement of pavement layers [1]. The subbase slope is maximized and the spacing between drainage pipes and outlets minimized to increase the rate at which water moves through the granular material toward the underdrains [1]. Lateral underdrains are positioned in identified wet zones and at cut‑to‑fill transitions, and their outlets are protected from crushing or plugging to preserve long‑term structural support, durability against moisture damage, constructability, and consistent pavement performance [1]. All base drains incorporate Class 1 geotextile as required by the standards, providing both separation of fine soils and filtration of water while preventing soil migration into the drainage system [2]. Perforated pipe is installed with perforations facing downward and its ends sealed to prevent intrusion of surrounding material, thereby maintaining unobstructed flow through the underdrain network [2]. The trench for the underdrain is excavated according to standard drawings, extending at least two inches into the subgrade, and backfilled with fine‑aggregate Type A placed in hydraulically compacted lifts not exceeding nine inches in thickness; each lift receives a water spray of one gallon per linear foot to achieve the prescribed density and moisture content [2]. Connections and fittings are limited to manufacturer‑approved details that avoid flow restriction, ensuring a positive gravity‑drainage system capable of removing water within twenty‑four hours after trenching is completed [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 521–522)

Structural details and interfaces

In subsurface drainage design each structural interface—such as the junction between base and sub‑base, the transition from pavement edge to shoulder or median, and cut‑to‑fill changes—is treated as a location for highly permeable material together with appropriately positioned drains [1]. The base and sub‑base layers are specified to be of high permeability so that water can move across their boundary by gravity, and the slope of the sub‑base is maximized to accelerate that flow [1]. Drainage pipes and outlets are spaced closely enough to intercept water before it accumulates at any interface, with lateral underdrains placed in wet subgrade zones and at every cut‑to‑fill transition, while continuous edge drains run along the outer pavement edge, the low side of superelevated sections, and median areas where moisture problems have been identified; outlet protection such as headwalls is provided [1]. The physical installation of these interfaces begins with a trench excavated to the dimensions shown on the standard drawings and extending at least two inches into the subgrade, the bottom and side walls being smoothed and firm to assure positive gravity flow toward the outlet [2]. Prior to pipe placement a minimum two‑inch layer of satisfactorily tamped embankment material is laid as bedding for the pipe invert, after which the drainage pipe—whether an underdrain or a base drain—is positioned on this bedding and connected to existing structures using approved couplings [2]. All pipe bends are limited to 45 degrees or less, and splices overlap by at least six inches—or by an amount equal to the trench width—to maintain continuity and prevent soil intrusion [2]. When a prefabricated pavement‑base drain is used, the trench is backfilled with fine aggregate in compacted lifts typically four inches thick, each layer being thoroughly tamped while preserving continuous contact with Class 1 geotextile fabric that is laid flat without stretching [2]. The geotextile overlaps are required to be a minimum of six inches or an end‑to‑end lap equal to the trench width, and the fabric is protected from traffic for no longer than two weeks after placement [2]. After pipe installation the remaining voids are filled with compacted aggregate up to the pipe invert, then the rest of the trench is backfilled and compacted in accordance with the pavement layer requirements, and pipe ends—especially when plastic—receive concrete endwalls or other protective measures; each outlet is identified by a red marker set at least eighteen inches below the traffic‑facing surface [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 173–174), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 521–526)

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

The primary goal of subsurface drainage design is to eliminate free water from beneath the pavement by gravity while limiting capillary rise in fine soils. To achieve rapid removal, the guides require that base and sub‑base layers be composed of highly permeable granular material and that those layers be placed on a maximized slope so that water can flow quickly through the structure. Where the subgrade is naturally wet or where cut‑to‑fill transitions occur, lateral underdrains are strongly encouraged, and the trench for these drains must extend at least two inches into the subgrade before being sealed and backfilled with compacted fill. All drainage cores are to be wrapped in Class 1 geotextile, stored on elevated platforms protected from weather and ultraviolet exposure until placement, and installed promptly so that positive gravity flow is established within twenty‑four hours of trenching. The installation procedure also mandates that trenches not be left open during precipitation or overnight to prevent moisture infiltration and adverse environmental exposure. Drain outlets are protected against crushing or plugging, ensuring long‑term functionality of the gravity‑drainage system. Before any overlay is placed, the existing subsurface drainage must be verified as adequate—or upgraded—to a satisfactory condition because interlayer systems only address surface moisture and cannot resolve underground sources such as springs, groundwater seepage, or standing water in ditches. When such underground water sources are identified, dedicated drainage work must be completed prior to pavement construction to prevent frost heave and other climate‑induced distress. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 521–522)

Subsurface drainage systems must be designed to protect drain outlets from crushing or plugging, requiring headwalls in mowable areas and other shielding measures. The geometric layout calls for maximizing the slope of subbase layers to accelerate gravity flow within granular materials, and providing continuous edge drains along the pavement perimeter, especially on the low side of superelevated sections and at medians where water accumulation is a concern. At cut‑to‑fill transitions lateral underdrains are installed with appropriately spaced pipes to maintain consistent gravity drainage throughout the structure. Trenches for the drains are excavated to the width shown on standard drawings and must extend at least two inches below the existing subgrade, with sides and bottom smoothed so that geotextile fabric does not bridge depressions or become punctured by rock projections. The pipe is placed on a minimum of two inches of well‑tamped embankment material, and any fittings or outlet connections are limited to deflection angles of forty‑five degrees or less to preserve flow efficiency. After the pipe is set, aggregate backfill is placed and compacted up to the pipe invert level, then the remainder of the trench is filled with further compacted aggregate while ensuring the geotextile remains protected; overlapping of the fabric must extend at least one trench width beyond each end and be wide enough across the top to meet required overlap criteria. The geotextile must be laid flat without stretching, cannot remain exposed for more than two weeks, and traffic or construction equipment may not travel directly on uncovered fabric. For safety and accessibility, trenches are never left open during precipitation or overnight and must be barricaded at all times in accordance with applicable codes. Each outlet is marked with a red subsurface‑drain marker positioned on the side of the opening facing approaching traffic, embedded a minimum of eighteen inches (or as recommended by the manufacturer) to ensure visibility and protection. Plastic pipe ends are required to be protected from sunlight, and end protection is also mandated where the pipe passes beneath pavement or shoulder. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 173–174), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 521–526)

Verification and expected performance

The completed subsurface drainage is verified by following the prescribed construction sequence and documented inspections. After fine grading, the pipe is placed in a trench of the dimensions shown on the Standard Drawings, using Class 1 geotextile and approved fittings; each splice and outlet connection must be installed so that flow is unrestricted and soil cannot enter. The Inspector‑in‑Charge must inspect the pipe and its joints before the final backfill is placed, confirming that the trench has been properly compacted with fine aggregate Type A lifts, that all geotextile overlaps meet the minimum requirement, and that the end of the drain is sealed against intrusion. Compliance with the project specifications is further ensured by submitting the installation method to the Representative in advance and, when required, having a manufacturer’s technical representative present during installation. [2]

Proper subsurface drainage is expected to extend pavement service life by maintaining subgrade support and limiting moisture‑related deterioration, resulting in more uniform performance across the structure. By preventing water‑induced failures such as transverse cracking in concrete, rutting and surface roughness in asphalt, and localized frost heave, it also protects ride quality. These outcomes are verified through routine visual inspections that monitor the presence of those distresses and the frequency of maintenance actions. [1]
