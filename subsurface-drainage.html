Subsurface Drainage

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Subsurface Drainage is a topic under Pavement Design.
subsurface-drainage
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Subsurface drainage is an engineered system installed beneath pavement layers that provides a reliable pathway for excess water to be removed from the base, sub‑base and subgrade, thereby preserving subgrade support, aggregate strength and overall structural integrity of the roadway[1][2]. The system consists of a smooth‑walled trench extending at least two inches into the subgrade, a Class 1 geotextile filter, and perforated pipe with sealed joints and protected ends; highly permeable granular backfill and a positive gravity slope convey water continuously to a marked outlet, typically within 24 hours of trenching[2][1]. By maintaining uninterrupted flow and preventing moisture‑related distress such as cracking, rutting, frost heave and loss of ride quality, subsurface drainage extends pavement service life and ensures uniform performance throughout its design life[1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Excess moisture that accumulates within and beneath pavement layers is a primary cause of loss of subgrade support, which reduces the strength of the aggregate base, increases deflection under traffic loads, and initiates a range of distress mechanisms including cracking, rutting, frost‑heave, stripping of asphalt from aggregate, potholes, localized failures, and crack propagation[1]. The engineering need is therefore to provide an effective means of removing that water so that structural integrity, service life, and uniform ride quality are preserved[1]. Subsurface drainage systems are installed to create a reliable pathway for water beneath the pavement, establishing positive drainage quickly after trenching—typically within 24 hours—to prevent water accumulation and maintain continuous flow[2]. The design requires sealing pipe ends and using proper fittings that preclude soil or foreign material from entering the drainage core, thereby ensuring continuity of flow and protecting the pavement structure from moisture‑related distress and blockage[2]. By promptly removing water and maintaining uninterrupted drainage, the system mitigates loss of subgrade support, preserves aggregate base strength, reduces traffic‑induced deflection, and prevents the various forms of distress associated with excess moisture[1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 509–510)

Subsurface drainage must continuously remove free water and capillary moisture from the base, sub‑base and subgrade to preserve sufficient support and deflection resistance throughout the pavement’s design life. This removal creates a continuous unrestricted flow path that conveys the water by positive gravity to a marked outlet, ensuring uniform performance over time. To achieve these functional goals the system is installed after fine grading and before placement of the base, with pipe joints sealed and ends protected against intrusion. Structurally the trench is excavated at least two inches into the subgrade without disturbing the existing subbase, and its side and bottom surfaces are kept smooth so that the geotextile does not bridge depressions or become punctured by rock projections. The backfill is placed in compacted lifts not exceeding nine inches using Type A fine aggregate sprayed with one gallon of water per linear foot to ensure full contact with a Class 1 geotextile, and additional four‑inch layers of satisfactory embankment material are thoroughly tamped until the pipe invert level is reached. After the pipe invert is covered, further fully compacted aggregate protects the fabric, while the geotextile is laid flat without stretching, overlapped by at least six inches at splices and covered within two weeks to prevent exposure. Durability is attained by maintaining highly permeable aggregate layers, adequate slope and outlet spacing, lowering the water table, intercepting seepage from cuts, and using manufacturer‑approved fittings that preclude soil intrusion together with endwalls or suitable backfill to shield pipe ends from sunlight. The design also requires that the subsurface drainage system be in good condition at construction start or be upgraded to a satisfactory state during construction, thereby protecting structural integrity over the pavement’s service life. Safety is enhanced by eliminating non‑uniform frost heave, surface roughness and loss of ride quality, while construction safety measures such as trench barricading, prohibiting open trenches during precipitation or overnight, completing backfill promptly after pipe inspection, installing the outlet within twenty‑four hours, restricting traffic on uncovered fabric, and providing a red drain outlet marker visible to approaching traffic are mandated. Serviceability is ensured through these construction controls that maintain firm, smooth trench conditions, proper compaction, reliable gravity flow at the outlet, and overall drainage effectiveness throughout the pavement’s service life. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

Subsurface drainage is appropriate for any pavement type—concrete, asphalt, and asphalt‑over‑concrete overlays—and should be incorporated whenever moisture in the subgrade or subbase threatens support. Typical conditions that trigger its use include wet subgrades, high water tables intersecting the subbase, cut‑to‑fill transitions, superelevated sections, median zones with known moisture problems, sag zones, and any situation where frost heave, rutting, or loss of ride quality could develop. The design calls for highly permeable base and subbase materials combined with lateral underdrains placed in the identified problem areas and continuous edge drains along the pavement’s outer edge and on the low side of superelevated sections to provide reliable gravity drainage. Both new construction and rehabilitation projects benefit from this approach. In addition, subsurface drainage is considered acceptable for thin Superpave wearing‑course overlays less than 1½ in thick applied to non‑Interstate pavements whose existing surface is asphalt, provided the underlying structure is sound with less than about two percent of the pavement requiring patching and no structural upgrade is needed. In such cases the project must already have good surface drainage or include improvements that bring surface and subsurface drainage to a satisfactory condition. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

Subsurface drainage becomes unsuitable when the underlying soils are fine‑grained and exhibit capillary activity that holds water in the base layer, because gravity alone cannot evacuate the moisture. In such fine soils the retained water can even be drawn upward like a wick, further limiting the effectiveness of a conventional drain system. A groundwater table that intersects or lies within the subbase also defeats gravity flow, requiring the water level to be lowered before drainage can function properly. When either of these soil‑water conditions exists, designers are directed to consider alternatives such as installing lateral underdrains at appropriate locations, providing surface grading to divert runoff, or selecting a base material that is less permeable but offers greater stability. If achieving the high permeability needed for subsurface drainage would compromise the stability of construction equipment or raise project costs beyond acceptable limits, a hybrid approach that combines limited drainage with stronger materials may be favored instead. The standard installation sequence—placing the base drain after the adjacent pavement is constructed and excavating a trench without disturbing the subbase—is also impractical when the drain must pass beneath curbs or other special features, because avoiding disturbance of the existing pavement becomes paramount. In those curb‑adjacent situations the need to preserve the integrity of the existing subbase makes an alternative design approach preferable to conventional subsurface drainage. [1][2]

Design inputs and requirements

Design of subsurface drainage for pavement projects is required to conform to the applicable state roadway construction standards and design manuals, specifically the PennDOT Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards together with the Design Manual Part 2 (Publication 13M) [1]. All drainage outlets that lie within mowable areas must be protected by headwalls as prescribed in those standards [1]. The sizing of pavement base drains and the spacing of their outlets are determined by the procedure set out in Chapter 10, Section 10.3.D of Publication 13M [1]. Continuous edge drains are mandated along the outer perimeter of the pavement; this requirement is explicit for interstate facilities in RC‑30M of Publication 72M and is applied in a comparable manner to non‑interstate routes [1]. At cut‑to‑fill transitions, the guides strongly encourage the installation of lateral underdrains to achieve rapid gravity drainage through highly permeable base and subbase materials placed on maximized slopes [1]. For thin‑asphalt overlays on non‑interstate segments, placement is permitted only when the existing subsurface drainage system already meets or will be upgraded to a “good” performance level, and such overlays must respect the pavement depth limits shown in Table 9.5 and the weather restrictions of Publication 408 Specification Section 413.3(b) [1]. Execution of subsurface drainage work must follow the project specifications and referenced contract documents, with the overall procedure governed by Section 600, Subsection 610.3, which requires pipe underdrain installation after fine grading and placement of a pavement base drain only after the adjacent roadway pavement has been constructed, except in specially identified locations [2]. Trench dimensions are required to match the Standard Drawings, and each trench must extend at least two inches into the subgrade with a uniformly firm and smooth bottom to assure positive gravity flow toward the outlet [2]. Class 1 geotextile is mandatory for all base‑drain construction and, where indicated, for pipe underdrain work; it must be laid flat without stretching, with side and bottom trench surfaces prepared smooth to prevent bridging or damage, and adjacent rolls must overlap an amount equal to the full trench width, providing a minimum six‑inch overlap and prohibiting exposure of the geotextile for more than two weeks without aggregate cover [2]. Prior to pipe placement a two‑inch layer of satisfactorily tamped embankment material is placed as a bedding course, after which the pipe is installed in accordance with approved manufacturer details, using only listed connections and outlet fittings, limiting bends to no more than forty‑five degrees, and protecting plastic pipe ends from sunlight [2]. The pipe installation must be completed within twenty‑four hours of trenching as stipulated in Section 615.3 [2]. Backfill material is limited to fine aggregate Type A when hydraulic compaction is employed; it is placed in lifts not exceeding nine inches, each lift being hydraulically compacted and sprayed with water at a rate of one gallon per linear foot, while where tamped backfill is specified the trench is filled in four‑inch lifts with thorough tamping after each layer [2]. Aggregate is compacted up to the pipe invert before the pipe is set, and the final cover must extend at least six inches beyond the geotextile overlap [2]. Each outlet shall be identified by a red marker embedded at least eighteen inches below the surface on the side facing approaching traffic [2]. Trenches deeper than two inches may not be left open overnight, and the installation method must be submitted in writing to the engineer at least two weeks before work commences [2].

Design methods and criteria

Subsurface drainage design proceeds through a sequence of evaluation steps that first assess the potential for gravity‑driven removal of free water, then identify moisture held by capillary forces, seek to lower intersecting water tables, and finally intercept seepage originating from cuts or slopes. [1] The guides require the use of highly permeable base and subbase materials so that water can flow readily through them, and they advise maximizing the slope of the subbase to enhance drainage capacity. [1] In locations where the pavement is wet or at cut‑to‑fill transitions, lateral underdrains are installed, and all drain outlets situated in mowable zones must be protected with appropriate headwalls. [1] The spacing and sizing of base drains and their outlets are determined by the method prescribed in Publication 13M Design Manual Part 2, Chapter 10, Section 10.3.D, while headwall details follow the specifications of Publication 72M. [1] Rather than relying on laboratory permeability tests, gradation is employed in the field as an indicator of material permeability for the design process. [1] Installation begins with excavating a trench whose side and bottom surfaces are smoothed to prevent fabric bridging over depressions and to avoid damage from rock projections. [2] A geotextile is then laid flat in the prepared trench without any stretching, and adjacent rolls are overlapped by an amount equal to the width of the trench. [2] Aggregate backfill is placed and compacted up to the invert level of the drain pipe, after which the pipe is set in position. [2] The remaining portion of the trench is subsequently filled with additional compacted aggregate, completing the backfill. [2] Any exposed geotextile must be covered with aggregate within two weeks to protect it from environmental exposure. [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 168–169)

The subsurface drainage system shall be designed so that any free water present in the base and subbase layers can be removed by gravity, while capillary moisture that does not drain naturally is also accounted for. [1] Where the groundwater table intersects the subbase, drains must be installed to lower it, and seepage from cuts or slopes must be intercepted. [1] Drain outlets are required to be protected against crushing or plugging to maintain system functionality. [1] Lateral underdrains should be placed in zones of wet subgrade and at every cut‑to‑fill transition, and continuous edge drains are mandated along pavement edges on the low side of superelevations and in median sections where water problems have been identified, providing a reliable long‑term drainage network. [1] The base and subbase materials must be highly permeable to allow rapid gravity drainage, and the slope of the subbase should be maximized within geometric constraints. [1] Pipe and outlet spacing shall be kept as small as practicable, with pipe sizing performed in accordance with Publication 13M. [1] For thin asphalt overlays on non‑Interstate pavements, the existing subsurface drainage must be classified as good or be upgraded to a good condition before overlay acceptance; no additional safety factor or reliability threshold is imposed beyond this classification requirement. [1] The trench that houses the drain shall penetrate the subgrade by at least two inches, providing a uniformly firm and smooth bottom and side surfaces to assure positive gravity flow toward the outlet. [2] This smoothness also prevents the geotextile from bridging depressions or being damaged by rock projections. [2] A Class 1 geotextile is required, laid flat without stretch, with each roll overlapped by an amount equal to the trench width and not less than six inches, and the fabric must be covered with aggregate within two weeks of exposure. [2] Prefabricated drains are installed vertically in the trench, after which a fine‑aggregate Type A backfill is placed in hydraulically compacted lifts that do not exceed nine inches in height, each lift receiving one gallon of water per linear foot to achieve proper moisture content. [2] The pipe itself rests on at least two inches of satisfactorily tamped embankment material, and the trench is subsequently backfilled in four‑inch layers of satisfactory material, each layer being thoroughly tamped, with the portion beneath the pavement compacted according to specifications. [2] All connections are limited to a maximum bend angle of forty‑five degrees to ensure unobstructed flow. [2]

Materials and mixture design

Subsurface drainage specifications require that the base and subbase layers be constructed from granular material possessing high permeability, achieved through a particle‑size distribution that creates large voids to promote rapid gravity flow and limit capillary rise [1]. The guides indicate that this high permeability is verified by gradation testing, although they do not prescribe exact mixture proportions for the base or subbase [1]. In addition, a Class 1 geotextile fabric must be placed flat in the drainage trench without stretching, and the trench walls and bottom are required to be smooth enough to prevent the fabric from bridging depressions or being punctured by rock projections [2]. The fabric width shall be sufficient to provide at least one full‑trench‑width overlap across the backfill at the trench crown, and adjacent rolls must be overlapped an amount equal to the trench width as shown on standard drawings [2]. Once the geotextile is positioned, the void is filled with a fine‑aggregate designated as Type A, which is placed in hydraulically compacted lifts that do not exceed nine inches in thickness [2]. Each lift must be sprayed with water at a rate of one gallon per linear foot to achieve the required moisture condition before compaction [2]. The aggregate is compacted up to the pipe invert level, after which the drainage pipe is installed and the remaining trench is backfilled and compacted with the same Type A material to the surface [2]. Finally, the geotextile must be covered with aggregate within two weeks so that it is not left exposed for longer than that period [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 168–169), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 509–510)

In subsurface drainage design, the base and subbase layers are selected to be highly permeable so that gravity‑drainable water is removed rapidly, while retaining enough coarse aggregate to provide a stable platform for construction equipment [1]. The permeability of these granular layers is evaluated through their gradation, allowing designers to balance drainage capacity with workability and, where necessary, modestly reduce drainability to improve stability during placement [1]. To further enhance the rate at which water moves through the subbase, the slope of the subbase layer is maximized within geometric constraints [1]. Drainage pipe spacing is minimized, and outlet spacing is reduced, because closer pipe intervals increase the overall removal rate from the base and subbase [1]. Lateral underdrains are positioned in identified wet zones and at cut‑to‑fill transitions to intercept water where it accumulates most intensely [1]. The structural integrity of the drainage system is protected by using a Class 1 geotextile surrounding all base‑drain installations, which prevents soil intrusion and shields pipe outlets from crushing or plugging [2][1]. The trench backfill consists of fine aggregate Type A placed in hydraulically compacted lifts not exceeding nine inches, with water applied at a rate of one gallon per linear foot to achieve the required density and moisture condition [2]. Perforated pipe is installed with its perforations facing downward, and joints are constructed according to manufacturer recommendations; fittings are limited to deflection angles of 45° or less to preserve flow capacity [2]. All splice connectors and other connections are sized so that they do not restrict flow, and each connection is wrapped with at least six inches of overlapping geotextile to maintain durability against soil migration [2]. By integrating these material selections—highly permeable yet stable aggregates, fine Type A backfill, Class 1 geotextile, appropriately sized perforated pipe, and carefully controlled spacing and slope—the subsurface drainage system satisfies structural support, long‑term durability, constructability, and consistent pavement performance [1][2].

Structural details and interfaces

The subsurface drainage system must be assembled so that every pipe joint follows the manufacturer’s approved procedure and uses fittings limited to a 45‑degree deflection. Pipe sections are linked with either connecting bands or split couplings, and splice connectors are sized to avoid any restriction of flow; at least six inches of geotextile overlap is required between successive sections to maintain continuity. Connections to underdrains or pavement base drains are made with suitable couplers, fittings or special connectors, again observing the 45‑degree bend limit for each connection or series of bends. Where an outlet is not tied into an existing drain, a concrete endwall is provided to protect the pipe termination; when plastic pipe is employed, the ends are shielded from sunlight by satisfactory endwalls and backfill material. The trench that houses the drainage core is prepared with smooth side and bottom surfaces so that the geotextile fabric does not bridge depressions or become punctured by rocks. The fabric is laid flat without stretch, its edges overlapped by an amount equal to the trench width, and the overall width of the fabric is selected to give at least a full‑width overlap across the backfill at the trench top. After compacting aggregate up to the pipe invert, the pipe is placed and the remaining trench is filled with further compacted aggregate, preserving the integrity of the geotextile. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 168–169), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 509–514)

In subsurface drainage design each interface—such as between the base and subbase, at pavement edges and shoulders or median, and where cut‑to‑fill transitions occur—is treated as a location for highly permeable material and appropriately placed drains [1]. The base and subbase layers are specified to be highly permeable so that water can pass across their boundary by gravity, and the slope of the subbase is maximized to accelerate that flow [1]. Continuous edge drains are provided along the outer pavement edge, the low side of superelevated sections, and the median where moisture problems have been identified, with outlet protection such as headwalls [1]. At each cut‑to‑fill transition lateral underdrains are installed to intercept water before it can accumulate at the interface [1]. When a subsurface drain is placed, trench excavation begins by creating a uniformly firm and smooth bottom and sides free of protruding rock so that gravity flow is unhindered and the surrounding geotextile will not bridge depressions or be damaged [2]. The trench is located adjacent to the pavement edge without disturbing the underlying subbase, thereby preserving the structural integrity of the existing roadway [2]. A two‑inch layer of well‑tamped embankment material is placed first as a stable bedding for the drainage pipe [2]. The pipe is then positioned on this bedding and the remaining void is backfilled in four‑inch lifts of satisfactory material, each lift being thoroughly tamped before the next is placed to achieve proper compaction throughout the interface [2]. Connections between the new drain and any existing underdrain or base‑drain structures are made with approved couplings and fittings, limiting bends to a maximum of 45 degrees either as a single bend or a series of 45‑degree bends to maintain hydraulic efficiency [2]. End protections such as concrete walls or shielding from sunlight are provided where required to safeguard the pipe and its interface [2]. Transitions from the pipe to surrounding backfill and to outlet structures are designed to be smooth and non‑restricting, ensuring an uninterrupted flow path across the interface [2]. A geotextile filter is laid flat in the prepared trench without stretching, with its top folded back on the sides to receive aggregate, and adjacent rolls are overlapped by at least six inches or by an amount equal to the trench width, whichever provides greater continuity [2]. The fabric must be covered with compacted aggregate within two weeks of placement so that it remains in proper contact with the pipe, surrounding soil, and pavement layers, thereby protecting the interface from exposure and preserving drainage performance [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

In subsurface drainage design the primary goal is to remove free water from beneath the pavement by gravity while limiting capillary rise in fine soils. [1] To achieve rapid gravity drainage, base and sub‑base layers are selected for high permeability and are placed on a maximized positive slope that accelerates flow through the granular material. [1][2] Where wet subgrade conditions or cut‑to‑fill transitions exist, lateral underdrains are installed, with pipe ends sealed to prevent soil or foreign material entry. [1][2] Prior to installing any overlay the existing drainage system must be verified as adequate; if underground water sources such as springs, seepage, or standing water in ditches are identified, dedicated subsurface drainage work is required before pavement construction can proceed. [1] The trench for the underdrain is fine‑graded to provide smooth side and bottom surfaces, extending at least two inches into the prepared subgrade so that geotextile fabric will not bridge depressions or be punctured by rock projections. [2] All pipe and fitting connections are sealed, and the trench is never left open when rain is forecast or overnight to protect the work area from moisture intrusion. [2] Drainage components are stored on elevated platforms and wrapped to shield them from weather and ultraviolet exposure until installation. [2] Backfill material consists of fine‑aggregate Type A placed in hydraulically compacted lifts not exceeding nine inches, each lift being sprayed with water at a rate of one gallon per linear foot to achieve the required moisture content before compaction. [2] The aggregate is compacted up to the pipe invert level, after which the pipe is positioned, and remaining voids are filled with further compacted aggregate while maintaining continuous contact between pipe and surrounding geotextile. [2] Geotextile fabric is laid flat without stretch, overlapped per standard drawings, and must be covered with aggregate within two weeks of placement to prevent exposure to climatic conditions. [2] By controlling moisture ingress, providing adequate slope, sealing connections, and protecting materials from weather, the design mitigates freeze‑thaw effects that could otherwise cause frost heave and loss of structural support. [1] The combined measures ensure that water is conveyed away from the pavement structure within 24 hours of trenching, preserving uniform performance under varying environmental conditions. [2][1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 168–169), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 509–510)

Subsurface drainage designs must incorporate safety measures that protect drain outlets from crushing or plugging, typically by providing headwalls in mowable areas and shielding the openings. These protective features are combined with geometric provisions such as maximizing subbase slope to enhance gravity flow within granular materials. Continuous edge drains are required along the outer pavement perimeter, on the low side of superelevated sections, and in medians where water accumulation is a concern, while lateral underdrains at cut‑to‑fill transitions are spaced appropriately to sustain uniform performance. During construction the excavation must never be left open when rain is forecast or after work hours, and it must remain continuously barricaded according to applicable code requirements to safeguard workers and future road users. The trench profile is required to have smooth side and bottom faces so that the geotextile liner stays flat, un‑stretched, and free from puncture by rock projections, with a minimum depth extending two inches into the subgrade. All pipe fittings are limited to a maximum deflection of forty‑five degrees, whether a single bend or a series of bends, to preserve hydraulic efficiency. Any outlet opening that will be visible to traffic is marked with a red marker on the side facing approaching vehicles and embedded at least eighteen inches below the surface to prevent damage, and exposed plastic pipe ends are protected by concrete endwalls or equivalent measures against ultraviolet degradation. Geotextile continuity is achieved by overlapping adjacent rolls an amount equal to the full trench width with a minimum six‑inch overlap, laying the fabric flat without tension, and protecting it from traffic or construction equipment until fully covered; exposure may not exceed two weeks before aggregate backfill is placed. Aggregate backfill is placed in four‑inch lifts with thorough tamping to create a firm, smooth trench bottom, and each layer is compacted to maintain the designed drainage gradient and avoid surface irregularities that could affect pavement users. Together these safety, accessibility, geometric, and user‑oriented requirements ensure that subsurface drainage functions effectively while preserving structural integrity and providing safe conditions for both workers and road users. [1][2]

Verification and expected performance

The finished subsurface drain is verified by a formal inspection after the pipe and the lower two inches of backfill are placed; the Inspector‑in‑Charge confirms that the installation follows the Standard Drawings, uses Class 1 geotextile, has trench dimensions and backfill lifts as specified, and that all joints, fittings and splices meet manufacturer recommendations and do not restrict flow or allow soil intrusion. Compliance with constructability requirements is demonstrated by prior written submission of the installation method to the Representative and by using approved connections with deflection angles no greater than 45°, while performance is assured through smooth, non‑restricting transitions, proper outlet pipe shielding, and sealed pipe ends. Consistency with project specifications is further checked by confirming that geotextile overlaps are at least six inches, that perforated pipes have the perforations facing down, and that any damaged material has been rejected before placement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 509–510)

Proper subsurface drainage is expected to extend pavement service life by maintaining subgrade support and limiting moisture‑related deterioration, resulting in more uniform performance across the structure. By preventing water‑induced failures such as transverse cracking in concrete, rutting and surface roughness in asphalt, and localized frost heave, it also protects ride quality. These outcomes are verified through routine visual inspections that monitor the presence of those distresses and the frequency of maintenance actions. [1]
