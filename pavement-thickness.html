Pavement Thickness

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of

Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Pavement thickness is the engineered depth of a pavement layer selected to provide the structural capacity required for anticipated traffic loading and environmental conditions, with the amount determined by design analysis, non‑destructive testing, or other acceptable procedures[1]. It is expressed as a nominal design thickness that must fall within prescribed limits—such as 4 inches to 18 inches for roller‑compacted concrete—and be placed on site with tight tolerances, typically not more than a quarter‑inch short of the planned depth and no less than half an inch below the design thickness, to ensure load‑bearing performance, durability, and ride quality[2]. Verification is performed by coring or nondestructive testing in the presence of the Representative; any core deficient by more than ¼ inch triggers additional checks, while a deficiency exceeding ½ inch classifies the pavement as defective, guaranteeing that the constructed slab attains its intended structural depth[2].

Purpose and design objectives

Pavement thickness is selected primarily to provide the structural capacity required for anticipated traffic loading and environmental conditions. The need for additional structural layer thickness is determined by non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures. When replacement of more than 500 feet of full‑width PCC is required, a pavement design analysis shall be performed to determine if a thicker slab depth is required; prestressing may allow reductions in slab thickness of up to 4 inches relative to non‑prestressed designs but will not result in a thickness less than 8 inches. Full lane additions (≥ 10 feet) shall be designed to the same thickness as the original pavement unless circumstances dictate heavier traffic loadings for the additional lane; partial lane widening (< 10 feet) shall match existing pavement type and depth, and the same requirement applies to asphalt pavements. Roller‑compacted concrete pavements specify a minimum lift thickness of 4 inches and limit single‑lift placements to between 4 inches and not exceed 9 inches, with compaction behind the paver required to achieve at least 85 % of maximum wet density for lifts under 6 inches and 90 % for lifts 6 inches or greater, thereby providing the structural performance needed for a durable pavement. In the PCPS system the specified slab thickness includes a 1/4‑inch sacrificial layer over the nominal design thickness so that excess concrete can be removed by diamond grinding, achieving acceptable surface flatness and proper load transfer without exceeding the allowable 1/4‑inch differential across joints. Long‑life concrete pavement sections are considered defective if the average core depth is less than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch or any individual sublot core depth is less than that limit, ensuring sufficient structural depth to achieve the intended load‑bearing performance. Plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements are similarly required to have an average core depth not less than the design thickness minus 1/2 inch, a tolerance that prevents undersized sections lacking the required load‑bearing capacity and durability. Verification by coring each lot must show measured depth within ¼ inch of the planned depth; otherwise the pavement is deemed defective and must be removed or replaced, guaranteeing the constructed slab attains the intended structural depth for service performance. For asphalt‑treated permeable base courses the specified thickness is required to achieve a uniform surface that provides smooth longitudinal joints after compaction and maintains free‑draining conditions, thereby supporting the structural performance of the pavement; any section deficient in depth by 1/2 inch or more must be removed and replaced. Overlays are employed to correct structural deficiencies, surface deterioration, or rideability problems, with a leveling course of at least 1 inch providing a uniform working platform that is counted in the structural calculations. Superpave wearing and binder courses must meet the minimum thickness requirements shown in the applicable tables, and on Interstates overlays thinner than 1½ inches are prohibited without prior approval, while on non‑Interstates such thin overlays are allowed only when the existing surface is asphalt, no structural upgrade is required, and other conditions are satisfied. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses must be placed with a compacted depth between 3 inches and 5 inches, providing enough load‑bearing capacity after curing to support traffic while staying within the prescribed limits. Full‑depth reclamation layer thickness is selected based on both the composition of existing pavement/subgrade materials that may be incorporated and the structural requirement for anticipated traffic and environmental conditions, ensuring the reclaimed layer contributes appropriately to the total required pavement structure. Hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt overlays must have a final thickness at least 1½ inches greater than the milling depth, guaranteeing sufficient structural depth to support traffic and avoid thin‑layer failures that would compromise the mixture’s ability to remediate rutting and other surface distress. The calculated Design Structural Number derived from the chosen layer thicknesses must exceed the required Design SN, providing the strength needed to support anticipated traffic loads while satisfying minimum and maximum course‑depth limits and minimizing total pavement cost. For AC overlays of jointed plain concrete pavements the first step is to calculate the pavement thickness for future traffic; this thickness, together with the effective existing thickness determined from a condition survey, yields the actual overlay thickness required to ensure sufficient structural capacity for projected truck movements without premature failure. Bonded PCC overlays require calculation of the pavement thickness for future traffic and the effective existing thickness so that the concrete depth, rounded up to the nearest half‑inch and kept within specified limits, can act as a monolithic slab capable of sustaining anticipated future traffic loads. Unbonded PCC overlays similarly determine the pavement thickness for future traffic and the existing effective thickness to compute the required overlay thickness that provides uniform support and shallow repairs while preserving overall pavement integrity. The selected pavement thickness for roller‑compacted concrete shall be chosen between 4 inches and 18 inches using current PennDOT design procedures so that the slab can sustain the anticipated traffic loads and achieve a typical design life of 20 years, thereby meeting functional and structural objectives. When the RCC surface is exposed and the posted speed limit is 35 mph or greater, the surface must be textured by diamond grinding to satisfy safety requirements. Control‑joint spacing for RCC pavements is linked to thickness: slabs less than 8 inches thick require joint spacing of 15 ft to 20 ft, while slabs 8 inches or greater shall have spacing equal to the slab thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet, supporting serviceability and load transfer. For cold‑recycled asphalt base courses the compacted depth shall be at least 3 inches but shall not exceed 5 inches; this thickness must be sufficient to develop the required structural strength after curing, provide adequate durability against abrasion, and ensure serviceability through a protective wearing surface, with a minimum of one week of curing under low‑humidity, high‑temperature conditions before placement of the wearing course. In full‑depth reclamation designs the pavement thickness shall be sufficient to meet the structural requirement based on anticipated traffic and environmental conditions while reflecting the composition of existing pavement and subgrade materials that become part of the reclaimed layer; adequate thickness also contributes to a well‑drained system because excess moisture can cause loss of material integrity and weakening of structural capacity. The hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) design calls for a reclaimed asphalt layer typically 1½ inches thick, with an allowable range of 1 to 2 inches; the existing asphalt must be at least 1½ inches thicker than any milling depth, and composite pavements shall include a minimum of 1 to 2 inches of asphalt below the HIR depth, while full‑depth asphalt pavements shall provide a minimum of 2 to 3 inches below that depth, all to preserve structural integrity for the interim wearing surface. Rigid‑pavement calculated thickness is rounded to the nearest half inch in most cases and, when the slab exceeds 7 inches and the area is less than 30 000 square yards, to the nearest inch; the selected thickness must also fall within the minimum and maximum depths listed in Table 8.3 and shall not be reduced by one inch for tied concrete shoulders because that effect is already included in the load‑transfer coefficient J, thereby ensuring serviceability and ride quality. Although no specific frost‑design requirements are prescribed, the slab must provide enough structural reserve to survive spring‑thaw reductions in support, supporting durability objectives. The overlay thickness shall be sufficient to meet the functional purpose of improving ride quality or addressing low‑friction values while also providing a structural capacity that satisfies the required design life of at least eight years and not more than twenty years, thereby addressing functional, durability and serviceability goals. For binder layers over existing concrete or brick a minimum thickness of 2½ inches is required, and the wearing course shall meet the minimum thickness indicated for the material to be used; where a parking lane with critical curb reveal is present, the wearing course may be tapered to one inch at the curb but travel lanes must retain the prescribed minimum thickness, supporting serviceability. The concrete slab’s required depth will be verified by thickness testing in the presence of the Representative either with drilled cores (PTM No. 606) or with non‑destructive measurements (PTM No. 605); any core measurement deficient by more than ¼ inch requires additional coring, and a deficiency greater than ½ inch renders the pavement defective, establishing serviceability and durability tolerances for thickness. When non‑destructive testing is used, an average sublot thickness deficient by more than ¼ inch permits one retest; if the retest still exceeds the limit, a confirmatory core must be taken and the same ¼‑inch/½‑inch criteria apply, reinforcing serviceability objectives. Full‑depth precast concrete slabs shall be cast ¼ inch thicker than the nominal design thickness so that a sacrificial layer can be removed by grinding; after grinding the finished vertical differential across any joint must not exceed ¼ inch, satisfying serviceability and safety requirements for a smooth ride and proper load transfer. The slab must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 4 925 psi and flexural strength of 650 psi as specified in the shop drawings, ensuring that thickness together with concrete quality provides the structural capacity needed for functional performance. Pavement thickness must meet the structural objective of staying within the design‑thickness tolerance so that the average core depth of a lot is more than the design thickness (D) minus ½ inch and no individual sublot core depth falls below that same limit, and all pavement in a lot must be placed at a constant depth; these limits are set to ensure the pavement can perform its functional load‑carrying role while providing durability, safety and serviceability expected of a long‑life concrete pavement. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements the functional and structural objective for thickness is that the installed RCC slab must be no more than ¼ inch thinner than the design depth; a core taken from each lot must measure within ¼ inch of the planned depth, and if three cores are required their average may not exceed a ¼‑inch deficiency, otherwise the pavement is deemed defective and must be removed or replaced. Scope and applicability

The Pavement Thickness parameter is employed for a broad spectrum of rigid and flexible pavement systems as described in the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) and the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification Publication 408/2020. In the Pavement Policy Manual, Superpave wearing and binder courses applied to hot‑mix or warm‑mix asphalt projects must meet the minimum thickness values shown in Table 9.5 and assume relatively uniform layer thicknesses; the existing pavement must be stable, properly leveled, sealed and patched before resurfacing [1]. The same manual requires that reinforced or plain concrete cement pavements constructed on a prepared surface have a uniform nominal depth of not less than 5,600 square yards, and for concrete‑pavement patching the conventional jointed plain concrete design is used, with prestressing allowing reductions in slab thickness of up to 4 inches relative to non‑prestressed designs but never below an overall minimum of 8 inches [1]. When more than 500 feet of PCC lanes are replaced in all lanes, a pavement‑design analysis should be performed to determine whether a thicker slab depth is required [1]; full lane additions that are at least 10 feet wide shall be designed to the same thickness as the original concrete unless heavier traffic loadings dictate otherwise, while partial lane widenings less than 10 feet must match the existing pavement type and depth [1]. The design‑thickness procedure for roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) applies to roadways with an average daily traffic of ≤2 000 vehicles and a speed limit of ≤55 mph, and the manual states that RCC thickness may range from 4 inches to 18 inches [1]. The specifications further require a minimum lift thickness of 4 inches for RCC; lifts between 4 inches and ≤9 inches may be placed in a single pass, whereas lifts greater than 9 inches must be placed in multiple equal‑thickness lifts [2]. For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements the manual directs that steel forms whose depth and base width equal the required pavement depth be used, with built‑up form sections permitted but not greater than ½ inch, and that core measurements taken in the presence of the Representative must not be deficient by more than ¼ inch, while a deficiency exceeding ½ inch renders the pavement defective [2]. The same concrete specifications apply to RPS concrete where lots of 5 600 sq yd are evaluated; the average core depth must exceed the design thickness minus 0.5 inch with no more than one sublot below that limit, and a lot is defective if the average core depth is less than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch or if more than one sublot falls below it [2]. Full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slab pavements must be cast ¼ inch thicker than the nominal design thickness as a sacrificial layer that is excluded from structural calculations and retained for later diamond grinding [2]. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses are designed with a compacted pavement thickness between three and five inches, the minimum three‑inch thickness being achieved by at least two inches of existing asphalt plus either one inch of underlying untreated reclaimed aggregate or one inch of virgin aggregate; this approach is appropriate for collector and local access highways, shoulder reconstruction, or widening projects where traffic can be controlled or detoured during base construction and curing, the existing pavement has a sound, well‑drained base, and average daily truck traffic does not exceed 200 ESALs [1]. Full Depth Reclamation is deemed appropriate where support‑layer improvement is needed rather than merely surface distress, and the selected thickness must reflect both the composition of the existing pavement and subgrade materials that can be incorporated into the reclaimed layer as well as the structural requirement derived from anticipated traffic loads and environmental conditions [1]. Crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder dense‑graded (CRMAB) mixtures may be considered for new construction or overlay projects on Interstates and Interstate look‑alikes when average daily traffic exceeds 25 000 vehicles, cumulative ESALs exceed 10 million over a 20‑year design life, and the project area is larger than 50 000 square yards; however, layer thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches [1]. Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) asphalt typically has a depth of 1½ inches with an allowable range of 1 to 2 inches; composite pavements must retain at least 1 to 2 inches of existing asphalt beneath the recycled depth, full‑depth asphalt pavements must retain at least 2 to 3 inches beneath it, and when milling precedes HIR the remaining asphalt thickness must be at least 1½ inches greater than the milling depth [1]. The Pavement Policy Manual specifies that flexible pavement design should use either the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method available in DARWin, expressly prohibiting the Layered Analysis Thickness Design method, and when the approved structure includes an aggregate/cement or aggregate/lime/pozzolan base course both base materials must be offered as alternatives in the project bid [1]. Thin bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements are prescribed a thickness of 2.5 to 4 inches, limited to no more than 3 000 square yards per traffic lane; for projects at intersections or smaller areas two cores or nondestructive measurements are required, and core holes must be filled with the same concrete mix or an approved non‑shrink grout [2]. Bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements require a thickness of 4 to 8 inches and must be bonded to the underlying asphalt surface [2]. Pervious asphalt pavement systems require test sections of at least 225 square feet placed on a prepared subgrade; the compacted thickness may not be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness, placement is limited to air or surface temperatures of 50 °F or higher, compaction with smooth steel‑wheeled rollers is limited to four static passes, and infiltration must average greater than 200 in/hr [2]. Pervious concrete pavement systems have analogous thickness tolerances—compact thickness may not be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness—and acceptance additionally demands an average infiltration exceeding 200 in/hr, a void content of 20 % ± 5 %, and a hardened density not to exceed 130 lb/ft³ with a tolerance of ± 5 lb/ft³ [2].

When nondestructive testing or other acceptable design procedures indicate that the existing pavement cannot provide adequate structural capacity, or when the surface is not stable, properly leveled, sealed and patched, the design should shift from adding thickness to an asphalt or bonded‑concrete structural overlay—or, if required, total reconstruction (section 2.1 GENERAL) [1]. Superpave Restricted Performance Specification projects that cannot meet the minimum uniform layer thicknesses trigger consideration of alternative design options, and the impracticality of costly detours may also prompt a different approach [1]. Where vertical clearance or utility constraints limit the cross‑section, prestressed precast concrete paving can be employed because prestressing allows reductions in slab thickness of up to 4 inches relative to non‑prestressed designs (section 4.2 CONCRETE PAVEMENT PATCHING) [1]. If rapid traffic reopening is essential, accelerated concrete should be considered, and where work‑zone safety or minimum pavement time concerns dominate, precast concrete paving systems may serve as an alternative (section 4.2 CONCRETE PAVEMENT PATCHING) [1]. For lane widening, partial lane widening (< 10 feet) of asphalt pavements must match the existing pavement structure, and full‑width lane additions (≥ 10 feet) should also match the existing structure unless circumstances dictate heavier or lighter traffic loadings for the additional lane; in those cases design for the actual traffic rather than copying the original thickness is required (section 5.11 WIDENING) [1]. If an existing pavement shows structural deficiencies, surface deterioration, or poor rideability, an overlay—either unbonded concrete or asphalt—may be selected instead of adding thickness; a separate leveling course of at least 1 inch can be placed to correct profile and may be counted as a 1‑inch thick paving course in structural calculations (section 5.12 OVERLAYS) [1]. Thin asphalt overlays thinner than 1½ inches are permitted only when the existing surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than 2 % requiring patching, both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded, and no structural upgrade of the pavement is required; otherwise a thicker overlay or another treatment is preferred (section 5.12 OVERLAYS) [1]. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses should not be selected for projects carrying significantly heavy truck traffic (i.e., 200 or more daily ESALs), where the existing road has deteriorated due to poor drainage, where traffic cannot be controlled or detoured during base construction and curing, and they are constrained to a compacted depth between 3 inches and 5 inches with at least 2 inches of existing asphalt; if any of these conditions cannot be met, another design approach is preferable (section 5.13 COLD RECYCLING) [1]. Full‑depth reclamation techniques are less appropriate when the existing roadway exhibits mainly surface‑related distresses, when the current pavement is too thin or lacks sufficient subgrade material to form a viable reclaimed layer, or when drainage problems or traffic loading exceed what full‑depth reclamation can accommodate; in such cases an alternative rehabilitation strategy is preferred over relying on pavement thickness alone (section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION) [1]. Crumb‑rubber‑modified asphalt binder dense‑graded courses should not be used if a layer thicker than 2½ inches would be required, if placement in lower layers is contemplated, or if the project involves significant handwork or raking because of the mixture’s stiffness; conventional Superpave design is more appropriate under those conditions (section 5.17 CRUMB RUBBER MODIFIED) [1]. Hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt should be excluded when rutting severity exceeds 1½ inches, transverse or miscellaneous cracking depth reaches 1 inch or average crack width exceeds a medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, raveling or weathering is beyond a medium rating, rubber is present in the pavement, fiber materials exist within the top 2 inches, mastic patches larger than 5 square feet are present, asphalt thickness is less than at least 1½ inches more than the milling depth, minor corrugation or shoving deeper than 1½ inches occurs, or durable markings or raised pavement markers cannot be removed; under any of these circumstances a different design approach should be selected (section 5.19 HOT IN‑PLACE RECYCLING) [1]. When differential frost heave is expected, increasing slab thickness has limited success for rigid pavements, so a design approach that addresses frost effects as described in Section 6.2.B should be used instead of relying solely on pavement‑thickness calculations (section 8.11 CALCULATED DESIGN THICKNESS) [1]. When the design is constrained by the minimum and maximum course depths listed in Tables 9.4 and 9.5, the Specified Thickness Design method or Optimized Thickness Design method should be employed rather than a simple thickness calculation, and the Layered Analysis Thickness Design method shall not be used (section 8.11 CALCULATED DESIGN THICKNESS) [1]. When cost minimization requires adjusting surface, base and sub‑base lifts so that the least total pavement cost is incurred, alternative methods are preferred because structural adequacy demands that the calculated SN exceed the design SN (section 9.8 DESIGN STRUCTURAL NUMBER) [1]. For bonded concrete overlays of asphalt or AC/PCC composite pavements, the 1993 AASHTO thickness‑design method is inadequate and the design must shift to the bonded‑concrete‑overlay mechanistic‑empirical procedure (BCOA‑ME) instead of conventional pavement‑thickness calculations (section 10.5 AC OVERLAY OF AC/PCC) [1]. The composite AC‑overlay method is appropriate only when the existing asphalt overlay can remain in place; if material problems or deficiencies are suspected, cores shall be obtained and analyzed, and if deficiencies exist the existing asphalt overlay must be completely removed and a design for an “AC Overlay of PCC Pavement” should be performed instead of the “AC Overlay of AC/PCC” method (section 10.5) [1]. When the underlying concrete exhibits a great extent of cracking or faulting, using a bonded PCC overlay that depends on a calculated pavement thickness is not advisable; an unbonded overlay should be considered because significant distress would be reflected through the bonded layer if it is not repaired (section 10.6 BONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT) [1]. Unbonded concrete overlays are also considered when the existing concrete cannot be cost‑effectively repaired to good condition yet can serve as a subbase that offers relatively uniform support (section 10.6) [1]. When a geotextile interlayer is specified, joints with greater than 1/4‑inch faulting must be milled, and if joint faulting exceeds 1/2 inch a Superpave wearing course (Scratch) mix must be placed at the joint (section 10.7 UNBONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT) [1]. The partial‑depth repair method is limited to situations where the required repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness; when a crack, joint or spall demands a depth greater than half the slab thickness, full‑depth concrete pavement patches are called for (section 525.1 DESCRIPTION) [2].

Design inputs and requirements

Accurate site surveys that establish the theoretical surface elevations of the proposed pavement and the elevations of any existing abutting pavement, together with identified core locations on the existing pavement, are required for full‑depth precast concrete slab thickness design. For other pavement types the site assessment begins with a reliable estimate of future traffic loading, a recent pavement condition survey that documents current distress, and in‑situ testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer or lightweight deflectometer to evaluate subgrade stiffness and strength. Any drainage problems identified on site must be corrected before the new layer is placed. RCC pavement thickness design considers traffic loading information such as an average daily traffic of less than or equal to 2,000 vehicles and a speed limit of 55 mph or less for candidate roadways. Cold‑recycled base courses are not selected for projects that carry significantly heavy truck traffic, defined as 200 or more daily ESALs, and the traffic must be controllable or detoured during base construction and curing. CRMAB mixtures require traffic data showing an average daily traffic greater than 25,000 vehicles and cumulative ESALs exceeding 10 million over a 20‑year design life. For an AC overlay the future‑traffic Structural Number is computed using the values directed in Table 10.7 and flexible pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 for ESAL calculations. RCC mix design calls for a water‑to‑cement ratio as low as 0.30 (typically 0.40), aggregate size less than 1 inch, and a potential compressive strength between 4,000 psi and 10,000 psi. Cold‑recycled base mixtures require that the underlying reclaimed aggregate be recyclable and not larger than 2 inches, with a minimum existing asphalt thickness of 2 inches and, if needed, an additional 1 inch of reclaimed or virgin aggregate to achieve the minimum compacted depth of 3 inches (maximum 5 inches). CRMAB binders normally replace a dense‑graded wearing or binder course using polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, may incorporate RAP or RAS as allowed by the SSP, must be produced by the wet process, and the structural layer coefficient from Table 10.6 is used for design. AC overlay design requires the depths of all pavement layers together with their structural coefficients from Table 9.3 (with drainage coefficients set to 1.0), while subbase is excluded for Interstates and omitted when design 18‑kip ESALs exceed 5,000,000 unless its quality and drainability have been verified. Full‑depth precast concrete slab thickness design uses approved concrete mix designs that meet the minimum compressive and flexural strengths and includes a sacrificial ¼‑inch thickness added to the nominal slab depth. The estimated design life of twenty years for RCC depends on construction materials, environmental conditions and traffic volumes. Cold‑recycled base courses require a minimum one‑week cure under favorable weather—low humidity and high temperature—before the wearing course is placed. For precast concrete slabs the ambient average daily temperature and exposure conditions at the project site are used to size panels and locate expansion joints. RCC design assumes that the subgrade and subbase be prepared to support RCC compaction, and removal of soft, wet or otherwise unsuitable subbase or subgrade material is required for full‑depth precast slabs, with placement and compaction of No. 2A coarse aggregate within a ±½‑inch grade tolerance. Geotechnical data also call for drainage conditions to be adequate because poor drainage disqualifies recycling projects. Existing‑condition information indicates that RCC can be placed on unmodified surfaces, rehabilitated subgrades/subbases or newly constructed ones. Cold‑recycled base design requires the existing road to have at least 2 inches of asphalt material and uses representative material samples to characterize the existing pavement and subgrade layers. Full‑depth reclamation projects begin with a recent pavement condition survey, in‑situ testing, and core data that document current distress and layer composition. For CRMAB projects the existing‑condition information should identify whether the work is new construction or an overlay on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements, and the specified layer thickness shall not exceed 2 ½ inches. AC overlay design obtains existing pavement structure data from pavement cores; if cores are unavailable, Pavement History data from the Roadway Management System are used. In precast slab projects core locations and surveyed elevations of the existing abutting pavement are incorporated into the working drawings. [1][2]

Design methods and criteria

For full‑depth concrete repairs the required pavement thickness is obtained from a pavement design analysis performed in accordance with Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards (RC‑21M for jointed reinforced concrete pavement or RC‑27M for jointed plain concrete pavement) and the guidance of Chapter 8, using the conventional JPCP design to establish a baseline thickness [1]. When prestressing is applied, each 50 psi of mid‑slab prestress may be considered equivalent to a 1‑inch reduction in slab thickness provided that the resulting slab does not become less than 8 inches thick, and for reconstruction lengths exceeding 500 feet a thicker slab depth is evaluated through the same design procedure [1]. For lane widening of rigid pavements, full lane additions that are at least 10 feet wide shall be designed to the same thickness as the original pavement unless heavier traffic loadings dictate designing for the actual traffic, while partial lane widenings less than 10 feet shall match the existing pavement type and depth [1]. When the original transverse joint spacing is 20 feet or less, slabs of the same length as on the original pavement are used; when it exceeds 20 feet the joint spacing is adjusted to a minimum length of 10 feet and a maximum length of 17 feet [1]. The design thickness for roller‑compacted concrete pavements is obtained by applying the current PennDOT concrete pavement design procedures found in Chapter 8 Section 8.15, which can yield a thickness ranging from 4 inches to 18 inches; control joint spacing equals the slab thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet for slabs eight inches or greater and shall be 15–20 feet for slabs less than eight inches thick [1]. Overlay thicknesses are selected by consulting overlay design tables—Tables 9.3, 9.5, 10.4 and 10.5 provide the structural coefficient together with minimum and maximum placement depths and allowable scratch or leveling‑course thicknesses—and Tables 10.1 and 10.2 are used to verify that average daily traffic limits are satisfied; when a separate leveling course is required a minimum 1‑inch layer may be counted in the pavement’s structural calculations as a 1‑inch paving course [1]. The cold‑recycled asphalt base course thickness is established through the mix‑design procedure prescribed in Publication 27 Chapter 2, which specifies test methods and allowable emulsified asphalts; polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended when traffic volumes exceed 3 000 two‑lane AADT, truck percentages are high, recycling occurs early or late in the season, or rapid strength is needed, and the compacted layer shall be no less than 3 inches and not more than 5 inches thick [1]. For asphalt rubber gap‑graded (AR‑GG) wearing courses and crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder dense‑graded (CRMAB) courses the structural layer coefficients listed for Superpave courses in Table 10.6 are used as the design tool, and the layer thickness shall not exceed 2 ½ inches [1]. The calculated design thickness for a rigid pavement is obtained by entering all required input values into the DARWin software and pressing its Calculate button; the load transfer coefficient J is incorporated to reflect any tied concrete shoulders, and the resulting slab thickness must be rounded to the nearest half‑inch, except when the pavement exceeds 7 inches in thickness and the total area is less than 30 000 square yards, in which case rounding is to the nearest whole inch; the final recommended depth has to be checked against the minimum and maximum limits listed in Table 8.3 before approval [1]. In full‑depth flexible pavement design the required pavement thickness is derived by first calculating a Design Structural Number (SN) and then converting that SN to individual layer thicknesses with the equation SN = a₁d₁ + a₂d₂m₂ + a₃d₃m₃ … ; the conversion uses structural coefficients aᵢ taken from Table 9.3 and sets all drainage coefficients mᵢ to 1.0, after which either the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method in DARWin is applied to obtain structurally adequate layer depths while respecting minimum and maximum lift limits, and the Layered Analysis Thickness Design method must not be used [1]. Overlay thicknesses for flexible pavements may be obtained by applying any of the seven overlay types that can be designed using DARWin in accordance with the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures, while bonded concrete overlays require the bonded concrete overlay of asphalt mechanistic‑empirical design procedure (BCOA‑ME); traffic inputs—current ADT, projected growth, truck percentage and distribution—are used to compute the design daily 18‑kip ESALs as required by Chapter 7, and a minimum of 2½ inches of binder material shall be provided when an asphalt binder is placed over a cement concrete or brick pavement for the first time [1]. The AC overlay of jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) design begins with calculating the required pavement thickness for future traffic using a secondary screen in DARWin that mirrors the rigid‑pavement design process and requires input values guided by Table 10.8; effective existing thickness is determined with the Condition Survey Method, which uses STAMPP condition data from the Roadway Management System and guidance from Table 10.9, after which selecting the Calculate button yields the required overlay thickness directly rather than a structural number [1]. The AC overlay of AC/PCC pavement follows an analogous sequence—determining future‑traffic pavement thickness per Table 10.8, computing effective existing thickness with the Condition Survey Method per Table 10.9, and obtaining the required overlay thickness from DARWin; rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 are employed for ESAL calculations [1]. For bonded PCC overlays the procedure is to calculate future‑traffic pavement thickness in DARWin, determine effective existing thickness with the Condition Survey evaluation method, and then press Calculate to obtain the concrete overlay thickness, which must be rounded up to the nearest half‑inch and checked against the minimum and maximum depths listed in Table 10.10 [1]. The unbonded PCC overlay design uses rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 to compute ESALs, then determines future‑traffic pavement thickness via Table 10.8 and existing effective pavement thickness via Table 10.9 so that the required overlay thickness can be calculated and verified against the minimum allowed by Table 10.10 [1]. Under the slip‑form paving method of plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements, pavement thickness is established by either core drilling in accordance with PTM No. 614 or non‑destructive testing according to PTM No. 605; any core that is more than ¼ inch short of the required depth triggers additional cores at 100‑foot intervals and a deficiency greater than ½ inch classifies the slab as defective [2]. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs the nominal design thickness is increased by a sacrificial layer of ¼ inch that is excluded from structural calculations, and the thermal expansion coefficient used to determine panel size and expansion‑joint locations must be taken from the standard values provided in the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide [2]. In roller‑compacted concrete pavements lift‑thickness procedures require a minimum lift of 4 inches, limit single lifts to between 4 inches and not exceed 9 inches, and mandate that any thickness greater than 9 inches be placed in multiple equal lifts; the required compaction level varies with lift size—minimum 85 % of maximum wet density for lifts less than 6 inches and 90 % for lifts greater than or equal to 6 inches—and final rolling must achieve a minimum compaction greater than 96 % of the maximum wet density, with the compaction percentage calculated as X = (ρ_w / ρ_t) × 100 [2]. For long‑life concrete pavement the design thickness D serves as the reference when cores are taken; a lot is considered acceptable only if the average core depth exceeds D − 0.5 inch and no individual sublot core depth falls below D − 0.5 inch, otherwise the lot is deemed defective, and lots are defined as 5 600 square yards divided into four equal sublots of 1 400 square yards each [2]. Under the plain or reinforced cement concrete specifications (Section 506), pavement thickness is evaluated by comparing measured core depths to the design thickness (D); a lot is deemed defective if its average core depth is less than D − 0.5 inch or if any individual sublot core depth falls below that same threshold, triggering removal and replacement as prescribed, and acceptance requires that cores showing compressive strength less than 3 500 psi prompt an immediate investigation of the concrete operations [2]. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slab repairs pavement thickness is verified by coring the existing pavement in traffic lanes; a minimum of one core per lane is required at each repair location when the repair area is less than 100 feet in length, and if the repair length exceeds 100 feet cores must be taken at minimum 100‑foot intervals [2]. In the pervious asphalt pavement system the compacted thickness shall not be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness; no equations, models, empirical relationships, or design tools are provided for determining thickness [2]. The pervious concrete pavement system controls thickness by producing two test sections of at least 225 square feet each, and the test‑section thickness may not be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness specified in Section 501.3(s); infiltration is evaluated according to ASTM C 1701 with an average value that must exceed 200 inches per hour, void content is measured by ASTM C 1754 within a tolerance of ±5 %, and hardened density is accepted within a tolerance of ±5 pounds per cubic foot but not to exceed 130 pounds per cubic foot [2]. Thin bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavement are divided into lots representing not more than 3 000 square yards of pavement in each traffic lane; a random coring location is selected per lot according to PTM 1 and cores are drilled in accordance with PTM 606 in the presence of an inspector, while projects at intersections or under 3 000 square yards require two cores or nondestructive thickness measurements as prescribed by PTM 605 (or other approved methods), after which core holes are cleaned of laitance and filled with the original mix or a premixed non‑shrink grout [2]. Asphalt‑treated permeable base courses verify pavement thickness by carefully digging or drilling one 6‑inch diameter test hole to the full depth of the permeable base for each 3 000 square yards—or less—of completed base course; the representative measures the depth, backfills the hole with acceptable material and compacts it flush with the finished surface, and any section found deficient in depth by ½ inch or more must be removed and replaced until the depth is within ½ inch of the indicated depth [2]. Empirical quality‑index relationships are used for plain or reinforced cement concrete: for Class AA 28‑day compressive strength Q_L = ( X̄_4 − 3300 psi ) / s, and for percentage of air content Q_L = ( X̄_4 − 5.0 % ) / s together with Q_U = ( 8.5 % − X̄_4 ) / s [2].

When an asphalt‑rubber gap‑graded (AR‑GG) mixture is used as a wearing course the design shall limit the layer thickness to no more than 2½ inches and the structural layer coefficients given for Superpave courses in Table 10.6 must be employed in the structural pavement design [1]. In hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) applications the HIR layer is typically 1½ inches thick with an allowable range of 1 to 2 inches; asphalt pavement thickness must be at least 1½ inches more than the milling depth, and if paving fabric is present it must lie at least 1½ inches below the HIR cut depth; for composite pavements a minimum of 1 to 2 inches of asphalt beneath the HIR depth is required, whereas full‑depth asphalt pavements require a minimum of 2 to 3 inches beneath the HIR depth [1]. Calculated design thicknesses must be rounded to the nearest half‑inch, except when the slab exceeds 7 inches and the total area is less than 30,000 square yards, in which case rounding is to the nearest inch, and the final depth must fall within the minimum and maximum limits listed in Table 8.3; no specific frost design requirements are imposed for rigid pavements, and the designer must select a combination of variables that yields the most economical solution while preserving required durability and rideability qualities [1]. Overlay designs are intended for a minimum 8‑year structural design life and a maximum of 20 years, which drives pavement thickness selection; when an asphalt overlay is placed over cement‑concrete or brick for the first time a minimum of 2½ inches of binder material is required, and the wearing course shall have at least the minimum thickness indicated for the material to be used, with Table 10.4 providing the allowable minimum and maximum layer thicknesses for resurfacing or overlays using asphalt materials [1]. Structural section and dimensions

The Superpave base course must have a total thickness greater than 6 inches, and when an Asphalt Rich Base Course is specified it shall occupy between 3 and 6 inches of the first lift as dictated by the pavement design; fiber‑modified courses are not permitted to reduce this minimum pavement design thickness. Cold‑recycled base courses are required to be compacted to a depth of at least 3 inches and may not exceed 5 inches, with the existing pavement providing at least 2 inches of asphalt material and an additional 1 inch of reclaimed or virgin aggregate needed to achieve the minimum thickness. Roller‑compacted concrete pavements shall be placed in lifts that are at least 4 inches thick; a single lift may be between 4 inches and 9 inches inclusive, and any total pavement thickness greater than 9 inches must be obtained by using multiple lifts of equal thickness. Bonded concrete overlays applied over asphalt‑surfaced pavements are required to have a thickness ranging from 4 to 8 inches, with panel dimensions limited so that the longest dimension does not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the ratio of longer to shorter dimension does not exceed 1.5; joints in these panels shall be cut to a depth equal to one‑third of the overlay thickness with a minimum width of 1/8 inch. Pervious asphalt pavement systems must be compacted to a final thickness that is no more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness, and they are placed in lifts not exceeding 4 inches. Gefoam lightweight fill defines the pavement thickness as a minimum 12‑inch Type 2 cap placed over the main body of Type 1 blocks, and the total cover consisting of the pavement section, soil embankment and capping material shall be at least 4 feet but shall not exceed 6 feet. The subbase required beneath full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slabs must consist of compacted No. 2A coarse aggregate and be finished to a firm, even surface within plus or minus ½ inch of the specified grade and contour. Neither document provides separate dimensional requirements for treated‑soil layers. Full‑depth concrete repair slabs shall be no wider than one lane, shall have a length of at least 6 feet but not more than 15 feet (up to 20 feet when prestressed), and any patch must be at least 6 feet long; longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only on patches longer than 65 feet. The same length limits of a minimum 6 feet and a maximum of 15 feet (extended to 20 feet for prestressed units) apply to full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, whose nominal design thickness is increased by a sacrificial layer of ¼ inch; slab widths are governed by the longitudinal joint layout shown on the shop drawings. When prestressing is employed the slab thickness may be reduced but shall never be less than 8 inches. For these precast slabs the transverse joint width shall not exceed ½ inch and shall not be less than ¼ inch along its entire length, and vertical differentials across any joint are limited to ¼ inch or less. For full‑lane additions that are ten feet wide or greater the pavement thickness shall be designed to match the existing pavement unless heavier traffic loads require a design based on actual traffic, while partial lane widenings less than ten feet shall match the existing pavement type and depth. The Safety Edge edge‑support treatment applied to the outside edge of asphalt pavements and shoulders must have a depth of at least 1½ inches but shall not exceed 5 inches in total. Joint spacing on the original transverse joints influences slab lengths in widened areas; when the original spacing is twenty feet or less, slabs in the widened portion shall be the same length as on the existing pavement, and when it exceeds twenty feet the joint spacing shall be adjusted to a minimum of ten feet and a maximum of seventeen feet with intermediate joints so that slab lengths are approximately equal. Materials and mixture design

When a pavement‑thickness specification is prepared, the required material type and its properties are dictated by the particular pavement element being designed. For concrete pavement patching the designer must identify the concrete as standard Portland cement concrete defined in Publication 408 Section 704 with an optimized aggregate gradation, or alternatively a high‑strength Class AA mix for precast units; the specification shall also state whether accelerated concrete (reaching required compressive strength in 4 to 7 hours) or standard concrete (opening to traffic in 24 to 48 hours) is used [1]. For full‑depth prestressed precast slabs the required thickness and any allowable reduction shall be recorded, with reductions permitted up to four inches but never producing a slab less than eight inches thick, and the non‑prestressed baseline thickness must also be noted; the mix design for these slabs must achieve at least 4,925 psi compressive strength and 650 psi flexural strength, trial‑batch data must be submitted at least twenty‑eight days before production, the slab shall be cast a quarter inch thicker than the nominal design as a sacrificial layer, and the mix must incorporate the standard aggregate expansion coefficient from the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide to account for site temperature conditions [1][2]. For roller‑compacted concrete pavement the mix shall be defined by its basic ingredients—cement, aggregate, and water—and may employ a water‑to‑cement ratio as low as 0.30 but typically 0.40; a dense aggregate gradation with a maximum size less than one inch is permitted, air‑entrainment or limited admixtures should be indicated, the target compressive strength may range from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi, and the design thickness may range from four inches to eighteen inches [1]; acceptance of the installed pavement requires that a core depth be measured within one quarter inch of the plan depth, that the average density be at least 98 % of maximum wet density (no individual test less than 96 %), and that compressive strength be at least 3,000 psi at three and seven days and a minimum of 3,500 psi at twenty‑eight days [2]. When widening an existing asphalt pavement, partial lane widenings of less than ten feet shall match the existing pavement structure with equal depth of the same material, while full‑width lane additions of ten feet or more should also match the existing structure unless traffic conditions justify a different design; drainage layers should be kept consistent with those of the existing pavement [1]. For asphalt overlays the specification must name the exact asphalt mix and binder grade: a Superpave 6.3‑mm wearing course shall use PG 64E‑22 binder, whereas a Superpave 9.5‑mm fine‑graded wearing course shall not use PG 64E‑22; a leveling course shall have a minimum thickness of one inch, and thin overlays on Interstates may not be less than one and one‑half inches unless approved [1]. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses shall be placed with a compacted depth of at least three inches and not more than five inches; the mix design must incorporate recycled asphalt pavement that is either wearing‑course or fine‑graded together with virgin coarse aggregate of size No. 8, 57, or 67, and polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended where traffic volumes exceed 3,000 two‑lane AADT, truck percentages are high, early or late season recycling may impair curing, or rapid strength development is desired [1]. Crumb‑rubber‑modified asphalt binder dense‑graded courses shall have each layer not exceeding 2½ inches; the mixture must be produced by the wet process that modifies the binder with crumb rubber and may contain RAP or RAS material as defined in the SSP; if warm‑mix asphalt is used a chemical warm‑mix additive shall be utilized and foaming is prohibited [1]. In flexible‑pavement structural‑number design each layer’s structural coefficient a_i shall be obtained from Table 9.3 and all drainage coefficients m_i shall be set to 1.0; when the approved structure includes an aggregate/cement or aggregate/lime/pozzolan base course both materials must be listed as alternatives in the bid proposal [1]. For unbonded PCC overlays of existing PCC pavement the interlayer material shall be identified as either a geotextile interlayer or asphalt concrete, and the wearing‑course mix shall be specified; a nominal depth of one inch of a Superpave fine‑graded 9.5 mm wearing course is required, and if joint faulting exceeds one‑half inch a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix must be placed at the joint, while joints with more than one‑quarter inch faulting shall be milled before installing the geotextile [1]. Thin bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavement shall have a Portland cement concrete layer thickness between 2½ and 4 inches, and bonded concrete overlays shall have a thickness between 4 and 8 inches; no additional material properties or mixture proportions are stipulated for these overlay types [2]. Long‑life concrete pavements require that core measurements show an average depth not less than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch and no individual sublot below that limit; the associated concrete mix must achieve a compressive‑strength quality index of at least 55 %, satisfy plastic‑air content limits, use a water‑to‑cementitious material ratio between 0.37 and 0.42, attain a minimum compressive strength of 3,000 psi before traffic opening, maintain entrained air content between 3.5 % and 8.5 %, and any voids encountered during coring must be filled with the same mix or a premixed non‑shrink grout [2]. Plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements have similar core‑depth requirements and demand that the mix achieve Class AA 28‑day compressive strength based on a reference of 3,300 psi, with plastic air content not less than 5.0 % and not greater than 8.5 %, determined according to AASHTO T152 for stone‑and‑gravel aggregate or T196 for slag coarse aggregate [2]. Geofoam lightweight fill sections must identify block types, provide a minimum twelve‑inch thick layer of Type 2 blocks capping the main body, and ensure that total cover over the blocks is at least four feet but does not exceed six feet, which includes the pavement section, soil embankment and capping material [2]. Asphalt‑treated permeable base courses shall be produced by drying aggregate as needed, heating asphalt material and coating the aggregate so that a film of adequate thickness provides required binding properties; production temperature shall be below 320 °F to achieve suitable viscosity without segregation, and each placement is limited to maximum four‑inch compacted lifts [2].

Structural details and interfaces

When a concrete patch or reconstruction is designed, the design shall include load‑transfer devices at transverse joints by dowelling every patch joint and bonding the dowels along their full embedded length with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders, and designers also shall specify new pavement joints as either skewed or non‑skewed [1]. For full‑width reconstructions of all lanes, joint spacing is dictated by the chosen pavement type: a jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) calls for 30‑foot joint spacing, perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, while a jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) uses 15‑foot spacing with the same dowel and seal requirements [1]. Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only in patches longer than 65 feet [1]. In widening or lane additions the transverse joints shall be aligned to match those on the original pavement, and joint spacing follows the original pattern; when the original transverse joint spacing is 20 feet or less the slabs are constructed with the same length as on the original pavement, while if the original spacing exceeds 20 feet the joint spacing must be adjusted to a minimum of 10 feet and a maximum of 17 feet with intermediate joints added as necessary [1]. Widening of JPCP shall be constructed with plain cement concrete, whereas JRCP may be widened with either reinforced or plain cement concrete [1]. Full lane additions (≥ 10 feet) shall be designed to the same thickness as the original pavement unless heavier traffic loads require a different design, and all lane additions and widenings shall be tied to the existing pavement, providing the required connection [1]. Roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements do not require steel reinforcing or dowel bars, and forms are unnecessary; control joints may be omitted except where the Department mandates them in shoulders so that they align with the adjacent concrete mainline, and when required the joint spacing is governed by slab thickness—specifically, for RCC pavements less than 8 inches thick the control joints should be spaced between 15 feet and 20 feet, while for pavements 8 inches or greater the spacing equals the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet [1]. When a fiber‑modified course (FMC) overlay is placed on an existing concrete pavement, the overlay must be sawed and sealed over any underlying transverse joints; no other joints, reinforcement, load‑transfer systems, transitions, or connections are required by pavement thickness in this guidance, and FMC use does not permit reductions to the minimum pavement design thickness specified for the project [1]. An overlay may include a separate leveling course of at least 1‑inch thickness that may be counted as a 1‑inch thick paving layer in the structural calculations [1]. Reinforcement for thin overlays can be provided by an asphalt paving interlayer such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grids, or composite reinforcing grids, each installed beneath the new overlay to improve tensile and torsional strength and to act as a moisture barrier [1]. For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) asphalt the required pavement thickness includes that the asphalt layer be at least 1½ inches thicker than any milling depth, and if a paving fabric is present the fabric must lie a minimum of 1½ inches below the HIR cut depth; when the HIR overlay is placed on a composite concrete pavement the existing concrete joints must be stable with no movement [1]. An unbonded PCC overlay shall provide a separation layer—either a geotextile interlayer or asphalt concrete—and any concrete joint with greater than ¼‑inch faulting shall be milled; if joint faulting exceeds ½ inch, a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix shall be placed at the joint or the joints milled/grinded to reduce faulting, proper drainage of the interlayer shall be ensured by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain, and the interlayer must extend through any reconstructed shoulder and be tied to the mainline [1]. Slip‑form concrete pavements shall incorporate full‑width transverse contraction joints that contain dowel bars installed in load‑transfer unit assemblies, and each dowel may not exceed a vertical tilt or horizontal skew of 0.25 inch, nor a longitudinal translation of 1 inch and a horizontal translation of 1 inch [2]. Longitudinal joints shall be provided with tie bars, but no tie bar may be placed within 24 inches of a dowelled contraction joint and one tie bar shall be omitted adjacent to any dominant joint; fifteen tie bars shall be selected for pull‑out testing after the concrete reaches a compressive strength of 3,500 psi [2]. Reinforcement mesh must be free of rust, dirt, oil or grease and positioned on acceptable supports before placement, and edge slumping is limited to 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes and 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders measured over the six‑inch edge zone [2]. Full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs shall include a sacrificial layer that is ¼ inch thicker than the nominal design thickness; transverse joints shall be no wider than ½ inch and shall have a minimum width of ¼ inch along their entire length, while longitudinal joints shall not exceed ¾ inch [2]. Cast load‑transfer dowels and tie bars shall be placed square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface within ±1/8 inch of the locations shown on the shop drawings, a bond‑breaker coating shall be applied to the dowel portions, and where indicated foam gaskets and slab‑setting bolt hardware constitute part of the load‑transfer system [2]. A temporary asphalt wedge transition is required when a vertical drop greater than 2 inches exists between the new slab and existing pavement [2]. After placement the dowel‑bar and tie‑bar slots are patched, slab undersealing applied, and joints sawed and sealed as shown [2]. In roller‑compacted concrete construction a fresh vertical or horizontal joint may be left uncut only when the adjacent lane or lift is placed within 60 minutes; otherwise a cold joint must be prepared by cutting the full depth at least 6 inches from the exposed edge, cleaning and moistening the face before placement [2]. Control joints shall be saw‑cut to one‑quarter the slab thickness after rolling and no later than 18 hours after placement, and where indicated those joints shall be sealed in accordance with Section 501.3(n) [2]. For a bonded concrete overlay over an existing concrete‑surfaced pavement all existing transverse joints shall receive joint backing or an approved filling material placed to within ±¼ inch of the prepared surface, any exposed reinforcement or steel plates must be removed before placement, and transverse joints are saw‑cut directly over the existing joints to a depth equal to the full overlay thickness plus ½ inch while protecting dowel load‑transfer devices [2]. Intermediate transverse joints shall be provided whenever the panel length would exceed 15 feet using the same depth and width criteria; longitudinal joints shall be located accurately and sawed to create a sealant reservoir ¼ inch wide and full overlay depth plus ½ inch, after which the joint is sealed as prescribed [2]. Edge slump of the finished overlay may not exceed ¼ inch in the outer six inches adjacent to the shoulder and may not exceed ⅛ inch in the outer six inches next to an adjoining lane [2]. For a bonded concrete overlay over an asphalt‑surfaced pavement joint layout shall be based on the overlay thickness, panel dimensions shall not exceed 18 times the concrete overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio shall not exceed 1.5, joints shall be spaced as shown on the plans or approved by the Department, and sawing is the only acceptable method for creating joints, using a green cut saw to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness (D/3) and a minimum width of ⅛ inch; longitudinal joints should be avoided in wheel paths, transitions are required at the overlay limits, and reinforcement is not prescribed for this overlay [2]. Permeable concrete pavement construction joints are required either by tooling into the fresh concrete to approximately one‑quarter of the slab thickness or by saw‑cutting after sufficient compressive strength has been attained, with any saw‑cut fines removed; exterior formed edges must be rounded to a radius of at least ¼ inch, and no reinforcement, load‑transfer systems, transitions, or other connections are prescribed for pavement thickness [2]. In the Geofoam lightweight fill system vertical joints between adjacent longitudinal rows of blocks must be offset at least two feet, the top of the Type 1 fill is capped with a minimum twelve‑inch thick layer of Type 2 blocks providing at least two layers overall, and the pavement cover over the blocks shall be no less than four feet and shall not exceed six feet [2]. For the asphalt‑treated permeable base course longitudinal joints are formed by spreading the material so that it overlaps the edge of the previously placed lane by one inch to two inches and by maintaining a uniform uncompacted depth next to the compacted lane to achieve a smooth joint after compaction, while transverse construction joints are required at the end of each day’s work or whenever an interruption longer than thirty minutes occurs; a temporary vertical bulkhead is installed to form a straight joint that shall extend the full depth and width of the ATPBC, although the contractor may alternatively saw the joint [2].

For a bonded PCC overlay the interface between the new concrete layer and the existing pavement is designed to be a direct bond so that the two act as a single monolithic slab; the design process begins by calculating the required Pavement Thickness for future traffic, then determining the Effective Existing Thickness from a condition survey, and finally using the software’s calculate function to obtain the concrete thickness needed for the bonded overlay, rounding this number up to the nearest half‑inch and checking that it falls within the minimum and maximum depths specified for bonded PCC overlays [1]; the existing surface shall be scarified, shot‑blasted or water‑blasted to achieve a uniformly roughened condition, joint backing shall be installed within ±¼ inch of the existing pavement surface, placement shall occur only when the surface temperature is below 120 °F, and after placement transverse and longitudinal joints shall be saw‑cut to a depth equal to the full thickness of the overlay plus ½ inch and at least as wide as the underlying joint, then sealed in accordance with Section 501.3(n) [2]. In cold‑recycled base courses the interface between the existing asphalt layer and the new recycled mix is controlled by keeping the compacted depth between three and five inches, with at least two inches of original asphalt retained and an additional one inch of reclaimed or virgin aggregate added to meet the three‑inch minimum thickness; milling should not be cut below the bottom of the existing asphalt unless the underlying material can be recycled, thereby preserving a proper bond for the subsequent wearing course after the required curing period [1]. When designing the interfaces for a Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) treatment thin layers beneath the HIR surface should be avoided, especially if the cut is less than 1½ inches; instead a minimum of 1 to 2 inches of asphalt should be specified below the HIR depth for composite pavements and a minimum of 2 to 3 inches should be specified for full‑depth asphalt pavements, with milling before the HIR process used to address deeper cracks and help retain vertical clearance for overhead structures, curbs, gutters, and guide rails; a leveling course, minimum 1‑inch thickness, is placed to provide a uniform working platform on which the binder and/or wearing course is placed and may be counted in structural calculations, and the Safety Edge treatment should be used on the outside edge of asphalt pavements and shoulders with a depth of at least 1½ inches and not more than 5 inches [1]. In slip‑form cement concrete pavements the longitudinal joints shall be constructed parallel to and within 12 inches of final pavement markings unless the construction drawings specify otherwise, while transverse joints shall be sited over accurately marked dowel‑bar locations with load transfer units pre‑staked; dowel misalignment before concrete placement may not exceed 0.25 inch maximum displacement at each end of the dowel relative to its midpoint and longitudinal translation may not exceed 1 inch; edge slump shall be limited to 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and to 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders or ramps, and paving shall be suspended if these limits cannot be prevented; tie bars are prohibited within 24 inches of a dowelled contraction joint and not installed within 30 inches of a transverse joint, and any widened joint must be sealed before equipment or traffic is allowed on the pavement [2]. For a bonded concrete overlay on an asphalt‑surfaced pavement the existing asphalt surface shall be milled to the specified depth, cleaned of all debris and standing water, and any cracks larger than ¾ inch shall be filled with non‑shrink grout or another Department‑approved material before the final cleaning, which shall occur no more than 30 minutes prior to placement; transitions at the overlay limits shall include a geotextile debonding layer when an existing treated base course or concrete pavement lies directly beneath, joint layout shall follow the approved plan, panel dimensions shall not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio shall not exceed 1.5, and longitudinal joints should be avoided in wheel paths [2]. In pervious concrete pavement systems the thickness at layer interfaces shall be controlled by requiring test sections to be no more than ¼ inch thinner than the specified design thickness and construction joints shall be cut or tooling depth roughly one‑quarter of the slab thickness; exterior edges shall be rounded with a radius of not less than ¼ inch to ensure proper transition between the pavement and adjacent features [2]. GEOFOAM lightweight fill requires that shop drawings show the exact location and thickness of each interface—bedding material, capping material, embankment material, and the pavement section—to align the layers correctly; the main body uses Type 1 blocks and a minimum 12‑inch thick layer of Type 2 blocks caps the top, a minimum of two block layers (one Type 1 and one Type 2) is required except on side slopes, all block planes shall be parallel to the roadway longitudinal profile, vertical joints shall be offset at least 2 feet, and the uppermost layer’s longitudinal axes shall be perpendicular to that profile; the blocks must be covered by a total of at least 4 feet but not more than 6 feet of material, which includes the pavement section, soil embankment and capping material [2]. In an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base Course the interfaces are created by overlapping the newly placed lane edge over the previously placed lane by 1 inch to 2 inches for longitudinal joints, preserving a uniform uncompacted depth that yields a smooth joint after compaction; whenever work stops for more than a 30‑minute interruption or at the end of each day a temporary vertical bulkhead spanning the full depth and width of the ATPBC shall be installed, or the contractor may saw the joint, to maintain continuity of the base thickness; during spreading and finishing the screed shall be adjusted to achieve the cross‑section and depth indicated on the design grade line, and the mixture shall be allowed to cool to 100 °F before any subsequent layer is placed [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

When pavement thickness is altered for lane widening, designers should keep drainage layers consistent with those of the existing pavement and verify that adequate drainage is provided throughout the cross‑section. In thin asphalt overlay applications, a reduced pavement thickness is conditioned on surface drainage being good or upgraded to good, subsurface drainage being good or upgraded to good, and the existing pavement being structurally sound; when moisture, climate or freeze‑thaw effects are a concern, the interlayer selection criteria recommend a thicker overlay combined with an appropriate interlayer to provide a moisture barrier and mitigate cracking in harsh environments, so pavement thickness decisions must incorporate drainage adequacy, moisture protection, and climatic exposure as part of the design requirements. For full‑depth reclamation projects, selecting the reclaimed layer thickness requires balancing the composition of existing layers with the structural needs dictated by anticipated traffic and environmental conditions, and a well‑drained system is vital; any drainage deficiencies or wet subgrade locations should be identified and effective drainage installed before constructing the reclaimed pavement layer to prevent excess moisture from compromising material integrity and structural capacity. In roller‑compacted concrete design, pavement thickness—referred to as lift thickness—must be selected between a minimum of 4 inches and no more than 9 inches for single lifts, with any greater depth placed in multiple equal lifts. Placement must not occur during hard rain that could damage the surface, and placement shall be discontinued when ambient air temperature falls to 40 °F unless otherwise approved in writing. The mixture temperature shall be maintained between 50 °F and 90 °F, with monitoring if air exceeds 85 °F, and when the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 pounds per square foot per hour, windbreaks or fog misting may be used to retain moisture; the subgrade/subbase must be clean, free of ponded water, and uniformly moistened before placement to control drainage and moisture loss. For gefoam lightweight fill, the design shall provide a total cover of at least 4 feet but not more than 6 feet, the thickness including the pavement section, soil embankment and capping material, thereby protecting against moisture and other environmental influences, and where blocks are placed adjacent to a river, stream, creek or similar watercourse, the bottom layer shall be positioned at least 3 feet above the 100‑year storm elevation and at least 1 foot above the 500‑year storm elevation. In asphalt‑treated permeable base course construction, the mixture shall be placed in compacted lifts not exceeding 4 inches in thickness, and compaction shall be limited to a degree that keeps the aggregate free draining and prevents crushing; placement is prohibited when the surface or air temperature is below 35 °F or when rain is occurring, and the material must be allowed to cool to 100 °F before any subsequent layer is placed. Verification and expected performance

The completed pavement thickness for Hot‑In‑Place Recycled asphalt is verified by confirming that the installed HIR layer falls within the typical 1 ½‑inch to 2‑inch range, that the underlying asphalt meets the specified minimum of 1‑to‑2 inches for composite pavements or 2‑to‑3 inches for full‑depth asphalt pavements, and that the overall asphalt pavement thickness is at least 1 ½ inches more than the milling depth; meeting these dimensional criteria ensures compliance with design intent, constructability, performance expectations, and project requirements. For rigid pavement designs the designer enters all input values in DARWin, obtains a design thickness, rounds the result to the nearest half‑inch (for example 10.24 inches becomes 10.0 inches and 10.25 inches becomes 10.5 inches), and if the slab is thicker than 7 inches and the quantity is less than 30,000 square yards rounds to the nearest inch; the rounded value must lie within the minimum and maximum depths provided in Table 8.3. The designer then determines the combination of design variables that yields the most economical rigid pavement while maintaining required durability and rideability qualities, thereby checking performance, and finally contacts the District Pavement Management Engineer/Pavement Manager to confirm that the thickness is consistent with overall project requirements. For flexible pavements, after the thickness design is completed the calculated structural number derived from the chosen layer thicknesses must be compared to the required design SN; it shall be greater than the Design SN for the pavement to be structurally adequate. The layer thicknesses are obtained using either the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method in DARWin, with all layer drainage coefficients set to 1.0 and structural coefficients taken from Table 9.3; the resulting layer depths must also fall within the minimum and maximum course depths listed in Tables 9.4 and 9.5, and any adjustments are made to achieve the lowest total pavement cost while respecting surface, base, subbase unit costs and lift‑depth limits. For a bonded PCC overlay verification begins by calculating the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic in DARWin, then determining the Effective Existing Thickness using the Condition Survey evaluation method; the resulting concrete thickness is obtained with the Calculate button, rounded up to the nearest half‑inch, and finally confirmed that this recommended depth falls within the minimum and maximum limits set out in Table 10.10, thereby satisfying compliance, constructability, performance, and project requirements. For an unbonded PCC overlay verification starts by determining both the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic (Table 10.8) and the Existing Effective Pavement Thickness (Table 10.9), then calculating the required overlay thickness; the resulting thickness is checked against Table 10.10 to ensure it exceeds the minimum allowed for unbonded overlays, with compliance further demonstrated by preparing cross‑sections at 50‑ft intervals to estimate material quantities and by adjusting core sampling procedures to verify depth where the cross‑section varies. The Representative determines pavement thickness in his presence either by drilling cores per PTM No. 606 or by non‑destructive measurements per PTM No. 605; the pavement is divided into lots not exceeding 3,000 square yards per traffic lane and one random coring location per lot is selected with a measuring apparatus conforming to PTM No. 614, and if any core measurement is deficient by more than 1/4 inch additional cores are drilled at 100‑foot intervals until the deficiency is no greater than 1/4 inch, while the pavement is considered defective if any core measurement is deficient by more than 1/2 inch. Compliance with required thickness is also confirmed by a field‑operation QC Plan submitted at least two weeks before paving that specifies the depth‑determination method; steel forms are set so that form depth and base width equal the design thickness, may be built up no greater than 1/2 inch to achieve the required pavement design thickness, and the face of the form must not exceed 1/8 inch in 10 feet from the true plane, with daily non‑destructive testing per Section 501.3(s)2 using PTM No. 605 targets verified in the Representative’s presence. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs each slab is cast 1/4 inch thicker than the nominal design thickness to provide a sacrificial layer that is not included in design calculations, and the overall slab dimensions—including thickness—must match the authorized shop drawings and fall within the tolerances listed in Table A of Section 529.3; any slab whose fabricated dimensions lie outside those tolerances is rejected, while the 1/4‑inch sacrificial thickness permits diamond grinding to bring the slab into tolerance. For long‑life concrete pavement the Representative verifies thickness on a lot‑by‑lot basis by coring each full‑width LLCP lot; a lot is accepted when the average core depth of the lot exceeds the design thickness minus 0.5 inch and no individual sublot core depth is less than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch, with a full lot defined as 5,600 square yards consisting of four equal sublots of 1,400 square yards each. After all surface corrective work the completed LLCP thickness is further verified by testing one core or one non‑destructive measurement in each sublot, with the Representative selecting test locations and requiring that the first sublot core and every tenth core thereafter be submitted as information cores; the average lot pavement thickness is then obtained by averaging the individual sublot core thicknesses. For reinforced concrete pavements covered by RPS the completed pavement is checked by coring each sublot and comparing measured thickness to design thickness, requiring that the average core depth not be less than the design thickness minus 1/2 inch and that no individual sublot fall below that same limit; in addition the Percent Within Limits for compressive strength or plastic air content must be at least 55 percent and surface defects such as rain damage, improper joint construction, curing errors, poor finish, cracking or honeycombing that cannot be satisfactorily repaired render the pavement defective, as do depressions or high points exceeding the tolerances of Section 506.3(o) or Section 501.3(g). The Representative accepts each lot—defined as 5,600 square yards of uniform depth divided into four equal sublots of 1,400 square yards—when the average core depth is more than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch and no individual sublot core depth is less than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch, and this depth check is performed together with lot‑by‑lot acceptance of compressive strength and air content to ensure structural performance and compliance. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements the Representative selects a spot within each RCC lot using PTM No. 1 and drills one core in accordance with AASHTO T 24; if the measured depth is within 1/4 inch of plan depth the pavement thickness is deemed acceptable, whereas a deficiency greater than 1/4 inch triggers two additional cores and the average of the three must be less than a 1/4‑inch short to remain acceptable, otherwise the thickness is considered defective and must be removed and replaced. For pervious asphalt pavement the compliance check consists of constructing two test sections that use the approved joint mix formula and the same placement and finishing operations planned for production; each section must have an area of at least 225 square feet, and after curing infiltration testing performed in accordance with ASTM C 1701 on both sections must yield an average value that exceeds 200 inches per hour, while the compacted thickness may not be more than ¼‑inch less than the design thickness. Constructability is confirmed by following the prescribed subgrade preparation, employing smooth steel‑wheeled static rollers, placing the mixture in maximum 4‑inch compacted lifts and limiting compaction to no more than four passes so that porosity is retained; performance consistency with project requirements is demonstrated when the test sections meet the infiltration and thickness criteria and all construction steps—subgrade repair, detention basin preparation, aggregate placement and timely placement of the wearing course—are performed as specified. For pervious concrete pavement compliance is first verified by constructing two test sections and confirming that the test section thickness cannot be more than ¼‑inch less than the design thickness; subsequently cores are extracted from the in‑place hardened concrete and the average core length must fall within –3/8‑inch to +3/4‑inch of the specified thickness, and no individual core may deviate by more than –½‑inch. For a thin bonded concrete overlay over asphalt‑surfaced pavement the Department divides the overlay into lots not exceeding 3,000 square yards in each traffic lane; the Inspector selects one coring location at random within each lot according to PTM No. 1, and for projects at intersections or where the area is less than 3,000 square yards two cores or nondestructive thickness measurements are required in accordance with PTM No. 605, any alternative nondestructive method needing written approval from the District Executive. After coring the vertical surface of the core hole is cleaned of laitance and loose material and then filled with the mixture used to construct the course (or a premixed nonshrink grout), confirming that the completed pavement thickness complies with the design specifications and project requirements. For geof foam lightweight fill systems compliance, constructability, performance and consistency of the finished pavement thickness are verified by submitting shop drawings that detail the proposed location and layout of all blocks together with the specified thicknesses of bedding material, capping material, embankment material and the pavement section; these drawings are reviewed and accepted by the Representative, the design must show a minimum 12‑inch thick layer of Type 2 blocks capping the Type 1 block body and a total cover that is at least 4 feet but does not exceed 6 feet, where the total cover thickness includes the pavement section, soil embankment and capping material, and confirming that these dimensional requirements and the prescribed block orientations are met in the submittal checks the completed pavement thickness against the project’s structural and performance criteria. For an asphalt‑treated permeable base course the Representative requires a depth test for each 3,000 square yards (or less) of finished base by carefully digging or drilling one 6‑inch diameter hole to the full ATPBC depth, measuring the depth, and backfilling with acceptable material compacted flush; any section found deficient in depth by ½ inch or more must be removed and replaced, correcting longitudinally and transversely until the depth is within ½ inch of the indicated value. Surface irregularities are also checked with a 10‑foot straightedge moved no more than 5 feet per stage, and any irregularity exceeding ½ inch must be corrected by loosening the surface mixture and adding or removing ATPBC without grinding, ensuring constructability, performance and consistency with project specifications. The roller‑compacted concrete pavement described in the Pavement Policy Manual is expected to achieve a design life estimated at 20 years [1]. Its distress resistance is linked to the prescribed design thickness, which may range from 4 inches to 18 inches, and to control‑joint spacing that is limited to 15 feet–20 feet for pavements less than 8 inches thick and, for pavements 8 inches or greater, obtained by multiplying the pavement thickness by 2.5 feet, a practice that helps limit cracking [1]. Ride quality for this RCC type will vary with the equipment used during placement and should not be relied upon where the International Roughness Index is to be measured; when IRI is applied it serves as the measurement of ride quality [1]. For overlay designs, pavement thickness shall be selected so that the structural design life is at least 8 years and shall not exceed 20 years, defining the service‑life performance outcome for the thickness selection [1]. The specified RCC pavement thickness in the Department of Transportation specifications is expected to produce a structurally sound slab that meets prescribed density and surface‑smoothness criteria, thereby supporting long‑term performance and acceptable ride quality [2]. Density is verified by in‑place field density tests performed according to ASTM C1040 within 30 minutes of rolling, requiring a minimum compaction of 85 % of the maximum wet density for thicknesses less than 6 inches and 90 % for thicknesses greater than or equal to 6 inches directly behind the paver, and after final rolling a minimum compaction greater than 96 % of the maximum wet density for all thicknesses [2]. Surface quality is measured with a 12‑foot straightedge (or crown template) and any deviation greater than 1/4 inch must be corrected by grinding to bring the surface within tolerance [2]. The slab must also be kept moist for at least 72 hours of curing until it reaches a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi before traffic is allowed, confirming that the thickness has achieved its intended structural performance [2]. For pervious‑asphalt pavement systems, the compacted layer thickness is expected to be within a tight tolerance so that the finished thickness is not more than 1/4‑inch less than the design thickness, and this tolerance is verified on site by measuring the compacted depth after rolling [2]. Performance of the pervious system is also judged by its ability to infiltrate water, which is measured by constructing two test sections of at least 225 ft² each and testing infiltration in accordance with ASTM C1701; the average infiltration rate for the pair must exceed 200 inches per hour [2].
