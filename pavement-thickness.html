Pavement Thickness

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of

Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Pavement thickness is the designed depth of a pavement layer that provides the structural capacity needed to support anticipated traffic loads and environmental conditions, ensuring service performance throughout its design life. It is determined through engineering analysis—such as Design Structural Number calculations or concrete design procedures—and verified in the field by coring or nondestructive testing, with tolerances typically limited to a quarter‑inch shortfall before additional investigation and a half‑inch defining a defective section. The specified thickness varies with material type (e.g., 4 inches to 18 inches for roller‑compacted concrete) and construction method, but always serves to prevent premature cracking, honeycombing, or loss of load‑bearing ability while meeting minimum lift and compaction requirements. [1][2]

Purpose and design objectives

Pavement thickness is specified primarily to provide the structural capacity required for a pavement to sustain anticipated traffic loading and environmental conditions, thereby guaranteeing the service performance that would be compromised if the depth were insufficient [1][2]. An insufficient depth diminishes load‑bearing ability, promotes early cracking or honeycombing in cementitious slabs, prevents achievement of target wet‑density compaction in roller‑compacted concrete lifts, and for pervious asphalt allows the compacted layer to fall more than a quarter‑inch below design thickness, compromising both load support and intended infiltration rates [2]. The need for additional structural layer thickness is determined by non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures, and pavement thickness is verified by coring or nondestructive measurement; any core that is short by more than ¼ inch triggers additional coring, while a shortfall exceeding ½ inch renders the pavement defective for plain or reinforced concrete, roller‑compacted concrete, long‑life concrete, and pervious asphalt [1][2]. When replacement of more than 500 feet of full‑width Portland cement concrete is required, a pavement design analysis shall be performed to determine if a thicker slab depth is needed, and reductions in slab thickness may not result in a thickness less than 8 inches [1]. Full lane additions (≥ 10 feet) shall be designed to the same thickness as the original pavement unless circumstances dictate heavier traffic loadings for the additional lane, in which case design for actual traffic is required; partial lane widening (< 10 feet) shall match the existing pavement type and depth [1]. Roller‑compacted concrete pavements can be designed to support a wide range of traffic loading conditions, with design thicknesses ranging from 4 inches to 18 inches according to current PennDOT procedures, while minimum lift thickness is 4 inches and single‑lift sections are placed between 4 inches and ≤ 9 inches so that compaction of at least 85 % of maximum wet density for lifts under 6 inches and 90 % for lifts ≥ 6 inches can be achieved, avoiding cold joints [1][2]. All new and reconstructed pavements whose total base course thickness exceeds 6 inches shall incorporate aggregate‑reinforced base course (ARBC) material, which, being limited to mix designs with less than 0.3 million ESALs, promotes better resistance to bottom‑up cracking and moisture damage during service life [1]. For cold‑recycled asphalt base courses the minimum compacted depth is 3 inches and the maximum is 5 inches, ensuring that the reclaimed layer provides sufficient structural strength after curing before a wearing course is placed [1]. Full‑depth reclamation techniques select layer thickness based on both the composition of existing pavement and subgrade materials to be incorporated and the structural requirement derived from anticipated traffic and environmental conditions [1]. Hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt overlays must be at least 1½ inches thicker than the milling depth, providing enough structural depth to support traffic and avoid thin‑layer failures that would compromise the mixture’s ability to remediate rutting and other surface distress [1]. The calculated Design Structural Number derived from the chosen layer thicknesses must exceed the required Design SN, thereby delivering the strength needed to support anticipated traffic loads while satisfying minimum and maximum course‑depth limits [1]. Overlay designs are intended to correct structural deficiencies, surface deterioration, or low ride quality; a leveling course of at least 1 inch is placed to provide a uniform working platform, thin Superpave wearing overlays must be no less than 1½ inches unless the existing pavement is sound and no structural upgrade is required, and all overlay designs target a minimum 8‑year and maximum 20‑year structural design life with a binder layer of at least 2½ inches when asphalt is placed over cement concrete or brick to ensure the overall Structural Number exceeds the Overlay Design SN after accounting for the Effective Existing SN [1]. For an AC overlay of jointed plain concrete pavement, the first step is to calculate the pavement thickness required for future traffic; once this thickness is determined, the effective existing thickness is evaluated so that the actual overlay thickness can be calculated, ensuring sufficient structural capacity for projected truck movements without premature failure [1]. Bonded PCC overlays are designed by calculating the pavement thickness for future traffic and the effective existing thickness; the resulting concrete depth, rounded up to the nearest half‑inch and constrained within specified minimum and maximum limits, ensures that the new layer can sustain anticipated loads while acting monolithically with the existing slab [1]. Unbonded PCC overlays similarly determine future‑traffic pavement thickness and existing effective thickness so that the required overlay thickness provides the structural capacity needed for future traffic and supplies uniform support for shallow repairs addressing faulting in lieu of cracking the concrete pavement [1].

For roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements the design thickness shall be selected within a four‑inch to eighteen‑inch range using current PennDOT concrete pavement design procedures so that the slab can support the expected traffic loading and achieve the typical design life of twenty years. When an RCC surface is exposed and the posted speed limit is thirty‑five miles per hour or greater, the surface must be textured by diamond grinding to satisfy safety requirements. Control‑joint spacing for RCC slabs less than eight inches thick shall be fifteen to twenty feet, whereas for slabs eight inches or greater the spacing shall equal the slab thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet. For cold‑recycled asphalt base courses the compacted depth shall be at least three inches but shall not exceed five inches, and this thickness must be sufficient to develop the required structural strength after curing, provide adequate durability against abrasion, and ensure serviceability through a protective wearing surface; a minimum of one week of curing under low‑humidity, high‑temperature conditions is required before the wearing course can be placed. In full‑depth reclamation designs the selected pavement thickness shall be sufficient to meet the structural requirement based on anticipated traffic and environmental conditions while reflecting the composition of existing pavement and subgrade materials that become part of the reclaimed layer, and it shall also contribute to a well‑drained system because excess moisture can result in loss of material integrity and weakening of structural capacity; any existing drainage problems must be identified and corrected before constructing the reclaimed layer. The hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) design mandates that the reclaimed asphalt layer be thick enough to sustain performance, with a typical HIR depth of one‑and‑a‑half inches (range one to two inches), and the existing asphalt must be at least one‑and‑a‑half inches thicker than any milling depth; for composite pavements a minimum of one to two inches of asphalt shall be provided below the HIR depth and for full‑depth asphalt pavements a minimum of two to three inches shall be provided, while layers thinner than one‑and‑a‑half inches should be avoided because they may undermine the structural integrity needed for the interim wearing surface. In rigid‑pavement design the calculated thickness shall be rounded to the nearest half inch in most cases and, when the slab is thicker than seven inches and the area is less than thirty thousand square yards, to the nearest inch; the selected thickness must also fall within the minimum and maximum depths listed in Table 8.3 and shall not be reduced by one inch for tied concrete shoulders because that effect is already included in the load‑transfer coefficient J. Although no specific frost‑design requirements are prescribed, the slab must provide enough structural reserve to survive spring‑thaw reductions in support, and the designer must select variables that give the most economical solution while preserving required durability and rideability (serviceability). Overlay thickness shall be sufficient to meet the functional purpose of improving ride quality or addressing low‑friction values while also providing a structural capacity that satisfies the required design life of at least eight years and not more than twenty years. To achieve durability and serviceability, the binder layer over existing concrete or brick shall be at least two‑and‑a‑half inches thick and the wearing course shall meet the minimum thickness indicated for the material to be used; where a parking lane with critical curb reveal is present, the wearing course may be tapered to one inch at the curb but travel lanes shall retain the prescribed minimum thickness. For construction of RCC pavements the minimum lift thickness is four inches, and when placed in a single lift the thickness must be between four inches and nine inches inclusive; thicker sections are required to be placed in multiple lifts of equal thickness. Directly behind the paver a lift thinner than six inches must achieve at least eighty‑five percent of the maximum wet density, while a lift that is six inches or greater must reach at least ninety percent, and after final rolling all lifts must exceed ninety‑six percent of the maximum wet density, thereby satisfying structural integrity and serviceability goals. For rigid concrete pavements each lot’s average depth shall be more than the design thickness minus one‑half inch, and no more than one individual sublot may have a depth less than the design thickness minus one‑half inch; if the measured depth is within one‑quarter inch of plan depth the pavement is acceptable, whereas a deficiency greater than one‑quarter inch triggers additional coring and evaluation, with an average deficiency of less than one‑quarter inch deemed acceptable and any greater average deemed defective. The long‑life concrete pavement (LLCP) specification requires that the structural objective for thickness be met by keeping the average depth of a lot no lower than the design thickness minus one‑half inch and ensuring that no individual sublot falls below that same limit, failure to meet this tolerance classifying the work as defective. In concrete overlay design each unbonded panel shall not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and its aspect ratio shall be as close to one point zero as feasible but shall not exceed one point five, thereby limiting cracking and satisfying durability and serviceability goals; joint saw cuts must penetrate at least one‑third of the slab depth within twelve inches of the edge and at least one‑quarter of the depth elsewhere to ensure functional joint performance. Curing compound shall be applied at a minimum rate of one gallon (± 0.1 gallon) per one hundred fifty square feet for overlays thicker than six inches and per one hundred twenty‑five square feet for overlays six inches or thinner, supporting proper strength development and safe opening to traffic. The asphalt treated permeable base course thickness shall be kept within tight tolerances so that the completed depth is no more than one‑half inch off the indicated design depth; any portion deficient by one‑half inch or more shall be removed and replaced, and surface irregularities exceeding one‑half inch must be corrected to maintain a smooth riding surface, which supports safety and serviceability. Compaction of the base course shall avoid crushing aggregate or eliminating free drainage, thereby preserving durability. Longitudinal joints shall overlap the adjacent lane edge by one to two inches to provide structural continuity. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

Pavement thickness is employed as a design and acceptance criterion for a wide range of rigid and flexible pavement types under defined project conditions. For plain or reinforced cement‑concrete pavements the forms used to place the slab must have a depth and base width equal to the intended pavement depth, may be built up no greater than ½ inch, and any shims employed to maintain vertical alignment may not exceed a height of 1 inch [2]. Verification of installed slab thickness during new rigid‑pavement construction is performed in the presence of the Representative by core drilling according to PTM No. 606 or by nondestructive testing according to PTM No. 605, and any core that is deficient by more than ¼ inch triggers additional cores while a deficiency greater than ½ inch renders the pavement defective [2]. For plain or reinforced cement‑concrete sections classified as RPS each full‑width placement lot covering 5 600 square yards must have an average depth greater than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch and no individual sublot may be less than that limit; lots are divided into four equal sublots of 1 400 square yards [2]. Under the Long‑Life Concrete Pavement specification a lot’s average depth must also exceed the design thickness minus 0.5 inch and no sublot may fall below that same threshold, and pavement with differing depths may not be combined within any lot or sublot [2]. Full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slab systems require each slab to be cast ¼ inch thicker than the nominal design thickness; this sacrificial layer is excluded from structural calculations and provides a smooth surface after diamond grinding [2]. Roller‑compacted concrete pavements are placed using lifts sized according to a plan thickness with a minimum lift thickness of 4 inches; single‑lift sections may be placed between 4 inches and ≤9 inches, while any thickness greater than 9 inches must be built in multiple equal‑thickness lifts to satisfy density requirements, and acceptance is based on a core within ¼ inch of the planned thickness [2]. For pervious asphalt pavement systems each test section may not be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness, and during full installation the mixture may be placed only in maximum 4‑inch compacted lifts; these tolerances govern all subsequent placement of the pervious asphalt wearing course [2]. Pervious concrete pavement systems require that each test section, at least 225 square feet and built on a prepared subgrade and base, may not be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness, and this same tolerance governs production pavement [2]. Thin bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements are appropriate when placed with a thickness of 2.5 to 4 inches and divided into lots that do not exceed 3 000 square yards per traffic lane; at intersections or on projects smaller than this area two cores or nondestructive thickness measurements must be performed in accordance with PTM No. 605, and core holes are to be cleaned and then filled with the same mixture used to construct the concrete course or a premixed non‑shrink grout [2]. Bonded concrete overlays over existing asphalt‑surfaced pavements are specified where the design calls for a Portland cement concrete layer 4 to 8 inches thick, including surface preparation, transition construction, joint sawing and sealing [2]. Unbonded concrete overlays of existing concrete‑surfaced pavements control panel dimensions, joint layout and saw‑cut depths as well as curing‑compound application rates; the maximum panel size may not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness, saw cuts must be no less than D/3 within 12 inches of pavement edges and no less than D/4 elsewhere, and the curing compound must be applied at a minimum rate of 1 gallon per 150 ft² for overlays thicker than 6 inches or 1 gallon per 125 ft² for overlays 6 inches or less [2]. For flexible pavements that incorporate an asphalt paving interlayer system the first lift of the asphalt overlay over geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grid, or composite reinforcing grid must have a minimum compacted thickness of at least 1½ inches, and the interlayer shall not be installed in zones where the overlaid asphalt tapers to a compacted thickness less than 1½ inches [2]. Under the Superpave Restricted Performance Specification applied to Hot Mix Asphalt or Warm Mix Asphalt projects that use Superpave wearing and binder courses, the surface‑course thickness must satisfy the minimum values shown in Table 9.5 and the specification assumes relatively uniform layer thicknesses; the existing pavement must be stable, properly leveled, sealed and patched before resurfacing [1]. For reinforced or plain concrete cement pavements constructed on a prepared surface the specification requires a uniform nominal depth of not less than 5 600 square yards [1]. Concrete‑pavement patching follows the conventional jointed plain concrete pavement design for both non‑prestressed precast slabs and full‑depth PCC replacements, and when prestressing is employed the slab thickness may be reduced by up to 4 inches relative to the non‑prestressed design but never below an overall minimum of 8 inches, with reductions limited to 2 inches on granular bases and up to 4 inches when a stabilized base is used [1]. If more than 500 feet of PCC lanes are being replaced in all lanes, a pavement‑design analysis should be performed to determine whether a thicker slab depth is required [1]. When widening or adding lanes to uniform‑depth rigid pavements built since 1945, full lane additions that are at least 10 feet wide shall be designed to the same thickness as the original concrete unless heavier traffic loadings require design for the actual traffic; partial lane widenings that are less than 10 feet must match the existing pavement type and depth, assuming Class AA cement concrete (or JPCP/JRCP where permitted) and that the new section is tied to the existing pavement [1]. The pavement‑thickness design procedure of Chapter 8, Section 8.15 applies to Roller Compacted Concrete pavements used as a concrete pavement, a concrete shoulder, or a base course under an asphalt wearing surface on roadways whose average daily traffic does not exceed 2 000 vehicles and whose speed limit does not exceed 55 mph, as well as on temporary median crossovers, widening areas, and shoulders adjacent to reinforced or plain cement‑concrete pavements; the RCC mix is a very dry concrete with a water‑cement ratio typically around 0.40 and aggregate size less than 1 inch, and the design thickness may range from 4 inches to 18 inches [1]. For cold‑recycled base courses the compacted pavement thickness may be between three and five inches; the minimum three‑inch thickness is achieved by having at least two inches of existing asphalt plus either one inch of underlying untreated reclaimed aggregate or one inch of virgin aggregate, and this approach is appropriate for collector and local access highways, shoulder reconstruction, or widening projects where traffic can be controlled or detoured during base construction and curing, the existing pavement has a sound, well‑drained base, and the average daily truck traffic does not exceed 200 ESALs [1]. Full Depth Reclamation is appropriate where support‑layer improvement is needed rather than merely surface distress; the selected thickness must reflect both the composition of the existing pavement and subgrade materials that can be incorporated into the reclaimed layer and the structural requirement derived from anticipated traffic loads and environmental conditions, with practicality also depending on the thickness of the existing pavement and the type and amount of subgrade material available [1]. Crumb‑Rubber Modified Asphalt Binder dense‑graded courses may be used for new construction or overlay projects on Interstates and Interstate look‑alikes that have an average daily traffic greater than 25 000 vehicles, cumulative ESALs exceeding 10 million over a 20‑year design life, and a project area larger than 50 000 square yards, provided the layer thickness does not exceed 2½ inches [1]. Hot‑In‑Place Recycled asphalt projects typically place a recycled layer that is 1½ inches thick with an allowable range of 1 to 2 inches; composite pavements must retain at least 1 to 2 inches of existing asphalt beneath the recycled depth and full‑depth asphalt pavements must retain at least 2 to 3 inches beneath it, and when milling precedes HIR the remaining asphalt thickness must be at least 1½ inches greater than the milling depth [1]. The Pavement Thickness design approach is intended for full‑depth flexible pavements where a Design Structural Number is first computed and then translated into individual layer thicknesses; it should be applied when the project uses either the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method in DARWin, must not use the Layered Analysis Thickness Design method, and is appropriate for flexible pavement structures that employ an aggregate/cement or aggregate/lime/pozzolan base course offered as alternatives in the bid [1]. Overlay designs—whether required to improve ride quality, remedy structural inadequacy, or address low‑friction conditions—apply pavement‑thickness criteria to a variety of existing surfaces including asphalt concrete pavements, fractured Portland cement concrete slabs, intact PCC slabs, AC/PCC composite pavements, and both bonded and unbonded concrete overlays; when asphalt is placed over cement concrete or brick for the first time the binder layer shall include a minimum of 2½ inches of material, the wearing course shall have the minimum thickness indicated for the material to be used, and Table 10.4 defines allowable minimum and maximum layer thicknesses for all asphalt resurfacing or overlay applications [1]. A leveling course, with a minimum thickness of 1 inch, is placed to provide a uniform working platform on which the binder and/or wearing course is placed, and thin asphalt overlays may be placed with a thickness of less than 1½ inches only if the existing pavement surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound (less than 2 % requires patching), surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded, and no structural upgrade is required; otherwise a minimum of 1½ inches is required [1].

When non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures reveal that the existing pavement cannot provide adequate structural capacity, or when the surface is not stable, properly leveled, sealed and patched, the design should shift from adding thickness to an asphalt or bonded‑concrete structural overlay—or, if required, total reconstruction (section 2.1 GENERAL). Superpave Restricted Performance Specification projects must meet minimum uniform layer thicknesses; if those conditions are not satisfied, alternative design options are considered, and the impracticality of costly detours may also prompt a different approach. For concrete reconstructions that involve more than 500 feet of pavement in all lanes, a pavement design analysis may indicate a need for a thicker slab, but where vertical clearance or utility constraints limit the cross‑section, prestressed precast concrete paving can be employed because prestressing allows reductions in slab thickness of up to 4 inches relative to non‑prestressed designs (section 4.2 CONCRETE PAVEMENT PATCHING). If rapid traffic reopening is essential, accelerated concrete should be considered, and where work‑zone safety or minimum pavement time concerns dominate, precast concrete paving systems may serve as an alternative. When a lane is widened by less than ten feet, the new asphalt must be built to the same structural depth as the existing pavement and maintain consistent drainage layers; for full‑width additions of ten feet or more, the default also matches the existing structure, but if the added lane will experience heavier or lighter traffic loads, the design should be based on that actual traffic rather than simply copying the original thickness (section 5.11 WIDENING). If an existing pavement shows structural deficiencies, surface deterioration, or poor rideability, an overlay—either unbonded concrete or asphalt—may be selected instead of adding thickness; a separate leveling course of at least 1 inch can be placed to correct profile and may be counted as a 1‑inch thick paving course in structural calculations (section 5.12 OVERLAYS). Thin asphalt overlays thinner than 1½ inches are permitted only when the existing surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than 2 % requiring patching, both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded, and no structural upgrade of the pavement is required; otherwise a thicker overlay or another treatment is preferred (section 5.12 OVERLAYS). Interlayer systems are recommended when reflective cracking occurs on a sound base because they address cracking not associated with lack of structure. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses should not be selected for projects carrying significantly heavy truck traffic (i.e., 200 or more daily ESALs), where the existing road has deteriorated due to poor drainage, where traffic cannot be controlled or detoured during base construction and curing, and they are constrained to a compacted depth between 3 inches and 5 inches with at least 2 inches of existing asphalt; if any of these conditions cannot be met, another design approach is preferable (section 5.13 COLD RECYCLED ASPHALT BASE COURSES). Full‑depth reclamation techniques are less appropriate when the existing roadway exhibits mainly surface‑related distresses, when the current pavement is too thin or lacks sufficient subgrade material to form a viable reclaimed layer, or when drainage problems or traffic loading exceed what full‑depth reclamation can accommodate; in such cases an alternative rehabilitation strategy is preferred over relying on pavement thickness alone (section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES). Crumb‑rubber‑modified asphalt binder dense‑graded courses should not be used if a layer thicker than 2½ inches would be required, if placement in lower layers is contemplated, or if the project involves significant handwork or raking because of the mixture’s stiffness; conventional Superpave design is more appropriate under those conditions (section 5.17 CRUMB RUBBER MODIFIED ASPHALT BINDER DENSE‑GRADED COURSES). Hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt should be excluded when rutting severity exceeds 1½ inches, transverse or miscellaneous cracking depth reaches 1 inch or average crack width exceeds a medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, raveling or weathering is beyond a medium rating, rubber is present in the pavement, fiber materials exist within the top 2 inches, mastic patches larger than 5 square feet are present, asphalt thickness is less than at least 1½ inches more than the milling depth, minor corrugation or shoving deeper than 1½ inches occurs, or durable markings or raised pavement markers cannot be removed; under any of these circumstances a different design approach should be selected (section 5.19 HOT IN‑PLACE RECYCLED ASPHALT). When differential frost heave is expected, increasing the slab thickness is considered to have limited success for rigid pavements, so a design approach that addresses frost effects as described in Section 6.2.B should be used instead of relying solely on pavement‑thickness calculations (section 8.11 CALCULATED DESIGN THICKNESS). When the design is constrained by the minimum and maximum course depths listed in Tables 9.4 and 9.5, the Specified Thickness Design method or Optimized Thickness Design method should be employed rather than a simple thickness calculation, and the Layered Analysis Thickness Design method shall not be used (section 8.11 CALCULATED DESIGN THICKNESS). When cost minimization requires adjusting surface, base and sub‑base lifts so that the least total pavement cost is incurred, these alternative methods are preferred because structural adequacy demands that the calculated SN exceed the design SN (section 9.8 DESIGN STRUCTURAL NUMBER). When an overlay involves bonding a concrete layer to existing asphalt pavement—or to an AC/PCC composite pavement—the 1993 AASHTO thickness‑design method is inadequate, and the design must shift to the bonded‑concrete‑overlay mechanistic‑empirical procedure (BCOA‑ME) instead of conventional pavement‑thickness calculations (section 10.5 AC OVERLAY OF AC/PCC). The composite AC‑overlay method is appropriate only when the existing asphalt overlay can remain in place; if material problems or deficiencies are suspected, cores shall be obtained and analyzed, and if deficiencies exist the existing asphalt overlay must be completely removed and a design for an “AC Overlay of PCC Pavement” should be performed instead of the “AC Overlay of AC/PCC” method (section 10.5). When the underlying concrete exhibits a great extent of cracking or faulting, using a bonded PCC overlay that depends on a calculated pavement thickness is not advisable, and another design approach such as an unbonded overlay should be considered because significant distress would be reflected through the bonded layer if it is not repaired (section 10.6 BONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT). The Department encourages the use of an unbonded concrete overlay where it can provide uniform support and shallow repairs for unstable (visibly rocking) slabs instead of cracking the pavement, but it does not recommend the fracturing method prior to this work; if fracturing is preferred, only an asphalt overlay using the “AC over Fractured PCC” module should be employed (section 10.6 BONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT). An unbonded overlay is also considered when the existing concrete cannot be cost‑effectively repaired to good condition yet can serve as a subbase that offers relatively uniform support (section 10.6 BONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT). When a geotextile interlayer is specified, joints with greater than 1/4‑inch faulting must be milled, and if joint faulting exceeds 1/2 inch a Superpave wearing course (Scratch) mix must be placed at the joint (section 10.7 UNBONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT). Design inputs and requirements

The RCC pavement‑thickness design calls for site information that the candidate roadway have an average daily traffic of less than or equal to 2,000 vehicles and a speed limit of 55 mph or less, and that the subgrade and subbase be prepared to support RCC compaction. Material characteristics required for RCC include a water‑to‑cement ratio as low as 0.30 (typically 0.40), aggregate size less than 1 inch, and a potential compressive strength between 4,000 psi and 10,000 psi. Environmental considerations for RCC note that the estimated design life of 20 years depends on construction materials, environmental conditions and traffic volumes. Existing‑condition information for RCC indicates that it may be placed on unmodified surfaces, rehabilitated subgrades/subbases or newly constructed ones, and that a recent pavement condition survey and in‑situ testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer or lightweight deflectometer should be obtained. For cold‑recycled base courses the site must have at least 2 inches of existing asphalt and, if needed, an additional 1 inch of reclaimed or virgin aggregate to achieve a minimum compacted depth of 3 inches (maximum 5 inches). Traffic data for cold‑recycled bases must show that the project does not carry significantly heavy truck traffic—specifically fewer than 200 daily ESALs—and that traffic can be controlled or detoured during base construction and curing. Material information for cold‑recycled bases requires that the underlying reclaimed aggregate be recyclable, not larger than 2 inches, and that drainage conditions be adequate because poor drainage disqualifies recycling. Environmental input for cold‑recycled bases calls for a minimum one‑week cure under favorable weather (low humidity high temperature) before placing the wearing course. Full‑depth reclamation projects must begin with a reliable estimate of future traffic loading, a recent pavement condition survey documenting current distress, and representative material samples to characterize existing pavement and subgrade layers; layer‑thickness selection then considers both composition of reclaimed materials and structural requirements derived from anticipated traffic and environmental conditions. Design of CRMAB mixtures requires site information indicating that the project is on an Interstate or Interstate‑look‑alike, with a minimum project area greater than 50,000 square yards. Traffic data for CRMAB must show average daily traffic greater than 25,000 vehicles and cumulative ESALs exceeding 10 million over a 20‑year design life. Material inputs for CRMAB include a crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder that would normally replace a dense‑graded wearing or binder course using polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, may incorporate RAP or RAS as allowed by the SSP, and must be produced by the wet process; the structural layer coefficient from Table 10.6 is used for design. Existing‑condition information for CRMAB should identify whether the work is new construction or an overlay on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements, and the specified layer thickness shall not exceed 2 ½ inches. For an AC overlay of an existing AC pavement the design must obtain traffic inputs to compute the future‑traffic Structural Number using values directed in Table 10.7 and flexible pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 for ESAL calculations; material data require the depths of all pavement layers together with their structural coefficients from Table 9.3 (with drainage coefficients set to 1.0), while subbase is excluded for Interstates and omitted when design 18‑kip ESALs exceed 5,000,000 unless its quality and drainability have been verified; existing‑condition information is gathered from pavement cores or, if cores are unavailable, from the Pavement History data in the Roadway Management System. The design of a full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete slab must incorporate site temperature and exposure data to size panels and locate expansion joints. Material strengths required for the precast slab include a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a flexural strength of 650 psi as shown on the shop drawings, and slabs are cast ¼ in thicker than the nominal design thickness as sacrificial concrete. Geotechnical input calls for removal of unsuitable subbase material and placement of compacted No. 2A coarse aggregate shaped within ±½ in of grade. Existing‑condition data required include locations of cores taken from the existing pavement and surveyed elevations of adjoining pavement to establish theoretical slab elevations. [1][2]

Design methods and criteria

The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) requires that full‑depth concrete repairs be sized by a pavement design analysis performed in accordance with Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards (RC‑21M for jointed reinforced concrete or RC‑27M for jointed plain concrete) and the conventional jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) design, with a prestress reduction rule that treats each 50 psi of mid‑slab prestressing as equivalent to a 1‑inch thickness reduction provided the slab does not become less than 8 inches thick; when reconstruction exceeds 500 feet the same design procedure is used to evaluate a thicker slab [1]. For widening or lane additions on rigid pavements, full lane additions that are at least 10 feet wide shall be designed to the same thickness as the original pavement unless heavier traffic loadings dictate designing for the actual traffic, while partial widenings less than 10 feet shall match the existing pavement type and depth; transverse joints on the addition must align with those on the original pavement, and when the original joint spacing is 20 feet or less slabs of the same length are used, whereas when it exceeds 20 feet the joint spacing is adjusted to a minimum length of 10 feet and a maximum length of 17 feet [1]. Roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavement thickness is obtained by applying the current PennDOT concrete pavement design procedures found in Chapter 8 Section 8.15, which can produce a design thickness ranging from 4 inches to 18 inches; for RCC pavements less than 8 inches thick control joints should be spaced 15 feet to 20 feet, and for pavements eight inches or greater the joint spacing equals the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet [1]. Overlay thicknesses are selected by consulting overlay design tables (Tables 9.3, 9.5, 10.4, 10.5) that provide structural coefficients together with minimum and maximum placement depths and allowable scratch or leveling‑course thicknesses, while Tables 10.1 and 10.2 are used to verify that average daily traffic limits are satisfied; when a separate leveling course is required a minimum 1‑inch layer may be counted in the pavement’s structural calculations as a 1‑inch paving course [1]. The thickness of a cold‑recycled asphalt base course is established through the mix‑design procedure prescribed in Publication 27 Chapter 2, which specifies test methods and allowable emulsified asphalts; polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended when traffic volumes exceed 3,000 two‑lane AADT, truck percentages are high, recycling occurs early or late in the season, or rapid strength is needed, and the compacted layer shall be no less than 3 inches and not more than 5 inches thick [1]. For asphalt rubber gap‑graded (AR‑GG) wearing courses and crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder dense‑graded (CRMAB) courses the structural layer coefficients listed for Superpave courses in Table 10.6 are used as the design tool, and the layer thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches [1]. The calculated design thickness for a rigid pavement is obtained by entering all required input values into the DARWin software and pressing its Calculate button; the software incorporates the load‑transfer coefficient J to reflect any tied concrete shoulders, rounds the resulting slab thickness to the nearest half‑inch (except when the pavement exceeds 7 inches in thickness and the total area is less than 30,000 square yards, in which case rounding is to the nearest whole inch), and requires that the final recommended depth be checked against the minimum and maximum limits listed in Table 8.3 before approval [1]. Full‑depth flexible pavement design first calculates a Design Structural Number (SN) and then converts that SN to individual layer thicknesses with the equation SN = a₁d₁ + a₂d₂m₂ + a₃d₃m₃ … ; the structural coefficients aᵢ are taken from Table 9.3, all drainage coefficients mᵢ are set to 1.0, and either the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method in DARWin is applied to obtain structurally adequate layer depths while respecting minimum and maximum lift limits; the Layered Analysis Thickness Design method must not be used [1]. Overlay design may employ any of seven overlay types that can be designed using DARWin in accordance with the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures, with thicknesses for these overlays determined by the bonded concrete overlay of asphalt mechanistic‑empirical design procedure (BCOA‑ME); traffic inputs required for these calculations—current ADT, projected growth, truck percentage and distribution—must be used to compute the design daily 18‑kip ESALs as required by Chapter 7, and a minimum of 2½ inches of binder material shall be provided when an asphalt binder is placed over a cement concrete or brick pavement for the first time [1]. When designing the thickness of an AC overlay on an existing flexible pavement, the engineer determines the future‑traffic SN from Table 10.7, computes the Effective Existing SN using either the Component Method or the Non‑Destructive Testing Method (the Remaining Life Method is also available but not recommended), enters layer depths and structural coefficients from Table 9.3 into DARWin, applies the structural number equation of Section 9.8 with drainage coefficients equal to 1.0, and then uses DARWin’s Specified or Optimized Thickness Design methods on the Design tab to ensure that the calculated SN exceeds the overlay design SN [1]. The AC Overlay of Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement (JPCP) procedure begins by calculating the required pavement thickness for future traffic with a secondary screen in DARWin guided by Table 10.8, determines the Effective Existing Thickness with the Condition Survey Method using STAMPP condition data as directed by Table 10.9, and obtains the overlay thickness directly from DARWin after selecting the Calculate button [1]. The AC Overlay of AC/PCC Pavement follows the same sequence—future‑traffic thickness from Table 10.8, Effective Existing Thickness via the Condition Survey Method per Table 10.9, and overlay thickness derived from DARWin rather than a structural number [1]. Designing a bonded PCC overlay requires first calculating the future‑traffic pavement thickness in DARWin (Table 10.8), then determining the Effective Existing Thickness with the Condition Survey evaluation method (Table 10.9); after both values are entered, selecting the Calculate button yields the concrete thickness needed for the overlay, which must be rounded up to the nearest half‑inch and checked against the minimum and maximum depths listed in Table 10.10 [1]. The design of an unbonded PCC overlay relies on using rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 to compute ESALs, then determining the future‑traffic thickness (Table 10.8) and Existing Effective Pavement Thickness (Table 10.9) so that the required overlay thickness can be calculated and verified against the minimum allowed by Table 10.10 [1]. The Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS requires pavement thickness to be established either by drilling cores in accordance with PTM No. 606 or by nondestructive testing per PTM No. 605; a random coring location is selected within each lot, and any core found deficient by more than ¼ inch triggers additional cores at 100‑foot intervals until the deficiency is reduced, while a deficit greater than ½ inch classifies the slab as defective, and when nondestructive measurements are used one retest may be permitted if an average sublot measurement exceeds the ¼‑inch limit before confirmation with a core [2]. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs the specification mandates that each slab be fabricated with a sacrificial layer that makes it ¼ inch thicker than the nominal design thickness, and this extra thickness is excluded from structural calculations; panel size and expansion‑joint locations are determined using the standard expansion‑coefficient values found in the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide [2]. Roller‑compacted concrete pavements control thickness by first constructing a test section to the plan thickness and then placing RCC lifts that obey lift‑thickness procedures: the minimum lift thickness is 4 inches, single lifts may be between 4 inches and ≤ 9 inches, and lifts greater than 9 inches must be placed in multiple equal‑thickness lifts; field density testing per ASTM C1040 uses the equation X = (ρw/ρt) × 100 to compute compaction percentage and requires a minimum of 85 % of maximum wet density for thicknesses < 6 inches and 90 % for thicknesses ≥ 6 inches, with a final‑rolling requirement of > 96 % for all thicknesses; acceptance is verified by coring one location within each lot in accordance with AASHTO T 24 and measuring the core depth with apparatus specified in PTM No. 614, the measured depth must be within ¼ inch of the planned depth, and if the initial core exceeds this tolerance two additional cores are taken and the average compared to the ¼‑inch limit [2]. Plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (RPS) define a lot as 5 600 square yards divided into four sublots of 1 400 square yards each; the lot is accepted when the average pavement depth exceeds the design thickness (D) minus 0.5 inch and no individual sublot depth is less than D minus 0.5 inch, with depth testing performed by either a core or a nondestructive measurement in each sublot, the first sublot core and every tenth core thereafter submitted as information cores, and cores measured with PTM No. 614 apparatus; if entrained air content of evaluated cores falls below 3.5 % or exceeds 8.5 %, a written evaluation and proposed solution must be provided within five days [2]. Long‑Life Concrete Pavement ties pavement thickness to the design thickness (D) by requiring that the average depth of each lot be greater than D minus 0.5 inch and no individual sublot may fall below D minus 0.5 inch; the lower quality index (QL) for Class AA 28‑day concrete compressive strength is computed using QL = ( X̄₄ − 3300 psi ) / s where X̄₄ is the average of the sublot test results and s is the standard deviation [2]. Unbonded concrete overlays specify that the saw‑cut depth be expressed as a fraction of the overlay thickness D, being no less than D⁄3 within 12 inches of the pavement edges and no less than D⁄4 elsewhere; allowable panel size shall not exceed 18 × D and the aspect ratio may not exceed 1.5, while curing‑compound application rates are differentiated by thickness with a minimum rate of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 150 ft² for pavements whose thickness exceeds 6 inches and a rate of 1 gallon per 125 ft² when the thickness is ≤ 6 inches, and FHWA’s HIPERPAV or an approved equivalent may be employed to determine the project‑specific sawing window [2]. Pervious asphalt pavement systems achieve thickness control by constructing two test sections on the prepared subgrade and base, each having an area of at least 225 square feet, measuring infiltration in accordance with ASTM C1701 so that the average exceeds 200 in/hr, and limiting the compacted thickness of the installed pavement to no more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness while placing the mixture in lifts no thicker than 4 inches [2]. Pervious concrete pavements apply design‑thickness control through test sections that must be built with a thickness not more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness specified in Section 501.3(s), and the average hardened density from those sections becomes the acceptance basis for the remainder of the project [2]. Thin bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements divide the overlay into lots representing not more than 3 000 square yards in each traffic lane; the inspector selects one coring location at random within each lot according to PTM No. 1 and cores are drilled in accordance with PTM No. 606 in the presence of the inspector, while for projects at intersections or where the area is less than 3 000 square yards two cores are taken or nondestructive thickness measurements are performed according to PTM No. 605, and other nondestructive methods may be requested in writing for approval by the District Executive [2].

Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses must have a compacted depth between a minimum of 3 inches and a maximum of 5 inches; the existing road must contain at least 2 inches of asphalt material and an additional inch of reclaimed or virgin aggregate must be incorporated to achieve the 3‑inch minimum, and projects carrying 200 or more daily ESALs should not be selected for cold recycling. When an Asphalt‑rubber Gap‑graded (AR‑GG) mixture is used as a wearing course the design shall limit the layer thickness to no more than 2½ inches, and the structural layer coefficients given for Superpave courses in Table 10.6 must be employed in the structural pavement design. When crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder dense‑graded (CRMAB) mixtures are placed, the pavement thickness is limited by a maximum layer thickness of 2½ inches, and the CRMAB layer must also use the Superpave structural layer coefficients specified for that purpose. In hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) applications the HIR layer is typically 1½ inches thick with an allowable range of 1 to 2 inches; asphalt pavement thickness must be at least 1½ inches more than the milling depth, and if paving fabric is present it must lie at least 1½ inches below the HIR cut depth; for composite pavements a minimum of 1 to 2 inches of asphalt beneath the HIR depth is required, whereas full‑depth asphalt pavements require a minimum of 2 to 3 inches beneath the HIR depth. Calculated design thicknesses shall be rounded to the nearest half‑inch, except when the slab exceeds 7 inches and the total area is less than 30,000 square yards, in which case rounding is to the nearest inch, and the final depth must fall within the minimum and maximum limits listed in Table 8.3; no specific frost design requirements are imposed for rigid pavements, and the designer must select a combination of variables that yields the most economical solution while preserving required durability and rideability qualities. In full‑depth flexible pavement design each layer’s thickness shall be chosen so that the computed structural number exceeds the required Design Structural Number, all drainage coefficients shall be set to 1.0, and the thicknesses must obey the minimum and maximum lift depths given in Tables 9.4 and 9.5; acceptable methods for obtaining these thicknesses are the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method available in DARWin, while the Layered Analysis Thickness Design method shall not be used. Overlay designs are intended for a minimum 8‑year structural design life and a maximum of 20 years, which drives pavement thickness selection; when an asphalt overlay is placed over cement‑concrete or brick for the first time a minimum of 2½ inches of binder material is required, and the wearing course shall have at least the minimum thickness indicated for the material to be used, with Table 10.4 providing the allowable minimum and maximum layer thicknesses for resurfacing or overlays using asphalt materials. For an AC overlay on existing AC pavement the designed thickness is acceptable only if the structural number calculated from the proposed layer configuration exceeds the Overlay Design SN required for future traffic; the effective existing SN must be obtained with either the Component Method or the Non‑Destructive Testing Method using core data (or RMS history when cores are unavailable), entering layer depths and the structural coefficients of Table 9.3 with drainage coefficients set to 1.0, subbase may be excluded on Interstates and on non‑Interstate roads when the design 18‑kip ESALs exceed 5,000,000 unless its condition has been verified, and future traffic SN inputs follow the values in Table 10.7 while using the average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 for ESAL calculations. Designing a bonded PCC overlay requires first calculating the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic in DARWin (Table 10.8) and then determining an effective existing thickness via the condition‑survey evaluation method in DARWin (Table 10.9); the resulting concrete overlay thickness is rounded up to the nearest half‑inch and must lie between the minimum and maximum depths listed for concrete overlays in Table 10.10. Designing an unbonded PCC overlay uses the rigid pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 for ESAL calculations, determines both the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic (Table 10.8) and the Existing Effective Pavement Thickness (Table 10.9), and then verifies that the calculated thickness is above the minimum thickness allowed for unbonded PCC overlays as shown in Table 10.10. In plain or reinforced cement‑concrete pavement specifications the designed thickness is controlled by the form depth, which shall be equal to the intended pavement depth; any built‑up on the form may be used only to achieve the required design thickness but may not exceed ½ inch, the finished face of the form shall not deviate more than 1/8 inch in 10 feet, and shims employed to adjust vertical alignment may raise the form no greater than 1 inch. The slip‑form paving method further requires that the allowable pavement thickness be verified by coring in accordance with PTM No. 606 or by non‑destructive testing according to PTM No. 605 at locations selected by the Representative; if a core measurement is more than ¼ inch shallow relative to the required depth, additional cores must be taken at 100‑foot intervals until the deficiency is reduced, and any individual measurement that exceeds a ½ inch shortfall declares the pavement defective, while an average sublot thickness shown by non‑destructive testing to be more than ¼ inch below the required depth triggers a confirmatory core subject to the same ¼‑inch and ½‑inch thresholds. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slabs a sacrificial concrete layer that is ¼ inch thicker than the nominal slab thickness shall be provided on delivery; this extra layer is excluded from structural calculations, may be milled off at the Representative’s discretion, and must already be present so that any required diamond grinding does not exceed ¼ inch; cracks wider than 0.007 inches whose depth exceeds one‑third of the slab depth D shall require replacement. Roller‑compacted concrete pavements require a minimum lift thickness of 4 inches; single lifts may be placed between 4 inches and ≤9 inches, while any total thickness greater than 9 inches must be constructed in multiple equal‑thickness lifts. For lifts less than 6 inches the paver shall achieve at least 85 % of maximum wet density directly behind the machine, for lifts of 6 inches or more the minimum is 90 %, and after final rolling all lifts shall exceed 96 % of maximum wet density; placement shall occur within a temperature range of 50 °F to 90 °F together with prescribed environmental controls. Acceptance requires a representative core from each lot measured with the apparatus specified in PTM No. 614, and the pavement shall be considered acceptable only if the measured depth is within ¼ inch of the plan depth; if the first core exceeds this tolerance two additional cores shall be taken and the average of the three measurements evaluated, an average that is less than a ¼‑inch deficiency satisfying the thickness criterion whereas an average greater than ¼‑inch renders the pavement depth defective. The asphalt paving interlayer system stipulates that wherever a geotextile fabric is installed the overlying compacted asphalt thickness shall not be less than 1½ inches, and the first lift of an asphalt overlay over any interlayer system shall also be at least 1½ inches thick; for fiberglass‑mesh grid installations the leveling course beneath the grid must have a minimum thickness equal to two times the Nominal Maximum Aggregate Size. The 60‑month warranty for asphalt‑concrete pavement specifies that the pavement must meet the designated minimum thickness, and only when this thickness is achieved together with a density greater than 92 % of theoretical maximum and mix‑design tolerances are satisfied does the contractor become exempt from liability for base‑condition distresses. Under the plain or reinforced cement‑concrete pavement RPS specifications a lot shall be deemed defective if the average depth of the lot falls below the design thickness D minus ½ inch or if more than one individual sublot depth is less than that same limit; acceptance requires that the average depth be greater than D minus 0.5 inch and that no more than one sublot depth fall below that tolerance, with a full lot defined as 5,600 square yards of uniform depth. Long‑life concrete pavement follows the same acceptance rule: the Representative shall accept a lot when its average depth exceeds the design thickness D minus 0.5 inch and no more than one sublot depth is less than D minus 0.5 inch, each lot comprising 5,600 square yards of constant‑depth pavement. For unbonded concrete overlays panel dimensions shall not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter side ratio shall not exceed 1.5; saw cuts that form the joints must be cut to at least one‑third of the overlay thickness (D/3) within twelve inches of any edge and to at least one‑quarter of the thickness (D/4) elsewhere, and the curing compound application rate is tied to thickness, requiring a minimum of 1 gallon per 150 ft² for overlays thicker than six inches and 1 gallon per 125 ft² for those six inches or thinner. Pervious asphalt pavement systems require that the compacted thickness shall not be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness, and verification test sections must each cover at least 225 square feet with an average infiltration rate exceeding 200 inches per hour. Pervious concrete pavement systems shall be placed with a thickness that cannot be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness called out in Section 501.3(s). Thin bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements shall have a thickness of 2.5 to 4 inches. Bonded concrete overlays shall be placed with a thickness between 4 and 8 inches. Asphalt treated permeable base courses may be placed in compacted lifts not exceeding 4 inches thickness, the finished base shall be within ½ inch of the indicated depth, and any area that is deficient by ½ inch or more shall be removed and replaced. Structural section and dimensions

For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs the nominal slab thickness must be increased by a sacrificial ¼ inch, and the slab length shall not exceed 15 feet (20 feet when prestressed) nor be less than 6 feet. The transverse joint gaps between such slabs must be at least ¼ inch and no more than ½ inch, while longitudinal joints may not exceed ¾ inch; vertical differential across any joint shall stay within ¼ inch, with a differential greater than ½ inch requiring removal or grinding. The subbase surface that receives these slabs must be finished to a grade tolerance of ±½ inch and dowel‑slot tolerances are limited to ±⅛ inch with a minimum 1‑inch recess for lifting devices. For full‑depth concrete repairs the slab width shall be no more than one lane and the slab length shall be at least 6 ft but not more than 15 ft (20 ft for prestressed units); joint spacing is limited to 30 ft for jointed reinforced concrete pavement or 15 ft for jointed plain concrete pavement, and longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only on patches longer than 65 ft. When prestressing is used the slab thickness may be reduced but shall never be less than 8 in. In overlay work a leveling course must be at least a minimum 1‑inch thickness and may be counted as a paving course in structural calculations; thin asphalt overlays on both Interstates and non‑Interstates are required to have a thickness of not less than 1½ inches, and when an existing asphalt layer is milled over a concrete or brick base the practice is to retain a minimum of 1½ inches of that material. The Superpave base course must have a total thickness greater than 6 inches; if an Asphalt Rich Base Course is called for it shall occupy between 3 and 6 inches of the first lift as dictated by the pavement design, and fiber‑modified courses are not permitted to reduce the required minimum pavement design thickness. The Safety Edge edge‑support treatment shall be applied to the outside edge of asphalt pavements and shoulders with a depth of at least 1½ inches but the total depth shall not exceed 5 inches. For cold‑recycled base courses the compacted depth must be at least 3 inches and may not exceed 5 inches; the existing pavement must have at least 2 inches of asphalt material, and an additional 1 inch of reclaimed or virgin aggregate is required to achieve the 3‑inch minimum thickness. AR‑GG (asphalt rubber gap‑graded) wearing courses shall not exceed a layer thickness of 2½ inches. In pervious asphalt pavement systems test sections shall be at least 225 square feet and their compacted thickness may not be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness; the detention‑base bottom requires a minimum of 2 inches of fine aggregate, coarse‑aggregate lifts are limited to a maximum of 8 inches, and the mixture is placed in compacted lifts no greater than 4 inches. For pervious concrete test section thickness may not be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness and edge exterior formed surfaces shall have a radius of at least ¼ inch. A thin bonded concrete overlay over an asphalt‑surfaced pavement shall have a thickness ranging from 2½ to 4 inches, while a bonded Portland cement concrete overlay on an asphalt‑surfaced pavement shall be between 4 and 8 inches thick. For unbonded concrete overlays the panel dimensions shall not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio shall not exceed 1.5, with a preference for a ratio as close to 1.0 as feasible; saw cuts must be at least D/3 deep within 12 inches of slab edges and at least D/4 deep elsewhere, and curing compound shall be applied at a minimum rate of one gallon per 150 square feet for pavements thicker than 6 inches and one gallon per 125 square feet for pavements 6 inches or thinner. Section 506.3 requires that the average pavement depth of a lot be greater than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch and that no individual sublot depth be less than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch, while Section 502.3(m) permits a core measurement to be acceptable when it is within ¼ inch of the planned thickness, with additional cores taken if one exceeds this tolerance. In the portion of the Pavement Policy Manual covering Superpave base courses no separate dimensional requirements are provided for subbase, treated‑soil, slab, shoulder, edge‑support, or reinforcement layers. Materials and mixture design

When specifying pavement thickness for concrete‑pavement patching the designer must identify the concrete material, which may be standard Portland cement concrete as defined in Publication 408 Section 704 with an optimized aggregate gradation, or a high‑strength Class AA mix for precast units; the specification shall also indicate whether accelerated concrete (reaching required compressive strength in 4 to 7 hours) or standard concrete (opening to traffic in 24 to 48 hours) is used [1]. For prestressed precast slabs the required thickness and any reduction shall be stated, reductions being allowed up to four inches but never producing a slab less than eight inches thick, and the non‑prestressed baseline thickness must also be recorded; the slab shall be cast one‑quarter inch thicker than the nominal design thickness as a sacrificial layer [1][2]. Roller‑compacted concrete pavement mixes shall be defined by cement, aggregate and water, may use a lower water‑to‑cement ratio as low as 0.30 but typically 0.40, may employ a dense aggregate gradation with a maximum aggregate size less than one inch, should indicate any air‑entrainment or limited admixtures, target compressive strength can range from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi, and design thickness may range from four inches to eighteen inches [1]. For widening an asphalt pavement partial lane widenings of less than ten feet shall match existing asphalt layers with an equal depth of asphalt material and any concrete layers with an equal thickness of concrete, while full‑width lane additions of ten feet or more should also match the existing pavement structure unless traffic conditions justify a different design; drainage layers should be kept consistent with those of the existing pavement [1]. Overlay specifications must name the exact asphalt mix and required binder grade: a Superpave 6.3‑mm wearing course shall use PG 64E‑22 binder, a Superpave 9.5‑mm fine‑graded wearing course shall not use PG 64E‑22, a leveling course shall have a minimum thickness of one inch, and thin overlays on Interstates may not be less than one‑and‑a‑half inches unless approved [1]. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses shall have a compacted depth of at least three inches and not exceed five inches; the mix design must incorporate recycled asphalt pavement that is either wearing‑course or fine‑graded together with virgin coarse aggregate of size No. 8, 57, or 67, and polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended where traffic volumes exceed 3,000 two‑lane AADT, truck percentages are high, early or late season recycling may impair curing, or rapid strength development is desired [1]. Crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder dense‑graded courses shall have each layer not exceed two‑and‑a‑half inches; the mixture must be produced by the wet process that modifies binder properties with crumb rubber, may contain RAP or RAS material as defined in the SSP, and if warm‑mix asphalt is used a chemical warm‑mix additive shall be utilized while foaming is prohibited [1]. In structural number design for flexible pavements each layer’s structural coefficient a_i shall be obtained from Table 9.3 and all drainage coefficients m_i shall be set to 1.0; when the approved pavement structure includes an aggregate/cement or aggregate/lime/pozzolan base course both materials must be listed as alternatives in the bid proposal [1]. For unbonded PCC overlays of existing PCC pavement the specification shall identify the interlayer material—either a geotextile interlayer or asphalt concrete—and prescribe the wearing‑course mix; a nominal depth of one inch of a Superpave fine‑graded 9.5 mm mix is required, and when joint faulting exceeds one‑half inch a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix must be placed at the joint, while joints with more than one‑quarter inch faulting are milled before installing the geotextile [1]. Full‑depth precast and prestressed concrete slab pavements shall link pavement‑thickness specifications to a concrete mix design submitted at least twenty‑eight days before production; the mix design must include trial batch data demonstrating that each mix will achieve a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a minimum flexural strength of 650 psi, and it must account for ambient average daily temperatures and exposure conditions by using the standard aggregate expansion coefficient from the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide [2]. Asphalt paving interlayer systems shall identify the interlayer material—geotextile paving fabric, fiberglass mesh grid, or composite reinforcing grid—and prescribe a liquid‑asphalt application rate of 0.22 to 0.28 gal/yd² for geotextile fabric, 0.02 to 0.05 gal/yd² for fiberglass mesh, or 0.19 to 0.28 gal/yd² for composite grid; the specification also shall set asphalt‑binder temperature limits of at least 290°F and not exceeding 320°F, and require that the first lift be compacted to at least one‑and‑a‑half inches where an interlayer system is installed [2]. Under the sixty‑month warranty for asphalt concrete pavements any replacement material must be plant‑mixed asphalt concrete unless another material is expressly approved in writing; the contractor shall verify that the installed layer meets the specified minimum thickness, attains a density greater than 92 % of the theoretical maximum, and that the percent asphalt content and aggregate gradation are within the tolerances defined in the approved mix design [2]. Long‑life concrete pavement specifications shall identify the concrete depth, requiring that the average lot depth exceed the design thickness (D) minus 0.5 inch and that no individual sublot depth be less than D minus 0.5 inch; required material properties include the 28‑day compressive strength as governed by Sections 105 and 106 and the percentage of plastic air content measured on samples from each sublot, with acceptance based on those test results [2]. Plain or reinforced cement concrete pavement specifications shall include the required concrete depth, a 28‑day compressive strength and an air‑content percentage; average lot depth must be more than D minus 0.5 inch and no individual sublot less than D minus 0.5 inch, a lower quality index compares the mean compressive‑strength result to 3,300 psi, and lower and upper quality indices for air content compare the mean percentage to 5.0 % and 8.5 %, respectively, with acceptance when the percent within limits is at least 55 %; required tests are performed on samples from each sublot as specified in Sections 105 and 106 [2]. For rigid concrete pavements thickness measurement shall be based on concrete that meets the requirements of Section 506.3(u)1, and the hardened entrained‑air content must fall between 3.5 % and 8.5 %; if it is below or above those limits a written evaluation and corrective action are required, and when cores are taken the voids shall be filled with the same mixture used for the pavement course or alternatively a premixed non‑shrink grout [2]. Unbonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements shall require application of a white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene liquid membrane‑forming curing compound at a minimum rate of one gallon per 150 ft² for overlays thicker than six inches and one gallon per 125 ft² for those six inches or less; insulation blankets may be removed only after the concrete’s average compressive strength reaches 3,600 psi and the surface temperature change does not exceed 40°F in any one‑hour period [2]. Asphalt‑treated permeable base courses shall require a dry aggregate coated with heated asphalt material to form a film of adequate thickness for binding; the mix shall be produced at a temperature below 320°F to achieve suitable viscosity without segregation, and it must be placed in compacted lifts not exceeding four inches [2]. Thin bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavement shall consist of a Portland cement concrete layer applied with a thickness between two‑and‑a‑half and four inches [2]. Bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavement shall consist of a Portland cement concrete overlay with a thickness ranging from four to eight inches [2].

The design Structural Number must first exceed the required strength and then be translated into individual layer thicknesses using the equation SN = a₁d₁ + a₂d₂m₂ + … where all drainage coefficients mᵢ shall be set to 1.0; thicknesses are chosen with the Specified Thickness Design or Optimized Thickness Design method in DARWin, constrained by the minimum and maximum course depths of Tables 9.4 and 9.5 and adjusted to achieve the lowest total pavement cost while meeting those structural requirements [1]. In overlay design the appropriate asphalt material is first chosen from Tables 10.1‑10.3 according to the highway’s ADT and then the pavement thickness is limited by the minimum and maximum layer values listed in Table 10.4 for that material; when an asphalt overlay is placed over cement concrete or brick for the first time a binder course must be at least 2½ inches thick and the wearing course shall have the minimum thickness indicated for the selected material, with any required tapering allowed only when travel‑lane thicknesses still satisfy those minima [1]. An asphalt rubber (AR‑GG) mixture may be selected when traffic exceeds 25,000 ADT, cumulative ESALs exceed 10 million over a 20‑year design life and the project area is larger than 50,000 square yards; because AR‑GG is a premium higher‑cost mix it should only be used when the anticipated performance benefits justify the expense and the structural layer coefficients for Superpave courses in Table 10.6 shall be applied to the AR‑GG layer in the structural design, the mixture must be produced by the wet process with the CRM blended into the binder before aggregate addition, and the wearing‑course thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches to maintain constructability and durability, and it should not be used where significant handwork or raking is required nor in shoulders unless lane and shoulder are paved in one pass [1]. When using crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder (CRMAB) mixtures the design should place the CRMAB in the upper layers of the pavement section—specifically the wearing and binder courses—so that its superior performance is utilized, structural layer coefficients from the Superpave tables shall be applied for thickness calculations; the individual layer thickness must not exceed 2½ inches, and the mix may be employed on Interstates or Interstate look‑alikes only when traffic exceeds 25,000 ADT, cumulative ESALs are greater than 10 million over a 20‑year design life and the project area is larger than 50,000 square yards, and it should not be used where significant handwork or raking is required nor in shoulders unless lane and shoulder are paved in one pass for efficiency [1]. For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) projects the HIR layer is normally placed at a depth of 1½ inches and may range from 1 to 2 inches; when cutting less than 1½ inches the existing asphalt must not be left as a thin layer, so for composite pavements a minimum of 1 to 2 inches of asphalt below the HIR depth and for full‑depth asphalt pavements a minimum of 2 to 3 inches is required, and the total asphalt pavement thickness must be at least 1½ inches more than the milling depth [1]. For an unbonded PCC overlay material and mixture choices are driven by the condition of the existing concrete slab and measured joint faulting: a geotextile interlayer is specified when joints have greater than ¼‑inch faulting, while a nominal depth of 1 inch of a Superpave Wearing Course with a 9.5 mm Fine‑Graded mix serves as the standard overlay thickness; if joint faulting exceeds ½ inch a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix must be placed at the joint or the joints milled/grinded to reduce faulting, and the calculated overlay thickness must also meet or exceed the minimum thickness allowed for unbonded PCC overlays as shown in Table 10.10 [1]. In full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, the concrete mixture is selected by developing a mix design that demonstrates, through trial batch test data, at least the minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and the minimum flexural strength of 650 psi; these designs must be submitted to the Representative for acceptance at least twenty‑eight days before slab production [2]. The slab is cast one‑quarter inch thicker than the nominal design thickness as a sacrificial layer that is not counted in structural calculations but provides margin for post‑placement grinding and durability, while thermal expansion considerations are incorporated to determine panel size and joint locations so that the selected materials and proportions satisfy structural integrity, durability, constructability, and performance criteria [2]. For asphalt interlayer systems, material selection and proportioning follow manufacturer‑approved specifications reviewed by a trained technical representative who prescribes the binder type, application width, and rate; asphalt is applied with a calibrated distributor at rates that fully saturate each reinforcement—0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard for geotextile fabric, 0.02 to 0.05 gallons per square yard for fiberglass mesh grid, and 0.19 to 0.28 gallons per square yard for composite reinforcing grid—and the spray temperature must be at least 290°F but not exceed 320°F to achieve proper bonding and performance [2]. The overlay thickness is required to be no less than 1½ inches of compacted lift wherever an interlayer is placed, and fabric or grid may not be installed in areas where the overlaid asphalt tapers below that minimum, thereby ensuring structural integrity, durability, constructability, and overall performance [2]. In plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements, the required pavement thickness is achieved by using steel forms whose depth equals the pavement depth; forms may be built‑up no greater than ½ inch to meet the required design thickness [2]. Structural and performance requirements are met by monitoring aggregate gradations each day and, if necessary, adjusting aggregate proportions during production to maintain the combined gradation percent retained within limits according to PTM No. 529; any adjustments that also require changes to cementitious material or water content will trigger the development of new mix designs, including trial concrete batches [2].

Structural details and interfaces

When a pavement thickness is specified for concrete patching or reconstruction, the design shall include load‑transfer devices at transverse joints by dowelling every patch joint and bonding the dowels along their full embedded length with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders; designers also shall specify new pavement joints as either skewed or non‑skewed. For full‑width reconstructions of all lanes the joint spacing is dictated by the chosen pavement type: a jointed reinforced concrete pavement calls for 30‑foot joint spacing, perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, while a jointed plain concrete pavement uses 15‑foot spacing with the same dowel and seal requirements. Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are only required in patches longer than 65 feet. For widening or lane additions the transverse joints shall be aligned to match those on the original pavement, and joint spacing follows the original pattern; when the original transverse joint spacing is 20 feet or less the slabs are constructed with the same length as on the original pavement, while if the original spacing exceeds 20 feet the joint spacing must be adjusted to a minimum of 10 feet and a maximum of 17 feet with intermediate joints added as necessary. The concrete type for the addition depends on the existing pavement; widening of jointed plain concrete pavement shall be constructed with plain cement concrete, whereas jointed reinforced concrete pavement may be widened with either reinforced or plain cement concrete. Full lane additions (≥ 10 feet) shall be designed to the same thickness as the original pavement unless heavier traffic loads require a different design, and all lane additions and widenings shall be tied to the existing pavement, providing the required connection. Roller‑compacted concrete pavements do not require steel reinforcing or dowel bars, and forms are unnecessary; control joints may be omitted except where the Department mandates them in shoulders so that they align with the adjacent concrete mainline, and when required the joint spacing is governed by slab thickness—specifically, for RCC pavements less than 8 inches thick the control joints should be spaced between 15 feet and 20 feet, while for pavements 8 inches or greater the spacing equals the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet. When a fiber‑modified course overlay is placed on an existing concrete pavement, the overlay must be sawed and sealed over any underlying transverse joints; no other joints, reinforcement, load‑transfer systems, transitions, or connections are required by pavement thickness in this guidance, and FMC use does not permit reductions to the minimum pavement design thickness specified for the project. When an overlay is designed a separate leveling course of at least 1 inch thickness may be counted as a 1‑inch thick paving layer in the structural calculations. The Safety Edge shall be applied to the outside edge of asphalt wearing or binder courses with a depth of 1½ inches or greater but not exceeding 5 inches total. Reinforcement for thin overlays can be provided by an asphalt paving interlayer such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grids, or composite reinforcing grids, each of which is installed beneath the new overlay to improve tensile and torsional strength and to act as a moisture barrier. For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled asphalt the required pavement thickness includes that the asphalt layer be at least 1½ inches thicker than any milling depth and, if a paving fabric is present, the fabric must lie a minimum of 1½ inches below the HIR cut depth; when the HIR overlay is placed on a composite concrete pavement the existing concrete joints must be stable with no movement. For an unbonded PCC overlay provide a separation layer—either a geotextile interlayer or asphalt concrete—and mill any concrete joint with greater than ¼‑inch faulting; if joint faulting exceeds ½ inch place a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix at the joint or mill/grind the joints to reduce faulting, ensure proper drainage of the interlayer by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain, and the interlayer must extend through any reconstructed shoulder and be tied to the mainline. For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements placed by the slip‑form method load‑transfer units must be secured at their designated locations before paving; dowel bars are positioned so that transverse contraction joints can be sawed over their centers and any misalignment of a dowel end may not exceed a vertical tilt or horizontal skew of 0.25 in nor a longitudinal or horizontal translation greater than 1 in. Transverse joints run across the full width as shown on the standard drawings, while longitudinal joints must contain tie bars placed according to the drawing and no tie bar may be within 24 in of a dowelled contraction joint; one tie bar is omitted adjacent to cracks larger than 1/8 in or dominant joints when paving between October 1 and April 1. Reinforcement mesh or rebar shall be free of rust, oil, grease or other contaminants; for two‑layer reinforced slabs the reinforcement is laid full length on the bottom concrete before the top layer is placed, and for one‑layer placement the mesh is positioned on approved supports prior to concreting. Selected tie bars are pull‑out tested after the slab reaches a compressive strength of 3 500 psi using a hydraulic jack capable of 12 000 lb or 1/32 in slip and must meet the resistance values in Table B. Premolded expansion‑joint filler shall be used with a thickness of at least ½ in around manholes and similar appurtenances, at least ¾ in where the pavement meets other rigid structures or crosses railway tracks, and at least ¼ in along each mortar‑filled rail. Forms may be built up no greater than ½ in to achieve the specified design thickness and must be secured with steel pins and bracing so they do not deflect more than 0.01 in under a 1 700‑pound center load, shims being limited to a maximum height of 1 in. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs reinforcement size and position shall follow the shop drawings, a sacrificial slab thickness of ¼ in is added to the top surface beyond the nominal design thickness, and load‑transfer dowels and tie bars are cast in place with dowel slots and tie‑bar slots located within ±⅛ in of the drawing locations together with a bond‑breaker coating applied to the dowel portions. Transverse joints shall be between ¼ in minimum and ½ in maximum in width, while longitudinal joints may not exceed ¾ in; where a temporary vertical drop exceeds 2 in, a temporary asphalt wedge transition shall be provided, and setting bolts, foam gaskets, lifting devices and strongback beams are used as connections to control slab elevation during placement. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements a fresh joint that is vertical between lanes or horizontal between lifts must be placed when the adjacent material is laid within 60 minutes of the previous placement; any joint exceeding that interval is treated as a cold joint and requires full‑depth vertical cutting at least 6 in from the exposed edge, cleaning and moistening before the next lift. Control joints are to be saw‑cut to one‑quarter depth of the compacted pavement as shown on the plans, and any joints designated for sealing shall be sealed in accordance with Section 501.3(n). For bonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements all transverse joints shall be saw‑cut to a depth equal to the full thickness of the overlay plus ½ in and the cut width shall be at least as wide as the underlying joint; intermediate transverse joints receive the same treatment, while longitudinal joints require a ¼‑in‑wide sealant reservoir sawed to the full overlay depth plus ½ in. Joint backing or other approved filling material shall be installed within ±¼ in of the existing pavement surface before placement, and any exposed reinforcement or steel plates shall be cut off and removed so that dowel load‑transfer devices are not damaged. A paving notch shall be provided at the overlay transition. For pervious concrete pavement systems joints must be placed either by tooling into the fresh concrete to approximately one‑quarter slab thickness or by saw‑cutting after sufficient compressive strength is reached, with fines removed; exterior formed edges shall be rounded to a radius not less than ¼ in. No reinforcement, load‑transfer systems, transitions, or other connections are prescribed. For flexible pavement interlayer systems geotextile paving fabric, fiberglass mesh grid or composite reinforcing grid may be used as reinforcement provided the overlay asphalt tapers to a compacted thickness of at least 1½ in; where the taper is less than 1½ in such reinforcement shall not be installed. The first lift of an asphalt overlay over an interlayer system must be at least 1½ in thick. All joints in the interlayer system must be overlapped by a minimum of 1 in with overlaps oriented in the direction of subsequent pavement placement, and any wrinkles larger than 1 in must be sliced and re‑overlapped. Reinforcement materials are to be saturated with asphalt at rates specified (for example 0.22–0.28 gal/yd² for geotextile) and extended a width equal to the reinforcement plus 6 in on each side; placement requires smooth lay‑down, brooming or rubber‑tire rolling and avoidance of abrupt starts, stops or wet pavement. When defective reinforced cement concrete pavement must be removed at least a ten‑foot strip between existing transverse joints or an entire panel shall be replaced; if the removal reaches an existing transverse joint, that joint is to be replaced in kind, and any new cuts created by the removal must be provided with new transverse joints constructed according to the joining methods shown on the Standard Drawings. For asphalt‑treated permeable base course longitudinal joints are created by spreading the material so that it overlaps the edge of the previously placed lane by 1 in to 2 in, providing a smooth joint after compaction. Transverse construction joints are required at the end of each workday or whenever an interruption longer than 30 minutes occurs; a temporary vertical bulkhead extending the full depth and width of the ATPBC shall be installed, although the contractor may alternatively saw the joint. For bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavement structural details consist of building transitions that tie the new 4‑to‑8‑inch thick concrete layer to the existing asphalt surface and providing joints that are sawn and sealed as part of the work; reinforcement or load‑transfer systems are not specified. For bonded PCC overlays, the interface between the new concrete layer and the existing pavement is designed to be a direct bond so that the two act as a single monolithic slab; design begins by calculating required Pavement Thickness for future traffic, determining Effective Existing Thickness, then using software to obtain the concrete thickness needed, rounding up to the nearest half‑inch and checking it against the minimum and maximum depths specified for bonded PCC overlays. In bonded concrete overlay construction over existing pavement, the existing surface shall be scarified as needed and then shot‑blasted or water‑blasted to create a uniformly roughened surface; joint backing or an approved filling material shall be installed within ±¼ inch of the surface in all transverse joints so that it cannot be displaced during paving, and saw‑cut depth must equal the full thickness of the overlay plus ½ inch, with cut width at least as wide as the underlying joint to prevent debonding; edge slump in the outer six inches adjacent to a shoulder may not exceed ¼ inch and next to an adjoining lane may not exceed ⅛ inch. For unbonded PCC overlays, the design uses either a geotextile interlayer or asphalt concrete to separate the layers; when a geotextile is specified any joint with greater than ¼‑inch faulting must be milled, and if joint faulting exceeds ½ inch a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch), 9.5 mm mix is placed at the joint or the joint is milled/grinded to reduce faulting, with a nominal depth of 1 inch of a Superpave Wearing Course, 9.5 mm Fine‑Graded mix serving as the standard thickness for this surface layer; proper drainage of the interlayer is achieved by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain, and the interlayer also must extend through the shoulder when shoulders are constructed or reconstructed. In unbonded concrete overlay construction, panel dimensions are limited to a maximum of eighteen times the overlay thickness and the aspect ratio should be as close to 1.0 as feasible but shall not exceed 1.5; transverse and longitudinal joints are located according to project plans or an approved layout, with saw cuts required to be at least D/3 within twelve inches of pavement edges and at least D/4 elsewhere, all joint faces must be cleaned of deleterious material and sealed after cutting, and longitudinal joints shall be avoided in wheel paths. For cold‑recycled base courses, the interface between the existing asphalt layer and the new recycled mix is controlled by keeping the compacted depth between three and five inches, with at least two inches of original asphalt retained and an additional one inch of reclaimed or virgin aggregate added to meet the three‑inch minimum thickness; milling should not be cut below the bottom of the existing asphalt unless the underlying material can be recycled, thereby preserving a proper bond for the subsequent wearing course after the required curing period. In crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder (CRMAB) designs, the layer thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches and it is recommended for new construction to be placed in the upper layers (wearing and binder courses) where its superior performance is best utilized; lower‑layer placement provides no added benefit, and the structural layer coefficients given for Superpave courses should be used for the CRMAB layer in the structural pavement design to ensure proper interaction with underlying materials. When designing interfaces for a Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) treatment, thin layers beneath the HIR surface should be avoided, especially if the cut is less than 1½ inches; instead a minimum of 1 to 2 inches of asphalt should be specified below the HIR depth for composite pavements and a minimum of 2 to 3 inches should be specified for full‑depth asphalt pavements; milling before the HIR process can address deeper cracks and help retain vertical clearance for overhead structures, curbs, gutters, and guide rails, thereby ensuring adequate bonding between layers and accommodating adjacent features. A leveling course, minimum 1‑inch thickness, is placed to provide a uniform working platform on which the binder and/or wearing course is placed and may be counted in structural calculations; the Safety Edge treatment should be used on the outside edge of asphalt pavements and shoulders with a depth of at least 1½ inches and not more than 5 inches. For pervious concrete pavement systems, slab thickness governs interface placement; test sections may not be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness and construction joints are formed to a depth of approximately one‑quarter of the slab thickness by cross‑rolling or saw cutting after sufficient strength is achieved, with any fines removed to keep voids open. In asphalt‑treated permeable base courses, longitudinal joints are created by overlapping the newly placed material over the edge of the previously laid lane by one to two inches, producing a smooth transition after compaction; when work stops for a day or any interruption exceeds thirty minutes a temporary vertical bulkhead—or alternatively a saw cut—must be installed to produce a full‑depth transverse construction joint across the entire width of the ATPBC, and each lift may not exceed four inches in compacted thickness. Drainage, environment, and accessibility

When pavement thickness is altered for lane widening, designers are required to keep drainage layers consistent with those of the existing pavement and to verify that adequate drainage is provided throughout the cross‑section. In thin asphalt overlay applications, a reduced pavement thickness may be used only when surface drainage is good or will be upgraded to good, subsurface drainage is good or will be upgraded to good, and the existing pavement is structurally sound; if moisture, climate or freeze‑thaw effects are of concern, the interlayer selection criteria call for a thicker overlay together with an appropriate interlayer that serves as a moisture barrier and mitigates cracking in harsh environments. For full‑depth reclamation projects, selecting the reclaimed layer thickness must balance the composition of existing layers with the structural requirements dictated by anticipated traffic and environmental conditions, and any drainage deficiencies or wet subgrade locations must be identified and effective drainage installed before constructing the reclaimed pavement layer to prevent excess moisture from compromising material integrity and structural capacity. In full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete slab design, blockouts are provided for existing utility castings and proposed drainage structures, all soft, wet or unsuitable subbase and subgrade material must be removed prior to slab placement, and the mix design must account for ambient average daily temperatures and exposure conditions at the project site in order to select appropriate panel size and expansion‑joint locations; each slab is also cast with a sacrificial thickness of 1/4 inch above the nominal design thickness. For bonded concrete overlays, construction must include Department‑approved measures to drain or remove standing water from the prepared surface, placement is limited to when the concrete temperature is between 65 °F and 90 °F, insulating blankets are provided if the forecast air temperature is 45 °F or less so that the surface temperature does not change more than 40 °F in any one‑hour period, and transverse saw cuts are made to a depth equal to the full overlay thickness plus 1/2 inch to ensure load transfer under environmental variations. In asphalt paving interlayer systems, the existing surface must be dry, clean and drainable with no depressions; the interlayer may never be installed on wet pavement; the first lift of asphalt overlay over the interlayer must achieve a minimum compacted thickness of not less than 1 ½ inches to provide adequate structural depth despite exposure; and the interlayer material is required to remain dry during shipping and storage by being wrapped, elevated off the ground and covered from precipitation, ultraviolet light and other weather elements, with placement proceeding only when weather conditions meet the manufacturer’s recommendations. For pervious asphalt pavement, the subgrade is kept permeable and compaction is limited to no more than four passes so that the surface remains free‑draining, over‑compaction that would eliminate drainage or crush aggregate is prohibited, placement is avoided when air or surface temperature is below 50 °F, and for test sections the compacted thickness may not be more than 1/4 inch less than the design thickness to preserve the intended structural depth while maintaining porosity. Verification and expected performance

For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) asphalt the completed pavement thickness is verified by confirming that the installed HIR layer falls within the typical 1 ½‑inch to 2‑inch range, that the underlying asphalt meets the specified minimum of 1‑to‑2 inches for composite pavements or 2‑to‑3 inches for full‑depth asphalt pavements, and that the overall asphalt pavement thickness is at least 1 ½ inches more than the milling depth; meeting these dimensional criteria ensures compliance with design intent, constructability, performance expectations, and project requirements [1]. For rigid pavement designs the designer enters all input values in DARWin, obtains a design thickness, rounds the result to the nearest half‑inch (for example 10.24 inches becomes 10.0 inches and 10.25 inches becomes 10.5 inches), and if the slab is thicker than 7 inches and the quantity is less than 30,000 square yards rounds to the nearest inch; the rounded value must lie within the minimum and maximum depths provided in Table 8.3 and the designer then determines the combination of design variables that yields the most economical rigid pavement while maintaining required durability and rideability qualities, finally contacting the District Pavement Management Engineer/Pavement Manager to confirm that the thickness is consistent with overall project requirements [1]. For flexible pavements, after the thickness design is completed the calculated structural number derived from the chosen layer thicknesses must be compared to the required design SN; it shall be greater than the Design SN for the pavement to be structurally adequate and the resulting layer depths must also fall within the minimum and maximum course depths listed in Tables 9.4 and 9.5, with adjustments made to achieve the lowest total pavement cost while respecting surface, base, subbase unit costs and lift‑depth limits [1]. For a bonded PCC overlay verification begins by calculating the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic in DARWin, then determining the Effective Existing Thickness using the Condition Survey evaluation method; the resulting concrete thickness is obtained with the Calculate button, rounded up to the nearest half‑inch, and finally confirmed that this recommended depth falls within the minimum and maximum limits set out in Table 10.10, thereby satisfying compliance, constructability, performance, and project requirements [1]. For an unbonded PCC overlay verification starts by determining both the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic (Table 10.8) and the Existing Effective Pavement Thickness (Table 10.9), then calculating the required overlay thickness; the resulting thickness is checked against Table 10.10 to ensure it exceeds the minimum allowed for unbonded overlays, with compliance further demonstrated by preparing cross‑sections at 50‑ft intervals to estimate material quantities and by adjusting core sampling procedures to verify depth where the cross‑section varies [1]. For slip‑form cement concrete pavements the completed pavement thickness is verified in the presence of the Representative by drilling cores according to PTM No. 606 or by non‑destructive testing according to PTM No. 605; any core measurement that is deficient by more than ¼ inch triggers additional coring at 100‑foot intervals until the deficiency is no greater than ¼ inch, and the pavement is deemed defective if any core is deficient by more than ½ inch [2]. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slabs the installed slab is measured against the authorized shop drawings and the tolerances shown in Table A; any slab outside those limits is rejected and the contractor must cast each slab with an additional ¼ inch of sacrificial concrete over the nominal thickness, a layer that is included when field personnel confirm as‑built thickness using a straight‑edge at least ten feet long and a depth gauge at least six feet long set to the slab’s thickness [2]. After placement of an asphalt interlayer system the first compacted lift must be at least 1½ inches thick and subsequent lifts are built to achieve the total thickness required by the design; the field quality‑control plan records compacted depth on each lift to ensure the minimum 1½‑inch requirement is satisfied before additional layers are placed and that the final assembled pavement conforms to the overall project‑documented thickness [2]. For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements under the RPS the completed pavement is inspected against design thickness by measuring the average depth of each lot; it is considered defective if that average is less than the design thickness minus ½ inch or if any individual sub‑lot falls below the same limit, and performance criteria also require the Percent Within Limits for compressive strength or plastic air content to be at least 55 %, failure to meet which renders the lot defective; surface defects such as rain damage, improper joint construction, curing problems, cracking, honeycombing, unacceptable depressions or high points, and edge slumping that cannot be satisfactorily repaired also constitute non‑conformance with project requirements [2]. Acceptance for cement concrete pavements is confirmed by grouping placement into full‑width lots of 5 600 square yards (four equal sublots of 1 400 square yards) that retain a constant depth; for each lot the Representative accepts work only if the average depth is more than design thickness minus 0.5 inch and no individual sublot depth is less than design thickness minus 0.5 inch, thereby confirming compliance with the specification and ensuring constructability, performance, and consistency with project requirements [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements the Representative designates one location within each lot using PTM No. 1; a core is drilled in accordance with AASHTO T 24 and its depth measured with an apparatus conforming to PTM No. 614, and if that measured depth is within ¼ inch of the plan depth the pavement thickness is deemed acceptable; when the depth is deficient by more than ¼ inch two additional cores are taken in the presence of the Representative and the average of the three measurements must be less than a ¼‑inch deficiency for acceptance, otherwise the pavement is considered defective [2]. For Long‑Life Concrete Pavement the contractor measures both the average depth of each lot and the depth of every individual sublot and compares those values to the design thickness (D); the lot is defective if the average depth is less than D minus 0.5 inch or if any single sublot depth falls below D minus 0.5 inch, in which case removal and replacement are required; after surface corrective work is finished depth is verified in each sublot by obtaining one core or one nondestructive test as directed by PTM No. 1, with the first sublot core and every tenth thereafter submitted as information cores to the Representative, who cleans the core holes, refills them with the original mix or non‑shrink grout, measures the core length according to PTM No. 614, calculates the average lot pavement thickness by averaging the individual sublot core thicknesses, and checks entrained air content, requiring a written evaluation within five days if the air content is below 3.5 % or above 8.5 % [2]. For pervious asphalt pavement the compacted layer shall not be more than ¼ inch below the design thickness; two test sections of at least 225 square feet each are constructed and their average infiltration rate must exceed 200 in/hr per ASTM C1701, with compaction limited to no more than four static‑roller passes so that the surface remains free‑draining, thereby confirming constructability and consistency with project specifications [2]. For pervious concrete pavement the test‑section thickness shall not be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness and three 4‑inch diameter cores per 5 000 square feet are removed; the average core length must equal 3/8 inch + 3/4 inch and no individual core may be less than –½ inch, confirming that the as‑built thickness meets design, constructability, performance and project requirements [2]. For thin bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavement the pavement is divided into lots of no more than 3 000 square yards per traffic lane; for each lot the inspector randomly selects one coring location according to PTM No. 1 and cores are drilled in the presence of the Inspector as required by PTM No. 606, while at intersections or when a lot is smaller two cores are taken or nondestructive thickness measurements are performed in accordance with PTM No. 605; the measured depth must not be deficient by more than ¼ inch, and if it is the Department will refuse payment for additional cores and adjust lot payment as specified in Table A, with final payment limited to 5 % of the contract unit prices when defective pavement is left in place [2]. For asphalt‑treated permeable base course the Representative verifies thickness through systematic test holes: for every completed base course covering up to 3 000 square yards a 6‑inch diameter hole is dug or drilled to the full depth of the layer; any location that is short by ½ inch or more must be removed and replaced, then backfilled with acceptable material and compacted until the surface is flush and the final thickness is within ½ inch of the indicated value, thereby confirming compliance, constructability and performance against project requirements [2].

For overlay designs covered in Section 10.1, pavement thickness shall be chosen so that the structural design life is at least 8 years and shall not exceed 20 years, defining the service‑life performance outcome for those overlays [1]. Under Section 502 of the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, each lift of RCC must be placed in a single increment between 4 inches and 9 inches; compaction is verified by field density tests performed according to ASTM C1040, requiring at least 85 % of the maximum wet density for lifts thinner than 6 inches and at least 90 % for lifts 6 inches or thicker when measured directly behind the paver, and after final rolling all thicknesses must exceed 96 % of the maximum wet density [2]. Performance is measured through the documented compaction values, timed density testing, and compressive‑strength testing to confirm that the pavement thickness delivers the intended structural integrity and longevity [2]. For Long‑Life Concrete Pavement under Section 530, the thickness performance is verified by confirming that the average depth of a lot is not less than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch and that no individual sub‑lot falls below that same limit; compressive strength must reach at least 55 % of the specified PWL, and visual inspection must show an absence of surface defects such as rain damage, improper joints, curing errors, cracking or honeycombing that cannot be satisfactorily repaired, with depressions or high points required to remain within the tolerances set in Section 506.3(o) and edge slumping meeting the requirements of Section 501.3(g)5 [2]. These outcomes are measured by depth surveys, compressive‑strength testing, and field inspections for surface condition, profile tolerance, and edge integrity [2].
