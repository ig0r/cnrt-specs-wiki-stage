Cross-Slope

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Cross-Slope is a topic under Pavement Design.
cross-slopecross-slopecross-slopessuperelevationsuperelevations
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Cross‑slope is a deliberately imposed positive transverse grade on the pavement and shoulder that forces water to drain from the travel surface, preventing ponding that can lead to skidding, hydroplaning, and wet‑weather crashes[1]. It must satisfy the minimum values set out in Publication 13, Design Manual Part 2 (often at least ¼‑inch per foot) and be held within tight tolerances—typically no more than 1/8 inch deviation over any 12‑foot length, with any segment exceeding ¼ inch corrected during profile milling, diamond grinding, subgrade work, or other preservation actions[2]. By providing this drainage angle, cross‑slope enhances safety, maintains ride quality, and supports the long‑term serviceability and durability of the pavement structure[1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Cross‑slope is provided to create a positive transverse grade that enables water on the pavement and shoulder to drain away, thereby preventing water accumulation that can lead to skidding, hydroplaning, and wet‑weather crashes; this drainage supports the performance goals of diamond grinding and grooving treatments which aim to restore transverse drainage and improve safety. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require a positive cross‑slope so that water is shed from the pavement surface, preventing standing water that could impair safe vehicle operation. In profile milling of asphalt pavements (Section 492—Profile Milling of Asphalt Pavement Surfaces, 492.3 Construction) the milling equipment must include automatic controls for the transverse cross‑slope and may produce either a variable or constant cross‑slope, ensuring the finished surface conforms to the specified drainage geometry. In diamond grinding of concrete pavements (Section 514—Diamond Grinding of Concrete Pavement, 514.3 Construction) any deviation greater than ¼ inch in 12 feet must be corrected so that a uniform transverse slope and a positive cross‑slope exist for the pavement to drain. Subgrade construction (Section 210—Subgrade, 210.3 Construction) relies on maintaining this cross‑slope to provide reliable drainage from the pavement surface; disturbed subgrade surfaces are sealed at the end of each workday and the transverse grade is verified with a template and leveling device to confirm the required slope. For next‑generation concrete surfaces (Section 509—Next Generation Concrete Surface, 509.3 Construction) cross‑slope is required on adjacent shoulders and ramps to prevent water buildup that could compromise safety, and the specification limits any depression or misalignment of the slope to not exceed 1/8 inch over a 12‑foot length measured perpendicular to the centerline while retaining the existing roadway cross‑slope. In corrective actions for concrete pavement ride quality (Section 507—Evaluation of Concrete Pavement Ride Quality and Payment of Incentive, 507.3 Construction) surfaces must be corrected to provide a uniform texture and a positive cross‑slope so that the pavement drains properly. Likewise, acceptance criteria for asphalt pavement ride quality (Section 404—Evaluation of Asphalt Pavement Ride Quality and Payment of Incentive, 404.3 Construction) require correcting surfaces to achieve a uniform texture and a positive cross‑slope because without it water can pool and create ridges or valleys that exceed allowable tolerances, degrading ride quality and safety. [1][2]

The design must provide for new construction, reconstruction, and 3R projects that the finished roadway surface provides at least the minimum cross‑slope prescribed in Publication 13, Design Manual Part 2, and this cross‑slope must be provided as a positive slope so that pavement and shoulder areas drain as necessary, thereby restoring transverse drainage and allowing water to escape from the surface. [1] For profile milling of asphalt pavement surfaces, the cross‑slope shall be generated by transverse controls equipped with an automatic system capable of maintaining either a variable or constant slope as shown in the plans; it shall be positive to provide drainage and shall be held within 1/8 inch over any 12‑foot length, and the milled surface produced under this control shall be free from grooves, ridges, gouges or other irregularities that could endanger vehicle safety. [2] In diamond‑grinding of concrete pavement, the original cross‑slope shall be restored and kept uniform; inspections shall occur at 100‑foot longitudinal intervals, any segment deviating by more than 1/4 inch over a 12‑foot length shall be corrected, and a positive cross‑slope shall be provided so the pavement drains, thereby meeting functional drainage, safety against water‑related loss of control and serviceability requirements. [2] Subgrade construction requires the cross‑slope to be formed so that the subgrade surface promotes continuous positive drainage and minimizes infiltration of surface water; the finished cross‑section shall be checked with a template and leveling device, and any irregularity exceeding 1/2 inch shall be corrected by loosening, adding or removing material and re‑compacting, ensuring serviceability and durability. [2] For next‑generation concrete surfaces, the cross‑slope shall be maintained at the roadway’s existing grade so that a 12‑foot straightedge placed perpendicular to the centerline shows no depressions or misalignment greater than 1/8 inch; a feather pass extending at least 2 feet into adjacent shoulders, gore areas, auxiliary lanes or ramp lanes and running a minimum of 100 feet shall be provided to ensure proper roadway drainage, and the overall pavement surface shall meet ride‑quality limits of a maximum IRI of 70 in/mile where posted speeds exceed 45 mph and a maximum IRI of 90 in/mile where posted speeds are 45 mph or less, satisfying serviceability objectives. [2] In full‑depth reclamation the cross‑slope shall meet a minimum final surface cross slope of 1/4‑inch per foot, or any steeper value required by the project design, to satisfy functional drainage and safety serviceability objectives as part of the overall surface tolerance criteria that ensure adequate transverse grading. [2] Concrete pavement ride‑quality evaluation mandates that the cross‑slope be maintained as a positive, uniform slope that allows drainage; any ridge or valley between adjacent pavements may not exceed 1/8 inch on the transverse profile, and corrective work shall be completed before depth testing to achieve this condition, providing a uniform texture and satisfying functional drainage and serviceability goals. [2] Asphalt pavement ride‑quality evaluation requires a positive cross‑slope to permit drainage; corrective work shall avoid creating any ridge or valley greater than 1/8 inch on the transverse profile while keeping surface irregularities within the 3/16 inch straightedge tolerance, thereby ensuring uniform texture, proper drainage and meeting serviceability and safety objectives. Scope and applicability

For new construction, reconstruction and 3R roadway projects, including any shoulder reconstruction, the finished surface must meet the minimum cross‑slope requirements set out in Publication 13, Design Manual Part 2; when those criteria cannot be satisfied a design exception request is required [1]. Pavement preservation work should follow the guidance provided in Publication 13, Chapter 4, Section 4.3 [1]. Diamond grinding or grooving may be specified on Portland cement concrete pavements—jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP), jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP), continuously reinforced concrete pavement (CRCP), post‑tensioned slab (PCPS) and roller‑compacted concrete pavement (RCCP)—to restore drainage, friction or macrotexture, and the method may be applied to new or in‑service pavements [1]. For rigid (concrete) pavements that are diamond‑ground under the procedures of Section 514, the original cross‑slope shall be restored and kept uniform, inspections shall be made at 100‑foot longitudinal intervals and any deviation exceeding 1/4 inch over a 12‑foot length shall be corrected to provide a positive slope for drainage [2]. When a rigid pavement is treated with the Next Generation Concrete Surface process described in Section 509, the existing roadway cross‑slope shall be retained, any deviation shall be limited to no more than 1/8 inch in 12 feet, and a feather pass of at least 2 feet onto adjacent shoulders, gore areas, auxiliary lanes or ramp lanes shall be performed to maintain consistent drainage [2]. Full‑depth reclamation base courses, including both asphalt‑stabilized and chemically stabilized reclaimed material, require a minimum final surface cross‑slope of 1/4‑inch per foot unless the project design specifies otherwise [2]. Corrective surface actions on reinforced cement concrete panels or plain cement concrete sections shall achieve a uniform texture and positive cross‑slope for drainage, and deviations greater than 1/8 inch in the transverse profile shall not be produced [2]. For flexible (asphalt) pavement lots where corrective action is performed to eliminate surface irregularities, ridges or valleys exceeding 1/8 inch in transverse profile or individual bumps larger than 3/16 inch shall be removed by carbide grinding, diamond grinding, or removal and replacement of the surface course so that a uniform texture and positive cross‑slope allow drainage [2].

When the cross‑slope design criteria cannot be satisfied for new‑construction, reconstruction, or 3R projects, a design‑exception request must be prepared rather than forcing the required slope, and pavement‑preservation work follows separate guidance so that the standard cross‑slope requirements are not applied and an alternative design may be used. Additionally, although cross‑slope provides the basic means for water to run off a concrete pavement, designers may opt for diamond grooving when additional skid resistance is needed; transverse grooving should only be considered where braking traction is required because it provides a shorter drainage path, whereas longitudinal grooving is generally preferred because it is less time‑consuming and more economical for most applications. Design inputs and requirements

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10) cross‑slope design for new construction, reconstruction and 3R projects is governed by the minimum cross‑slope values set out in Publication 13, Design Manual Part 2, Contextual Roadway Design, Chapters 3 and 4, and shoulder work must also conform to those same chapters together with Section 2.6. Superelevations on curves must be checked and corrected as needed, and if the prescribed criteria cannot be satisfied a design‑exception request must be prepared in accordance with Publication 10X, Appendix P. For pavement preservation projects the guidance in Publication 13, Design Manual Part 2, Contextual Roadway Design, Chapter 4, Section 4.3 governs the cross‑slope. On concrete pavements the cross‑slope must be provided as a positive slope to allow drainage of the pavement and shoulder, a requirement referenced in Publication 408, Specifications, Section 514 for diamond grinding, and water drainage for diamond grooving is achieved by pavement cross‑slope according to FHWA TA 5040.36. The profile‑milling specification in Section 492 requires that the milling machine be equipped with transverse controls that automatically control cross‑slope at a given rate and may produce either a variable or constant cross‑slope through automatic adjustments, and the finished surface must conform to the design cross‑slope within 1/8 inch over a 12‑foot span while providing a positive cross‑slope as shown on the plans so that water can drain from the pavement. Under the diamond‑grinding requirements of Section 514, the original pavement cross‑slope shall be restored and kept uniform; inspections are required at 100‑foot longitudinal intervals and any deviation greater than 1/4 inch over a 12‑foot length must be corrected, with a positive cross‑slope also required for drainage. The Earthwork specifications governing subgrade in Section 210 mandate that the finished surface be checked for grade and slope using a transverse string line or a template equipped with a leveling device; any deviation exceeding ½ inch must be corrected, and the checking interval may not exceed 25 feet for conventional construction, 50 feet when an automatic grader is used, or 500 feet when GPS or laser‑controlled equipment is employed, provided the quality‑control plan in Section 210.3(d) is satisfied. For next‑generation concrete surfaces, Section 509 requires preservation of the existing cross‑slope, with no depression or misalignment greater than 1/8 inch over a 12‑foot span when measured perpendicular to the centreline; drainage feather passes must extend at least 2 feet onto adjacent shoulders, gore areas, auxiliary lanes, or ramp lanes and be no shorter than 100 feet. The full‑depth reclamation provisions of Section 344 state that irregularities in the existing centreline, grades or cross‑slope may not exceed 1½ inches, and a minimum final surface cross‑slope of 1/4 inch per foot shall be provided unless the project design specifies a different value. In the concrete pavement ride‑quality program of Section 507, ridges or valleys measured on the transverse profile must not exceed 1/8 inch; any greater deviation must be corrected so that the surface provides a uniform texture and a positive cross‑slope to allow drainage, using carbide grinding, diamond grinding, or removal and replacement as appropriate. The flexible‑pavement ride‑quality criteria in Section 404 prohibit creating ridges or valleys exceeding 1/8 inch on the transverse profile and require corrective work until a uniform texture and positive cross‑slope are achieved for drainage; individual bumps whose irregularity exceeds 3/16 inch when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge must be ground to meet ride‑quality requirements. [1][2]

Design methods and criteria

For new construction, reconstruction, and 3R projects the cross‑slope design must follow the minimum values prescribed in Publication 13, Design Manual Part 2, Contextual Roadway Design, Chapters 3 and 4 [1]. Superelevations in curved sections shall be checked and corrected if necessary, which may involve adding leveling material or removing surface material, and any shoulder reconstruction must also satisfy the same Publication 13 requirements [1]. When the required cross‑slope cannot be achieved for those projects a design‑exception request is prepared in accordance with Publication 10X, Appendix P [1]. Pavement‑preservation work follows the guidance set out in Publication 13, Chapter 4, Section 4.3 [1]. The profile‑milling procedure requires equipping a self‑propelled milling machine with a built‑in automatic grade control system that governs both the longitudinal profile and the transverse cross‑slope [2]. Longitudinal controls may be referenced to a string line, ski of at least 30 feet, mobile string line of at least 30 feet, or matching shoe, while the transverse controls use an automatic system to set the cross‑slope at a prescribed rate [2]. The finished milled surface must match the plan’s positive cross‑slope within a tolerance of 1/8 inch in 12 feet [2]. For diamond‑grinding projects the transverse tolerance clause requires restoring the original pavement cross‑slope, maintaining uniformity, checking cross‑slope at 100‑foot longitudinal intervals, correcting any segment where deviations exceed 1/4 inch in 12 feet, providing a positive cross‑slope to ensure drainage, and supplying documentation of each test location together with the maximum depth of depression or slope misalignment recorded there [2]. Verification of cross‑slope during subgrade construction is performed by stretching a string line transversely between reference grade lines and measuring the vertical distance from that line to the finished surface at multiple points across the width, or alternatively by using a template cut to the required cross‑section together with a 10‑foot straightedge and a leveling device [2]. The longitudinal interval for checking the surface cross‑section shall not exceed 25 feet, may be increased to not more than 50 feet when an automatic grading machine is employed, and can be extended to not more than 500 feet when GPS or laser‑controlled equipment is used in accordance with the quality‑control plan [2]. Any surface irregularity that exceeds 1/2 inch must be corrected by loosening the surface and adding or removing material as required, compacting the area, and rechecking the grade and slope [2].

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10), for new construction, reconstruction, and 3R projects the finished roadway surface must provide at least the minimum cross‑slope specified in Publication 13, Design Manual Part 2, Contextual Roadway Design, Chapters 3 and 4; this requirement also applies to shoulder reconstruction, which shall conform to the same cross‑slope requirements set out in Publication 13 and Section 2.6. The manual further states that the cross‑slope must be a positive slope so that both pavement and shoulder areas can drain as necessary, thereby ensuring adequate water runoff from the surface. Any superelevations on curves shall be checked and corrected if necessary, which may require adding or removing material. When the design criteria for cross‑slopes cannot be satisfied for new construction, reconstruction, or 3R projects, a design‑exception request must be prepared in accordance with Publication 10X, Appendix P. In profile milling, as specified in the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATION Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS (Section 492), the milling machine shall be equipped with an automatic grade‑control system capable of setting the transverse cross‑slope; it may produce either a variable or a constant cross‑slope as shown on the plans, and the finished surface must match the specified cross‑slope within 1/8 inch over any 12‑foot length. For diamond grinding (Section 514), the contractor shall restore the original pavement cross‑slope and shall maintain a uniform cross‑slope throughout the treated area; uniformity is to be checked at 100‑foot longitudinal intervals and any segment where the deviation exceeds 1/4 inch in 12 feet must be corrected, and a positive cross‑slope must be provided for drainage. During subgrade construction (Section 210), transverse irregularities may not exceed ½ inch; if they do, the area shall be loosened and material added or removed and then re‑compacted, and the finished cross‑section is verified with a taut string line across the subgrade at longitudinal intervals of not more than 25 feet (or up to 50 feet when an automatic grading machine is used, or up to 500 feet when GPS or laser control is employed provided the QC plan requirements are met). Random acceptance checks are performed every 500 feet and any deviation greater than ½ inch must be corrected before proceeding. When a feather pass is applied to an existing roadway (Section 509), the existing cross‑slope shall be maintained and the pass shall extend at least 2 feet into adjacent shoulders, gore areas, auxiliary lanes or ramp lanes and shall be at least 100 feet long; any depression or misalignment measured with a 12‑foot straightedge placed perpendicular to the centreline may not exceed 1/8 inch over a 12‑ft span. In full‑depth reclamation (Section 344), the stabilized base surface shall provide a minimum cross‑slope of 1/4‑inch per foot, or as otherwise required by the project design, and during finishing the FDR material surface may not exceed a 1½‑inch irregularity of the existing centreline, grades or cross‑slope of the proposed roadway. For ride‑quality corrective action (Section 507), the pavement surface shall be finished to give a uniform texture and a positive cross‑slope that allows drainage; any ridge or valley measured on the transverse profile may not exceed 1/8 inch, an individual bump showing an irregularity of more than 1/4 inch when checked with a 12‑foot straightedge renders the lot defective, and a ridge or valley greater than 1/8 inch adjacent to another ride‑quality lot also constitutes a defect. Materials and mixture design

In Full‑Depth Reclamation the material blend is chosen by a mix design that specifies the reclaimed pavement material, any required aggregates or RAP, the stabilizing additive(s) and water, all proportioned to meet the design moisture content; the blended mix must be compacted to at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum density at optimum moisture and the compaction moisture may not exceed the optimum by more than 3 %. Structural performance is verified by achieving the prescribed strength—chemical stabilization requires an unconfined compressive strength of 300 to 500 psi (or 280 to 500 psi for thicker overlays) after seven days—and durability is supported by maintaining the required density and moisture limits throughout construction. Constructability is addressed by controlling pulverization, mixing, compaction passes, and curing so that the final surface meets a minimum cross‑slope of 1/4‑inch per foot. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 227–229)

Structural details and interfaces

Corrective work that involves removal and replacement shall be done between existing transverse joints of reinforced cement concrete pavements or across an entire plain‑cement panel, with a minimum of 10 feet measured between those joints. If the repair reaches an existing transverse joint, the joint shall be replaced in kind as directed, and any new joints created by the removal shall be constructed using the methods for joining pavements shown on the Standard Drawings. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 395–396)

In Section 514—Diamond Grinding of Concrete Pavement, the original pavement cross‑slope must be restored and kept uniform; inspections are required at 100‑foot intervals along the lane and any segment where the transverse deviation exceeds greater than 1/4 inch over a 12‑foot length shall be corrected to provide a positive cross‑slope that drains water. Grinding operations are prohibited within 2 feet of existing curbs, bridge barriers or roadway barriers so that the interface with adjacent features is preserved, and slurry must not be allowed to flow into gutters or other drainage facilities. In Section 210—Subgrade, cross‑slope control is achieved by first sealing the constructed or disturbed subgrade surfaces each day with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode, which promotes continuous positive drainage and limits water infiltration. A Class 4, Type A geotextile is then placed over the finished subgrade across the full lane width of eight feet or more before the subbase, installed continuously in the longitudinal direction with its transverse edges kept between a minimum of 6 inches and a maximum of 12 inches from the interior edge of the pavement base drain, and must not be damaged during base‑drain installation. The finished surface cross‑slope is verified using a template fitted with vertical extensions at each end to support a leveling device, with the bottom of the template set at the top of the subgrade, and any longitudinal irregularities are checked with a 10‑foot straightedge held parallel to the road centerline. In Section 509—Next Generation Concrete Surface, the cross‑slope at the interface between the concrete surface and adjoining shoulders, gore areas, auxiliary lanes or ramp lanes must be kept consistent with the existing roadway grade, which is achieved by a feather pass of diamond grinding extending at least 2 feet onto those adjacent features. The feather pass is required to provide proper drainage while ensuring that no depression or misalignment of slope exceeds 1/8 inch in a 12‑foot span measured perpendicular to the centerline. In Section 507—Evaluation of Concrete Pavement Ride Quality, cross‑slope at any interface—such as where one ride‑quality lot meets an adjacent lot—must be kept uniform and positive so water can drain, and the transverse profile may not show a ridge or valley greater than 1/8 inch. In Section 404—Evaluation of Asphalt Pavement Ride Quality, when cross‑slope is designed at the interface between pavement layers or adjacent lanes, the surface must be shaped so that a ridge or valley with the adjoining pavement does not exceed 1/8 inch on the transverse profile, preventing abrupt height differences that could impair drainage. The corrected surface should provide a uniform texture and a positive cross‑slope to ensure water runs off the pavement at the joint, and these requirements are enforced during corrective actions such as carbide grinding, diamond grinding, or removal and replacement of the surface course. [2]

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Cross‑slope is required to be a positive slope so that water can escape from both the pavement surface and the shoulder area, thereby providing necessary drainage. In Section 514—Diamond Grinding of Concrete Pavement, the design mandates that the original pavement cross‑slope be restored and kept uniform, with a positive cross‑slope to ensure drainage of water from the surface. The same section specifies inspection of the cross‑slope at 100‑foot longitudinal intervals, and any segment showing a deviation greater than 1/4 inch over a 12‑foot length shall be corrected to reestablish the proper slope. In Section 210—Subgrade, the transverse grade for drainage is verified using a template cut to the required surface shape and equipped with vertical extensions that support a leveling device. Each workday the finished subgrade surfaces are sealed with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage and limit water infiltration; any surface irregularities exceeding 1/2 inch must be corrected by loosening, adding or removing material and re‑compacting. When the completed subgrade has been in place for more than 60 days or at least one winter season, a full compaction and proof roll is required before installing the Class 4, Type A geotextile separator and subbase, ensuring that the underlying layer can support effective drainage under seasonal moisture conditions. In Section 509—Next Generation Concrete Surface, cross‑slope consistency is maintained by performing a feather pass that extends at least 2 feet onto adjacent shoulders, gore areas, auxiliary lanes or ramp lanes and runs a minimum of 100 feet; this feather pass provides proper roadway drainage. The existing cross‑slope is to be maintained and any depression or misalignment measured with a 12‑foot straightedge placed perpendicular to the centerline may not exceed 1/8 inch in 12 feet. In Section 507—Evaluation of Concrete Pavement Ride Quality and Payment of Incentive, corrected pavement surfaces must maintain a uniform texture and a positive cross‑slope so water can drain from the surface, and any ridge or valley exceeding 1/8 inch on the transverse profile must be eliminated to keep the cross‑slope consistent across adjacent sections. According to the Pavement Policy Manual, for new construction, reconstruction, and 3R projects, the finished roadway surface should provide at least the minimum cross‑slope called out in Publication 13, Design Manual Part 2, Contextual Roadway Design (Chapters 3 and 4). The same manual requires that superelevations on curves must be checked and corrected if necessary, and any shoulder reconstruction likewise has to conform to those same Publication 13 cross‑slope requirements. When the design criteria for cross‑slopes cannot be met, a design‑exception request must be submitted per Publication 10X Appendix P, as stipulated in the Pavement Policy Manual. In diamond grinding and grooving operations the Pavement Policy Manual specifies that the cross‑slope must be provided as a positive slope so that the pavement surface and shoulder area can drain as necessary, thereby supporting the safety function of removing water to reduce skidding and hydroplaning risks; this drainage capability is explicitly achieved through the pavement cross‑slope referenced in FHWA guidance. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 Specifications states that during profile milling of asphalt pavement surfaces, the cross‑slope must be established with equipment that includes a built‑in automatic grade control system for the transverse direction and an automatic system capable of controlling cross‑slope at a given rate; the finished profile shall conform to the specified cross‑slope within 1/8 inch in 12 feet, and it must be a positive cross‑slope as shown on the plans so that water drains from the pavement, supporting safety and functional performance. For diamond grinding of concrete pavement, the same specifications require that the original pavement cross‑slope shall be restored and kept uniform; uniformity shall be verified at 100‑foot longitudinal intervals, any transverse deviation exceeding ¼ inch over a 12‑foot length must be corrected, and a positive cross‑slope must be provided to allow drainage. In subgrade construction, the specifications indicate that cross‑slope is verified during grade and slope checks; surface irregularities that affect the cross‑slope shall not exceed ½ inch, and verification may use a template with vertical extensions or a 10‑foot straightedge held parallel to the centerline with measurements taken at longitudinal intervals of no more than 25 feet (or up to 50 feet when an automatic grading machine is used, and up to 500 feet when GPS/laser control is employed); if an irregularity exceeds the ½‑inch limit, the area must be loosened, corrected by adding or removing material, compacted, and rechecked before subgrade acceptance. For next‑generation concrete surface work, the specifications require that the existing roadway cross‑slope shall be retained; drainage shall be achieved by a feather pass extending at least 2 feet onto adjacent shoulders, gore areas, auxiliary lanes or ramp lanes, with each feather pass having a minimum length of 100 feet, and any deviation in slope may not exceed 1/8 inch over a 12‑foot span when checked with a 12‑foot straightedge placed perpendicular to the centerline, ensuring consistent geometric alignment for safe water runoff and user comfort. In full‑depth reclamation, the specifications set a geometric requirement that cross‑slope must be at least ¼‑inch per foot final surface slope unless the project design specifies otherwise, and the reclaimed pavement surface shall be shaped so that its irregularity does not exceed 1 ½ inches relative to the existing centerline, grades or cross‑slope, thereby supporting proper drainage and safe vehicle operation. The evaluation of concrete pavement ride quality, as described in the specifications, requires that cross‑slope be positive to allow water runoff, which supports safety and accessibility by preventing standing water on the travel surface; the transverse profile shall not contain a ridge or valley exceeding 1/8 inch relative to adjacent pavement, and when such deviations are present corrective action using carbide grinding of areas up to 15 feet long with a maximum single‑pass depth of 1/8 inch is required to restore a uniform texture and positive cross‑slope. Similarly, the evaluation of asphalt pavement ride quality in the specifications mandates that the pavement surface be shaped so that no transverse deviation exceeds 1/8 inch, any individual bump whose irregularity is more than 3/16 inch on a 12‑foot straightedge must be ground, and adjacent lots may not contain a ridge or valley of more than 1/8 inch; the corrected surface shall provide a uniform texture and a positive cross‑slope so that water drains from the pavement, meeting ride‑quality specifications and ensuring safe drainage. Verification and expected performance

The completed cross‑slope is verified by confirming that the finished roadway surface provides at least the minimum cross slope called out in Publication 13, Design Manual Part 2, Chapters 3–4 for new construction, reconstruction and 3R projects [1]. Superelevations in any curved sections are checked and corrected if necessary; corrections may involve adding leveling material or removing surface material, and when the design criteria cannot be met a design‑exception request is prepared according to Publication 10X Appendix P [1]. For profile milling (Section 492) the completed cross‑slope is measured against a tolerance of within 1/8 inch in 12 feet, confirming that the positive slope shown on the plans is achieved and that adjacent passes match within the same tolerance; compliance is ensured by using a self‑propelled milling machine equipped with an automatic grade control system and transverse controls that automatically maintain the specified cross‑slope during profiling, and the resulting surface must be free of grooves, ridges or gouges and provide drainage [2]. For diamond grinding of concrete (Section 514) uniformity of the cross‑slope is measured along the pavement at 100‑foot intervals in the longitudinal direction and the original cross‑slope must be restored with a positive drainage slope; any segment where the deviation exceeds 1/4 inch over a 12‑foot length shall be corrected, and the contractor shall submit documentation to the Representative listing each test location together with the maximum depth of depression or slope misalignment observed [2]. After subgrade compaction (Section 210) reference grade lines are established with stakes and the finished surface is checked transversely with a taut string line or a template fitted with vertical extensions that support a leveling device; measurements of the vertical distance from the string (or template) to the surface are taken at intervals not more than 25 feet, or not more than 50 feet when automatic grading equipment is used and not more than 500 feet when GPS/laser control is employed, and any irregularity exceeding 1/2 inch must be corrected by loosening, adding or removing material, re‑compacting the area, and then rechecking; acceptance testing includes random cross‑section checks at hinge points, lane edges and shoulders every 500 feet, and if more than 10 % of all acceptance tests exceed 1/2 inch additional staking and string‑line verification may be required [2]. For next generation concrete surface work (Section 509) a feather pass that extends at least 2 feet into each adjacent shoulder, gore area, auxiliary lane or ramp lane and runs a minimum of 100 feet is first made, then the slope is measured with a 12‑foot straightedge placed perpendicular to the centerline; any depression or misalignment greater than 1/8 inch in 12 feet is not allowed and must be reground [2]. In full depth reclamation (Section 344) the finished base is examined for transverse grade accuracy by testing surface smoothness and confirming that the measured cross‑slope meets the minimum final surface cross slope of 1/4‑inch per foot unless the project design calls for a different value, while also ensuring the shape does not exceed a 1½‑inch irregularity from the existing centerline, grades or proposed cross‑slope; any 3,000‑square‑yard lot where the average surface irregularity exceeds 1/2 inch over a 10‑foot template must be corrected [2]. For ride quality evaluation (Section 507) the transverse profile of each pavement lot is measured and any ridge or valley that exceeds 1/8 inch relative to adjacent pavement is considered non‑compliant and must be corrected before depth testing, using methods such as carbide grinding on sections up to 15 feet long with a maximum single‑pass depth of 1/8 inch, diamond grinding of the full lane width, or removal and replacement of at least 10 feet of pavement; after correction the surface profile is retested to confirm a uniform texture and positive cross‑slope that provides drainage [2].

Cross‑Slope is expected to ensure proper drainage and contribute to ride quality across the various pavement treatments described in this report. For profile milling of asphalt pavement surfaces (SECTION 492), the performance outcome is a smooth milled surface that drains, demonstrated by achieving a smoothness tolerance of 1/8 inch in 12 feet and a transverse slope within 1/8 inch in 12 feet of the design value, verification performed with the milling machine’s automatic grade‑control system. For diamond‑grinded concrete pavements (SECTION 514), the outcome is restoration of the original uniform cross‑slope and provision of a positive drainage angle; uniformity is checked at 100‑foot intervals and any deviation greater than 1/4 inch in 12 feet must be corrected, while ride quality must meet a maximum International Roughness Index (IRI) of 70 in/mile where posted speeds exceed 45 mph or 90 in/mile where speeds are 45 mph or less, measured with the surface‑profile equipment used for longitudinal roughness testing before and after grinding. For the Next Generation Concrete Surface treatment (SECTION 509), cross‑slope must stay within a tolerance of no more than 1/8 inch over any 12‑foot length measured perpendicular to the centerline, verification using a 12‑foot straightedge, and ride quality must meet the same IRI limits (70 in/mile for speeds greater than 45 mph, 90 in/mile for speeds of 45 mph or less) measured according to the procedures in Section 507.3(a)‑(b). Under the concrete pavement ride‑quality evaluation (SECTION 507), cross‑slope is expected to provide a uniform texture and positive drainage; performance is evaluated through ride‑quality criteria that prohibit ridges or valleys exceeding 1/8 inch on the transverse profile and consider any bump greater than 1/4 inch defective, while smoothness verification requires that the measured roughness index (MRI) does not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile on at least 90 % of the 528‑foot lots for each lane and that the average verification MRI does not exceed the average acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile for all lots tested in each lane. For asphalt pavement (SECTION 404), cross‑slope contributes to a ride‑quality pavement meeting acceptance limits; no individual bump may exceed 3/16 inch when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge, ridges or valleys between adjacent lots must be less than 1/8 inch on the transverse profile, and the MRI for at least 90 % of the 528‑foot lots must not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile, with the average verification MRI also limited to that difference; verification uses an inertial lightweight profiler for MRI values together with straightedge testing of bumps and transverse‑profile checks, performed on at least one day covering a minimum of 10 % of lane miles.
