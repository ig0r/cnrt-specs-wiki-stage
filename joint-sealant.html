Joint Sealant

Part of: Joint Sealing is-subtopic-of
Joint Sealant is a topic under Joint Sealing.
sealant
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Joint sealant is a specially formulated material placed in prepared reservoirs of pavement joints and cracks to create a watertight barrier while accommodating the joint’s thermal‑ and load‑induced movements. It is installed only after thorough cleaning and drying of the joint faces, often as a rubberized, silicone or hot‑pour polymer that must be heated within a manufacturer‑specified range (typically 265 °F–320 °F for hot asphalt binders) and applied when ambient temperature is at least 40 °F, with backer rods and bond‑breaker tape used to achieve the required shape factor and depth‑to‑width ratio. The sealant’s performance is defined by a set of ASTM‑based physical properties—including maximum tensile stress at 150 % elongation, minimum side‑wall bearing pressure of 4 psi, ozone and ultraviolet resistance, and serviceability from –20 °F to +140 °F—ensuring durability against water, chlorides and incompressible material infiltration. [1][2]

Material purpose and applicability

Joint sealant is a material applied to pavement joints and cracks to form a watertight barrier while permitting the joint to move with temperature and load changes. In concrete pavement restoration, the sealant is placed after diamond grinding; the joints are cleaned and the sealant is installed so that a proper shape factor for the sealant reservoir is achieved, which helps prevent damage to the sealant and maintains joint integrity, and the cleaning and sealing must be performed after diamond grinding to obtain the proper shape factor. For Portland cement concrete pavements that are being patched but not overlaid, the specification calls for a rubberized joint‑sealing material applied to clean joints and cracks; this seal creates a barrier that prevents water, chlorides, and incompressible materials from infiltrating the pavement structure, thereby improving performance and extending service life. In cement concrete pavements, hot‑pour, silicone or neoprene sealants are placed in a joint reservoir after the joint has been thoroughly cleaned and prepared, including media blasting of sidewalls; a backing material is placed to the proper depth and a tape bond breaker is installed on the bottom of the reservoir without extending more than 1/8 inch up the face, and sealing must be completed before any pavement section is opened to construction equipment or traffic. When a bonded concrete overlay is applied over an existing cement‑concrete pavement, a sealant reservoir is cut across transverse and longitudinal joints, the longitudinal joint being sawed 1/4 inch wide to the full depth of the overlay plus 1/2 inch, flushed, backed, and filled with an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant; joint faces must be protected from curing compound or cleaned thoroughly before sealant installation. For a bonded concrete overlay over an asphalt‑surfaced pavement, the same type of approved hot‑pour or silicone material is applied after the joints have been cut and the faces either protected from curing compound or thoroughly cleaned, in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. In Superpave mixture designs, joint sealant consists of hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder that is heated and maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F and placed only on clean, dry joints in an approximately 4‑inch wide by 1/16‑inch thick strip; placement is not permitted when the air temperature is below 40 °F unless the Representative allows it. Notched wedge joints are sealed with hot‑asphalt material of the class and type designated for the wearing course, or optionally an emulsified asphalt such as PennDOT Material Class TACK, after cleaning and removal of foreign material with at least 6 inches on each side so that residual asphalt completely fills surface voids and provides a watertight joint. Ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses use hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement applied to longitudinal joints in a straight band about 4 inches wide (±1 inch) and 1/16 inch thick (±1/32 inch), heated between 265 °F and 320 °F, placed only on clean, dry joints, and installed at least 24 hours before paving to close openings. Joint rehabilitation involves constructing a new sealant reservoir around an existing concrete joint after spall repairs, positioning a tape bond breaker on the bottom of the reservoir, and applying sealant as specified in Section 501.3(n) to close the opening and isolate the joint from water and contaminants; this practice revitalizes transverse contraction, construction, or expansion joints by providing effective joint seals. During dowel retrofit, a caulking compound is placed into the sides and bottom of a transverse joint or crack after saw cutting to close the joint, prevent concrete patching material from entering, and maintain the integrity of the transverse contraction joint across the lane width. Asphalt joint and crack sealing employs an asphalt‑based sealant listed in Bulletin 15 or 41; the material is heated within the manufacturer’s safe temperature range and applied to joints and cracks that are between 1/4 inch and 1 inch wide after cleaning and drying, placed level with the pavement surface and wiped flush so that a thin film of about 1/32‑to‑1/16 inch remains, thereby creating a watertight barrier; placement is prohibited when air temperature is below 40 °F or above 90 °F unless written approval is obtained. The specification also defines joint sealing material more broadly to include low‑modulus silicone, rubberized compounds (ASTM D6690 Types I, II, IV), preformed neoprene compression seals, closed‑cell polyethylene fillers, polyurethane foam fillers, and asphalt‑rubber sealing compound, all of which must satisfy physical requirements such as a maximum tensile stress at 150 % elongation after a 7‑day cure at 77 °F ± 3 °F and 45–55 % relative humidity, an extrusion rate measured with a 1/8‑inch orifice caulking gun at 90 psi, ozone and ultraviolet resistance (ASTM C793), a tack‑free time not exceeding the specified minutes, functional performance from –20 °F to +140 °F, and a minimum side‑wall bearing pressure of 4 psi at 85 % nominal width. In concrete pavement cross‑stitching, the sealant is placed into routed longitudinal cracks or joints after cleaning; the crack is routed to a width of 3/4 inch and a depth of 1 inch and then filled to a level 1/8‑to‑1/4 inch below the surface, with placement prohibited when ambient or surface temperature is below 40 °F. Pressure‑relief joints in rigid pavements receive sealant or lubricant adhesive applied to cleaned vertical faces after saw cutting, providing a protective coating on the joint surfaces. Continuously reinforced concrete pavement patching requires sealant placement in a reservoir at the interface between existing rigid pavement and a full‑depth concrete patch after partial depth saw‑cuts and installation of load transfer units, sealing the joint to create a continuous connection between old pavement and new patch. Composition and characteristics

The joint sealant called for in concrete pavement repairs is a rubberized material, which may be supplied as a pure rubberized joint‑sealing product or as a rubberized/asphalt blend, and it is installed only after the joint has been thoroughly cleaned; the material is placed into a purpose‑formed reservoir that is commonly a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch cavity whose shape factor must satisfy the applicable specification sections. [1] According to the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, joint sealants may be based on a low‑modulus non‑sag silicone formulated as a one‑part, non‑acid‑curing system. [2] The silicone sealant must exhibit a maximum tensile stress at 150 % elongation measured in accordance with ASTM D412 (Die C) after a seven‑day cure at 77 °F ± 3 °F and 45–55 % relative humidity, a minimum elongation at maximum tensile strength also per ASTM D412, an extrusion rate tested through a 1/8‑inch orifice caulking gun at 90 psi, a specific gravity determined by ASTM D792, Shore “A” durometer hardness as specified in ASTM D2240 after the same cure, ozone and UV resistance per ASTM C793, flow characteristics per ASTM C639‑Type II, a bond to cement mortar of at least the test value described, a tack‑free time not exceeding the limit set by ASTM C679, and movement capability and adhesion as defined in ASTM C719. [2] Rubberized sealants are required to conform to ASTM D6690 Type II or IV for asphalt joints (or Type I where the specification calls for it). [2] Neoprene compression‑strip seals must be free of pin holes, operate over a temperature range from –20 °F to +140 °F, meet dimensional tolerances of no more than 1/8 inch misalignment and specified width/height allowances, provide a minimum side‑wall bearing pressure of 4 psi at 85 % nominal width, achieve the prescribed seal recovery, and may not be elongated more than 3 % or compressed longitudinally more than 2 %. [2] Closed‑cell polyethylene filler seals are limited to a compressive load not less than 5 psi for 25 % compression and not greater than 30 psi for 85 % compression. [2] Flexible polyurethane foam fillers must have a density of 6.0–10.0 lb/ft³, recover at least 95 % after 60 seconds, and absorb no more than 30 % of their volume in water. [2] Asphalt‑rubber mixes used as joint sealants are defined as rubberized sealant that must be placed into longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks that are 1/4‑inch and wider, with over‑band thickness and width limited by the tolerances set out in Section 469. [2] For concrete pavements the joint sealant may also be a hot‑pour polymeric or silicone material applied as a flowable fill, a preformed neoprene strip bonded with a manufacturer‑specified adhesive, or a silicone sealant placed directly into the joint; installation requires a prepared joint reservoir that may include backer rod and bond‑breaker tape limited to an extension of 1/8 inch. [2] Hot‑pour sealants must be placed when air temperature is at least 40 °F, require indirect double‑boiler heating with controlled temperature and agitation, and the poured material is filled to the level shown on the Standard Drawings. [2] For surface‑course longitudinal joints the specified sealant is hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder applied as a continuous band; the binder must be heated and maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F, may be placed only on joints that are clean, dry and free of loose material, and placement is prohibited when air temperature is below 40 °F unless the Representative permits it. [2] The asphalt band is applied with a pressure wand or nozzle to produce a band approximately 4 inches ± 1 inch wide and a thickness of about 1/16 inch ± 1/32 inch, centered within 1 inch of the joint. [2] For notched wedge joints the sealant may be hot asphalt material matching the class and type specified for the wearing course or an emulsified asphalt that meets PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT; it is applied as a thin coating to fill surface voids, extending at least 6 inches on each side of the joint, with excess material removed and optionally covered with acceptable dry sand. [2] In flexible pavements the joint sealant is an asphalt‑based material listed in Bulletin 15 or 41; it must be heated only within the safe heating temperature range and recommended pouring temperatures shown on the manufacturer’s label, batches may not be held at pouring temperature for more than six hours, and for joints and cracks between ¼‑inch and 1 inch wide the sealant is placed level with the pavement and then wiped flush so that a continuous film remains about 1/32‑inch to 1/16‑inch thick and extends 1 to 3 inches beyond the joint; placement is prohibited when ambient air temperature is below 40 °F or above 90 °F unless written approval is obtained. [2] Joint sealant installation requires that the joint be cleaned to a minimum depth of 1½ inches, all existing sealing material removed, and the faces be dry; a backer rod 25‑35 % larger in diameter than the opening is placed so its top sits one to two times the joint width plus ¼ inch below the pavement surface, and the sealant itself is filled to a level 1/8‑¼ inch below the pavement surface, with placement permitted only when ambient air and pavement temperatures are 40 °F or greater. [2] When an elastomeric gasket is used as joint sealant it may be a rubber or neoprene compound conforming to ASTM C443 for rubber gaskets and ASTM C361 for neoprene gaskets, or a pre‑formed flexible sealant composed of butyl rubber; under ASTM C990 the butyl‑rubber sealant must satisfy a Cleveland Open Cup flash point minimum of 350 °F and a fire point minimum of 375 °F. [2]

Placement is also limited to air temperatures between 40 °F and 90 °F unless written approval is obtained, indicating that temperature extremes can impair the sealant’s ability to form a proper film and bond. Key material properties

In Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements (Slip‑Form Paving Method), joint sealants must retain usable characteristics within a defined temperature envelope; they may not be placed when the air is less than 40 °F and the material must be heated only within the manufacturer‑specified safe heating range, with any batch kept at pouring temperature for no more than 6 hours. The mechanical deformation of the sealant in this context is limited to an elongation of no more than 3 % and a longitudinal compression of no more than 2 %, which provides dimensional stability during service. In Section 413—Superpave Mixture Design, the joint sealant must be kept hot enough to stay fluid, specifically between 265 °F and 320 °F, and it may not be placed when ambient air temperature is below 40 °F unless an exception is granted; the applied film must form a continuous band about 4 inches wide (±1 inch) and a thickness of roughly 1/16 inch (±1/32 inch), and the joint surface must be clean, dry, and free of loose material before application. For notched wedge joints in the same specification, the sealant must be compatible with the pavement by using hot asphalt material of the class and type designated for the wearing course, may also be applied as emulsified asphalt when appropriate, and must be placed a minimum of 6 inches on each side of the joint to ensure sufficient bulk; traffic shall not be permitted until the newly sealed area has cooled uniformly to a temperature of 140 °F or less. In Section 489—Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course, the hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement sealant is required to be maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F, shall not be placed when air temperature is below 40 °F, and must be applied in a straight and consistent width band of 4 inches ±1 inch and thickness of 1/16 inch ±1/32 inch on clean, dry surfaces; these temperature limits and dimensional tolerances are the primary thermal and physical properties governing joint‑seal performance. In Section 469—Asphalt Joint and Crack Sealing, sealant placement is further limited by ambient conditions such that it shall not be placed when air temperature is below 40 °F or above 90 °F, and after filling a joint the sealant should be wiped flush to leave a thin film from 1/32 inch to 1/16 inch thick and from 1 inch to 3 inches wide, thereby controlling the physical thickness of the cured film. In Section 705.4—Joint Sealing Material, performance of silicone joint sealing material is governed by a suite of mechanical, chemical and thermal characteristics; after a 7‑day cure at 77 °F ± 3 °F and 45–55 % relative humidity the tensile stress at 150 % elongation (ASTM D412, Die C) must not exceed the specified maximum value while the elongation at maximum tensile strength must meet its minimum percentage, extrusion rate shall not fall below the prescribed grams‑per‑minute over the temperature range 0 °F to 100 °F when tested with a 1/8‑inch orifice caulking gun at 90 psi, and the material must function properly between –20 °F and +140 °F. Pavement seals must provide a minimum side‑wall bearing pressure of 4 psi at 85 % nominal width, and compressive strength limits are required to be no less than 5 psi for 25 % compression and not greater than 30 psi for 85 % compression; polyurethane foam fillers used with joint sealants must have a density between 6.0 and 10.0 lb/in³, water absorption not exceeding 30 % by volume, and must recover at least 95 % after 60 seconds. In Section 705.5(b) for gaskets, the butyl‑rubber preformed flexible joint sealant must remain elastomeric, possess a minimum flash point of 350 °F (C.O.C.) and a fire point of 375 °F (C.O.C.), thereby satisfying the chemical resistance requirements set forth in ASTM C990. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 276–346), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 687–690)

In Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, joint sealant is classified by type as hot‑pour, silicone, or preformed neoprene and must be installed in accordance with the manufacturer’s temperature and handling recommendations; before placement a QC wipe test per PTM No. 643 is performed in the presence of the Representative to verify that joint faces are free of dirt, dust, and debris, with two locations tested on each of the first three saw‑cut joints daily, and for preformed neoprene seals the seal’s deformation is limited to no more than 3 % elongation and no more than 2 % longitudinal compression, any seal exceeding those limits must be replaced. In Section 413—Superpave Mixture Design, the joint sealant used for longitudinal surface‑course joints is hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder that must be heated and maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F before application; it is applied with a pressure wand or nozzle to produce a straight, consistent band whose width is 4 inches ± 1 inch and thickness is 1/16 inch ± 1/32 inch, centered within 1 inch of the joint on a clean, dry surface free of loose material, and these dimensional tolerances together with the temperature range constitute the measurable properties by which the sealant’s performance is characterized. In Section 489—Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course, the same hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement must be kept between 265 °F and 320 °F and may not be placed when the air temperature is below 40 °F unless the Representative allows an exception; the required straight band of 4 inches wide with a tolerance of ±1 inch, thickness of 1/16 inch with a tolerance of ±1/32 inch, centered within 1 inch of the joint again serves as the measured properties used to characterize the sealant. In Section 705—Joint Material, silicone joint sealing material properties are measured after a seven‑day cure at 77 °F ± 3 °F and 45 %–55 % relative humidity by a series of ASTM tests that include tensile stress at 150 % elongation reported in pounds per square inch (maximum) per ASTM D412 Die C, elongation at maximum tensile strength, specific gravity per ASTM D792 Method A, durometer hardness Shore A per ASTM D2240, and ozone and ultraviolet resistance per ASTM C793; extrusion rate is determined in grams per minute over the temperature range 0 °F to 100 °F using an air‑powered caulking gun with a 1/8‑inch orifice at 90 pounds per square inch, flow is evaluated by ASTM C639‑Type II and tack‑free time is limited to minutes maximum according to ASTM C679; adhesion performance is characterized by bond strength to cement mortar measured in pounds per square inch (minimum) following the AASHTO T 132 procedure with molded briquets cured 28 days, oven‑dried, bonded with approximately 10 mils of sealant and tested in tension at a loading rate of 0.3 inch per minute, while movement capability and adhesion are further assessed by ASTM C719. In the same section, rubberized joint sealing material is classified by ASTM D6690 as Type I, II or IV depending on the application; preformed neoprene seals must meet dimensional tolerances, function between –20 °F and +140 °F, exhibit a side‑wall bearing pressure of at least 4 pounds per square inch at 85 % nominal width, and their movement range is measured per ASTM D2628 and D3542 with classification rounded down to the nearest one‑half‑inch increment; preformed closed‑cell polyethylene filler compressive strength is defined by loads required to compress specimens 25 % (minimum 5 pounds per square inch) and 85 % (maximum 30 pounds per square inch) per ASTM D7174 Type I, while polyurethane foam filler properties such as density (6.0‑10.0 pounds per cubic inch), compressive deflection, required pressures for 25 % and 65 % compression, recovery of at least 95 % after 60 seconds, and water absorption not exceeding 30 % volume are obtained from skinless samples using ASTM D7174. In Section 705—Gaskets and Waterstops, sealant types are classified by the specific ASTM test methods that define their performance criteria: elastomeric gaskets are characterized by ASTM C443 for rubber and ASTM C361 for neoprene; butyl‑rubber preformed flexible joint sealants are referenced to ASTM C990 and must meet a minimum flash point of F 350 and a fire point of F 375 as measured by the Cleveland Open Cup test; resilient connectors used at inlets, utility holes, or pipe interfaces are identified by the applicable system standards—ASTM C1478 for storm‑water applications and ASTM C923 for sanitary sewer systems. In Section 489—Preparation of Existing Surface, all longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks that are 1/4‑inch and wider must be sealed at least 24 hours before paving with the rubberized sealant specified in Section 469, the seal’s over‑band is then kept as thin and narrow as practicable while ensuring it does not exceed the over‑band tolerances set out in that same section; thus the principal properties of the joint sealant—crack width eligibility, over‑band thickness and width—are characterized simply by compliance with those dimensional limits. [2]

Behavior and mechanisms

Under the requirements of Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, joint sealants must be installed on joints that are clean and dry; the faces shall be free of moisture and other contaminants before any bond‑breaker or sealing material is placed. Poured sealant may not be applied when air temperature is less than 40 °F, and hot‑poured materials must be kept within the manufacturer’s safe temperature range and cannot remain at pouring temperature for more than 6 hours. Preformed neoprene seals are limited to a maximum elongation of 3 % and a maximum longitudinal compression of 2 %, which defines the allowable deformation under joint movement, and all sealing operations follow these limits to ensure performance under loading, moisture, and temperature service conditions. In Section 413—Superpave Mixture Design, the sealant applied at other joints must be placed so that residual asphalt completely fills surface voids and provides a watertight joint, thereby preventing moisture infiltration. Vehicular traffic or loads shall not be allowed on newly compacted courses for 24 hours or until the course uniformly cools to a temperature of 140 °F or less; this restriction governs behavior under loading and temperature changes. No explicit provisions are given in this section for chemical exposure or other service conditions. Section 705—Joint Material specifies that silicone joint sealing material retains elasticity after a seven‑day cure at 77 °F and 45–55 % relative humidity, providing tensile stress at 150 % elongation and offering ozone and ultraviolet resistance per ASTM C793; its movement capability and adhesion are evaluated by ASTM C719. Preformed neoprene compression seals shall function properly across the service temperature range of –20 °F to +140 °F and must develop at least 4 psi side‑wall bearing pressure when compressed to 85 % of nominal width, ensuring load‑bearing performance in joints. The preformed closed‑cell polyethylene filler must resist compression loads no less than 5 psi for a 25 % reduction in thickness and no greater than 30 psi for an 85 % reduction, indicating capacity to sustain loading without excessive deformation. Polyurethane foam joint filler is limited to a maximum water absorption of 30 % by volume and shall recover at least 95 % of its original shape after 60 seconds of relaxation, demonstrating resistance to moisture ingress and rapid elastic rebound under service conditions. [2]

In slip‑form concrete pavements the behavior of a joint sealant is first governed by physical preparation—both faces of the joint must be thoroughly cleaned of scale, dirt, curing compound and any foreign material and then media‑blasted to roughen the sidewalls so that the sealant can bond properly; chemically, hot‑poured or silicone sealants require an ambient air temperature of at least 40 °F and must be kept within the manufacturer’s recommended pouring temperature range to achieve proper cure; mechanically, an oversized backer rod is placed on the bottom of the joint and any tape bond‑breaker may not extend more than 1/8 inch up the reservoir face, with the sealant filled to the level shown on the drawings. For plant‑mixed asphalt courses the performance of a joint sealant is governed primarily by thermal and material flow conditions, chemical adhesion of the hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder, and mechanical delivery and compaction; the binder must be heated and maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F so that its viscosity allows it to form a continuous band, placement is prohibited when ambient temperature falls below 40 °F unless an exception is granted, the joint surface must be clean, dry and free of debris before application, a pressure applicator with a wand or nozzle deposits the sealant in a straight band 4 inches ±1 inch wide and 1/16 inch ±1/32 inch thick centered within 1 inch of the joint, after which roller passes embed the sealant to ensure proper mechanical bonding. In notched wedge joints the physical need is to completely fill surface voids and create a watertight barrier, the chemical nature of the sealing material may be hot asphalt of the designated class or an emulsified asphalt such as PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT, and mechanical adhesion is achieved through proper surface preparation and temperature control; the contractor cleans and removes harmful material, evenly applies the sealant at least 6 inches on each side of the joint, controls the application rate so that residual asphalt fully fills voids, may paint a thin coating of the appropriate PG‑Binder on the joint face before fresh mixture is placed, and traffic is not allowed until the surface cools uniformly to 140 °F or less. The ultra‑thin bonded wearing course specifies that thermal control of the hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement must be maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F to achieve proper viscosity for flow into the joint, ambient temperature limits prohibit placement when air temperature falls below 40 °F unless otherwise allowed by the Representative, and mechanical factors include applying the sealant with a pressure applicator that delivers a consistent band about 4 inches wide (±1 inch) and roughly 1/16 inch thick (±1/32 inch) after the joint surface has been cleaned, dried and cleared of loose material. For asphalt joint and crack sealing the sealant’s performance is governed primarily by thermal and surface‑condition controls: the material must be heated only within the safe heating temperature range and the pouring temperatures indicated on the manufacturer’s label, batches may not remain at that temperature for more than six hours, the crack or joint must be cleaned, dried and free of loose debris, a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi (or a hot lance) is required to remove moisture, ambient air temperature must be between 40 °F and 90 °F, and the sealant is finished level with the pavement leaving a thin film from 1/32 inch to 1/16 inch thick. Silicone joint sealants are governed by a combination of physical, chemical and mechanical mechanisms: physically their low modulus, non‑sag characteristics, tensile stress at 150 % elongation, durometer hardness, specific gravity and flow properties define the ability to deform without sagging; chemically the non‑acid‑curing one‑part formulation provides ozone and ultraviolet resistance (ASTM C793) and enables bonding to cement mortar after priming; mechanically movement capability and adhesion are measured by ASTM C719, side‑wall bearing pressure of a minimum 4 psi at 85 % nominal width is required for pavement seals, and compressive strength limits of a minimum load not less than 5 psi to achieve 25 % compression and a maximum load not greater than 30 psi for 85 % compression control how the seal accommodates joint movement and loads. In concrete pavement cross‑stitching the sealant’s performance is governed by physical conditions such as maintaining ambient or pavement surface temperatures above 40 °F and providing a routed joint width of 3/4 inch and depth of 1 inch; chemically the epoxy bonding compound must be injected into clean, dry holes and allowed to cure according to the manufacturer’s recommendations; mechanically the tie bar is rotated three to five times during insertion to purge air voids and the sealant is filled to 1/8‑to‑1/4 inch below the pavement surface. Along curbs the joint sealant performance is controlled physically by filling all surface voids, chemically by the type of asphalt used—either the hot asphalt material listed in Table A or an allowed emulsified asphalt (PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT)—and mechanically by applying the material evenly with a minimum width of 12 inches from the curb and controlling the application rate so that residual asphalt completely fills voids, followed by immediate edge work to remove ragged edges. Durability and deterioration

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, joint sealants must be placed after diamond‑grinding because grinding can destroy the sealant and prevent the reservoir from achieving the required shape factor [1]. Visual observation of durability problems—including aggregate problems and frost damage—may also affect sealant performance [1]. In the same manual, joint sealants become vulnerable as soon as joints begin to show distress, so resealing should be performed when the joints are just beginning to show distress; otherwise more extensive repairs will be necessary the longer distressed joints are left unattended [1]. When a seal fails it permits water, chlorides and incompressible materials to infiltrate the pavement structure, accelerating deterioration, and future infiltration of these agents must be prevented [1]. Loss of seal integrity is often manifested by joint performance problems such as faulting or poor load transfer, which may require additional remedial actions [1]. For slip‑form paving (Section 501), the sealant must not be placed when the air temperature is less than 40 °F, and a hot‑poured batch may not be kept at pouring temperature for more than 6 hours [2]. Likewise, for cross‑stitching work (Section 528) sealing material shall not be placed if either ambient air temperature or pavement surface temperature is below 40 °F because low temperatures can inhibit proper bonding [2]. Thorough cleaning of the joint faces is required; Section 501 calls for removal of all scale, dirt, curing compound, temporary sealant and other foreign material from both joint faces with a QC wipe test to verify cleanliness, while Section 545 mandates protection of joint faces from curing compound or thorough cleaning prior to sealant installation, and Section 528 requires the longitudinal crack or joint to be cleaned and kept dry immediately before sealant placement [2]. Mechanical restraint specifications include using oversized backer rods whose width is plus 50 % of the joint width and tape bond breakers that may not extend more than 1/8 inch up the reservoir face (Section 501), and Section 545 requires that the joint backing or filling material remain in place during paving and that saw‑cut widths be at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack to avoid debonding [2]. Pre‑formed neoprene or other seals are limited to elongation no greater than 3 % and longitudinal compression no greater than 2 %; any seal exceeding those limits must be replaced (Section 501) [2]. Material performance criteria in Section 705 specify that silicone sealants must provide ozone and ultraviolet resistance, rubberized and neoprene seals shall function properly at temperatures between –20 °F and +140 °F, polyurethane foam fillers may not absorb more than 30 % of their volume as water, and they must meet compressive strength limits such that the load required to compress a specimen by 25 % is not less than 5 psi and the load to compress it by 85 % does not exceed 30 psi [2]. Lubricant adhesive shall not be used more than nine months after its date of manufacture (Section 705) [2]. After placement, traffic may not be allowed until the anchoring material has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations (Section 528), and excess sealant spreading onto the pavement surface must be avoided to preserve durability (Section 528) [2].

Susceptibility of joint sealant to deterioration increases when water, chlorides, or incompressible materials can infiltrate the pavement structure, which occurs if drainage is not functional or the subgrade is unstable; conversely, early cleaning and resealing of joints that are just beginning to show distress with the specified rubberized sealant, together with a stable subgrade and a functional drainage system, helps prevent such infiltration and thus reduces deterioration. For poured sealants, durability declines when ambient air temperature is below 40 °F; Section 501.3(n) requires that poured material not be placed unless the temperature is at least 40 °F and that a hot‑poured batch shall not remain at pouring temperature for more than 6 hours, and strains greater than 3 % elongation or 2 % compression require replacement. Hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder sealants perform better when the binder is heated and maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F, are applied only when ambient air temperature is not below 40 °F, and are installed as a straight band about 4 inches wide (±1 inch) and 1/16 inch thick (±1/32 inch). Using an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations reduces deterioration provided joint faces are free of curing compound and other contaminants before installation, whereas exposure of joint faces to curing compound or standing water can increase susceptibility. When sealing notched wedge joints, Section 413 (1.c) requires cleaning and removal of harmful material prior to placement, use of a hot‑asphalt material of the class and type designated for the wearing course (or an approved PennDOT Class TACK or NTT/CNTT emulsified asphalt), and application of at least 6 inches of sealant on each side while controlling the rate so that residual asphalt completely fills surface voids, thereby creating a watertight joint; failure to clean contaminants, applying less than the required thickness, or allowing voids permits water infiltration and increases likelihood of breakdown. Section 489.3 requires that hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement be heated and maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F, applied as a band about 4 inches wide (±1 inch) and 1/16 inch thick (±1/32 inch), placed only on joints that are clean, dry and free of loose material or debris, not be placed when air temperature is below 40 °F unless an exception is granted, and states that minimizing transverse joints, orienting them perpendicular to traffic, and compacting the joint area further reduce the likelihood of cracking or loss of adhesion. Section 469.3 notes that exposure to ambient air temperatures below 40 °F or above 90 °F, or overheating the crack with a hot compressed‑air lance, increases susceptibility to failure, whereas proper surface preparation—cleaning, drying, and removing debris—helps keep the sealant film thin (1/32 inch to 1/16 inch) and adherent. Section 705.4 specifies material characteristics that improve durability, including adequate ozone and ultraviolet resistance per ASTM C793, a minimum shelf life from the date of manufacture, functional performance across the temperature envelope of –20 °F to +140 °F, water absorption not exceeding 30 % by volume for foam fillers, and meeting the required movement capability and adhesion per ASTM C719; exposure beyond the –20 °F to +140 °F range can accelerate deterioration. Section 521.3 states that joint sealants are less prone to deterioration when joint faces are cleaned to a minimum depth of 1½ in., kept dry, installed only if ambient air and pavement temperatures are 40 °F or greater, and when a backer rod that is 25 % to 35 % larger in diameter than the joint opening is positioned with its top one to two times the joint width plus ¼ inch below the pavement surface, creating the recommended recessed sealant depth‑to‑width ratio. Sections 528.3 and 590.3 each require that sealing material not be placed if either ambient air temperature or pavement surface temperature is below 40 °F because curing may be compromised, and that faces of the joint must be thoroughly clean and dry before sealant placement, with any oil, moisture, grease, fuel or other contaminants removed; when surfaces are free of such contaminants adhesion is not interfered with and the likelihood of deterioration is reduced. Section 489.3 (as modified) also requires use of rubberized sealant, minimization of the sealant over‑band thickness and width, and prohibition on exceeding Section 469 over‑band tolerances; keeping the over‑band thin and within those limits reduces deterioration, and following any manufacturer’s recommendation for coating the joint sides supports durability. Material interactions and compatibility

Joint sealant achieves its performance on concrete pavements by forming a mechanical bond to the prepared joint faces; the sidewalls of the joint reservoir are media‑blasted to clean and roughen the surface, and a wipe test must confirm freedom from dirt, dust, or moisture before any bond‑breaker tape, backer rod, or sealant material is placed. Immediately before placing the bond‑breaker or joint sealing material, the joint must be free of moisture and other contaminants. In bonded concrete overlay work, a backing or filling material that supports the sealant is required to be installed to within ±1/4 inch of the pavement surface and must remain in place so it cannot be dislodged by the paving operation; joint faces must be protected from exposure to curing compound or cleaned thoroughly prior to sealant installation, and after sawing any slurry must be removed by flushing the joint with water. When the sealant is applied as hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder, it must be placed only when the pavement surface at the joint is clean, dry and free of loose material; the binder temperature must be maintained between 265°F and 320°F, and placement is prohibited when ambient air temperature falls below 40°F unless an exception is granted. A pressure applicator delivers a continuous band about 4 inches +/- 1 inch wide with a thickness of 1/16 inch +/- 1/32 inch, ensuring adequate coverage and adhesion to the surrounding binder. For notched wedge joints the sealant may be hot asphalt material that matches the class and type of the wearing course or emulsified asphalt; it must be spread at least 6 inches on each side of the joint after the joint face has been cleaned and, when required, primed with a thin coating of the same PG‑binder used in the pavement. The application rate is controlled so that residual asphalt completely fills surface voids, creating a watertight seal; additional coats may be placed if needed, and excess material is removed before covering the joint with a light layer of dry sand. During joint rehabilitation a tape bond‑breaker is positioned on the bottom of the sealant reservoir, limiting the sealant’s contact with the substrate and may not extend up the reservoir face more than 1/8 inch; all vertical faces are media‑blasted and then cleaned with a compressed‑air stream measuring 100 pounds per square inch at the source to remove oil, moisture, and other contaminants. Silicone joint sealing material interacts with cement mortar through an adhesive tensile bond; the test procedure bonds two halves of a 28‑day cured cement‑mortar briquet with approximately 10 mils of silicone, cures the assembly for seven days at 77°F ± 3°F and 45–55 % relative humidity, and then pulls it in tension to achieve at least the minimum tensile bond strength specified. For longitudinal cracks or joints placement is prohibited when either ambient air temperature or pavement surface temperature is below 40°F; the faces must be thoroughly clean and dry to the satisfaction of the Representative, and the sealant is filled to a level about 1/8 inch to 1/4 inch below the pavement surface. Influence of production and placement

For slip‑form paving (Section 501) the sealant must be heated and kept within the manufacturer’s safe temperature range, placed at the recommended pouring temperature, and no single batch may remain at that temperature for more than 6 hours; placement is prohibited when air temperature is less than 40 °F, and the joint reservoir must be filled to the level shown on the Standard Drawings with no overflow onto the pavement surface. Section 469 requires heating of the sealant within the safe heating temperature range and the manufacturer‑specified pouring temperature, batches must not be held at that temperature for more than six hours, placement is prohibited when ambient air temperature is below 40 °F or above 90 °F unless written approval is obtained, and surface preparation must include cleaning, drying, and removal of debris with a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi or a hot lance without overheating the surrounding pavement. In Superpave longitudinal joints (Section 413) the joint face must first be painted with a uniform coating of the PG‑Binder; fresh mix must overlap the existing lane by about 1 to 1½ inches and the uncompacted material must be kept at least a quarter inch higher per inch of depth to allow full compaction; during compaction the roller drum should stay roughly 18 inches from the joint on the first forward pass and later passes should overlap the joint 2 to 6 inches, with the joint receiving at least as many roller passes as the rest of the mat. The sealant used in that context must be hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder kept between 265 °F and 320 °F, applied only when air temperature is not below 40 °F, onto a clean, dry joint surface, and delivered in a straight band about 4 inches ± 1 inch wide and 1/16 inch ± 1/32 inch thick. For notched wedge joints (Section 413 1.c) the contractor must clean away loose material and then evenly apply hot or emulsified asphalt sealant at least six inches on each side of the joint so that the residual asphalt completely fills surface voids; if one pass does not achieve full fill, two or more applications may be used, after which excess material is removed and the sealed area is immediately covered with a light layer of acceptable dry sand. After placement in an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course (Section 489) the material must be rolled before its temperature falls below 185 °F using a minimum of two passes with a steel double‑drum asphalt roller having a mass of not less than 8 tons; rollers are kept moving and compacted in static mode except where vibration may be necessary at joints, and finishing steps include removing excess sealant, resealing any inconsistently covered areas, and restoring pavement markings. In bonded concrete overlay work (Section 540) the curing membrane covering a joint must be removed no later than one‑half hour before the joint is cut, and the freshly exposed joint faces must be kept free of or cleaned of curing compound prior to applying the hot‑pour or silicone sealant so that the sealant can bond as intended. Dowel retrofit joints (Section 527) require strict placement tolerances—vertical tilt or horizontal skew may not exceed 0.25 inch at each end, longitudinal translation is limited to 1 inch, and horizontal translation also stays within 1 inch; chairs must provide at least a ½‑inch clearance and caps maintain a ¼‑inch gap, after which vibration must be applied to thoroughly consolidate the concrete in the slot and around the dowel bar, and the final surface is finished to match the existing pavement profile before the cured concrete develops its intended strength. For silicone joint sealing material (Section 705) extrusion with an air‑powered caulking gun at 90 psi must achieve a minimum grams‑per‑minute rate; tensile stress at 150 % elongation and durometer hardness are evaluated after a seven‑day cure at 77 °F ± 3 °F and 45–55 % relative humidity, and the sealant must become tack‑free within the maximum minutes specified; inadequate temperature or humidity can reduce strength and delay service readiness. Placement of joint sealant also requires that the joint be clean and dry immediately before application (Sections 521, 528, 590); cleaning may involve removal of debris or a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi measured at the source. The material shall not be placed unless both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are 40 °F or greater; the sealant should be poured so that its surface lies between one‑eighth inch and one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface, creating a recessed profile, and after placement the sealing material must not spread over the pavement surface. Traffic may not resume until the anchoring or curing material has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. [2]

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, the long‑term performance of a joint sealant is most sensitive to how the joint is prepared and sealed during construction; joint cleaning, sealing or rehabilitation must be performed after diamond grinding so that the shape factor of the sealant reservoir is correct and the sealant is not damaged. The manual also identifies construction defects that most affect durability as those that compromise load transfer and the integrity of the joint surface, such as missing tie bars in longitudinal contraction joints, badly spalled joints or interior slab spalls, and faulting or poor load transfer caused by inadequate support below the pavement; these conditions must be corrected before sealing, often requiring reconstruction of a proper sealant reservoir, for example a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch reservoir used for pavement/shoulder joints. In the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, slip‑form paving requires that joint faces be free of scale, dirt, curing compound, temporary sealing material or other foreign matter, must be kept dry, and a QC wipe test per PTM No. 643 must confirm cleanliness before sealant placement; an oversized backer rod whose width is the joint width plus 50 % must be installed to maintain joint geometry, and sealant may not be placed when air temperature is below 40 °F unless an exception is granted. Selection, specification, and improvement

In concrete pavement restoration projects, the joint‑sealant operation must follow diamond grinding so that the sealant reservoir attains its proper shape factor and the material is not damaged. The choice of sealant is further limited by the joint’s condition, type, and intended future treatment; only rubberized joint‑sealing material as prescribed in Publication 408 Sections 512 and 521 may be used when joints are just beginning to show distress and the pavement will not be overlaid, because sealing is not recommended for overlayed pavements. For longitudinal and shoulder joints the reservoir shall be constructed as a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch cavity, while transverse joints must satisfy the shape‑factor requirement set out in Section 521. A stable subgrade and functional drainage are also required to prevent infiltration of water, chlorides, or incompressible materials into the pavement structure. Prior to sealant placement the joint must be clean and dry, and both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature shall be at least 40 °F unless a Representative grants an exception. A backer rod whose diameter is 25 % to 35 % larger than the joint opening shall be installed so that its top sits one to two times the joint width plus ¼ inch below the surface, creating a recessed depth‑to‑width ratio of 1:1 to 2:1, after which the reservoir is filled to a level of ⅛ inch to ¼ inch below the pavement surface. For longitudinal cracks or joints the opening shall be routed to a width of ¾ inch and a depth of 1 inch, cleaned and dried, and sealant may only be applied when ambient and pavement temperatures are not below 40 °F; the material is then filled leaving a recess of ⅛ inch to ¼ inch below the surface and shall not be allowed to spread onto the surrounding pavement. When slip‑form paving is used, joint faces must pass a PTM No. 643 wipe test before sealant placement, poured seals may not be installed when air temperature is less than 40 °F unless permitted, and in colder conditions an alternative such as pre‑formed neoprene or silicone that can be applied without heating shall be used; the sealant type must follow the manufacturer’s recommendations and the tape shall not extend more than ⅛ inch up the joint face. Transverse joints shall have a width between a minimum of ¼ inch and a maximum of ½ inch, longitudinal joints may not exceed ¾ inch, and the sealant must satisfy the requirements of Section 501.3(n); cracks 0.007 inch wide or less are to be sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate after surface cleaning, while wider cracks are addressed by other repair methods. For surface‑course longitudinal joints the sealant shall be hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F, applied only when ambient air temperature is not below 40 °F unless a Representative permits otherwise; the joint must be clean, dry and free of loose material, and the applicator shall deliver a continuous band that is 4 inches ± 1 inch wide and 1⁄16 inch ± 1⁄32 inch thick with its centerline positioned within 1 inch of the joint. In concrete overlays all transverse and longitudinal joints shall be sealed with an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant material in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations and as specified in Section 501.3(n), after joint faces have been protected from curing compound or thoroughly cleaned. Where a new wearing course abuts existing pavement, the sealant shall be either a hot asphalt material of the class and type designated for the wearing course or an emulsified asphalt that meets PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT specifications; it shall be applied evenly to cover at least 6 inches on each side of the joint after all loose or harmful material has been removed, and the application rate shall be controlled so that residual asphalt completely fills surface voids and provides a watertight joint, with additional coats permitted if needed. Sealant material must be obtained from an approved source listed in Bulletin 15 or Bulletin 41; joints and cracks from ¼ inch to 1 inch wide are sealed with asphalt sealant and openings over 1 inch wide require Asphalt Wearing Course 4.75 mm, placement being restricted to ambient air temperatures between 40 °F and 90 °F unless written approval is granted, heating must follow the safe temperature range shown on the manufacturer’s label, surface preparation includes cleaning, drying, debris removal and, when needed, routing a ½‑inch deep reservoir before sealing, and the sealant shall be finished level with the pavement leaving a thin film of 1⁄32 inch to 1⁄16 inch thick and 1 to 3 inches wide. Selection of joint‑sealant material is also governed by performance limits such as maximum tensile stress at 150 % elongation, minimum elongation at peak strength, extrusion rate across the temperature range from 0 °F to 100 °F, durometer hardness after a seven‑day cure, minimum shelf life, resistance to ozone and ultraviolet exposure, flow characteristics, required bond strength to cement mortar, maximum tack‑free time, and defined movement capability and adhesion; for rubberized sealants the applicable ASTM D6690 type (II, IV or I) shall be chosen according to whether the joint is in asphalt pavement or another application. Pre‑formed neoprene seals must be free of pinholes, operate between –20 °F and +140 °F, meet strict dimensional tolerances on width, height and wall misalignment, provide at least a 4‑psi side‑wall bearing pressure at 85 % nominal width, and satisfy movement‑range classifications without negative tolerance. Polyethylene filler units shall compress under loads no less than 5 psi and not exceed 30 psi, while polyurethane foam fillers shall have a density of 6.0–10.0 lb/ft³, achieve specified compressive loads for 25 % and 65 % deflection, recover at least 95 % after relaxation, limit water absorption to 30 % volume, and all lots shall be accompanied by an eCAMMS laboratory report and proper lot marking before use. Elastomeric gaskets must be listed under ASTM C443 for rubber or ASTM C361 for neoprene, pre‑formed flexible butyl‑rubber sealants shall meet ASTM C990 with a Cleveland Open Cup flash point of at least F 350 and a fire point of at least F 375, and the choice for resilient connectors is dictated by system type referencing ASTM C1478 for storm‑water applications and ASTM C923 for sanitary‑sewer applications. Any longitudinal or transverse joint or crack one quarter inch or wider shall be sealed at least 24 hours before paving with the rubberized sealant identified in Section 469, and the over‑band thickness and width shall be minimized and never exceed the tolerances set out in that section. The sealant for surface courses must be a hot‑asphalt material whose class and type appear in Table A, or, if approved by the Inspector‑in‑Charge, an emulsified asphalt meeting PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT; it must completely fill surface voids to provide a watertight joint and be placed as a continuous strip at least 12 inches wide from the curb. In concrete pavement restoration projects the joint cleaning and sealing operation is performed only after diamond grinding, a timing that creates the proper shape factor for the sealant reservoir and protects fresh sealant from damage while also serving as a quality‑control measure to mitigate weaknesses related to improper joint geometry or handling. In plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements rigorous surface preparation is required, including media‑blasting of all vertical faces and thorough flushing of the joint reservoir before installing an oversized backer rod that is sized at the joint width plus 50 % and applying a bond‑breaker lubricant prior to sealant placement. Poured sealant may be placed only when the air temperature is not less than 40 °F and must be maintained within the manufacturer’s recommended heating range, and a QC wipe test per PTM No. 643 is performed immediately before placement and repeated at two locations on each of the first three saw‑cut joints daily; any seal that exceeds 3 % elongation or 2 % compression is replaced at no extra cost. Approved hot‑pour, silicone, or preformed neoprene seals installed with an adhesive lubricant may be used as alternative materials in accordance with manufacturer guidance. For very fine cracks 0.007 inches wide or less in full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slabs, high molecular weight methacrylate mixed according to the manufacturer’s recommendations is poured and squeegeed into the crack, and after approximately 20 minutes a layer of commercial‑quality dry‑blast sand is placed over the cured methacrylate before traffic is allowed. Joint backing or other approved joint‑filling material must be installed to within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface and be capable of resisting displacement during paving; joint faces must be protected from curing compound or cleaned thoroughly prior to sealant installation, and after sawing the joint shall be flushed with water to remove slurry that could impede bonding. ASTM C1583 bond‑strength tests on five random locations per 3,000 sq yd lot provide quality control, where an average below 175 psi or any individual result under 160 psi declares the area defective and triggers additional testing. In longitudinal joints of superpave mixtures performance is enhanced by using hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder as the sealant and strictly controlling its temperature between 265 °F and 320 °F during placement; the sealant shall not be applied when ambient air temperature is below 40 °F unless the Representative permits it, and the joint face must be clean, dry and free of loose material before a uniform coating of PG‑Binder or PG 64S‑22 is painted. The sealant is applied with a pressure applicator that delivers a straight band 4 inches ±1 inch wide and 1/16 inch ±1/32 inch thick, centered within 1 inch of the joint; excess material is removed, inconsistently covered areas are resealed, and pavement markings are replaced if marred. In bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements performance can be further improved by selecting an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant and following the manufacturer’s recommendations, after first protecting joint faces from any curing‑compound residue or thoroughly cleaning them; a comprehensive QC plan that documents surface preparation, timing of sawing and sealant installation reduces the risk of premature failure. For notched wedge joints the use of hot asphalt material that matches the class and type designated for the wearing course, or where appropriate an emulsified asphalt such as PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT, enhances performance; harmful or foreign material must be removed before sealing, the application rate is controlled so residual asphalt completely fills surface voids and provides a watertight joint, two or more coats may be applied if needed, excess material is trimmed, and a light layer of acceptable dry sand is placed over the sealed joint for protection. Joint rehabilitation requires immediate flushing after sawing, placement of bond‑breaker tape on the bottom of the joint reservoir with protrusion limited to 1/8 inch, water blasting at a minimum capacity of 10 gpm at 7,000 psi, and media‑blasting of all vertical faces followed by cleaning with compressed air at 100 psi free of oil, moisture or contaminants; sealant is then placed as called for in Section 501.3(n). In ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses performance is improved by applying hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement within the temperature window of 265 °F to 320 °F, avoiding placement when air temperature is below 40 °F unless otherwise allowed, ensuring joints are clean, dry and free of loose material before sealing, and using a pressure applicator that delivers a straight band 4 inches ±1 inch wide and 1/16 inch ±1/32 inch thick centered within 1 inch; minimizing transverse joints, constructing them perpendicular to traffic and monitoring joint number in a QC plan provide additional control to reduce sealant stress and trigger corrective action when needed. Dowel retrofit work mandates meticulous cleaning of joint surfaces by removing all loose material within 24 hours before concrete placement and completing a final cleaning no later than 30 minutes prior, with an additional quality‑control step of air‑blasting using oil‑ and moisture‑free compressed air at a pressure of at least 100 psi measured at the source; a caulking compound is applied to seal the sides and bottom of any temporary joint material before concrete placement, and the epoxy bonding compound recommended by the manufacturer is used to further secure the dowel‑joint assembly. Asphalt joint and crack sealing requires that sealant material be obtained from a source listed in Bulletin 15 or Bulletin 41, heated only within the safe temperature range indicated on the label and not held at pouring temperature for more than 6 hours before reheating; prior to placement cracks, joints and adjacent pavement surfaces must be cleaned, dried and cleared of debris, and a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi measured at the source (or a hot compressed‑air lance applied without overheating surrounding pavement) shall be used. Sealant may not be placed when air temperature is below 40 °F or above 90 °F, and for joints wider than one inch the specification calls for filling with Asphalt Wearing Course 4.75 mm rather than asphalt sealant. Alternative joint sealing materials that satisfy detailed physical requirements may be used, including low‑modulus non‑sag silicone (nonacid‑curing, one‑part formulation), rubberized sealants conforming to ASTM D6690, preformed neoprene compression seals, closed‑cell polyethylene fillers, polyurethane foam fillers, and asphalt‑rubber sealing compounds; quality control is enforced by limiting any seal lot to no more than 10,000 linear feet for pavement seals (or 3,000 ft for bridge seals), requiring an eCAMMS‑generated laboratory test report that approves or rejects the material before use, and mandating a permanent mark on the top of each seal with line characters at least 0.0625 inch high indicating lot, cure date and type. In transverse joint cleaning and sealing the joint must be cleaned to remove all existing sealing material and foreign debris until a clean concrete surface at least 1½ inches deep is exposed, followed by air‑blasting with compressed air of at least 100 psi measured at the source, free of oil, moisture and contaminants; a backer rod sized 25 % to 35 % larger than the joint opening is placed so its top sits about ¼ inch below the pavement surface, creating a recessed sealant depth‑to‑width ratio of roughly 1:1 to 2:1, and sealant may only be applied when the joint is clean and dry and ambient air and pavement temperatures are 40 °F or greater, with the joint reservoir filled to a level 1/8 inch to ¼ inch below the surface. For concrete pavement cross‑stitching a quality‑control plan must be prepared and approved at least 14 days before work and demonstrated to the Representative; sealing may only occur when ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are not below 40 °F, the joint is routed to a width of ¾ inch and depth of 1 inch, flushed with water, cleaned until completely dry, and the sealant filled so that its level remains 1/8 inch to ¼ inch below the pavement surface. In pressure relief joints the joint is cut four inches wide through the full depth and width of the pavement with all existing concrete removed; immediately before sealant application the vertical faces must be cleaned and verified free of oil, moisture and other contaminants, after which the joint sides are coated with either the sealant itself or a lubricant adhesive in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. Crack cleaning and sealing requires that cracks be thoroughly cleaned immediately before placement, a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi measured at the source should be used for cleaning, sealant may only be placed when both ambient air temperature and pavement temperature are 40 °F or greater, and the sealant reservoir is filled so that its surface ends between 1/8 inch and ¼ inch below the pavement surface. Rubberized sealant must be placed at least 24 hours before paving any longitudinal or transverse joints and cracks one quarter inch wide or larger, as called for in Section 469, and the over‑band thickness and width of the sealant shall be minimized to the practical size and must remain within the tolerances set out in Section 469. The mixture surface may be sealed with a hot asphalt material of the class and type listed in Table A; the Contractor may use emulsified asphalt consisting of PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT instead of hot asphalt binder material if allowed by the Inspector‑in‑Charge, the application rate being controlled so residual asphalt completely fills surface voids and provides a watertight joint along the curb, and the asphalt material is to be evenly applied with a minimum width of 12 inches from the curb. Performance evaluation and implications

In Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, joint‑sealant performance is verified primarily through field quality‑control procedures. A QC wipe test in accordance with PTM No. 643 must be performed in the presence of a Representative immediately before sealant placement to confirm that the joint surface is free of dirt, dust and debris; the same wipe test is repeated at two locations on each of the first three sawcut joints daily and thereafter at every twenty joints. In addition, at least three full‑width pavement joints are sealed as test sections under a QC Plan, with the manufacturer’s representative witnessing the installation and the Representative inspecting those sections before any broader sealing proceeds.

Section 705.4—Joint Sealing Material specifies laboratory evidence through a series of ASTM‑based tests. Tensile stress at 150 % elongation is measured according to ASTM D412 (Die C) after a seven‑day cure at 77 °F ± 3 °F and 45–55 % relative humidity, and the minimum elongation at maximum tensile strength is also required per ASTM D412. Extrusion rate is determined from 0 °F to 100 °F using an air‑powered caulking gun with a 1/8‑inch orifice at 90 psi; specific gravity is obtained by ASTM D792 Method A, and Shore “A” durometer hardness is measured after the same cure. Shelf life is expressed in days from manufacture, ozone and ultraviolet resistance are evaluated per ASTM C793, flow per ASTM C639‑Type II, bond strength to cement mortar per AASHTO T 132, tack‑free time per ASTM C679, and movement capability and adhesion per ASTM C719. Rubberized sealants are evaluated by ASTM D6690 (Types I, II or IV as appropriate) and preformed neoprene seals by ASTM D2628 and D3542 with dimensional tolerances. Acceptance of each lot requires an eCAMMS‑generated laboratory test report that states approval or rejection, and the lot length is limited to 10 000 linear feet for pavement seals (or 3 000 ft for bridge seals); this section does not prescribe any field or long‑term performance tests.

Section 705.5(b)—Gaskets provides additional laboratory evaluation criteria. Elastomeric rubber gaskets are assessed with ASTM C443 and neoprene gaskets with ASTM C361; pre‑formed flexible butyl rubber sealant is tested according to ASTM C990 together with a minimum flash point of 350 °F (Cleveland Open Cup) and a minimum fire point of 375 °F (C.O.C.). Resilient connectors for storm‑water systems are evaluated by ASTM C1478 and those for sanitary‑sewer systems by ASTM C923. No field or long‑term performance evidence is cited in this excerpt. [2]
