Joint Sealant

Part of: Joint Sealing is-subtopic-of
Joint Sealant is a topic under Joint Sealing.
sealant
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Joint sealant is the material placed into prepared concrete or asphalt pavement joints and cracks after thorough cleaning—typically following diamond grinding—to form a reservoir with the correct shape factor that protects the joint interior. It creates a watertight barrier that isolates the pavement structure from water, chlorides, debris and other deleterious agents, thereby preserving structural continuity and extending service life [1]. Specified sealants may be rubberized, hot‑pour silicone or neoprene products, each required to meet temperature, dimensional and mechanical limits before being applied as a recessed fill level below the surface [2].

Material purpose and applicability

Joint sealant is the material applied during joint cleaning and sealing in concrete pavement restoration; after diamond grinding the joints are cleaned and the sealant is placed so that a proper shape factor for the sealant reservoir is achieved, which helps prevent damage to the sealant and maintains joint integrity, and the cleaning and sealing must be performed after diamond grinding to obtain the proper shape factor for the sealant reservoir [1]. In Portland cement concrete pavement, joint sealant refers to a rubberized joint‑sealing material applied to clean joints and cracks when the pavement is being patched but not overlaid, creating a seal that prevents water, chlorides, and incompressible materials from infiltrating the pavement structure, thereby improving performance and extending service life [1]. Joint rehabilitation relies on constructing new sealant reservoirs and resealing the joints to provide effective joint seals [1]. Joint sealant is the material placed in pavement joints and cracks to create a barrier that isolates the interior of the pavement from water, cement mortar, debris and other deleterious agents, preserving structural continuity and integrity [2]. In Section 501 (plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements) the sealant is a hot‑poured silicone or neoprene material placed in a joint reservoir after a transverse joint has been sawed and thoroughly cleaned; it must be applied only when the joint faces are dry and free of foreign material, may not be placed if the air temperature is less than 40 °F, requires indirect double‑boiler heating, temperature control within the manufacturer’s range, and the batch must not remain at pouring temperature for more than six hours [2]. Section 548 (unbonded concrete overlay) calls for the same type of sealant to be applied on the faces of transverse and longitudinal joints after the joint surfaces have been cleaned and protected from curing compounds, with the purpose of sealing those joints [2]. In Section 545 (bonded concrete overlay) an approved joint backing or filling material is placed into a prepared sealant reservoir at existing transverse and longitudinal joints, positioned to within ±1/4 inch of the pavement surface before the concrete overlay is placed, and then sealed to create a durable joint that remains intact during paving operations [2]. Section 513 (joint rehabilitation) requires joint sealant to be placed into a purpose‑cut reservoir after spall repair; the joint faces are media‑blasted, cleaned of oil and moisture, and a tape bond breaker is positioned on the reservoir bottom before the sealant closes the opening and restores continuity of the pavement surface [2]. For an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course (Section 489) the sealant is hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement heated and maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F, applied in a straight, consistent band about 4 inches wide (±1 inch) and roughly 1/16 inch thick (±1/32 inch), centered within 1 inch of the joint on clean, dry surfaces to create a continuous barrier that protects the pavement surface by sealing the joint against water and debris infiltration; placement is prohibited when air temperature is below 40 °F unless the Representative permits it [2]. In dowel retrofit work (Section 527) the sealant refers to a caulking compound placed into the transverse contraction joint after saw‑cutting, applied to the sides and bottom of the joint or crack to create a sealed reservoir across the full lane width, preventing concrete patching material from entering the existing joint or crack [2]. Section 469 (asphalt joint and crack sealing) uses an asphalt‑based sealant for joints and cracks that are 1/4 inch to 1 inch wide; after cleaning and drying the opening is filled level with the surrounding surface and wiped flush so that a thin film about 1/32 inch to 1/16 inch thick and extending 1 to 3 inches beyond the opening remains, and placement is permitted only when air temperature is between 40 °F and 90 °F [2]. Section 521 (transverse joint cleaning and sealing) requires a sealant to be introduced after a backer rod creates a sealed reservoir in transverse joints of rigid pavement; the joint faces must be clean and dry, both ambient air and pavement temperatures must be at least 40 °F, and the material is filled to a level 1/8 inch to 1/4 inch below the pavement surface, providing a recessed seal that prevents water or other contaminants from entering the joint [2]. Section 528 (concrete pavement cross‑stitching) calls for routing longitudinal cracks or joints to a width of 3/4 inch and a depth of 1 inch, then cleaning and drying them before applying sealing material; placement is allowed only when neither ambient air temperature nor pavement surface temperature is below 40 °F, and the sealant is filled to a level of 1/8 inch to 1/4 inch below the surface without spreading onto the surrounding slab [2]. Section 540 (bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavement) requires an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant material whose purpose is to seal every transverse and longitudinal joint of the pavement, with application following the manufacturer’s recommendations so that the joints are fully closed [2]. Section 412 (Superpave mixture design for thin asphalt overlay courses) defines a filler‑sealant material applied to cracks or joints wider than 1/8 inch during surface preparation; it fills and seals those openings in accordance with PennDOT specifications after removal of loose material [2]. Across all sections the common function of joint sealant is to fill the joint or crack to a specified level, prevent ingress of water and deleterious materials, maintain structural continuity, and provide a watertight barrier that supports the long‑term performance of the pavement system [2].

Composition and characteristics

The joint sealant specified for concrete pavement repairs is a rubberized material, which may be either a pure rubberized joint sealing material or a rubberized/asphalt blend, and it is applied after the joint has been thoroughly cleaned [1]. This sealant is placed into a purpose‑formed reservoir that is typically a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch cavity, and the shape factor of the reservoir must satisfy the applicable specification sections [1]. For flexible pavements, joint sealant may be an approved hot‑pour or silicone material applied as a poured‑in‑place fill, a preformed neoprene seal, or a silicone sealant installed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions [2]. When a poured seal is used, it must be heated with indirect double‑boiler equipment and placed at the recommended pouring temperature, and no single batch may be maintained at that temperature for more than 6 hours [2]. The hot‑pour fill is required to be level with the pavement surface as shown on the Standard Drawings and must be prevented from spilling onto the surrounding pavement [2]. Preformed neoprene seals are installed in one piece, lubricated with adhesive, may not be elongated more than 3 % or compressed longitudinally more than 2 %, and any bond‑breaker tape used must not extend up the reservoir face more than 1/8 inch [2]. Silicone joint sealing material is described as a low‑modulus, non‑sag silicone in a nonacid‑curing, one‑part formulation, and it is characterized by tensile stress at 150 % elongation, specific gravity, durometer Shore “A”, ozone and ultraviolet resistance, flow, bond strength to cement mortar, and a maximum tack‑free time [2]. Rubberized joint sealing material supplied by listed manufacturers must conform to ASTM D6690 Type I, II, or IV depending on the application [2]. The hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder used for longitudinal joints in surface courses must be heated and maintained between 265°F and 320°F, applied with a pressure applicator delivering a straight band approximately 4 inches wide (±1 inch) and about 1/16 inch thick (±1/32 inch), centered within 1 inch of the joint, and placement is prohibited when air temperature is below 40°F unless an exception is granted [2]. The same hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement is also specified for ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses under identical temperature and application width and thickness tolerances, with the same restriction on ambient temperature [2]. For notched wedge joints, sealant may be either a hot asphalt material matching the wearing‑course class and type or an emulsified asphalt (PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT); it must be applied evenly, extending at least 6 inches on each side of the joint, with the application rate controlled so that residual asphalt completely fills surface voids to create a watertight seal, and multiple applications may be used if necessary [2]. Joint sealant for flexible pavements is an asphalt‑based material listed in Bulletin 15 or Bulletin 41, heated within its safe temperature range, placed only when ambient air temperature is between 40°F and 90°F unless written approval is obtained, and it is applied to joints and cracks ranging from 1/4 inch to 1 inch wide, filled level with the surface and then wiped flush, leaving a continuous film from 1/32 inch to 1/16 inch thick that extends laterally 1 inch to 3 inches beyond the joint or crack [2]. Preformed neoprene compression seals must be free of pin holes and uncured areas, uniform in all dimensions, function properly at temperatures from –20°F to +140°F, and provide a side‑wall bearing pressure of at least 4 pounds per square inch at 85 % nominal width [2]. Preformed closed‑cell polyethylene joint filler is required to achieve 25 % compression under a minimum pressure of 5 pounds per square inch and no more than 30 pounds per square inch for 85 % compression [2]. Polyurethane foam joint filler is defined by a density of 6.0‑10.0 pounds per cubic inch, a recovery of at least 95 %, and water absorption not to exceed 30 % by volume [2]. The preformed flexible joint sealant that is a butyl‑rubber product must meet ASTM C990, and for fire safety it must have a flash point (Cleveland Open Cup) of at least 350°F and a fire point not less than the same limit [2]. PVC waterstops are required to be manufactured from virgin polyvinyl chloride resin with only those plasticizers, stabilizers and other additives needed for aging stability; reclaimed or scrap PVC is prohibited, they must retain flexibility down to –20°F (maximum brittleness temperature), exhibit a modulus of flexure of at least 400 psi·in, tensile strength of at least 1,000 psi in the original condition (1,150 psi across a splice), elongation of at least 280 % (200 % across a splice), and tear strength of at least 260 lb/in, with aging tests limiting changes in tensile strength and elongation to no more than 20 % after 70 h at 212°F and to no more than 40 % after a 14‑day extraction at 140/150°F [2]. Metal waterstops consist of copper sheets that must contain a minimum copper mass of 99.5 % [2].

Because the PVC waterstop must be made from virgin polyvinyl chloride resin with only the plasticizers, stabilizers and other additives needed for aging stability, any change in composition—such as using reclaimed material or different additive levels—can cause the sealant to miss the prescribed physical limits. If the formulation deviates, brittleness temperature may exceed the -20 °F max, modulus of flexure can fall below the 400 min. inch requirement, tensile strength and elongation may drop beneath the required 1,000 min. psi (original) or 280 min. percent (original), and tear strength could be less than 260 min. psi/in; likewise aging tests would show changes in tensile strength greater than the allowed 20 max. percent after oven exposure. Only manufacturers listed in Bulletin 15 may supply the material and shipments must be labeled with style, lot, plant and date to satisfy certification, so using an unlisted source would not meet the acceptance criteria. Key material properties

The performance of a joint sealant is governed by a combination of physical, mechanical, chemical and thermal characteristics that are specified for each pavement treatment. Under Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements (Slip‑Form Paving Method) the seal may not be elongated more than 3 % or compressed longitudinally more than 2 %, and it must retain dimensional stability during placement. The same section also requires that poured joint‑sealing material shall not be placed when air temperature is less than 40 °F, unless an exception is granted; the material must be kept within the manufacturer‑specified heating range, and any single batch may be held at pouring temperature for no longer than six hours. Section 413—Superpave Mixture Design specifies that the hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder used as a sealant must be maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F, and it shall not be placed when air temperature is below 40 °F unless allowed by the Representative. The sealant may be applied only to joints that are clean, dry and free of loose material or debris, and it must be laid as a straight band about 4 inches wide (±1 inch) with a thickness of 1/16 inch (±1/32 inch). Section 489—Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course repeats the temperature window of 265 °F to 320 °F, the prohibition on placement when air temperature is below 40 °F, the requirement for clean and dry joint surfaces, and the same dimensional band of 4 inches ±1 inch wide by 1/16 inch ±1/32 inch thick; these conditions are described as essential for proper adhesion and long‑term performance. Section 469—Asphalt Joint and Crack Sealing adds that the material must be heated within the safe heating temperature range and the recommended pouring temperatures shown on the manufacturer’s label, and it may not be placed when ambient air temperature is below 40 °F or above 90 °F unless a written departmental exemption is obtained. Section 705—Joint Material (Silicone sealants) requires that the cured material meet a suite of mechanical and chemical criteria, including maximum tensile stress at 150 % elongation, minimum elongation at peak strength, specified extrusion rate, specific gravity, durometer hardness, shelf life, ozone and ultraviolet resistance, flow, bond strength to cement mortar and tack‑free time when cured for seven days at 77 °F ± 3 °F and 45–55 % relative humidity. Preformed neoprene compression seals must be free of pinholes, uniform in all dimensions, function properly at temperatures between –20 °F and +140 °F, and maintain a side‑wall bearing pressure of 4 psi at 85 % nominal width while satisfying strict movement‑range tolerances. For preformed closed‑cell polyethylene joint filler the compressibility pressure shall be at least 5 psi to achieve a 25 % reduction in thickness and shall not exceed 30 psi for an 85 % reduction, and polyurethane foam fillers shall have a density of 6.0–10.0 lb/ft³, recover at least 95 % of the original height after compression, and limit water absorption to no more than 30 % by volume. Section 705—Gaskets and Waterstops specifies that polyvinyl chloride waterstops shall not have a brittleness temperature exceeding –20 °F, shall possess a modulus of flexure of at least 400 lb/in²·in, shall achieve a tensile strength of no less than 1,000 psi in the original material and 1,150 psi across a splice, shall elongate to at least 280 % in the original material and 200 % across a splice, and shall have a tear strength of not less than 260 lb/in. After oven aging for 70 hours at 212 °F the loss in tensile strength and elongation may not exceed 20 %, and exposure to 140–150 °F for 14 days may not reduce tensile strength or elongation by more than 40 %. Section 521—Transverse Joint Cleaning and Sealing requires that the joint be free of moisture and contaminants, and sealant placement shall be avoided unless both ambient air and pavement temperatures are at least 40 °F. Section 528—Concrete Pavement Cross‑Stitching likewise prohibits placement when either ambient air temperature or pavement surface temperature is below 40 °F, mandates that joint faces be thoroughly clean and dry to the satisfaction of the Representative before sealing, and forbids opening to traffic until the anchoring material has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. [2]

Joint sealants are classified by material type—hot‑pour (poured‑in‑place) sealants such as asphalt cement or the PG 64S‑22 binder, pre‑formed neoprene or silicone seals, rubberized sealants, pre‑formed closed‑cell polyethylene filler, pre‑formed polyurethane foam filler, and asphalt‑rubber sealing compounds—and each class must be placed according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. For hot‑pour sealants the material temperature must stay within the safe heating range shown on the manufacturer’s shipping container; under Section 413.3 (and its modification in Section 489.3) the PG 64S‑22 binder must be heated and kept between 265 °F and 320 °F, and placement may not occur when ambient air temperature is below 40 °F unless the Representative permits otherwise, while some specifications also restrict placement to ambient temperatures not above 90 °F. The sealant is applied with a pressure applicator that produces a straight band 4 inches ±1 inch wide and a thickness of 1/16 inch ±1/32 inch, centered within 1 inch of the joint; these dimensional tolerances define how the sealant’s properties are verified during construction. After placement the film shall be level with the pavement and its thickness shall be between 1/32 inch and 1/16 inch over joints from ¼‑inch to 1‑inch wide, wider cracks being filled with an asphalt wearing course. Prior to any sealant application a QC wipe test per PTM No. 643 is performed in the presence of the Representative to verify that joint faces are clean, dry and free of debris, and where required cleaning is done with a compressed‑air stream registering at least 100 psi measured at the source. Silicone sealants are characterized by tensile stress at 150 % elongation limited to a maximum value per ASTM D412 (Die C) after a 7‑day cure at 77 °F ± 3 °F and 45–55 % relative humidity, extrusion rate minimum grams‑per‑minute from 0 °F to 100 °F using an air‑powered caulking gun with a 1/8‑inch orifice at 90 psi, specific gravity per ASTM D792, durometer shore “A” per ASTM D2240 under the same cure conditions, shelf life expressed as a minimum number of days from manufacture, and additional performance metrics such as ozone and ultraviolet resistance (ASTM C793), flow (ASTM C639‑Type II), bond strength to cement mortar (minimum psi per AASHTO T 132), and tack‑free time (maximum minutes per ASTM C679). Pre‑formed neoprene seals must satisfy movement‑range tolerances defined by ASTM D2628/D3542, wall misalignment not more than 1/8 inch in a compressed condition, nominal width excess not more than 1/16 inch for seals up to 1½ inches (larger allowances for wider seals), provide a minimum seal recovery of 0.365 inch for a 1‑inch seal, and maintain side‑wall bearing pressure of at least 4 psi at 85 % nominal width. Polyethylene fillers are characterized by a compressibility pressure of at least 5 psi to achieve 25 % compression and not more than 30 psi for 85 % compression. Polyurethane foam fillers are evaluated for density of 6.0‑10.0 lb/ft³, compressive deflection per ASTM D7174, a minimum pressure of 5 psi to reach 25 % compression, a maximum pressure of 30 psi at 65 % compression, recovery of at least 95 %, and water absorption not exceeding 30 % vol. Joints and cracks ¼‑inch and wider must be sealed at least 24 hours before paving using the rubberized sealant called out in Section 469; the over‑band thickness and width are then minimized and shall not exceed the tolerances set out in Section 469. Elastomeric gaskets are evaluated per ASTM C443 (rubber) or C361 (neoprene), butyl‑rubber pre‑formed flexible joint sealants per ASTM C990, metal waterstops per ASTM B370, and PVC waterstops must meet a brittleness temperature not exceeding –20 °F (ASTM D746), a modulus of flexure of at least 400 lb·in/in³ (ASTM D747), tensile strength of at least 1,000 psi for the original material and 1,150 psi across a job splice (ASTM D412), elongation of at least 280 % original and 200 % across a splice, tear strength of at least 260 lb/in (ASTM D624), and aging criteria such as no more than 20 % loss in tensile strength or elongation after 70 h oven aging at 212 °F (ASTM D573) and no more than 40 % loss after a 14‑day extraction at 140‑150 °F. [2]

Behavior and mechanisms

In the construction of notched wedge joints (Section 413), the sealant is applied evenly a minimum of six inches on each side, and the application rate is controlled so that residual asphalt completely fills surface voids, thereby providing a watertight joint that resists moisture infiltration. Vehicular traffic or loads are prohibited on newly compacted courses for twenty‑four hours or until the pavement uniformly cools to a temperature of 140 °F or less, ensuring adequate stability before loading. For silicone joint sealing material (Section 705.4), flexibility is retained after a seven‑day cure at 77 °F and the material must resist ozone and ultraviolet exposure; bonding to cement mortar is required only after the mortar has been moisture cured for at least twenty‑eight days. Rubberized sealants are limited to ASTM D6690 type specifications. Preformed neoprene seals are required to function properly between –20 °F and +140 °F and to sustain a side‑wall bearing pressure of at least 4 psi at eighty‑five percent nominal width, with wall misalignment not exceeding one‑eighth inch. Closed‑cell polyethylene filler must compress twenty‑five percent under a minimum of five pounds per square inch and may be compressed up to eighty‑five percent under no more than thirty pounds per square inch. Polyurethane foam filler must recover at least ninety‑five percent of its original height after sixty seconds of deflection while limiting water absorption to a maximum of thirty percent by volume. PVC joint sealant (Section 705.5) must retain flexibility down to a brittleness temperature of –20 °F and exhibit a flexural modulus of at least four hundred pounds per square inch, indicating resistance to deformation under load. Its tensile strength shall be no less than one thousand pounds per square inch in the original material and one thousand one hundred fifty pounds per square inch across a splice, with minimum elongations of two hundred eighty percent and two hundred percent respectively, providing capacity to accommodate joint movement. Tear resistance is required to be at least two hundred sixty pounds per inch. After oven aging at 212 °F for seventy hours the tensile strength may change no more than twenty percent, and after a fourteen‑day extraction at one hundred forty to one hundred fifty °F the tensile strength may change up to forty percent, defining its durability under elevated temperature and chemical exposure. [2]

The performance of a joint sealant is first governed by the physical preparation of the joint—both faces must be thoroughly cleaned of scale, dirt, curing compound and other foreign material and then media‑blasted to roughen the sidewalls so that the sealant can bond. Chemically, the joint must be dry and free of moisture before any bond‑breaker or sealing material is placed, and when neoprene seals are used a lubricant adhesive must be applied according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Mechanically, hot‑pour sealants require the concrete temperature to be at least 40 °F and the material must be kept within the manufacturer‑specified heating and pouring temperature range, while preformed neoprene seals may not be elongated more than 3 % or compressed longitudinally more than 2 % during installation. The joint sealant’s behavior is also governed by the temperature at which it is kept, requiring heating and maintenance between 265°F and 320°F and prohibiting placement when the air temperature is below 40°F; its ability to adhere chemically depends on the joint surface being clean, dry and free of any loose material or debris, often cleaned with a power broom before application. Mechanical control comes from using a pressure applicator with a wand or nozzle that delivers a straight, consistent band about 4 inches +/-1 inch wide and 1/16 inch +/- 1/32 inch thick, centered within 1 inch of the joint. The sealant must be placed evenly with a minimum of 6 inches on both sides of the joint and applied at a rate that completely fills surface voids, thereby providing a watertight joint; chemically it depends on the binder properties of hot asphalt material or, alternatively, emulsified asphalt (PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT) to give adhesion and water resistance. Mechanically the sealant must be installed after cleaning away loose or harmful material, excess material is removed and a light layer of dry sand placed over it, and traffic may not be allowed until the sealed area uniformly cools to a temperature of 140°F or less, ensuring adequate stability. For longitudinal joints sealed with hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement the temperature window of 265°F to 320°F must be maintained and placement is prohibited when air temperature falls below 40°F unless an exception is granted; the sealant is applied only to clean, dry joints so that proper adhesion can occur, and a pressure applicator must lay the sealant in a straight band about four inches wide and roughly one‑sixteenth inch thick. The material must be heated within the safe heating temperature range and recommended pouring temperatures, and it may not remain at the pouring temperature for more than six hours, which controls its physical state during placement. Prior to sealing, cracks and joints must be cleaned, dried, and cleared of debris—using a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi or a hot air lance—to provide the chemical conditions needed for adhesion. After filling, the sealant is wiped flush so that a thin film from 1/32 inch to 1/16 inch thick remains, establishing the mechanical thickness that resists water infiltration, and placement is prohibited when ambient temperature is below 40°F or above 90°F. Joint sealant behavior is further governed primarily by its physical properties such as tensile stress at 150 % elongation, durometer hardness on Shore A after a 7‑day cure at 77 °F ± 3 °F and 45–55 % relative humidity, specific gravity, extrusion rate, flow, and shelf life. Chemically it is influenced by the nonacid‑curing silicone formulation that provides ozone and ultraviolet resistance. Mechanically the seal is controlled by movement ranges defined in ASTM D2628/D3542, a minimum side‑wall bearing pressure of 4 psi at 85 % nominal width, compressibility pressures of at least 5 psi to achieve 25 % compression and no more than 30 psi for 85 % compression, and a recovery of at least 95 % of its original thickness while remaining free of pin holes, uncured areas, and functioning between –20 °F and +140 °F. Physical preparation—including routing the longitudinal joint to a width of 3/4 inch and a depth of 1 inch, flushing with water, and drying the faces—provides a clean substrate for the epoxy bonding compound, whose chemical adhesion depends on proper injection and full curing as directed by the manufacturer. Mechanical actions such as drilling holes no more than 3/8 inch larger than the tie bar, rotating the tie bar three to five complete rotations while inserting it to purge air voids, and troweling the epoxy flush with the surface govern how well the sealant can be filled to a level of 1/8 inch to 1/4 inch below the pavement surface without spreading over the slab. The performance of a joint sealant waterstop is governed primarily by its physical composition—virgin polyvinyl chloride resin with only essential plasticizers, stabilizers, and durability agents—and by key physical limits such as a brittleness temperature of –20°F max, a minimum flexural modulus of 400 psi·inch, tensile strength requirements of at least 1,000 psi (1,150 psi across a job splice), elongation minima of 280 % (200 % across a splice), and tear strength of at least 260 lb/in. Chemical durability is expressed through allowable changes in tensile strength and elongation after oven aging (20 % max change after 70 h at 212°F) and high‑temperature extraction (40 % max change after 14 days at 140–150°F). Durability and deterioration

Joint sealants are vulnerable to a variety of deterioration mechanisms and damage processes that can compromise their long‑term performance. According to the Pavement Policy Manual, if sealant is placed before the pavement surface has been diamond‑ground, the grinding operation can destroy the sealant and prevent the joint reservoir from achieving the required shape factor, so sealing must follow grinding; visual observation of durability problems—including aggregate issues and frost damage—may also affect sealant performance [1]. The manual further advises that resealing should be performed when joints are just beginning to show distress because the longer distressed joints are left unattended, the more extensive repairs will become necessary, and failure of the seal permits water, chlorides and incompressible materials to infiltrate the pavement structure, accelerating deterioration and potentially leading to faulting or poor load transfer [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that joint‑sealant durability is limited by handling, environmental, and material‑specific factors, beginning with temperature constraints: sealant may not be placed when air temperature is less than 40 °F, nor when ambient air or pavement temperatures are below 40 °F, while for asphalt joint sealing the permissible air‑temperature window extends up to 90 °F; placement outside these ranges is prohibited [2]. Hot‑poured sealants must remain within the manufacturer’s temperature range and a single batch may not be kept at pouring temperature for more than 6 hours, because longer exposure can cause degradation of both cementitious and asphalt‑based sealants [2]. Installation practices that damage the sealant are prohibited; the seal must be installed without cutting, nicking, twisting or otherwise damaging it, and it may not be stretched more than 3 % nor compressed longitudinally more than 2 %, with any seal exceeding those limits required to be replaced [2]. Proper joint preparation is essential: joint faces must be cleaned to a minimum depth of 1½ inches, the joint must be clean and dry before placement, and a quality‑control wipe test is required because contaminants impair bonding [2]. Mechanical performance requirements affect durability as well; preformed neoprene compression seals are required to function only within a temperature range of –20 °F to +140 °F and must develop a side‑wall bearing pressure of at least 4 psi at 85 % nominal width, with excessive movement or compression beyond these pressures leading to loss of seal integrity [2]. When foam fillers are used, water absorption is limited to a maximum of 30 % by volume to reduce the risk of water ingress [2]. Environmental exposure further influences deterioration: silicone‑based sealants must meet ozone and ultraviolet resistance requirements (ASTM C793) to prevent degradation from UV radiation and ozone, while PVC waterstop materials must be manufactured from virgin polyvinyl chloride resin with aging‑stability additives; their durability is limited by a maximum brittleness temperature of –20 °F and by changes in mechanical properties after heat exposure, where oven‑aging at 212 °F for 70 hours may cause up to a 20 % change in tensile strength and a 20 % change in elongation, and a 14‑day extraction at 140 °F/150 °F permits no more than a 40 % change in tensile strength or elongation, defining thermal brittleness and loss of strength or flexibility as principal deterioration mechanisms [2]. Additional protective measures include using a backer rod that is 25 % to 35 % larger in diameter than the joint opening and filling the joint reservoir to a level 1⁄8 inch to ¼ inch below the pavement surface, creating a recessed seal that resists water infiltration; shelf‑life specifications and the requirement for pin‑hole‑free, fully cured material address aging and curing‑related degradation, while adhesion durability is governed by maintaining minimum bond strength to cement mortar and observing proper tack‑free time [2].

Joint sealant becomes more susceptible to deterioration when water, chlorides, or incompressible materials are able to infiltrate the pavement structure, a condition that arises if the drainage system is not functional or the subgrade is unstable [1]. Conversely, early cleaning and resealing of joints at the first signs of distress with the specified rubberized sealant, together with a stable subgrade and a functional drainage system, helps prevent such infiltration and thereby reduces deterioration [1]. Sealants that are rated for ozone and ultraviolet resistance in accordance with ASTM C793, retain at least the minimum shelf‑life specified from the date of manufacture, absorb no more than 30 % water by volume (for polyurethane foam types), and are capable of functioning over a temperature range of –20 °F to +140 °F exhibit reduced susceptibility to cracking, loss of bond, or other degradation [2]. PVC waterstop sealants that maintain a brittleness temperature no lower than –20 °F, possess a minimum modulus of flexure of 400 psi‑in, a tensile strength of at least 1,000 psi (1,150 psi across a splice), an original elongation of at least 280 % (200 % across a splice), and a tear strength of 260 lb/in, and that limit property loss after oven aging for 70 hours at 212 °F to no more than 20 % in tensile strength and 20 % in elongation, as well as limit reductions after a 14‑day extraction at 140/150 °F to no more than 40 %, are less susceptible to deterioration; exceeding these temperature limits or experiencing greater property loss would increase susceptibility [2]. For slip‑form concrete pavements (Section 501.3(g)), the joint and its reservoir must be thoroughly cleaned of scale, dirt, curing compound, temporary sealing material and other foreign matter, each sidewall should be media‑blasted to roughen the surface, and the joint must be free of moisture immediately before placement of a bond breaker or sealant; failure to achieve this cleanliness and dryness increases the likelihood of sealant deterioration [2]. In unbonded concrete overlays (Section 548.3), joint faces must either be protected from exposure to curing compound or cleaned thoroughly prior to sealant installation, all transverse and longitudinal joints should be sealed, and the surface temperature of both fabric and pavement must not exceed 120 °F before overlay placement; exceeding the 120 °F limit raises susceptibility [2]. For bonded concrete overlays (Section 545.3), protection or cleaning from curing compound is required, and any sawing slurry left in a joint must be removed by flushing the joint with water immediately after cutting; residual slurry that remains would increase deterioration risk [2]. When hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder is used as a sealant (Section 413.3), it shall be heated and maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F, applied only to joints that are clean, dry and free of loose material or debris, and placed only while the ambient air temperature is not below 40 °F unless an exception is granted by the Representative; placement under cooler conditions or on contaminated surfaces raises susceptibility [2]. The same temperature range and cleanliness requirement apply in ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses (Section 489.3), where sealant must be applied with a pressure applicator that produces a band approximately 4 inches wide (±1 inch) and a thickness of 1/16 inch (±1/32 inch); placement in ambient temperatures below 40 °F or above 90 °F is prohibited unless authorized, and the material must not be kept at its pouring temperature for more than six hours, because prolonged exposure to elevated temperature increases deterioration risk [2]. After application, excess hot asphalt should be stripped and the sealed face covered with an approved light layer of dry sand, and vehicular traffic or loads must not be allowed on newly compacted courses for 24 hours or until the pavement uniformly cools to a temperature of 140 °F or less; premature loading would otherwise increase susceptibility [2]. Joint sealant performs best when joint faces are clean and dry before placement, and sealing is limited to times when ambient air and pavement temperatures reach at least 40 °F; applying sealant under cooler conditions or on unclean, moist joint faces raises its susceptibility to deterioration (Section 521) [2]. Using a backer rod sized 25 % to 35 % larger than the joint opening helps maintain proper seal depth‑to‑width ratios that also mitigate degradation (Section 521) [2]. Under the same temperature restriction—ambient air or pavement surface temperature below 40 °F shall not be allowed for placement—and with the requirement that crack faces be thoroughly clean and dry, joint sealant is most vulnerable when placed under cold conditions or on unclean, moist joint faces; susceptibility is reduced by flushing the routed joint with water, cleaning it immediately prior to sealant placement, filling the joint only to a depth of 1/8‑to‑1/4 inch below the surface, and preventing any sealant from spreading onto the pavement surface (Section 528) [2]. Similarly, for crack sealing (Section 590), sealant shall be placed only after the crack has been thoroughly cleaned and dried, and only when both ambient air temperature and pavement temperature are at least 40 °F; placement below these temperatures is prohibited because it can increase the likelihood of premature failure [2]. When sealing longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks that are 1/4‑inch and wider, using the rubberized sealant required by Section 469 and keeping the over‑band thickness and width as small as possible helps lower the sealant’s susceptibility to deterioration; exceeding the over‑band tolerances set out in Section 469 would increase the risk [2]. Removing thermoplastic or tape markings greater than 0.2 inches, thoroughly cleaning any grease, oil, or fuel from the pavement, and ensuring a clean surface before application also reduce exposure conditions that can degrade the sealant (Section 489) [2].

Material interactions and compatibility

Within slip‑form paving of plain or reinforced cement concrete (Section 501.3(g)), joint sealant must bond directly to the concrete joint faces; therefore the surfaces are required to be completely free of scale, dirt, curing compound, temporary sealing material, moisture and any other foreign substance, and they are cleaned and media‑blasted to roughen for proper adhesion. An oversized backer rod whose width exceeds the joint by 50 % is placed in the top of the sawed joint before sealant placement, and bond‑breaker tape may be applied as directed. Sealant may not be poured when the air temperature is less than 40 °F unless an exception is granted. For unbonded concrete overlays (Section 548.3), the faces of transverse and longitudinal joints must be free of curing compound; any residue shall be removed or the surfaces cleaned thoroughly so that the sealant can bond directly to the concrete substrate. In bonded concrete overlays (Section 545.3) joint sealant is placed over a prepared overlay after existing transverse joints have been sawed and a sealant reservoir formed; the joint backing or other approved filling material shall be installed to within ±1/4 inch of the pavement surface so that it cannot be dislodged by the paving operation, and the joint faces must be kept free of curing compound or thoroughly cleaned before sealant placement. Immediately after sawing, water flushing removes any slurry that could impede adhesion. For Superpave asphalt courses (Section 413.3(j)), joint sealant is applied as hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder that must be heated and maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F, and it may not be placed when the air temperature is below 40 °F unless the Representative allows an exception; the sealant is applied only to joints whose pavement surfaces are clean, dry and free of any loose material or debris, forming a continuous band about 4 inches wide (±1 inch) and roughly 1/16 inch thick (±1/32 inch) that bonds the adjacent asphalt layers together. In joint rehabilitation (Section 513.3), a tape bond‑breaker is placed on the bottom of the joint reservoir immediately before sealing, and the tape shall not extend up the reservoir face more than 1/8 inch; all vertical faces are media‑blasted and then cleared with compressed air at 100 psi to remove oil, moisture and other contaminants, ensuring proper bonding of the sealant to the prepared concrete substrate. Silicone joint sealing material is required to develop a minimum tensile bond strength to primed cement mortar as measured by AASHTO T‑132; other sealant types such as rubberized, neoprene, polyethylene, polyurethane foam and asphalt‑rubber compounds are defined by dimensional or compressibility criteria without explicit bond or chemical interaction requirements with cement, soil minerals, aggregates or fibers. In concrete pavement cross‑stitching (Section 528.3), sealant is applied to the concrete joint after epoxy tie bars have been installed, directly contacting the cleaned concrete faces; the crack or joint must be thoroughly clean and dry before placement, and both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature shall be at least 40 °F. The sealant is filled so that its top lies 1/8 inch to 1/4 inch below the pavement surface and must not spread onto the surrounding slab. [2]

Influence of production and placement

Mixing of joint sealant must be performed by heating the material within the safe heating temperature range shown on the manufacturer’s label and maintaining it between 265 °F and 320 °F; batches may not be kept at the pouring temperature for more than six hours. Under Section 501.3(g) sealant placement shall not occur unless the ambient air temperature is at least 40 °F and no single batch may remain at pouring temperature for more than six hours. Section 521 requires that sealant be placed only after the joint has been cleaned to a minimum depth of 1½ inches, that both ambient air and pavement temperatures are 40 °F or greater, and that the joint reservoir be filled to a level between 1⁄8 inch and ¼ inch below the pavement surface. Section 528 similarly mandates placement only when ambient air and pavement temperatures are at least 40 °F, after the longitudinal crack has been routed to a width of ¾ inch and depth of 1 inch, flushed with water, cleaned, and filled to a level between 1⁄8 inch and ¼ inch below the surface without allowing sealant to spread over the pavement surface. Section 545.3 specifies that approved joint backing material be installed within ±1/4 inch of the pavement surface in existing transverse joints prior to overlay placement, and that joints be immediately flushed with water to remove sawing slurry. When required, a bond‑breaker tape shall be placed on the bottom surface of the joint reservoir and may not extend more than 1⁄8 inch up the face, and a backer rod sized 25 % to 35 % larger in diameter than the joint opening shall be positioned so its top sits one to two times the joint width plus ¼ inch below the pavement surface. Prior to sealing, concrete adjacent to joints must be vibrated and finished to meet edge‑slump tolerances, then media‑blasted, kept dry, and a QC wipe test performed to confirm cleanliness before any sealant is applied. Section 527 requires a minimum clearance of ½ inch around each dowel cap, a maximum displacement of 0.25 inch at each end, and longitudinal translation not exceeding 1 inch when dowel bars are retrofitted; the concrete patching material shall be vibrated to thoroughly consolidate it around the dowel bar. Section 469 calls for the use of rubberized sealant on joints and cracks one‑quarter inch or wider, and Section 489 directs that the installer keep the sealant over‑band as thin and narrow as possible while not exceeding the over‑band tolerances set out in Section 469. For asphalt‑based sealants a pressure applicator with a wand or nozzle capable of laying a straight band 4 inches wide (±1 inch) and 1/16 inch thick (±1/32 inch) shall be used; the material shall be applied evenly at least six inches on each side of the joint, the application rate controlled so that residual asphalt completely fills surface voids and provides a watertight joint, excess material removed, and the sealed area promptly covered with a light layer of acceptable dry sand. After placement, finishing shall consist of wiping the sealant flush with the pavement surface so that a thin film from 1⁄32 inch to 1⁄16 inch thick and 1 in to 3 in wide remains, thereby controlling final thickness and durability. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses the fresh mix shall be rolled immediately after placement while its temperature remains above 185 °F, using at least two passes of a steel double‑drum roller weighing not less than eight tons and keeping the roller moving to avoid adhesion; this compaction must occur before sealant application and under the same temperature and air‑temperature requirements. Newly compacted courses shall not be subjected to vehicular traffic or loads for 24 hours or until the course uniformly cools to a temperature of 140 °F or less. Silicone joint‑sealing material must be extruded at a minimum rate over the temperature range 0 °F to 100 °F using an air‑powered caulking gun, and after placement shall cure for seven days at 77 °F ± 3 °F with relative humidity of 45 % to 55 %, while tack‑free time shall not exceed the maximum minutes specified. Sealing should not be performed when the air temperature is below 40 °F or above 90 °F, because extreme temperatures can impair proper curing and performance; durability also depends on waiting until the anchoring material has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations before opening to traffic. The long‑term performance of a joint sealant is highly dependent on the condition of the joint at the time it is prepared and sealed; the Pavement Policy Manual requires that joint cleaning, sealing or rehabilitation be performed after diamond grinding so that the shape factor of the sealant reservoir is correct and the sealant is not damaged [1]. Construction defects that most affect durability are those that compromise load transfer and the integrity of the joint surface, such as missing tie bars in longitudinal contraction joints, badly spalled joints or interior slab spalls, and faulting or poor load transfer caused by inadequate support below the pavement; these conditions must be corrected before sealing, often requiring reconstruction of a proper sealant reservoir, for example a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch reservoir used for pavement/shoulder joints [1].

For all sealant applications the specifications mandate thorough cleaning of both joint faces and the joint reservoir; scale, dirt, curing compound, temporary sealing material, oil, grease, sawing slurry or any foreign debris must be removed before sealant placement and a QC wipe test must be performed immediately prior to application to verify that the joint is free of surface dirt, dust and debris [2]. In slip‑form paving (Section 501.3) poured sealant may not be placed when the air temperature is less than 40 °F unless an exception is granted, any single batch must not be kept at pouring temperature for more than six hours, and preformed seals also must not be elongated more than 3 % or compressed longitudinally more than 2 % [2]. For hot‑mix asphalt sealant (Section 413.3, Mat Density Acceptance) the binder temperature must be maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F, sealant may not be applied when air temperature is below 40 °F unless permitted, and the joint surface must be clean, dry and free of loose material; the sealant band shall be a straight width of 4 inches ± 1 inch and thickness of 1/16 inch ± 1/32 inch, centered within 1 inch of the joint, with any deviation or incomplete coverage requiring resealing [2]. Notched wedge joints (Section 413.3) require sealant to be applied a minimum of 6 inches on each side of the joint after cleaning and removal of harmful material; surface irregularities exceeding 3/16 inch or pavement‑depth deficiencies greater than ¼ inch are deemed defective [2].

In unbonded concrete overlay work (Section 548.3) joint faulting greater than ¼ inch in geotextile interlayers or greater than ½ inch in asphalt interlayers must be milled, scratched or otherwise reduced before sealant can achieve proper contact, and saw cuts must be at least D/3 within 12 inches of the pavement edges and at least D/4 elsewhere; any cracking outside the sawed joints is considered defective [2]. In bonded concrete overlay specifications (Section 545.3) joint backing or approved filling material must be placed within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface and secured against displacement, while the saw‑cut width must equal or exceed the underlying joint; a seven‑day bond strength test that averages less than 175 psi or yields any individual result below 160 psi renders the area defective [2].

Transverse joint cleaning and sealing (Section 521.3) requires complete removal of existing joint sealing material, neoprene compression seals, asphalt or other foreign material, cleaning to a minimum depth of 1½ inches with a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi free of oil, moisture and other contaminants, and overfilling beyond the prescribed 1/8‑¼ inch below the surface diminishes durability [2]. Concrete pavement cross‑stitching (Section 528.3) notes that drilling holes that extend through the bottom of the slab, spalling of the concrete surface, and failure to clean and dry both drilled holes and routed cracks introduce voids or contaminants that can prevent proper epoxy bonding and sealant adhesion; placement when ambient air temperature or pavement surface temperature is below 40 °F, allowing material to spread onto the pavement surface, or opening the joint to traffic before the anchoring material has fully cured jeopardizes durability [2].

Crack cleaning and sealing (Section 590.3) specifies that cracks must be sawed or ground to the required width and depth (¾ inch wide by 1 inch deep, or where the original crack is between ¾ inch and 1 inch wide, to a width of 1 inch and a depth of 1¼ inches), cleaned with a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi, placed only when both ambient and pavement temperatures are 40 °F or greater, and filled so that the reservoir sits between 1/8 inch and 1/4 inch below the surface [2].

Joint material requirements (Section 705.4) demand seals be free of pin holes and uncured areas and uniform in all dimensions; walls may not be misaligned by more than 1/8 inch when the seal is in a compressed condition, nominal width may not be exceeded by more than 1/16 inch for seals up to 1½ inches, 3/16 inch for seals greater than 1½ inches up to 2½ inches, ¼ inch for seals greater than 2½ inches up to 4 inches, and 5/16 inch for seals greater than 4 inches, and height variation beyond 1/8 inch violates the specification [2].

Asphalt joint and crack sealing (Section 469.3) emphasizes that maintaining a batch at pouring temperature for more than six hours or heating it outside the label’s safe range may degrade the sealant; placing sealant when ambient temperature is below 40 °F or above 90 °F, failing to achieve the specified thin‑film thickness of 1/32‑to‑1/16 inch, or not providing the required width also leads to premature loss of sealing effectiveness. The same section requires that longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks ¼‑inch and wider be sealed, that over‑band thickness and width tolerances not be exceeded, and that thermoplastic or tape traffic markings thicker than 0.2 inches be removed [2].

Ultra‑thin bonded wearing course construction (Section 489.3) adopts the temperature window of 265 °F to 320 °F and the air‑temperature restriction below 40 °F, mandates clean, dry joint surfaces free of loose material, requires sealant band dimensions and centering tolerances identical to those in Section 413.3, and calls for removal of excess sealant; an excessive number of transverse joints or joints cut non‑perpendicular to traffic increase the likelihood of premature deterioration [2].

Joint rehabilitation (Section 513.3) mandates repair of spalls larger than 2 inches, limits bond‑breaker tape protrusion on the reservoir face to no more than 1/8 inch, requires water blasting at a minimum capacity of 10 gallons per minute at 7,000 psi followed by media blasting and compressed‑air cleaning at 100 psi, and for Type 2 joints any steel plate remaining in the reservoir must not protrude more than ¼ inch [2].

Dowel retrofit (Section 527.3) requires removal of all loose and foreign material within 24 hours before concrete placement and final cleaning no later than 30 minutes prior to placement, with air blasting using a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi free of oil, moisture and other contaminants; jackhammers used to remove concrete may not exceed 30 pounds (or 15 pounds if damage occurs), dowel ends may not be tilted or skewed more than 0.25 inch, and longitudinal or horizontal translation may not exceed 1 inch, with any work deemed defective required to be removed and replaced [2].

Superpave mixture design construction (Section 413.3) further requires that exposed outer edges be worked immediately after placement to eliminate sharp, ragged and open edges, that a hot asphalt sealant be applied a minimum width of 12 inches from the curb with an application rate that completely fills surface voids to create a watertight joint, and that any excess sealant material be removed; pattern segregation, flushing or inadequate compaction that cause displacement, shoving, cracking or aggregate breakage are identified as construction defects that can compromise the integrity of sealed joints [2].

Selection, specification, and improvement

In concrete pavement restoration projects the joint‑cleaning and sealing operation must be performed after diamond grinding so that the sealant reservoir attains the proper shape factor and the sealant is not damaged. The reservoir dimensions are prescribed: longitudinal and pavement‑shoulder joints require a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch reservoir, while transverse joints must satisfy the shape‑factor requirement of Section 521. Sealants must be rubberized joint‑sealing material as specified in Publication 408 Sections 512 and 521, and are appropriate when joints are just beginning to show distress and the pavement will not be overlaid; sealing joints and cracks is not recommended for overlayed pavements. A stable subgrade and functional drainage are required to prevent infiltration of water, chlorides, or incompressible materials into the pavement structure.

For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements joint sealant selection is governed by joint preparation, ambient temperature, material performance limits, and documented quality‑control procedures; joint faces must be thoroughly cleaned, dry, and pass a Representative‑witnessed wipe test at two locations on each of the first three sawcut joints per day before any sealant is placed. Poured hot‑pour sealants may only be used when the air temperature is not less than 40 °F, the material must be kept within its recommended pouring temperature range and may not be held longer than six hours at that temperature. Preformed neoprene seals are permitted only if they can be installed without damage and their elongation does not exceed 3 % nor longitudinal compression more than 2 %; a tape bond breaker or backer rod must be placed as specified, and a minimum of three full‑width joints must be sealed in a test section before the remaining joints are accepted.

For longitudinal surface‑course joints the sealant shall be hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder heated to between 265 °F and 320 °F, and may not be placed when the air temperature is below 40 °F unless the Representative permits it; the joint must be clean, dry and free of loose material or debris before application, and a pressure applicator with a wand or nozzle capable of laying a straight band 4 inches ± 1 inch wide and 1/16 inch ± 1/32 inch thick shall be used, the band being centered within 1 inch of the joint. For notched wedge joints where a wearing course abuts existing pavement the sealant must be hot asphalt material of the class and type designated for the wearing course, although the Contractor may use emulsified asphalt of PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT as an alternative; before sealing the area shall be cleaned of loose or harmful material, and the sealant shall be applied so that residual asphalt completely fills surface voids, providing a watertight joint with a minimum of 6 inches on both sides of the joint, using multiple applications if necessary and covering any excess with an acceptable dry sand.

In joint rehabilitation the joint’s classification (Type 1 for existing saw‑cut joints or Type 2 for existing metal‑plate joints) determines which sealant system is specified; the joint sealant is placed in a reservoir cut to the width and depth indicated for the appropriate existing joint spacing and centered on the opening, a tape bond breaker shall be installed but its face may not extend more than 1/8 inch up the reservoir, all vertical faces of the joint shall be media‑blasted and then cleaned with a compressed‑air stream measuring 100 psi at the source, free of oil, moisture, and other contaminants, or if water blasting is used a water blaster with a minimum operating capacity of 10 gallons per minute at 7,000 psi shall be employed, and for Type 2 rehabilitation the portion of the existing steel plate within the new reservoir must be removed allowing no more than a 1/4‑inch protrusion.

For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses longitudinal joints shall be sealed with hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F; placement is forbidden when the air temperature is below 40 °F unless the Representative allows an exception, the joint must be clean, dry and free of any loose material or debris, and a pressure applicator capable of producing a straight band 4 inches ± 1 inch wide and 1/16 inch ± 1/32 inch thick, centered within 1 inch of the joint, shall be used; excess sealant shall be removed with inconsistently covered areas re‑sealed at no additional cost.

Selection of an asphalt joint or crack sealant is guided by the requirement to obtain material from a source listed in Bulletin 15 or Bulletin 41; the material shall be heated within its safe heating temperature range and the recommended pouring temperatures shown on the manufacturer’s container, and batches may not be kept at the pouring temperature for more than 6 hours without re‑heating as directed. Cracks and joints shall be cleaned, dried and, when needed, routed to a half‑inch deep reservoir before sealing joints from ¼ inch to 1 inch wide with asphalt sealant applied level with the pavement and finished to a film thickness of 1/32 inch to 1/16 inch over a width of 1 to 3 inches; sealant may not be placed when the air temperature is below 40 °F or above 90 °F unless written approval is obtained.

Joint material selection is driven by performance criteria defined for each material type: silicone sealants must meet specified limits on tensile stress at 150 % elongation, minimum elongation at maximum tensile strength, extrusion rate, specific gravity, durometer hardness, shelf life, ozone and ultraviolet resistance, flow, bond to cement mortar, and a maximum tack‑free time, and must satisfy the listed ASTM test methods; rubberized sealants are selected by application with ASTM D6690‑Type II or Type IV required for asphalt pavement joints while only Type IV may be used for all other joint sealing applications; preformed neoprene compression seals must be free of pin holes, operate between –20 °F and +140 °F, stay within dimensional tolerances such that misalignment is not more than 1/8 inch, provide a minimum side‑wall bearing pressure of 4 psi at 85 % nominal width, and meet movement‑range and seal‑recovery requirements that vary with nominal width; preformed closed‑cell polyethylene fillers are limited to a compressibility pressure of at least 5 psi for 25 % compression and no more than 30 psi for 85 % compression, while polyurethane foam fillers must have a density between 6.0 and 10.0 lb per cubic inch, compressive pressures of at least 5 psi (25 % deflection) and not exceed 30 psi (65 % deflection), recover at least 95 % of original height, and absorb no more than 30 % water by volume.

For transverse joint cleaning and sealing the selection of a joint sealant must be based on ensuring the joint is clean to at least 1½ inches depth and completely dry before placement; ambient air and pavement temperature must not be below 40 °F. The backer rod shall be sized 25‑35 % larger than the joint opening and positioned so its top lies one to two times the joint width plus ¼ inch below the surface, creating a recessed sealant depth‑to‑width ratio of 1:1 to 2:1, and the sealant shall be filled to leave a level 1⁄8 inch to ¼ inch below the pavement surface.

For concrete pavement cross‑stitching placement shall not occur unless the ambient air temperature or pavement surface temperature is at least 40 °F; the crack must be routed to a width of 3/4 inch and a depth of 1 inch, then flushed with water and cleaned until dry to the Representative’s satisfaction, and the sealant is filled so that its top lies between 1⁄8 inch and ¼ inch below the pavement surface and shall not spread over the pavement surface.

For bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavement the sealant must be an approved hot‑pour or silicone material and shall be applied in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations and as specified in Section 501.3(n).

In thin asphalt overlay courses any crack or joint wider than 1⁄8 inch must be filled and sealed with fillers and sealants that conform to PennDOT specifications, and prior to placement the existing surface must be cleared of loose and foreign material and excess joint sealer removed. [1][2]

In concrete pavement restoration projects the joint‑cleaning and sealing operation is scheduled only after diamond grinding so that the sealant reservoir attains the proper shape factor and the fresh sealant is protected from damage, which serves as a quality‑control measure to mitigate weaknesses related to improper joint geometry or handling [1]. For all joint‑sealing work the faces of each joint must be thoroughly cleaned and media‑blasted to remove scale, dirt, curing compound, temporary sealing material, and any other foreign matter; a PTM No. 643 wipe test is required immediately before sealant placement to verify cleanliness [2]. Sealant may be placed using an approved hot‑pour or silicone material heated with indirect double‑boiler equipment, and the material must be kept within the manufacturer’s temperature range throughout placement [2]. No single batch of sealant shall be maintained at pouring temperature for more than 6 hours, a limit that applies to both hot‑pour and silicone products [2]. When a backer rod is used it shall be sized 25–35 % larger in diameter than the joint opening, with its top positioned one to two times the joint width plus ¼ inch below the pavement surface, thereby establishing a recessed sealant depth‑to‑width ratio of 1:1 to 2:1 [2]. A tape bond breaker is required on the bottom of the sealant reservoir and may not extend more than 1/8 inch up the joint face; this tape must be placed immediately before sealing [2]. If water blasting is employed, the blaster shall have a minimum capacity of 10 gallons per minute at 7 000 psi, and all vertical faces must subsequently be media‑blasted and cleaned with a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi that is free of oil, moisture, or other contaminants [2]. Preformed neoprene seals may be used provided a lubricant adhesive is applied and the seal is not elongated more than 3 % nor compressed longitudinally more than 2 %; any seal exceeding those limits shall be replaced at no additional cost [2]. Alternative sealant families are permitted when they satisfy detailed physical criteria, such as low‑modulus non‑sag silicone joint sealing material in a non‑acid‑curing one‑part formulation with ozone and ultraviolet resistance per ASTM C793; rubberized joint sealing material conforming to ASTM D6690 Type IV for asphalt joints or Type I/II for other applications; preformed neoprene compression seals meeting the movement‑range tolerances of ASTM D2628/D3542; closed‑cell polyethylene fillers whose compressibility is limited to a minimum 5 psi at 25 % and a maximum 30 psi at 85 %; and polyurethane foam fillers that must recover at least 95 % of their original height [2]. PVC waterstops used as joint sealants must be manufactured from virgin resin, have a brittleness temperature not exceeding –20 °F (ASTM D746), a modulus of flexure of at least 400 lb·in per square inch (ASTM D747), tensile strength of at least 1 000 psi original and 1 150 psi across splices, elongation of at least 280 % original and 200 % across splices, tear strength of at least 260 lb/in, and exhibit limited changes after oven aging (≤20 % in tensile strength and elongation) and after a 14‑day extraction (≤40 %) [2]. Acceptable alternatives to PVC waterstops include elastomeric rubber gaskets (ASTM C443 or C361), preformed butyl‑rubber joint sealant (ASTM C990), and metal copper waterstops (ASTM B370) that satisfy the same performance criteria [2]. The contractor must submit a detailed quality‑control plan that includes test‑section verification by the manufacturer’s representative; each lot of sealant not exceeding 10 000 linear feet must be accompanied by an eCAMMS laboratory test report indicating approval or rejection, and the top of every seal must be permanently marked with batch number, cure date, and type at one‑foot intervals [2]. Sealant application shall be performed with a pressure applicator equipped with a wand or nozzle capable of laying a straight band 4 inches ±1 inch wide and 1/16 inch ±1/32 inch thick centered within 1 inch of the joint; excess material is to be removed and any uncovered spots resealed [2]. Sealant placement is permitted only when ambient air temperature is at least 40 °F (and not above 90 °F unless written approval is obtained), and the sealant must be applied while the pavement surface temperature is also at least 40 °F; joints wider than 1 inch are better filled with Asphalt Wearing Course 4.75 mm rather than sealant [2]. For joints wider than ¼ inch, pre‑formed cellular polystyrene filler shall be replaced with an expansion joint material, and for all other joints the use of hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement heated between 265 °F and 320 °F is required, with placement prohibited when air temperature falls below 40 °F unless permitted by the Representative [2].

Performance evaluation and implications

Joint sealant performance is evaluated through a combination of field checks and prescribed laboratory tests, while long‑term performance testing is not specified in the referenced sections. In the field, a QC wipe test performed according to PTM No. 643 is required immediately before placing the joint sealing material; two locations are checked on each of the first three sawcut joints per day of production, and at least three full‑width pavement joints must be sealed as defined in the project QC plan and witnessed by the manufacturer’s representative. After surface preparation and prior to placing a concrete overlay, the joint system is further evaluated by a 7‑day bond‑strength test performed according to ASTM C1583; for each day’s placement covering up to 3,000 sq yd, five random locations are selected (lot testing) and three tests are run at each location, and the area is deemed defective if the average of the three results is below 175 psi or any individual result falls under 160 psi, which then triggers additional testing. Laboratory evaluation of sealants follows the test methods listed in Section 705.4: silicone sealants must meet tensile stress at 150 % elongation measured after a 7‑day cure at 77 °F ± 3 °F and 45–55 % relative humidity (ASTM D412, Die C), must meet minimum elongation at maximum tensile strength, extrusion rate, specific gravity, durometer hardness, ozone and ultraviolet resistance (ASTM C793), flow (ASTM C639‑Type II), bond strength to cement mortar (AASHTO T 132) and tack‑free time (ASTM C679); rubberized sealants are qualified by compliance with the appropriate ASTM D6690 type (I, II or IV); preformed neoprene seals must satisfy movement‑range requirements (ASTM D2628, D3542) and dimensional tolerances; preformed closed‑cell polyethylene fillers are assessed for compressibility pressures, and polyurethane foam fillers are characterized by density, compressive deflection (ASTM D7174), recovery, water absorption and related limits. Acceptance of each lot requires an eCAMMS‑generated laboratory test report that states approval or rejection. Additional laboratory verification of gasket and waterstop materials includes elastomeric gaskets verified by ASTM C443 for rubber and ASTM C361 for neoprene, pre‑formed flexible joint sealant (butyl rubber) tested to ASTM C990 with fire point limits, and PVC waterstops that must satisfy a brittleness temperature limited to –20 °F (ASTM D746), a modulus of flexure minimum 400 lb/in² (ASTM D747), tensile strength minima of 1,000 psi for the original material and 1,150 psi across a splice (ASTM D412), tear strength minimum 260 lb/in (ASTM D624), oven aging for 70 h at 212 °F with a maximum 20 % loss in tensile strength (ASTM D573), and a 14‑day extraction at 140–150 °F limited to a 40 % change in tensile strength or elongation. No long‑term performance evidence for the sealant material itself is described. [2]

Joint cleaning and sealing must be performed after diamond grinding to obtain the proper shape factor for the sealant reservoir and to prevent damage to the sealant [1]. Effective joint sealing with the specified rubberized sealant greatly improves pavement performance by preventing water, chlorides, and incompressible material from infiltrating the structure [1]. Resealing should be carried out when joints just begin to show distress because delaying increases the need for more extensive repairs and reduces durability [1]. Sealing is not recommended when an overlay is placed, since trapped moisture could compromise the overlying layer [1]. Proper construction of sealant reservoirs requires a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch reservoir for pavement/shoulder joints and compliance with the required shape factor for transverse joints to ensure correct function during construction [1]. Under Pennsylvania specifications, joint sealants must be placed on joints that have been thoroughly cleaned and dried and that pass a quality‑control wipe test; the reservoir is to be filled to the level shown on the standard drawing without allowing any material to spread onto the pavement surface, and tape bond breakers or backer rods may not extend more than 1/8 inch up the face of the joint [2]. The same specifications limit sealant elongation to no more than 3 % and longitudinal compression to no more than 2 %, and new pavement shall not be opened to traffic until all joints have been sealed unless an approved protective method is used [2]. For joint rehabilitation, a tape bond breaker must be placed on the bottom of the sealant reservoir without extending more than 1/8 inch up the face, vertical faces shall be media‑blasted and then cleaned with a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi to remove oil, moisture and other contaminants, and water‑blasting equipment must deliver a minimum of 10 gallons per minute at 7,000 psi for wide joints [2]. Transverse joint cleaning and sealing requires removal of existing joint material and concrete surface cleaning to a minimum depth of 1½ inches; a backer rod 25 % to 35 % larger in diameter than the opening shall be placed with its top positioned one to two times the joint width plus ¼ inch below the pavement surface, sealing may occur only when ambient air and pavement temperatures are 40°F or greater, and the sealant shall be filled to a level of 1/8 inch to ¼ inch below the pavement surface [2]. Joint sealant for longitudinal surface‑course joints must be heated and maintained between 265°F and 320°F and may be placed only when air temperature is not below 40°F; the same temperature limits apply for ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses where hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement is used [2]. Sealant batches shall not be kept at pouring temperature for more than six hours, and placement is restricted to ambient temperatures between 40°F and 90°F; application must be performed with a pressure applicator capable of delivering a straight band 4 inches ± 1 inch wide and 1/16 inch ± 1/32 inch thick, centered within 1 inch of the joint, and when a thin film is specified its thickness may range from 1/32 inch to 1/16 inch and its width from 1 inch to 3 inches [2]. For notched wedge joints, the sealant must be hot asphalt material matching the wearing‑course class or an approved emulsified asphalt (PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT), it shall be evenly applied a minimum of 6 inches on each side of the joint so that residual material completely fills surface voids and provides a watertight joint, with any excess removed and covered with a light layer of dry sand [2]. Section 705 specifies a low‑modulus, non‑sag silicone formulation that functions properly at temperatures between –20°F and +140°F, must sustain a side‑wall bearing pressure of at least 4 psi at 85 % nominal width, and has tensile stress limits at 150 % elongation with a required minimum elongation at that strength [2]. Polyurethane foam joint fillers are required to compress between 5 psi (25 % compression) and 30 psi (85 % compression), recover at least 95 % of their original thickness, and limit water absorption to no more than 30 % by volume [2]. PVC joint waterstops must have a brittleness temperature not exceeding –20°F, a modulus of flexure of at least 400 lb·in⁻¹, tensile strength of a minimum 1,000 psi (1,150 psi across a splice), elongation of at least 280 % (200 % across a splice), and tear strength not less than 260 lb·in⁻¹; oven aging for 70 hours at 212°F may change tensile strength by no more than 20 % and elongation by no more than 20 %, while a 14‑day extraction at 140–150°F may permit up to a 40 % change, limits intended to retain flexibility, strength and resistance throughout service [2]. For concrete pavement cross‑stitching, the joint must be routed to 3/4 inch wide and 1 inch deep, flushed with water, thoroughly cleaned and dried before sealant application; placement is prohibited when ambient or pavement surface temperature is below 40°F, the sealant shall be filled to a level of 1/8 inch to ¼ inch below the surface and prevented from spreading onto the pavement face, and traffic may not be allowed until the anchoring material has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations [2]. Crack cleaning and sealing requires cracks to be cut to a width of 3/4 inch by a depth of 1 inch, flushed with water and thoroughly cleaned before sealant placement; sealant may be placed only when both ambient air temperature and pavement temperature are at least 40°F, the reservoir shall be filled to a level between 1/8 inch and ¼ inch below the surface, and controlling the amount of material left exposed helps maintain a smooth riding surface and supports long‑term performance [2]. For plant‑mixed thin asphalt overlay courses, loose and foreign material as well as excess joint sealer or crack filler must be removed from the existing pavement surface because residual sealant could contaminate the overlay bond; all cracks or joints that exceed 1/8 inch in width shall be filled and sealed with fillers and sealants conforming to PennDOT specifications, providing a durable watertight substrate that supports long‑term performance of the overlaid pavement [2]. At least 24 hours before paving operations, longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks ¼‑inch and wider must be sealed using the rubberized sealant specified in Section 469; the over‑band thickness and width shall be minimized and must not exceed the tolerances set out in Section 469, ensuring proper surface conditioning for the wearing course and helping prevent premature pavement deterioration [2]. Sealing of curbs and gutters requires a hot‑asphalt material of the class and type listed in Table A (or, when permitted, an emulsified asphalt consisting of PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT) to be evenly applied a minimum width of 12 inches from the curb; the application rate must be controlled so that residual asphalt completely fills surface voids and provides a watertight joint along the curb, after which any excess sealant is removed, a practice that directly influences pavement durability by preventing water infiltration at edge joints [2].
