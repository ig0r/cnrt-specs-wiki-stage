Unbonded Concrete Overlay

Part of: Concrete Overlay is-subtopic-of

Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-28

Unbonded concrete overlay is a rehabilitation technique applied to existing pavements—whether Portland‑cement concrete, asphalt, or composite structures—that have lost reliable structural support, exhibit poor ride quality, low‑friction surfaces, or moderate‑to‑severe distress such as cracking, settlement, rocking slabs, and joint faulting[1][2]. Because a dependable bond between the old and new concrete cannot be guaranteed under these conditions, the design treats the deteriorated slab solely as a uniformly supporting subbase and places a fresh rigid Portland‑cement concrete slab separated from the underlying pavement by a one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt sheet or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer, which prevents bonding and permits independent movement to mitigate reflective cracking[1][2]. The overlay thereby restores structural capacity, provides a smooth low‑friction riding surface, and improves safety while extending service life without relying on the compromised existing pavement for load transfer[1].

Purpose and design objectives

Unbonded concrete overlay is selected when an existing pavement no longer provides reliable structural support, exhibits poor ride quality, or has low‑friction surface conditions caused by cracking, settlement, visibly rocking slabs, joint faulting, or other moderate to severe deterioration. The presence of unstable slabs or slab fragments that would otherwise give the new concrete non‑uniform bearing and induce premature reflective cracking also triggers the use of an unbonded overlay. Because a dependable bond between old and new layers cannot be assured under these conditions, the design treats the existing pavement solely as a subbase and provides a fresh rigid slab that restores structural capacity independently of the deteriorated base. The primary performance need addressed is to supply uniform support beneath the overlay while correcting structural deficiencies, surface distress, and safety‑related friction problems. By inserting a separation layer—commonly a geotextile placed over a repaired and cleaned surface—the overlay is prevented from bonding to the old concrete, allowing independent movement of the two layers and mitigating reflective cracking. Preparation includes stabilizing cracked areas, removing or replacing major deficiencies, installing continuous base drainage, applying a leveling course with a bond breaker, correcting cross‑slope, and sealing existing joints to achieve a uniformly supporting pavement layer. The approach can serve as either a minor rehabilitation for modest structural loss or a major rehabilitation when the existing concrete cannot be cost‑effectively repaired but can still function as a subbase. Overall, the unbonded overlay satisfies the engineering need to extend service life, improve rideability, and enhance safety where the underlying pavement condition precludes a bonded solution. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462)

The unbonded concrete overlay is intended to rehabilitate an existing pavement by restoring its structural capacity while providing a smooth, low‑friction riding surface that improves vehicle comfort and eliminates hazardous slip conditions [1]. To satisfy functional requirements the overlay must possess sufficient flexural strength to accommodate projected traffic loads and must correct existing surface distress [1]. Structurally the overlay must be placed on a uniformly supporting base, which requires repair or stabilization of any unstable slabs before the separation interlayer is installed [1][2]. Major deficiencies in the underlying concrete must be removed or replaced, whereas cracked areas may be stabilized rather than excavated, ensuring uniform support for the new slab [1]. The design mandates a separation layer of at least one inch of fine‑graded asphalt sheet or an equivalent geotextile, installed with proper overlap, protected from traffic, and kept below a surface temperature of 120 °F during placement [1][2]. Joint preparation is required so that joints faulting more than ¼ inch are milled and those exceeding ½ inch receive a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing course or are ground, after which all joints are sawed, cleaned and sealed to maintain load‑transfer efficiency [1][2]. Panel geometry must be controlled so that the aspect ratio of any panel does not exceed 1.5 and its dimensions remain within eighteen times the overlay thickness, limits that help restrict cracking [2]. Dowel basket assemblies incorporated in the overlay are to be anchored such that vertical or horizontal movement does not exceed a quarter inch, further supporting joint load transfer and safety [2][1]. The overlay thickness is selected to meet the minimum required for unbonded overlays after accounting for future traffic and the effective existing pavement thickness, providing a design life of at least eight years but not more than twenty years [1]. Adequate drainage of the interlayer is provided either by daylighting the layer or installing a vertical subdrain, preventing water accumulation that could compromise durability [1]. Curing must commence within half an hour after bleed‑water dissipation, using insulating blankets when ambient temperatures are low and limiting surface temperature change to no more than 40 °F per hour during insulation removal, measures that protect the overlay from thermal shock [2]. The design also calls for resistance to shrinkage, freeze‑thaw damage, and plastic deformation, thereby extending service life [1]. Safety is addressed by preventing slab rocking, pumping, and faulting, maintaining high joint load‑transfer efficiency, and delivering a crack‑free surface that meets ride‑quality expectations throughout the design period [1][2]. Serviceability is enhanced by reducing crack widths, slowing overall crack deterioration, preserving smoothness, and ensuring that the overlay continues to meet ride quality standards for the duration of its intended service life [1][2].

Scope and applicability

An unbonded concrete overlay is appropriate for any existing pavement—whether Portland‑cement concrete, an all‑asphalt surface, or a composite asphalt‑concrete structure—that exhibits moderate to severe deterioration, including loss of structural capacity, faulting, visible rocking slabs, spalling, stripping, or other deficiencies that make bonding unreliable. The existing layer must be capable of being made into a uniformly supporting base; major defects are removed and the remaining surface is stabilized by cleaning, filling cracks with asphalt material, applying leveling courses, or using full‑depth repairs where necessary to achieve uniform support. When these preparation steps provide a stable platform, the overlay can be placed without bonding. A separation layer of at least one inch of fine‑graded asphalt or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile is required between the old and new concrete. The overlay itself is a rigid Portland‑cement concrete slab whose thickness is determined by the pavement‑thickness required for future traffic, using the same design procedures as bonded overlays and targeting a structural life of eight to twenty years. This method is used in rehabilitation projects that aim to restore ride quality, increase structural capacity, or improve friction without relying on bond‑related constraints, and it is suitable where the underlying pavement will serve only as support rather than share load. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188)

An unbonded concrete overlay is only the preferred rehabilitation technique when the existing slab cannot serve as a uniform, stable subbase and the pavement condition ranges from moderate to severe deterioration. When the roadway segment requiring treatment exceeds roughly five hundred feet in length, or when intermittent patches would leave ten feet or less of original concrete between them over a stretch of about five hundred feet, the overlay becomes unsuitable; in such cases a full‑depth reconstruction or a conventional jointed concrete pavement is called for. Likewise, if more than five hundred feet of all lanes must be replaced, a design analysis will usually indicate that a thicker slab depth – and therefore a traditional jointed reinforced or plain concrete system – is more appropriate. When the existing concrete is in fair‑to‑excellent condition, the subgrade is stable, and drainage functions properly, a bonded overlay is favored because it incorporates the old slab into the structural system, permits a thinner placement, and avoids raising the roadway grade; this approach also handles surface spalling effectively. If the project intent includes fracturing the existing slab, the department recommends an asphalt overlay designed with the “AC over Fractured PCC” module rather than an unbonded concrete overlay. Finally, when the underlying pavement cannot provide relatively uniform support or cannot be repaired cost‑effectively, or when core testing shows no significant stripping and the pavement is in good condition, a bonded concrete overlay—or alternative rehabilitation method such as cracking and seating—should be selected instead of an unbonded overlay. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 10 (pp. 185–188)

Design inputs and requirements

Design of an unbonded concrete overlay begins with a structural evaluation of the existing pavement, which must include falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD) testing before any design calculations are performed. The existing slab condition is also examined; if movement, faulting, or rocking slabs are observed, stabilization or jacking is required prior to placement of the overlay. Adequate subdrainage must be confirmed where subsurface drainage problems exist, and full‑depth patching of the original pavement with concrete or asphalt base course is performed when approved. The source does not specify traffic volumes, material property data, or broader environmental or geotechnical investigations beyond these items. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Design of an unbonded concrete overlay follows the requirements set out in Chapter 10 of the pavement design guide, which mandates cleaning and sealing all existing joints before placement. A leveling course that incorporates a bond‑breaker is required to correct cross‑slope and provide a uniformly supporting base, with major slab deficiencies removed and replaced while cracked areas may be stabilized rather than excavated. Surface preparation must include either a fine‑graded minimum one‑inch asphalt separation layer or an approved non‑woven geotextile interlayer as specified in Publication 408 Section 548, and the interlayer is limited to three layers with each overlap extending eight inches plus or minus two inches and projecting at least four inches beyond the pavement edge. Prior to design a falling weight deflectometer test shall be performed on the existing concrete pavement, and any required full‑depth patching, slab stabilization, or subdrainage improvements must comply with Chapter 4.9 provisions. Structural design is based on the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures using rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 for ESAL calculations, with daily 18‑kip ESALs determined in accordance with Chapter 7 and a minimum structural design life of eight years and a maximum of twenty years. Overlay thickness is obtained by determining the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic (Table 10.8) and the Existing Effective Pavement Thickness (Table 9) and confirming that the result meets or exceeds the minimum shown in Table 10.10. Joint geometry must satisfy an aspect‑ratio not greater than 1.5 and a maximum panel dimension equal to eighteen times the overlay thickness, with joints sawed to at least one‑third of slab depth near edges (one‑fourth elsewhere) and a minimum joint width of one‑quarter inch; faulting limits require milling of joints exceeding ¼ inch and grinding or placement of a Superpave wearing course for faults over ½ inch. Curing shall commence within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates, using a white PAMS curing compound applied at a rate of at least one gallon per 150 ft² for overlays thicker than six inches, and temperature changes during insulation removal must not exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit per hour. The interlayer must be properly drained either by daylighting or a vertical subdrain, extend through any reconstructed shoulder, and be tied into the mainline to maintain drainage continuity. Concrete shoulders are required when existing shoulders are concrete; otherwise the shoulder type may follow the existing configuration, with fill widening employed to preserve required widths, bridge clearances, guide‑rail locations, and drainage structures. Design documentation includes completion of Form D‑4332 for district review and FHWA concurrence where applicable, and macrofibers, when used, contribute residual strength measured in accordance with ASTM C1609 and incorporated into the effective flexural strength for structural calculations. All specifications governing surface preparation through curing are captured in Section 548.3 of the Rigid Pavements specifications, which governs every phase of execution for unbonded concrete overlays. [1][2]

Design methods and criteria

The design of an unbonded concrete overlay follows the procedure outlined in Chapter 10 of the pavement manual and proceeds through a series of prescribed construction steps rather than relying solely on analytical equations [1][2]. The process begins with a falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD) test on the existing Portland cement concrete pavement to quantify its structural capacity [1]. Based on the test results any major deficiencies are removed and replaced, while cracked sections may be stabilized rather than excavated to provide a uniformly supporting platform for the overlay [1]. Surface preparation includes full‑depth patching of the original slab with either concrete or an approved asphalt base course, slab stabilization or jacking where movement is present, and the provision of continuous subdrainage [1]. A leveling course incorporating a bond breaker is placed when needed, and all existing joints are cleaned and sealed before overlay placement [1]. To achieve structural separation a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt layer or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer is installed between the old concrete and the new overlay, eliminating any direct bond [1]. Joint layout for the overlay follows the standards of Publication 72M, with spacing selected according to overlay thickness rather than aligning with existing joints [1]. Design calculations are performed with the DARWin software package, which implements the 1993 AASHTO mechanistic‑empirical pavement design procedures [1]. Equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) are derived from the rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors listed in Table 7.1 [1]. The required overlay thickness is obtained directly from the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic table (Table 10.8) after determining the Existing Effective Pavement Thickness (Table 10.9), and this computed thickness is subsequently checked against the minimum permissible value given in Table 10.10 [1]. When a geotextile interlayer is specified, concrete joints exhibiting fault greater than one‑quarter inch are milled and a nominal one‑inch Superpave wearing course of 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix is placed as part of the separation system, with additional measures applied for faults exceeding half an inch [1]. If an existing asphalt layer underlies the concrete, it is considered only a supporting layer and does not contribute structurally to the overlay design [1]. The overlay panels are controlled by geometric criteria so that the aspect ratio of longer to shorter dimension is as close to one as feasible and does not exceed 1.5, with the longer side limited to no more than eighteen times the overlay thickness [2]. Saw cuts are placed at a depth of at least one‑third of the slab thickness and joint spacing plans respect the maximum panel dimensions; when project‑specific sawing windows are required FHWA’s HIPERPAV software or an equivalent approved tool may be employed to determine appropriate timing [2]. Curing of the newly placed concrete must follow specified timing, insulation, and compound application rates to achieve proper strength development, completing the procedural design framework for the overlay [2].

Unbonded concrete overlays are designed according to the procedures set out in Chapter 10, which require cleaning and sealing all existing joints, providing a continuous pavement‑base drainage system, and installing a leveling course with an appropriate bond breaker wherever profile correction is needed. The design assumes that the existing pavement serves only as a stable platform and contributes no structural capacity, so uniform support must be achieved by removing major defects such as severely deteriorated joints or crushed sections while allowing less severe cracks to be stabilized rather than excavated. Spalled concrete may be cleaned and filled with asphalt material, and any profile irregularities are corrected with a leveling course; full CPR treatments such as partial‑depth repair, joint rehabilitation, or diamond grinding are not required. A fine‑graded asphalt separation layer at least one inch thick, or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer, is placed over the existing slab and must extend through any reconstructed shoulders to maintain the unbonded condition and provide drainage that can be achieved by daylighting the interlayer or installing a vertical subdrain. When a geotextile interlayer is used, its overlap shall be eight inches plus or minus two inches with a minimum four‑inch free edge extension, and joints exhibiting faulting greater than one‑quarter inch must be milled while faulting exceeding one‑half inch requires additional treatment before the separation layer is placed. Concrete shoulders are required when the existing shoulders are concrete; otherwise the overlay shoulder type must match the existing pavement, and any widening must preserve proper shoulder widths and bridge clearances. Joints in the overlay are detailed exactly as for new concrete pavements, need not align with existing transverse joints, and joint spacing is governed by slab thickness and type; saw cuts shall be at least one‑third of slab depth within twelve inches of panel edges and one‑quarter depth elsewhere, and all transverse and longitudinal joints must be sealed after cleaning. Panel geometry is limited so that the aspect ratio does not exceed 1.5 and the longest dimension is no more than eighteen times the overlay thickness, ensuring manageable slab dimensions for construction and performance. Surface preparation requires that any defect larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch be patched before placement of the concrete overlay. The temperature of both the geotextile fabric and the existing pavement may not exceed 120 °F at the time of overlay placement to avoid adverse effects on bond‑breaker performance. Curing procedures dictate that insulation be removed only after the concrete has reached a compressive strength of at least 3,600 psi and that the rate of surface temperature change during removal not exceed forty degrees per hour; for slabs thicker than six inches, curing compound must be applied at a minimum rate of one gallon per 150 square feet. The overlay is treated as a structural overlay, and its required thickness is derived from the pavement‑thickness needed for future traffic using rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors to compute daily 18‑kip ESALs, combined with the existing effective pavement thickness to determine the final overlay thickness, which must meet or exceed the minimum specified for unbonded PCC overlays. The design life of an unbonded concrete overlay is limited to a minimum of eight years and a maximum of twenty years, and accurate traffic data—including current ADT, projected growth, and truck percentages—must be employed in the analysis to satisfy reliability and safety requirements. Upon completion, Form D‑4332 is submitted for district concurrence and FHWA approval, providing documented assurance that the design satisfies the required reliability and safety criteria. [1][2]

Materials and mixture design

The overlay is required to be conventional Portland cement concrete that meets the requirements of Publication 408 Section 704 and utilizes an optimized aggregate‑gradation mix design. High‑early‑strength concrete is not permitted because its rapid shrinkage can induce cracking, while an accelerated concrete mix may be specified when a traffic opening within four to seven hours is needed. The new slab is placed as jointed plain concrete pavement and the joints are laid out in accordance with standard new‑pavement practice. To achieve the unbonded condition, a separation layer of at least one inch thickness must be provided between the existing slab and the overlay. The separation may consist of a fine‑graded asphalt wearing course applied as a nominal one‑inch depth Superpave mix design, using a 9.5 mm aggregate gradation. When the asphalt option is selected and existing concrete joints exhibit faulting greater than one‑half inch, a Superpave Scratch mix of the same 9.5 mm gradation must be placed over those joints or the joints must be milled to reduce the faulting. Alternatively, a non‑woven geotextile interlayer can be used; it is laid flat on the cleaned surface with its edges overlapped by approximately eight inches (± 2 in). If a geotextile interlayer is chosen, any pre‑existing concrete joint showing more than one‑quarter inch of faulting must be milled before the overlay is placed. Prior to placement, any shallow surface defects must be filled with an approved patching material that may be asphalt‑based, cementitious, or another repair product acceptable to the project representative. After concrete placement, a white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene liquid membrane forming curing compound is applied uniformly to all exposed surfaces at a minimum rate of one gallon per 150 ft² for slabs thicker than six inches, or one gallon per 125 ft² for slabs six inches thick or less, whichever is greater. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462)

In an unbonded concrete overlay the material selection begins with a conventional Portland‑cement concrete that complies with Publication 408 Section 704 and employs an optimized aggregate gradation. This baseline mix supplies the structural capacity required by pavement‑thickness tables and delivers durability, while permitting traffic to be reopened within twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours. High early‑strength concretes are excluded because their tendency to shrink excessively can compromise long‑term performance; when a faster opening is essential an accelerated concrete that reaches strength in four to seven hours or a precast slab using a Class AA high‑strength durable mixture may be employed instead. Prior to placement the existing pavement must be prepared to provide uniform support. Major defects are removed and replaced, and cracks are stabilized so that the overlay rests on a consistent sub‑base. The new concrete is deliberately separated from the underlying layer by either a geotextile interlayer or an asphalt concrete separator, which also facilitates drainage through daylighting or a vertical subdrain. Joint faulting governs additional treatment. Faults greater than one‑quarter inch are milled, and a nominal one‑inch (9.5 mm) Superpave wearing‑course layer is placed as the standard thickness. If faulting exceeds one‑half inch, a Superpave scratch mix of the same fine‑graded gradation is applied or the joints are ground to reduce the fault before the wearing course is installed. The overlay’s flexural capacity is enhanced by incorporating macro‑fibers. The engineer specifies a target residual strength (f150) in the range of one hundred to two hundred twenty‑five psi; the contractor then selects fiber type and dosage, typically between 0.2 and 0.5 percent by volume, to achieve that value. Because fibers diminish workability, water‑reducing admixtures are added to limit slump loss to about four inches, and air‑entraining agents are adjusted to satisfy fresh‑property specifications. These proportioning steps ensure constructability while the combined plain flexural strength and fiber residual strength raise the effective flexural strength, improving crack control, joint performance, and overall durability. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89), Ch. 10 (pp. 185–188)

Structural details and interfaces

Preparation begins with rehabilitation of any failed transverse joints, followed by thorough cleaning and sealing of all existing joints before the overlay is placed [1]. Where the design calls for it, a leveling course together with a bond‑breaker is installed to prevent bonding between the old slab and the new overlay [1]. The underlying pavement must provide continuous base drainage and present a uniformly supporting surface; consequently major deficiencies such as crushed or load‑transfer‑deficient joints are removed, while other cracks may be stabilized rather than replaced, and slab stabilization around patches is required to ensure uniform support [1]. A separation layer is placed between the existing concrete and the new slab, which can be a fine‑graded asphalt sheet at least one inch thick or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer, and this layer must extend continuously into any reconstructed shoulder and be tied back to the mainline [1]. All joints exhibiting more than a quarter‑inch of faulting are milled, and when faulting exceeds half an inch the joint is either treated with a one‑inch Superpave wearing (scratch) course or ground down to reduce the fault [1]. The overlay’s joints are cut by saw cutting in both transverse and longitudinal directions; cuts made within twelve inches of the pavement edge must reach at least one‑third of the slab thickness, while those farther from the edge must be at least one‑fourth thick, each joint having a minimum width of one‑quarter inch and being sealed after cleaning [2]. Joint spacing is determined by the thickness of the overlay rather than aligning with existing transverse joints, following the same construction practice as new concrete pavements [1][2]. Some guides state that no special reinforcement or load‑transfer devices beyond those normally used in new concrete construction are required, while other specifications call for anchored dowel‑basket load‑transfer systems that are fastened to the substrate and limited to movement of no more than a quarter inch vertically or horizontally, with a quality‑control plan approved by the Representative [1][2]. Panel dimensions are limited so that the aspect ratio (longer dimension to shorter) does not exceed 1.5 and the overall size may not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness; joint layout follows the project plan or an approved layout that avoids wheel‑path longitudinal joints [2]. Transition zones are constructed at the limits of the concrete overlay, and where existing treated base course or concrete pavement lies directly beneath, a geotextile debonding layer is placed in accordance with the specified overlap and edge extensions [2]. Proper drainage of the separation layer is achieved by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain, ensuring that water does not become trapped between the old and new slabs [1]. Concrete shoulders are provided to match the existing shoulder type, and guide rails, drainage structures, fill widening, and bridge clearances are adjusted or replaced as needed to maintain proper widths [1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 459–462)

Design of the interfaces begins with thorough preparation of the existing pavement to provide a uniformly supporting platform, which includes cleaning and sealing all existing joints, removing major deficiencies, and stabilizing cracked areas rather than excavating them [1]. Any unstable slabs or slab fragments that could cause non‑uniform support are repaired or stabilized before the interlayer is placed, ensuring the base will not rock under traffic loads [2]. After surface defects are patched and the pavement swept clean, a leveling course is installed where profile irregularities exist to create a smooth surface for the overlay [1]. The interface between the old slab and the new concrete is provided by a separation layer that must be continuous across the entire width, extend through reconstructed shoulders, and be tied into the mainline; this layer may be a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt concrete (a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing mix) or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer [1][2]. When a geotextile is specified, its edges are overlapped approximately eight inches (± 2 in) and the free edge is extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge to maintain drainage and prevent movement of the layer [2]. Existing transverse expansion joints are identified and cleaned; if faulting exceeds a quarter‑inch the joints are milled, and if faulting exceeds half an inch a scratch‑type Superpave wearing course is placed at the joint or the joint is milled/grinded to reduce the faulting before the separation layer is installed [1]. The new overlay’s joints are laid out independently of the underlying joints, using spacing based on overlay thickness and type, with panel dimensions chosen to keep an aspect ratio close to one and not greater than 1.5, thereby allowing the new slab to act independently while resting on the prepared base [2][1]. Full‑depth saw cuts are made in the new slab at depths of no less than one‑third of the slab thickness within twelve inches of the pavement edges and no less than one‑quarter of the thickness elsewhere, after which the cuts are cleaned and sealed to form the overlay joints [2]. Concrete shoulders are provided when the existing shoulders are concrete; otherwise the shoulder type is matched to the existing configuration, and guide‑rail and drainage structures are adjusted or replaced as needed to preserve required shoulder widths and bridge clearances [1]. Proper drainage at the interface is achieved by daylighting the separation layer where feasible or installing a vertical subdrain, ensuring that water does not become trapped between the old and new slabs [1].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

The design begins with a continuous base drain that conveys water away from the pavement surface, and any existing guide‑rail or drainage structures are evaluated for adjustment or replacement as needed [1]. All existing joints are cleaned and sealed before the overlay is placed to limit moisture infiltration [1]. Where subsurface drainage problems are identified, adequate subdrainage is provided to eliminate water accumulation beneath the pavement [1]. The old slab is isolated from the new concrete by a separation layer, which may be a fine‑graded asphalt layer at least one inch thick or a non‑woven geotextile interlayer, both of which prevent moisture migration and help mitigate climate effects on the overlay [1]. This interlayer must be provided with proper drainage, achieved either by daylighting the layer or installing a vertical subdrain, and it is required to extend continuously through any reconstructed shoulders, tying into the mainline [1]. In addition, the separation layer is extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge and terminated in a drainable layer or a daylighted ditch so that free drainage of the interlayer is not impaired [2][1]. When a geotextile is used, it is kept damp but not saturated before concrete placement, and its surface temperature must not exceed 120 °F; the same temperature limit applies to an asphalt interlayer [2]. If ambient air temperatures are 40 °F or lower, insulating blankets are applied until the concrete attains a compressive strength greater than 3,600 psi, and the blankets are removed so that the concrete surface temperature does not change more than 40 °F within any one‑hour period [2].

The overlay design must first provide a uniformly supporting base by stabilizing cracked zones and removing any major pavement deficiencies so that support is consistent across the full width [1]. Existing joints are cleaned and sealed, and when joint faulting exceeds one‑quarter inch a geotextile interlayer is placed, while faulting greater than one‑half inch requires either grinding the joint or installing a nominal one‑inch thick Superpave wearing course of fine‑graded mix at the joint [1]. The interlayer is daylighted or connected to a vertical subdrain to maintain drainage continuity through reconstructed shoulders and into the mainline, thereby protecting the unbonded slab from water accumulation [1]. Concrete shoulders are provided that match existing concrete when present or use an appropriate equivalent material, with fill widening applied as needed to retain required shoulder widths while confirming that bridge under‑clearances remain adequate [1]. Where cross‑slope correction is necessary a leveling course equipped with a bond breaker is placed so the finished surface achieves the intended profile without bonding to the underlying layer [1]. Safety‑related structures such as guide rails and drainage components are inspected and, if required, adjusted or replaced to meet current standards, ensuring user safety during and after construction [1]. Panel dimensions must respect lane geometry, keeping an aspect ratio no greater than about 1.5 and limiting overall panel size to roughly eighteen times the slab thickness, with joint spacing following the approved layout plan [2]. Longitudinal joints are prohibited in wheel paths, transverse and longitudinal joints are cut to at least one‑third of slab depth near edges and one‑quarter elsewhere, and all joints are sealed after cutting to maintain a smooth riding surface [2]. Construction traffic is kept off the geotextile interlayer, sharp turns and braking are minimized, and the fabric temperature is maintained below 120 °F before concrete placement to protect the integrity of the unbonded system [2]. Dowel basket assemblies are anchored so they cannot shift more than a quarter inch in any direction, with a quality‑control plan approved by the overseeing representative to verify compliance [2].

Verification and expected performance

The completed unbonded concrete overlay is first checked for compliance by confirming that the finished thickness meets or exceeds the minimum specified in the project documents [1]. All required pre‑overlay repairs and surface cleaning are verified as having been performed before placement of the new concrete layer [2]. The interlayer, whether geotextile or asphalt, must be installed with the prescribed overlap and edge extension, extending through any reconstructed shoulders and protected from traffic until the overlay attains sufficient strength [1][2]. Joint‑faulting limits are used to assess the need for additional treatment; joints exhibiting more than a quarter‑inch of fault are milled, and if fault exceeds half an inch a one‑inch Superpave wearing course is placed over the joint [1]. Joint layout follows the spacing shown on the project drawings, and where no spacing is indicated a joint plan that satisfies the dimensional criteria—aspect ratio close to 1.0 and not greater than 1.5, panel length limited to eighteen times the overlay thickness—is prepared and approved before concrete placement [2]. Saw cuts are required to be at least one‑third of slab depth within a foot of the edges (or one‑quarter elsewhere), and all joints must be clean, sealed, free of cracks, with dowel baskets fastened so that movement does not exceed a quarter inch in any direction [2]. Drainage provisions are confirmed by observing daylighted sections or installed vertical subdrains that provide adequate water egress beneath the overlay [1]. Curing practices are inspected to ensure that a curing compound is applied within thirty minutes after bleed‑water dissipation at the specified rate, and insulating blankets are used when ambient temperature is 40 °F or lower, with removal timed so that surface temperature does not change more than forty degrees per hour [2]. Constructability is further demonstrated through systematic field quality control, which includes producing cross‑sections at fifty‑foot intervals and extracting cores—especially in areas of variable depth—to verify actual overlay thickness, uniform support conditions, and overall adherence to the design specifications [1]. A comprehensive quality‑control plan that documents dowel anchorage, joint layout, panel dimensions, and interlayer installation is reviewed and approved by the project representative before traffic is permitted, ensuring that performance expectations and consistency with the contract requirements are met [2].
