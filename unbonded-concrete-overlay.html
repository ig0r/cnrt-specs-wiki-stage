Unbonded Concrete Overlay

Part of: Concrete Overlay is-subtopic-of
Unbonded Concrete Overlay is a topic under Concrete Overlay.
unbonded-concrete-overlayunbonded-concrete-overlaysunbonded-overlayunbonded-overlaysunbonded-pcc-overlay
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-30

An unbonded concrete overlay is a rehabilitation method that places a new rigid Portland‑cement concrete slab over an existing pavement that is treated solely as a uniformly supporting subbase, without relying on adhesion between the old and new layers[1][2]. It is employed when the existing pavement has lost structural capacity or exhibits cracking, settlement, rocking slabs, joint faulting, or low‑friction surface conditions that make a reliable bond impossible, with the goal of restoring structural strength, ride quality and safety[1][2]. A minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt sheet or equivalent non‑woven geotextile is installed as a debonding interlayer at the overlay limits, major defects are removed or stabilized, and joints are cut and sealed so that the new slab acts independently while providing uniform support for projected traffic loads over an eight‑ to twenty‑year design life[1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Unbonded Concrete Overlay” also include the following subtopics: Separation Layer.

The unbonded concrete overlay relies on the separation layer as its mandatory debonding interlayer, which may be a fine‑graded asphalt film or a Class 4 Type A geotextile extending across the full lane width. [1][2] The component isolates the new rigid slab from the existing pavement to prevent adhesion while providing a drainable path that mitigates faulting and ensures uniform support for projected traffic loads. [1][2]

Purpose and design objectives

The unbonded concrete overlay is used when an existing pavement no longer provides reliable structural support, exhibits poor ride quality, or has low‑friction surface conditions caused by cracking, settlement, visibly rocking slabs, joint faulting, or other moderate to severe deterioration. [1] Because a dependable bond between the old and new layers cannot be assured, the design treats the existing pavement solely as a subbase and places a new rigid concrete slab that restores structural capacity and smoothness without relying on adhesion. [1] The primary performance need addressed is to provide uniform support beneath the overlay while correcting structural deficiencies, surface distress, and safety‑related friction problems. [1][2] To satisfy the need for durable, crack‑free panels, a geotextile debonding layer is installed at the limits of the overlay where it meets the existing treated base or concrete pavement, preventing direct bonding that could generate stress concentrations and early cracking. [2] Preparation includes stabilizing cracked areas, removing major deficiencies, providing continuous base drainage, applying a leveling course with a bond breaker and cross‑slope correction, and sealing existing joints to ensure a uniformly supporting layer for the new slab. [1] By delivering an independent concrete layer that can act without reliance on the deteriorated base, the unbonded overlay serves as either a minor or major rehabilitation strategy that extends service life, improves rideability, and enhances safety. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 472–474)

The unbonded concrete overlay is intended to provide a rehabilitated pavement surface that restores and augments structural capacity while delivering a smooth, low‑friction ride for vehicles. [1] It must supply sufficient flexural strength to accommodate projected traffic loads, correct existing surface distress, improve vehicle comfort, and eliminate low‑friction conditions. [1][2] Structurally the overlay requires a uniformly supporting base; major deficiencies in the existing pavement are removed or replaced, whereas cracked areas may be stabilized rather than excavated, and the concrete panels are limited to an aspect ratio near unity not exceeding 1.5 with overall dimensions no greater than eighteen times the overlay thickness. [1][2] Joints are spaced as shown on plans, saw cuts must penetrate at least one‑third of slab depth near edges and one‑quarter elsewhere, and longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths. [2] A separation interlayer of at least one inch of fine‑graded asphalt sheet or an equivalent geotextile is placed to prevent bonding between old and new slabs, and joint preparation includes milling joints faulted more than ¼ inch and applying a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing course or grinding when faulting exceeds ½ inch. [1][2] The overlay thickness is selected to satisfy the minimum required for unbonded overlays after accounting for future traffic and effective existing pavement thickness, providing a design life of no less than eight years and not exceeding twenty years. [1] Durability is achieved by stabilizing moving or faulted slabs, ensuring adequate drainage of the interlayer through daylighting or vertical subdrain, limiting reflective cracking with the separation layer, resisting shrinkage, freeze‑thaw, and plastic deformation, applying a white PAMS liquid membrane curing compound at the prescribed rate, protecting joint faces, insulating when temperatures fall below 40 °F, limiting surface temperature change to less than 40 °F per hour, and sealing all transverse and longitudinal joints after cleaning debris. [1][2] Safety is addressed by preventing slab rocking, pumping, and faulting, maintaining high joint load‑transfer efficiency, keeping joints clean of deleterious material, delivering a crack‑free surface that meets ride‑quality expectations, and treating any cracking outside the sawed joints as defective. [1][2] Serviceability objectives are met by reducing crack widths, slowing overall crack deterioration, preserving smoothness throughout the design life, and extending pavement serviceability through reduced crack deterioration and maintained joint or crack load‑transfer efficiency. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 472–474)

Scope and applicability

An unbonded concrete overlay is appropriate for any existing pavement—whether Portland‑cement concrete, an all‑asphalt surface, or a composite asphalt‑concrete structure—that exhibits moderate to severe deterioration, including loss of structural capacity, faulting, visible rocking slabs, spalling, stripping, or other deficiencies that make bonding unreliable. The existing layer must be capable of being made into a uniformly supporting base; major defects are removed and the remaining surface is stabilized by cleaning, filling cracks with asphalt material, applying leveling courses, or using full‑depth repairs where necessary to achieve uniform support. When these preparation steps provide a stable platform, the overlay can be placed without bonding. A separation layer of at least one inch of fine‑graded asphalt or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile is required between the old and new concrete. The overlay itself is a rigid Portland‑cement concrete slab whose thickness is determined by the pavement‑thickness required for future traffic, using the same design procedures as bonded overlays and targeting a structural life of eight to twenty years. This method is used in rehabilitation projects that aim to restore ride quality, increase structural capacity, or improve friction without relying on bond‑related constraints, and it is suitable where the underlying pavement will serve only as support rather than share load. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188)

An unbonded concrete overlay is only the preferred rehabilitation technique when the existing slab cannot serve as a uniform, stable subbase and the pavement condition ranges from moderate to severe deterioration. When the roadway segment requiring treatment exceeds roughly five hundred feet in length, or when intermittent patches would leave ten feet or less of original concrete between them over a stretch of about five hundred feet, the overlay becomes unsuitable; in such cases a full‑depth reconstruction or a conventional jointed concrete pavement is called for. Likewise, if more than five hundred feet of all lanes must be replaced, a design analysis will usually indicate that a thicker slab depth – and therefore a traditional jointed reinforced or plain concrete system – is more appropriate. When the existing concrete is in fair‑to‑excellent condition, the subgrade is stable, and drainage functions properly, a bonded overlay is favored because it incorporates the old slab into the structural system, permits a thinner placement, and avoids raising the roadway grade; this approach also handles surface spalling effectively. If the project intent includes fracturing the existing slab, the department recommends an asphalt overlay designed with the “AC over Fractured PCC” module rather than an unbonded concrete overlay. Finally, when the underlying pavement cannot provide relatively uniform support or cannot be repaired cost‑effectively, or when core testing shows no significant stripping and the pavement is in good condition, a bonded concrete overlay—or alternative rehabilitation method such as cracking and seating—should be selected instead of an unbonded overlay. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 10 (pp. 185–188)

Design inputs and requirements

Design of an unbonded concrete overlay begins with a structural evaluation of the existing pavement, which must include falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD) testing before any design calculations are performed. The existing slab condition is also examined; if movement, faulting, or rocking slabs are observed, stabilization or jacking is required prior to placement of the overlay. Adequate subdrainage must be confirmed where subsurface drainage problems exist, and full‑depth patching of the original pavement with concrete or asphalt base course is performed when approved. The source does not specify traffic volumes, material property data, or broader environmental or geotechnical investigations beyond these items. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Design of an unbonded concrete overlay is performed in accordance with Chapter 10, which requires cleaning and sealing all existing joints before placement and providing a leveling course that incorporates a bond‑breaker to correct cross‑slope [1]. When the existing shoulders are concrete, new concrete shoulders must be installed; otherwise the shoulder type may follow the existing configuration, with fill widening used to preserve required shoulder widths, bridge clearances, guide‑rail locations and drainage structures [1]. The overlay must be placed on a uniformly supporting base, requiring removal and replacement of any major deficiencies in the underlying slab while cracked areas may be stabilized rather than excavated, satisfying the stabilization performance requirement of the preparation work [1]. Surface preparation mandates a fine‑graded minimum one‑inch asphalt separation layer or an approved non‑woven geotextile interlayer as specified in Publication 408 Section 548, and joint detailing follows the procedures of Publication 72M RC‑20M [1]. Prior to design a falling weight deflectometer test is required on the existing pavement, and any necessary full‑depth patching, slab stabilization or subdrainage improvements must be completed in accordance with Chapter 4.9 [1]. Structural design follows the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures, using rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 for ESAL calculations and traffic inputs per Chapter 7 to compute daily 18‑kip ESALs; all overlay designs must achieve a minimum structural design life of eight years and may not exceed twenty years [1]. Required overlay thickness is obtained by determining the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic (Table 10.8) and the Existing Effective Pavement Thickness (Table 10.9) and confirming that the result meets or exceeds the minimum shown in Table 10.10 [1]. Joint preparation must observe fault‑level limits, with joints exhibiting more than a quarter‑inch of faulting milled and those exceeding half an inch either receiving a Superpave wearing course or being ground down [1]. The interlayer, whether geotextile or asphalt concrete, must be properly drained by daylighting or a vertical subdrain, extend through any reconstructed shoulder and be tied to the mainline [1]. Cross‑sections are prepared at fifty‑foot intervals for material quantity estimation, and each design requires completion of Form D‑4332 for district review and FHWA concurrence where applicable [1]. Selection of an unbonded overlay is driven primarily by the condition of the existing pavement; cores must confirm that underlying asphalt stripping or deterioration is present before specifying an unbonded system [1]. When macrofibers are incorporated, their contribution is quantified by residual strength (f150) measured in accordance with ASTM C1609 and incorporated into the effective flexural strength used in structural calculations [1]. Panel dimensions for the overlay are limited to no more than eighteen times the overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter side ratio shall not exceed 1.5, with a target near unity [2]. Joint spacing follows the approved project plan or department layout and is arranged to avoid longitudinal joints in wheel paths [2]. Saw cuts defining the overlay joints must penetrate at least one‑third of slab depth within twelve inches of the edge and at least one‑quarter depth elsewhere, be cleaned of deleterious material and sealed promptly [2]. Curing requires either application of a white PAMS liquid membrane compound at a minimum rate of one gallon per 150 ft² for pavements thicker than six inches (or per 125 ft² for slabs six inches or thinner) or removal of insulating blankets so that surface temperature change does not exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit in any hour [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 472–474)

Design methods and criteria

The design process for an unbonded concrete overlay begins with a falling‑weight deflectometer test on the existing Portland cement concrete pavement to quantify its structural capacity, after which any major deficiencies are removed and cracked sections may be stabilized rather than excavated to provide a uniformly supporting platform[1]. Surface preparation includes full‑depth patching of the original slab with either concrete or an approved asphalt base course, slab stabilization or jacking where movement is present, provision of continuous subdrainage, and placement of a leveling course that incorporates a bond breaker when required; all existing joints are cleaned and sealed before overlay placement[1]. To achieve structural separation a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt layer or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer is installed between the old concrete and the new overlay, with the latter case requiring milling of joints that fault more than one‑quarter inch and provision of a nominal one‑inch Superpave wearing course of 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix as part of the separation system[1]. Joint layout for the overlay follows the standards of Publication 72M, selecting joint spacing according to overlay thickness rather than aligning with existing joints, while transverse expansion joints are located, precut in fresh concrete, and full‑depth saw cuts are made on each side of the joint to a depth of at least one‑third of the overlay thickness within 12 inches of the edge and at least one‑fourth elsewhere; all joints are sealed and panel dimensions are derived from lane geometry so that the aspect ratio approaches 1.0, never exceeds 1.5, and overall panel size does not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness[1][2]. The timing of these saw cuts is determined with FHWA’s HIPERPAV or equivalent software to establish a project‑specific sawing window, and curing is performed by applying a white liquid membrane‑forming compound at prescribed rates and protecting the surface during subsequent operations[2]. Design calculations are carried out using the DARWin software package, which implements the 1993 AASHTO mechanistic‑empirical pavement design procedures; existing asphalt layers are treated solely as support without structural contribution, equivalent single axle loads are derived from the rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors, and the required overlay thickness is obtained from the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic table after determining the Existing Effective Pavement Thickness, with the result checked against the minimum permissible thickness table[1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–186); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 472–474)

An unbonded concrete overlay is designed in strict accordance with the procedures set out for such overlays, requiring that all existing joints be cleaned and sealed, a continuous pavement‑base drainage system be provided, and a leveling course with an appropriate bond breaker be installed wherever profile correction is needed [1]. Concrete shoulders are required when the existing shoulders are concrete; otherwise the overlay must match the type of the existing shoulder while preserving proper shoulder widths and bridge clearances, and any widening must maintain these dimensions [1]. The design assumes that the underlying pavement serves only as a stable platform and contributes no structural capacity, making uniform support essential for the new slab [1]. Major defects such as severely deteriorated joints or crushed sections must be removed and replaced, whereas less severe cracks may be stabilized rather than excavated, and spalled concrete can be cleaned and filled with asphalt material before placement of the overlay [1]. Uniform support is further ensured by placing a fine‑graded asphalt separation layer at least one inch thick, or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer, over the existing slab; this layer must extend through any reconstructed shoulders and provide drainage that can be achieved by daylighting the interlayer or installing a vertical subdrain [1]. Joints in the overlay are detailed exactly as for new concrete pavements, need not align with existing transverse joints, and joint spacing is governed by the thickness and type of the new slab; saw cuts defining transverse and longitudinal joints must penetrate at least one‑third of the slab depth within a foot of any edge and at least one‑quarter elsewhere, be no narrower than a quarter inch, and all joint faces must be cleaned and sealed [1][2]. The overlay is laid out in panels whose longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio should be as close to unity as practicable and never exceed 1.5, with overall panel dimensions limited to eighteen times the overlay thickness, and longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths [2]. Curing must begin within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates by applying a white PAMS liquid membrane‑forming compound at a minimum rate of one gallon per 150 ft² for slabs over six inches thick (or the greater manufacturer rate), and insulating blankets may be used when ambient temperature is 40°F or below but must be removed before the concrete surface temperature changes more than 40°F in any hour or until compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi [2]. The required overlay thickness is derived from the pavement‑thickness needed for future traffic as shown in the appropriate tables, using rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors to compute daily 18‑kip ESALs and combining this with the existing effective pavement thickness; the resulting thickness must meet or exceed the minimum specified for unbonded PCC overlays [1]. All design calculations rely on accurate traffic information, including current ADT, projected growth, and truck percentages, to satisfy the structural design life that is limited to a minimum of eight years and a maximum of twenty years [1]. Upon completion, the design package—including Form D‑4332—is submitted for district concurrence and FHWA approval, thereby ensuring that the overlay satisfies the required reliability, safety, and factor‑of‑safety criteria established by the overseeing agencies [1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 472–474)

Structural section and dimensions

The overlay panels must be sized so that the longer side does not exceed eighteen times the thickness of the concrete overlay, and the ratio of the longer side to the shorter side should be kept as close to one as practical but never greater than 1.5; these limits define the slab dimensions for an unbonded concrete overlay, while no specific dimensional requirements are provided in this section for pavement base, subbase, treated‑soil, shoulder, edge support, or reinforcement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 472–474)

Materials and mixture design

An unbonded concrete overlay shall be placed using conventional Portland cement concrete that meets the requirements of Publication 408, Section 704 and incorporates an optimized aggregate‑gradation mix design. [1] High‑early‑strength concrete is prohibited because its rapid shrinkage can induce cracking, while an accelerated concrete mix may be specified when a traffic opening within four to seven hours is required. [1] The overlay is treated as jointed plain concrete pavement and joints are laid out in accordance with standard new‑pavement practice. [1] To prevent bonding between the existing slab and the new overlay, a separation layer of at least one inch thickness must be provided either as a fine‑graded asphalt wearing course or as a non‑woven geotextile interlayer. [1][2] When an asphalt separation is selected, the specification calls for a Superpave Wearing Course composed of a 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix applied to a nominal depth of one inch. [1] If existing concrete joints exhibit faulting greater than half an inch, a Superpave Scratch mix of the same 9.5 mm gradation must be placed over those joints or the joints must be milled to reduce the faulting; when a geotextile interlayer is used, any pre‑existing joint showing more than one‑quarter inch of faulting must be milled before overlay placement. [1] All exposed surfaces of the overlay shall receive a white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene liquid membrane‑forming curing compound applied uniformly, with a minimum dosage of one gallon per 150 square feet for sections thicker than six inches and one gallon per 125 square feet for sections six inches or thinner; where required, a two‑coat application shall bracket any saw‑cut operation. [2] At the interface between the overlay and an existing treated base or concrete, a geotextile layer must be installed as a debonding membrane. [2] When ambient temperature is forty degrees Fahrenheit or lower, insulating blankets are required to limit surface‑temperature change to no more than forty degrees per hour during removal of the curing compound. [2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 472–474)

In an unbonded concrete overlay the material selection begins with a conventional Portland‑cement concrete that complies with Publication 408 Section 704 and employs an optimized aggregate gradation. This baseline mix supplies the structural capacity required by pavement‑thickness tables and delivers durability, while permitting traffic to be reopened within twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours. High early‑strength concretes are excluded because their tendency to shrink excessively can compromise long‑term performance; when a faster opening is essential an accelerated concrete that reaches strength in four to seven hours or a precast slab using a Class AA high‑strength durable mixture may be employed instead. Prior to placement the existing pavement must be prepared to provide uniform support. Major defects are removed and replaced, and cracks are stabilized so that the overlay rests on a consistent sub‑base. The new concrete is deliberately separated from the underlying layer by either a geotextile interlayer or an asphalt concrete separator, which also facilitates drainage through daylighting or a vertical subdrain. Joint faulting governs additional treatment. Faults greater than one‑quarter inch are milled, and a nominal one‑inch (9.5 mm) Superpave wearing‑course layer is placed as the standard thickness. If faulting exceeds one‑half inch, a Superpave scratch mix of the same fine‑graded gradation is applied or the joints are ground to reduce the fault before the wearing course is installed. The overlay’s flexural capacity is enhanced by incorporating macro‑fibers. The engineer specifies a target residual strength (f150) in the range of one hundred to two hundred twenty‑five psi; the contractor then selects fiber type and dosage, typically between 0.2 and 0.5 percent by volume, to achieve that value. Because fibers diminish workability, water‑reducing admixtures are added to limit slump loss to about four inches, and air‑entraining agents are adjusted to satisfy fresh‑property specifications. These proportioning steps ensure constructability while the combined plain flexural strength and fiber residual strength raise the effective flexural strength, improving crack control, joint performance, and overall durability. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89), Ch. 10 (pp. 185–188)

Structural details and interfaces

Preparation begins with rehabilitation of any failed transverse joints, followed by thorough cleaning and sealing of all existing joints to provide a uniform surface for the overlay [1][2]. Where the design calls for it, a leveling course is placed together with a bond‑breaker to prevent bonding between the old slab and the new concrete [1]. The underlying pavement must supply continuous base drainage and present a uniformly supporting surface; consequently major deficiencies such as crushed or load‑transfer‑deficient joints are removed, while other cracks may be stabilized rather than replaced [1][2]. Slab stabilization around patches is required to ensure uniform support before the overlay is placed, and any non‑uniform areas that have lost load transfer are replaced [1][2]. A separation layer is installed between the existing concrete and the new slab; this may be a fine‑graded asphalt sheet at least one inch thick or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer, which must extend continuously into reconstructed shoulders and be tied back to the mainline [1][2]. All joints exhibiting more than a quarter‑inch of faulting are milled, and when faulting exceeds half an inch the joint is either treated with a one‑inch Superpave wearing (scratch) course or ground down to reduce the fault before overlay placement [1][2]. Existing transverse expansion joints are identified, marked, and precut in the plastic concrete; two full‑depth saw cuts are then made on each side of the original joint, the intervening concrete is removed, and all resulting transverse and longitudinal joints are cleaned and sealed [2]. Panel dimensions are controlled so that the longer dimension does not exceed 1.5 times the shorter, with a target aspect ratio close to one, and the overall panel size may not be larger than eighteen times the overlay thickness; longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths and joint spacing follows the project plan rather than being forced to align with existing transverse joints [2][1]. Saw cuts must penetrate at least one‑third of the slab depth within a foot of any edge and at least one‑quarter of the depth elsewhere, providing the required joint configuration for structural continuity [2]. No special reinforcement or load‑transfer devices such as dowels or tie bars are specified; structural continuity is achieved solely through the joint configuration and sealing, consistent with normal new concrete construction practice [1][2]. Concrete shoulders are provided that match the existing shoulder type, and guide rails, drainage structures, fill widening, and bridge clearances are adjusted or replaced as needed to maintain proper widths [1]. Proper drainage of the separation layer is achieved by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain, ensuring that water does not become trapped between the old and new slabs [1][2].

The design of the interfaces for an unbonded concrete overlay begins with thorough preparation of the existing pavement so that it serves only as a stable platform [1]. All existing joints are cleaned and sealed, major pavement deficiencies are removed and replaced, and cracked areas are stabilized rather than excavated to achieve a uniform supporting base without resorting to partial‑depth repair, joint rehabilitation or diamond grinding [1]. Where profile irregularities exist, a leveling course is placed to provide a smooth surface before the overlay is laid [1]. At the interface a continuous separation layer is required to prevent bonding between the old and new slabs; this may be a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt concrete wearing mix or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer that extends through any reconstructed shoulders and is tied into the mainline [1]. When a geotextile is specified, joints exhibiting more than a quarter‑inch of faulting are milled, and if faulting exceeds half an inch a scratch‑type Superpave wearing course is placed at the joint or the joint is milled/grinded to reduce the faulting [1]. Proper drainage for the separation layer is provided either by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain to maintain continuous pavement base drain [1]. Concrete shoulders are installed when existing shoulders are concrete; otherwise the shoulder type is matched to the existing configuration, and guide‑rail and drainage structures are adjusted or replaced to preserve required widths and bridge clearances [1]. The overlay’s joints are designed in the same manner as those for new concrete pavements, with spacing based on overlay thickness and type, and they are not aligned with the transverse joints of the underlying slab so that the new slab can act independently while resting on the prepared base [1]. Existing transverse expansion joints must be identified, marked, and incorporated into a systematic joint layout in which each joint is precut in the fresh concrete and then full‑depth saw cuts are made on both sides of the precut following the underlying joint edges [2]. The saw cuts shall have depths of at least one‑third of the slab thickness near the edges of the joint and at least one‑quarter of the slab thickness elsewhere, and all joints are cleaned and sealed as part of the interface preparation [2]. Panel dimensions are limited to no more than eighteen times the overlay thickness, with an aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5 and preferably close to unity, while longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths and spaced according to the approved joint layout plan [2]. At locations where the overlay terminates over existing treated base or concrete pavement, a geotextile debonding membrane is placed between the layers to provide the required unbonded interface [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

In designing an unbonded concrete overlay the pavement must first incorporate a continuous base drain that conveys water away from the surface, and any existing guide‑rail or drainage structures should be evaluated and adjusted or replaced as needed. All existing joints are cleaned and sealed to limit moisture infiltration before the overlay is placed. Where subsurface drainage problems exist, adequate subdrainage is provided to eliminate water accumulation beneath the pavement. The old slab is then isolated from the new concrete by a separation layer; this may be a fine‑graded asphalt layer at least one inch thick or, alternatively, a non‑woven geotextile interlayer, both of which prevent moisture migration and mitigate climate effects on the overlay. The interlayer itself must be provided with proper drainage, achieved either by daylighting the layer or installing a vertical subdrain, and it is required to extend continuously through any reconstructed shoulders, tying into the mainline. No additional design criteria are given for broader climatic or environmental conditions. [1]
