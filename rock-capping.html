Rock Capping

Part of: Subgrade is-subtopic-of
Rock Capping is a topic under Subgrade.
rock-capping
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Rock capping is a subgrade‑strengthening technique that installs a continuous layer of durable rock—generally 1 to 3 ft of R‑3 Rock, AASHTO No. 1 or the best available rock—over the existing subgrade and a Class 4 Type A geotextile separator [1]. The cap is modeled in pavement design with an effective California Bearing Ratio of 20, providing a load‑bearing foundation that protects the subgrade from traffic stresses, water infiltration, and frost effects while allowing thinner structural layers [1][2]. A minimum six‑inch compacted thickness of the chosen capping material (rock, subbase, embankment or topsoil) must be placed within two weeks of geotextile installation, using equipment that avoids pad‑foot rollers to prevent fabric damage and ensuring proper overlap and repair of any seams [2].

Purpose and design objectives

Rock capping is employed as a subgrade‑strengthening measure when the existing subgrade does not provide sufficient bearing capacity; by placing a durable rock layer typically 1 to 3 feet deep of R‑3 Rock, AASHTO No. 1 or best available rock, the effective subgrade CBR can be modeled at 20, allowing the pavement design to rely on a stronger foundation and avoid overly thick structural layers [1]. In areas where Foamed Glass Aggregate (FGA) is used, rock capping is required to provide a protective, load‑bearing layer over the FGA so that the subgrade can support construction traffic and resist water infiltration; consequently an evaluation of the capping material thickness must be performed before the area is used as a haul road, and at least six inches compacted thickness of capping material must be placed to act as ballast above flood elevation and protect the underlying geotextile [2].

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 173–174)

The rock‑capped system must function to protect the underlying subgrade by providing a continuous, minimum six inches compacted layer of approved capping material (embankment, subbase, topsoil or rock) over the foamed glass aggregate. Structurally it shall be installed promptly—within two weeks after the geotextile is placed—and must avoid any damage to the geotextile during blading, compaction, or traffic, prohibiting pad‑foot rollers and requiring repair of any tears before fill placement. Durability requirements include limiting traffic on exposed FGA, repairing rutting deeper than four inches, and ensuring that capping thickness is evaluated and approved before the area can serve as a haul road. Safety considerations demand storage away from flood‑prone zones, removal of standing water or ice prior to placement, and prevention of storm‑water accumulation until sufficient ballast exists. Serviceability is achieved by maintaining the specified compaction schedule and protecting the geotextile throughout construction. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 173–174)

Scope and applicability

Rock capping may be employed as a subgrade‑strengthening technique when the existing subgrade CBR is low enough to merit improvement—specifically when the engineer determines that strengthening is cost‑effective for subgrades below or above a CBR of 5 and the project involves at least 25,000 SY on a Traffic Pattern Group 1 to 4. The treatment is modeled using a CBR value of 20 and typically consists of a 1‑to‑3‑foot layer of R‑3 Rock, AASHTO No. 1 or best available rock placed over the subgrade. Rock capping is also appropriate when Foamed Glass Aggregate (FGA) is installed beneath a Class 4, Type A geotextile and rock is selected as the capping material. In that situation at least six inches compacted thickness of rock must be placed over the FGA in all locations within two weeks after the geotextile placement, and pad‑foot rollers shall not be used for compacting the rock so that the geotextile remains undamaged. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 173–174)

Rock capping becomes less attractive when suitable rock cannot be sourced, when the subgrade CBR is already above a CBR of 5 so that strengthening may not be needed, or when the project does not meet the cost‑benefit threshold of at least 25,000 SY on a Traffic Pattern Group (TPG) of 1 to 4; in such cases alternatives such as lime or cement stabilization, geogrids, or geotextiles should be evaluated. Additionally, rock capping is limited to a typical thickness of 1 to 3 feet and is modeled with a CBR = 20, so projects requiring different depths or modulus assumptions may also favor other treatments. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Design inputs and requirements

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10), rock capping design must be based on locally available durable rock obtained from project excavation, borrow pits, or a quarry, and the cap is typically placed at a thickness of one to three feet using R‑3 Rock, AASHTO No. 1, or the best available rock [1]. The same manual requires that the subgrade beneath the cap be modeled with a California Bearing Ratio value of twenty, and when lime or cement stabilization is incorporated, a structural coefficient of 0.12 shall be applied [1]. Both the Pavement Policy Manual and Publication 408/2020 SPECIFICATIONS require the inclusion of a Class 4, Type A geotextile as a separation layer in all rock‑capped treatments [1][2]. Publication 408/2020 SPECIFICATIONS specifies that the capping material placed over foamed glass aggregate must be compacted to at least six inches and may consist of embankment, subbase, topsoil, or rock [2]. The specification further directs that the site selected for rock capping be located in an area not prone to flooding, and any standing water deeper than six inches as well as ice must be removed prior to placement of the cap material [2]. Environmental controls in Publication 408 require that storm‑water accumulation be prevented until a sufficient thickness of capping material above the anticipated flood elevation provides adequate ballast [2]. Traffic planning under Publication 408 calls for an evaluation of the proposed capping material thickness to support the expected loads, and this evaluation must be submitted to the Department before the area is used as a haul road [2]. Geotechnical verification in Publication 408 includes bulk‑density testing of each truckload of cap material and submission of equipment make, model, and ground‑pressure data for compaction verification [2]. Existing‑condition documentation required by Publication 408 should note any damaged geotextile so that it can be repaired before fill placement [2].

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 173–174)

Rock capping shall be placed at a depth of 1 to 3 ft using R‑3 Rock, AASHTO No. 1 or the best available rock and is modeled in the pavement design with a subgrade CBR value of 20; the treatment must include a Class 4 Type A geotextile separator and granular material (2RC) to choke the rock as required by Publication 408 specifications. Section 218.3(d) requires that the capping material be placed and compacted within two weeks after the geotextile is installed, and it may be any of the materials listed in the referenced sections—embankment material (Section 206.3(b)), subbase (Section 350.3), topsoil (Sections 801.3 or 803.3), or rock (Section 850.3). Section 218.3(d) also mandates a minimum six‑inch compacted thickness of the capping material over the foamed glass aggregate in all locations, and it prohibits the use of padfoot rollers for compaction to protect the geotextile. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 173–174)

Design methods and criteria

Rock capping is treated as a subgrade strengthening technique that calls for placing a layer of durable rock—typically 1 to 3 feet deep of R‑3 Rock, AASHTO No. 1, or the best available rock—and modeling its contribution with a California Bearing Ratio value of 20 in the pavement design calculations; the design also requires a Class 4 Type A geotextile as a separation layer and granular material (2RC) to choke the rock as prescribed in Publication 408. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Rock capping shall be provided at a depth that is usually 1 to 3 feet and must consist of R‑3 Rock, AASHTO No. 1, or the best available rock; for design calculations the subgrade beneath the cap shall be modeled with CBR = 20, while any lime or cement stabilization used in conjunction shall be represented by a structural coefficient of 0.12, and a Class 4 Type A geotextile is required as a separation layer in all treatments [1]. For rock capping under Section 218.3(d), a minimum six inches compacted thickness of an approved capping material—embankment, subbase, topsoil or rock—must be placed over the foamed glass aggregate in all locations, and this placement must occur within two weeks after the geotextile is installed; padfoot rollers are prohibited for compacting the capping layer, and rollers may only be used after the capping material has been fully placed and any geotextile damage repaired [2].

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 173–174)

Structural section and dimensions

According to Section 218.3(d) of the Earthwork specifications, rock capping requires a minimum compacted thickness of six inches of the chosen capping material—whether embankment, subbase, topsoil, or rock—placed over the foamed glass aggregate in all locations. The section does not provide any additional dimensional requirements for pavement, base, sub‑base, treated‑soil, slab, shoulder, edge‑support, or reinforcement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 173–174)

Materials and mixture design

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), rock capping shall be placed to a depth of 1 to 3 feet and must consist of R‑3 Rock, AASHTO No. 1, or the Best Available Rock sourced from project excavation, borrow excavation, or a quarry [1]. The same manual requires that the subgrade model for this treatment use a California Bearing Ratio (CBR) value of 20 and mandates a Class 4 Type A geotextile as a separation layer, with Select Granular Material (designated 2RC) used to choke the rock [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification (Publication 408, Section 218) further requires that rock capping specify a single capping material selected from embankment material, subbase, topsoil, or rock as defined in the referenced sections [2]. For applications over foamed‑glass aggregate, the placed capping layer shall achieve at least six inches of compacted thickness and must be installed within two weeks after the geotextile is placed; compaction may not employ padfoot rollers so as to avoid damaging the underlying geotextile [2].

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 173–174)

Rock capping is usually 1 to 3 feet deep and consists of R-3 Rock, AASHTO No. 1, or best available rock; for design purposes the subgrade beneath the cap is modeled with CBR = 20 and a structural coefficient of 0.12 when compared with lime or cement stabilized alternatives. Geotextile, Class 4, Type A is required as a separation layer in all treatments, and granular material (2RC) is required to choke rock as specified in Publication 408, Specifications, Section 206. This combination of depth, durable rock type, prescribed CBR value, structural coefficient, separation fabric, and proper back‑fill material addresses the structural capacity, durability, constructability, and performance criteria for the pavement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Structural details and interfaces

Rock capping is defined as a layer ranging from one to three feet in depth composed of R‑3 rock, AASHTO No. 1 rock, or the best available rock placed over a Class 4 Type A geotextile separator [1]. The geotextile must be installed with a minimum twelve‑inch overlap of fabric in all directions, and each lap shall be secured by sewing, heat bonding, or simple overlapping; any damaged portion of the geotextile must be removed, replaced, or repaired using the same lap method [2]. After the separator is in place, a capping material—such as rock, subbase, embankment fill, or topsoil—must be placed to achieve at least six inches of compacted thickness over the underlying layer, and compaction equipment shall avoid damaging the geotextile [2]. Granular material meeting the 2RC specification is required to choke the rock as referenced in Publication 408 Section 206 [1]. No particular joints, load‑transfer systems, transitions, or other connections are prescribed for the cap beyond the fabric lap requirements already described [1].

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 173–174)

Rock capping shall be placed at a depth of 1 to 3 feet using R‑3 Rock, AASHTO No. 1, or the best available rock, and the subgrade beneath it is modeled with a CBR value of 20. [1] At the interface between the capped rock and the overlying pavement layers a Class 4 Type A geotextile shall be installed as a separation layer across the full lane width. [1] Granular material meeting the 2RC specification shall be placed to choke the rock in accordance with Publication 408 Section 206. [1] When rock capping is performed over foamed glass aggregate, a Class 4 Type A geotextile must be installed over the aggregate with seams overlapped at least twelve inches in all directions and securely fastened. [2] A capping layer of embankment material, subbase, topsoil or rock shall then be placed and compacted to a minimum six‑inch thickness, taking care not to damage the geotextile and avoiding pad‑foot rollers during compaction. [2] Any damage to the fabric must be repaired before additional fill is placed, and where equipment cannot reach around structures the capping material shall be placed in lifts no greater than twelve inches and compacted with a plate compactor. [2]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 173–174)

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

In section 6.2 of the Pavement Policy Manual, drainage for rock‑capped subgrades is provided by installing pavement base drains and any other appropriate drains, while moisture and climate effects such as frost heave are mitigated by removing highly frost‑susceptible soils or replacing them with a non‑frost‑susceptible layer thick enough to insulate the roadbed, and by providing a stronger base course that can tolerate reduced bearing support. When rock capping is employed as a subgrade strengthening technique, it is typically placed 1 to 3 feet deep using R‑3 Rock, AASHTO No. 1, or best available rock and is modeled with CBR = 20. Section 218.3 of the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications mandates that any standing water deeper than six inches and ice be removed before placing the underlying material, that storage areas for capping materials be selected away from flood‑prone zones, and that storm water be prevented from accumulating in the installation area until a sufficient thickness of fill or capping material is placed above the anticipated flood elevation to act as ballast. The same specification requires a minimum compacted thickness of six inches of capping material over the foamed glass aggregate, and it must be installed within two weeks after geotextile placement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 173–174)

Rock capping must be installed over a Class 4, Type A geotextile with at least six inches compacted thickness of an approved capping material (embankment, subbase, topsoil or rock) placed within two weeks after the geotextile is laid, and rollers may not be used directly on the underlying FGA to avoid damaging the fabric. Traffic on exposed or compacted FGA is limited until a sufficient capping thickness is in place, and any proposed haul road requires an evaluation of the capping material thickness to support the anticipated loads, which must be submitted to the Department for approval. Compaction equipment must meet ground‑pressure limits—tracked equipment between 625 lb/ft² and 1,025 lb/ft² for lifts up to 24 in, and plate compactors with a static weight of 110–220 lb for lifts not exceeding 12 in—and four full passes are required for each lift. All material storage and handling must follow manufacturer recommendations, be kept out of flood‑prone areas, and standing water deeper than six inches or ice must be removed before placement. [2]

Verification and expected performance

The completed rock‑capped section is verified by confirming that the capping layer meets the required minimum six inches compacted thickness over the FGA and that no damage to the underlying geotextile remains; each truckload of FGA must have been tested for moist loose bulk density in the presence of the Representative, and the equipment make, model and ground‑pressure data used for compaction must have been submitted and accepted by the Representative before work began. In addition, any proposed traffic loads on the capping material must be evaluated and the evaluation submitted to the Department for approval, ensuring that the installed cap can support the anticipated loads. These checks collectively demonstrate compliance with the specification, constructability of the placement methods, performance of the materials, and consistency with project requirements. [2]
