Stabilizing Agent

Part of: Full Depth Reclamation is-subtopic-of
Stabilizing Agent is a topic under Full Depth Reclamation.
stabilizing-materialstabilization-materialsstabilizing-agent
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

In full‑depth reclamation a stabilizing agent is an additive—chemical such as Portland cement, lime, fly ash, calcium or magnesium chloride, or an asphalt binder—that is mixed with the uniformly crushed pavement and underlying layers to create a stabilized base course with greater structural capacity than the reclaimed material alone [1]. It functions by chemically binding particles (cementitious reactions or particle‑cementing action of chlorides) or by coating them with emulsified or foamed asphalt, thereby raising stiffness, strength and resistance to moisture, freeze‑thaw cycling and fatigue while the mix is compacted to the required density [1];[2]. Proper dosage is essential because excess stabilizer can cause brittleness, and the cured layer must achieve a low moisture content (typically below 2 %) before overlay placement to realize its intended durability [1];[2].

PennDOT publications covering “Stabilizing Agent” also include the following subtopics: Asphalt Stabilization.

Asphalt Stabilization applies the general stabilizing agent principle by blending existing flexible pavement with an asphalt binder or other additives to produce a stabilized base course that raises structural capacity and enhances resistance to water-related damage. [1] The process requires controlled temperatures of 380 °F–450 °F for hot-asphalt grout injection or ≤350 °F for pre-coated aggregate seal coats, along with specified injection pressures of 25–45 psi and coverage criteria ensuring at least 90 % of the visible aggregate surface is coated. [2]

Asphalt Stabilization

Material purpose and applicability

In Full Depth Reclamation a stabilizing agent—referred to as additional stabilizing material—is mixed into the uniformly crushed or pulverized pavement and underlying layers to create a stabilized base course that enhances the integrity and structural capacity of the reclaimed product (section 2.1 GENERAL) [1]. Depending on the type of stabilization, such as calcium chloride, asphalt, cement, lime, or similar additives, the resulting layer can provide support characteristics ranging from subbase‑like performance to stiffness equal to or slightly better than that achieved with asphalt material stabilization (section 2.1 GENERAL) [1]. The reclaimed asphalt‑base mix is often treated with a stabilizing material, which may be a chemical agent such as cement, fly ash or lime kiln dust, an asphalt binder, or additives like calcium chloride; this stabilizer is mixed and reacts with the pulverized material to improve its quality, adding strength and flexibility to the new pavement base (section 5.15) [1]. By introducing the stabilizing material the reclaimed layer gains enhanced stability and durability, helping to eliminate existing distresses and providing a renewed structural foundation for traffic (section 5.15) [1]. A stabilizing agent is a chemical additive—such as Portland cement, lime or fly ash—applied to the reclaimed material during full‑depth reclamation; by chemically binding the particles it raises the strength and structural stability of the rebuilt base layer, allowing the pavement to better resist traffic loads (section 5.15 > B) [1]. Generally, increasing the amount of chemical stabilizer increases strength, but excessive amounts of stabilizing agent may result in brittleness (section 5.15 > B) [1]. Calcium chloride and magnesium chloride act by cementing particles, which gives a gain in strength, and they also depress the freezing point of the reclaimed material, thereby improving resistance to freeze‑thaw damage; generally calcium chloride is applied as a minimum 35 % solution at a rate of 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of depth reclaimed (section INTENTIONALLY BLANK) [1]. Emulsified asphalt provides water‑related damage resistance and enhances fatigue resistance under traffic loading, and it can be combined with granular materials, cement, or lime (section INTENTIONALLY BLANK) [1]. In a cold‑recycled asphalt base course the stabilizing agent is either an emulsified‑asphalt or foamed‑asphalt liquid additive that is introduced through a pug‑mill mixer equipped with a spray bar and volumetrically controlled according to milling depth, width and machine speed (section 341.1) [2]. The agent is blended with reclaimed asphalt pavement, reclaimed asphalt millings, cement and/or virgin aggregates in the proportions prescribed by the approved mix design, coating the reclaimed particles and, together with the specified optimum moisture content, permitting satisfactory compaction and enhancing the stability and performance of the base layer (section 341.3) [2]. After mixing, the stabilized material is placed, compacted to the required density and tested; the emulsified‑asphalt‑stabilized CRABC must be cured for at least two weeks or until the average moisture content falls below 2 %, while foamed‑asphalt‑stabilized material must be cured until the average moisture content is less than 2 % before any wearing or binder course is placed (section 341.3) [2].

Composition and characteristics

In full‑depth reclamation projects the stabilizing agents may be chemical additives such as calcium chloride, cement or lime, or asphalt binders (section 2.1 GENERAL) [1]. When a chemical approach is selected the material typically consists of Portland cement, fly ash, lime kiln dust or other cementitious powders that are mixed with the pulverized existing pavement to increase strength and flexibility (section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES) [1]. The most common chemical additives include Portland cement, lime, and fly ash, which may be used individually or in combination, and occasionally lime‑kiln dust from fluidized‑bed combustion (section 5.15 > B. Selection of Projects) [1]. These agents can be applied dry or as a slurry ahead of the reclaimer, or introduced as a slurry in the reclaimer’s mixing chamber through a spray bar (section 5.15 > B. Selection of Projects) [1]. A separate class of stabilizing agents consists of chloride salts—calcium chloride or magnesium chloride—which are applied as at least a 35 % solution at a rate of 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of depth reclaimed; the salts cement particles together, providing some gain in strength and lowering the material’s freezing temperature to improve freeze‑thaw resistance (section 5.15 > INTENTIONALLY BLANK) [1]. Emulsified asphalt represents an asphalt‑binder class that is used to improve resistance to water‑related damage and fatigue loading and may be combined with granular materials, cement or lime for additional stabilization (section 5.15 > INTENTIONALLY BLANK) [1]. The type of stabilizer controls the resulting structural capacity: basic pulverization yields a product similar in support characteristics to a standard 2A subbase material; calcium‑chloride‑based additives give slightly higher support; asphalt‑stabilized layers perform like cold‑recycled material; and cement‑lime stabilization provides stiffness equal to or better than that of asphalt‑stabilized mixes (section 2.1 GENERAL) [1]. Generally, increasing the amount of chemical stabilizer raises strength, but excessive amounts may result in brittleness (section 5.15 > B. Selection of Projects) [1]. In cold‑recycled asphalt base courses the stabilizing agent may be either an emulsified asphalt liquid or a foamed asphalt mixture (section 341.1 DESCRIPTION—) [2]. When supplied as an emulsified asphalt, it can be one of the specified types MS‑2, CMS‑2, SS‑1, CSS‑1, SS‑1h, CSS‑1h, HFMS‑2h, HFMS‑2, HFMS‑2s, HFMS‑2P, SS‑1hP, or CSS‑1hP (section 341.2 MATERIAL—) [2]. When supplied as a foamed asphalt, it requires accurate and uniform injection of water into hot binder to generate foam (section 341.3 CONSTRUCTION—) [2]. The agent is blended with reclaimed material—either reclaimed aggregate (RAM) or reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP)—that has been reduced so that 95 % passes the 2‑inch sieve, together with required aggregates meeting AASHTO Type A, No. 8, 57, or 67 specifications, and may also include cement in central‑plant mixes (section 341.1 DESCRIPTION—) [2]. The proportions of reclaimed material, aggregate, and binder are set by a mix design prepared according to Bulletin 27 for emulsified agents or AASHTO MP 38 for foamed agents, which also prescribe the optimum moisture level and the appropriate emulsion‑or‑foam content; at those levels the asphalt binder coats the reclaimed particles, creating a stabilized mixture whose structure is defined by that coating (section 341.3 CONSTRUCTION—) [2]. For cold‑in‑place construction the delivery equipment must be capable of automatically metering liquids with a variation of not more than ± 2.0 % by weight, and the pugmill mixer equipped with a spray bar must volumetrically control the additive based on milling depth, width and machine speed; temperature gauges on the hot‑asphalt supply tankers continuously display binder temperature during application (section 341.3 CONSTRUCTION—) [2]. In central‑plant mixed cold‑recycled asphalt base courses the stabilizing agent is similarly an emulsified or foamed asphalt that is blended with reclaimed asphalt pavement, reclaimed asphalt material, virgin aggregates, or cement in a continuous or batch mixer; the asphalt is dispersed as either an emulsion or foam and acts as the binder that coats and unifies the aggregate mixture during construction (section 342.1 DESCRIPTION—) [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–128); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 220–223)

Variations in the composition, source, processing method, or proportioning of a stabilizing agent can change the structural capacity of reclaimed base layers as reflected by the structure layer coefficient (Pavement Policy Manual, section 2.1 GENERAL | 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES) [1]. Basic pulverization will produce a product similar in support characteristics to a standard 2A subbase material, while stabilization with calcium chloride or similar additives will be slightly improved; asphalt‑stabilized layers can generally be considered similar to the coefficients used for cold recycling, and chemical stabilization that incorporates cement, lime, fly ash or limited‑use materials such as limekiln dust provides support stiffness equivalent to or slightly better than that achieved with asphalt material stabilization (Pavement Policy Manual) [1]. In chemical stabilization the specific composition of the additive directly controls the reclaimed layer’s performance; generally increasing the amount of chemical stabilizer increases strength, but excessive amounts may result in brittleness, so the type and content of stabilizer used should be determined by laboratory testing (Pavement Policy Manual) [1]. Stabilizing agents may be applied dry or as a slurry ahead of the reclaimer, or may be introduced as a slurry in the mixing chamber of the reclaimer through a spray bar, each method influencing how uniformly the additive interacts with the pulverized material (Pavement Policy Manual) [1]. Mechanical stabilization is accomplished by pulverizing the existing pavement, reshaping, and recompacting the reclaimed material as with pulverization stabilization, but also adding aggregate material or RAP material to improve gradation and structural integrity, whereas pure pulverization relies only on moisture‑aided compaction, making its inherent strength potential dependent on the original reclaimed material (Pavement Policy Manual) [1]. Using calcium chloride or magnesium chloride as a stabilizing material introduces particle cementing that yields a modest strength gain and also depresses the freezing point of the reclaimed mix, thereby enhancing freeze‑thaw resistance; generally calcium chloride is applied using a minimum 35 % solution at a rate of 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each 1 inch of depth reclaimed (Pavement Policy Manual) [1]. When emulsified asphalt is selected, its proportion in the mix influences water‑related damage resistance and fatigue performance, and it can be combined with granular materials, cement, or lime as part of the FDR mix design (Pavement Policy Manual) [1]. The performance of a stabilizing agent in a cold‑recycled asphalt base course is highly sensitive to the accuracy with which the liquid additive is metered and mixed; equipment must keep the variation of liquids within ± 2.0 % by weight, otherwise the mixture may not achieve the required uniformity and strength (Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DOT Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS, section 341.3 CONSTRUCTION) [2]. Adequate total liquid content must be maintained during mixing to ensure thorough coating of reclaimed material, and any shortfall can compromise stabilization (Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DOT Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS) [2]. After placement, both emulsified‑asphalt and foamed‑asphalt stabilized CRABC must cure until the average moisture content drops below 2 %; the emulsified type requires at least 2 weeks before a wearing or binder course is laid to develop the intended durability (Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DOT Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS) [2]. Because the mix design for a cold‑recycled asphalt base course is derived from laboratory tests on the actual reclaimed materials, any change in composition—such as different RAP gradations—or a shift in the source or grade of the asphalt binder forces the contractor to adjust the design (Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DOT Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS) [2]. When RAP gradations and composition vary sufficiently, field adjustments prescribed in the QC plan must be made to obtain satisfactory coating and the specified compaction; a change in source or grade of the asphalt material triggers the requirement for an entirely new mix design (Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DOT Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS) [2]. The proportioning of reclaimed material, aggregates, and emulsified or foamed asphalt is tied directly to these tested designs and must follow the optimum moisture and binder content identified for each specific blend (Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DOT Publication 408/2026 SPECIFICATIONS) [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–128); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 220–222)

Key material properties

In full‑depth reclamation projects the primary physical attributes of a stabilizing material are its contribution to structural capacity, expressed through the structure layer coefficient, and the resulting support stiffness.[1] Chemical additives such as calcium chloride, cement or lime raise this stiffness so that asphalt‑stabilized layers behave like cold‑recycled bases and cement‑ or lime‑based treatments can be equal to or slightly better than those results.[1] The mechanical performance of the stabilizing agent depends on its ability to develop strength while avoiding excessive brittleness; generally, increasing the amount of chemical stabilizer increases strength, but excessive amounts may result in brittleness.[1] Adequate moisture is also essential because pulverization stabilization relies only on moisture to aid compaction, and proper water content influences the physical densification of the mix.[1] The type and dosage of additives such as Portland cement, lime or fly ash therefore control these mechanical and chemical responses.[1] Calcium chloride and magnesium chloride additives provide a mechanical gain in strength by cementing particles, and they also lower the freezing temperature of the reclaimed material, improving resistance to freeze‑thaw cycling.[1] Emulsified asphalt stabilization adds further benefits by enhancing resistance to water‑related damage and by improving fatigue resistance under loading.[1] The liquid‑additive system must meter the emulsified or foamed asphalt within a variation of not more than ± 2.0 % by weight.[2] The hot‑asphalt supply must be monitored with gauges that continuously display its temperature.[2] The cured base must reach an average moisture content of less than 2 % before overlay placement.[2] The compacted layer must achieve at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density to meet performance criteria.[2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–128); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 221–222)

In the Pavement Policy Manual’s general discussion (section 2.1), the properties of a stabilizing agent are characterized chiefly by its structural capacity, expressed as a structure‑layer coefficient, with different stabilization materials—mechanical pulverization, calcium‑chloride additives, asphalt, or chemical binders such as cement and lime—producing distinct coefficient ranges that have been collected from industry literature and verified through testing, allowing classification according to expected support stiffness relative to standard subbase or stabilized layers [1]. The manual notes that the range of structure‑layer coefficients varies from values typical of subbase material to values representative of stabilized materials, for example basic pulverization yielding a product similar in support characteristics to a standard 2A subbase [1]. For project selection (section 5.15 B), the properties of a stabilizing agent are characterized primarily through laboratory testing that determines the appropriate type and dosage for the reclaimed material, guiding whether mechanical or chemical stabilization will be used [1]. In the case of chemical agents, increasing the amount of stabilizer generally raises strength, but excessive amounts may result in brittleness, so the mix design must balance these effects and classify additives such as Portland cement, lime, or fly ash by their laboratory‑derived performance rather than a fixed field threshold [1]. The full‑depth reclamation mix‑design process described in section 5.15 requires selecting an appropriate type and amount of stabilizer and applying the test procedures specified in Publication 27 (Bulletin 27) Chapter 2; for chloride stabilization this includes evaluating strength gain and freeze‑thaw resistance, while emulsified‑asphalt stabilization is assessed for water‑damage resistance and fatigue performance [1]. In the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications for cold‑recycled asphalt base course (Section 341.2 Material), stabilizing agents are measured in the field with equipment that can automatically meter liquids to a variation of not more than ± 2.0 % by weight of liquids [2]. The same specification requires that the moisture content of both emulsified and foamed asphalt‑stabilized mixes be less than 2 % before placement of the wearing or binder course, and that the base course be compacted to a minimum density of 96 % of the PTM No. 402 target density, with in‑place density determined for each 3,000‑square‑yard area according to AASHTO T 310 [2]. Hot asphalt supply tankers must be equipped with gauges that continuously display the temperature of the asphalt, and milling depth is recorded at least once per each 3,000‑square‑yard segment to confirm compliance [2]. For characterization, reclaimed material samples are obtained and tested to establish a mix design, applying Bulletin 27 for emulsified systems and AASHTO MP 38 for foamed systems; the asphalt binder must conform to AASHTO M 332 and be performance‑graded through the Multiple Stress Creep Recovery (MSCR) test, with quality‑control testing and certification required before use [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–128); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 220–222)

Behavior and mechanisms

Chloride stabilizers such as calcium chloride or magnesium chloride increase reclaimed material strength by cementing particles and lower the freezing point, thereby enhancing resistance to freeze‑thaw damage [1]. Emulsified asphalt used for stabilization improves the pavement’s ability to resist water‑related deterioration and provides greater fatigue resistance under repeated loading [1]. In cold‑recycled base courses, the emulsified or foamed asphalt stabilizing agents must be cured until the average moisture content drops below 2 %, typically after at least two weeks, before traffic service is allowed [2]. The compacted layer is required to reach a minimum of 96 % of the PTM 402 target density, which supplies the load‑bearing capacity expected for the base course [2]. Placement of the cold‑recycled asphalt base is restricted to periods without freezing temperatures and only when the ambient air temperature is at least 45 °F and rising, ensuring that temperature fluctuations do not compromise the stabilization process [2]. The specification also prohibits placement from September 1 to April 30, further protecting the material from low‑temperature exposure during curing [2]. Both chloride and asphalt stabilizers therefore contribute to a more durable response to moisture, temperature fluctuations, and mechanical stresses during service [1].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 126–128); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 221–222)

In full‑depth reclamation, the behavior of a stabilizing agent is governed by the type of process applied. A physical mechanism operates when the existing in‑place material is pulverized and compacted with moisture, creating a physically densified base that mimics a standard 2A subbase and develops strength through compaction alone (section 2.1 GENERAL) [1]. A mechanical mechanism is introduced when aggregate or reclaimed asphalt pavement is added during pulverization, improving gradation and the structural integrity of the recomposed base layer (section 5.15 B. Selection of Projects) [1]. A chemical mechanism involves wet or dry additives that react with the reclaimed material; common additives include Portland cement, lime, fly ash and calcium or magnesium chloride, which provide particle‑cementing action that increases stiffness and strength (section 2.1 GENERAL; section 5.15 INTENTIONALLY BLANK) [1]. The chloride additives also lower the freezing temperature of the mix, improving resistance to freeze‑thaw damage; they are typically applied as at least a 35 % solution at a rate of 0.1–0.15 gal per square yard for each inch of depth reclaimed (section 5.15 INTENTIONALLY BLANK) [1]. Generally, increasing the amount of chemical stabilizer increases strength, but excessive amounts of stabilizing agent may result in brittleness (section 5.15 B. Selection of Projects) [1]. When emulsified asphalt is used as a binder, its mechanism is largely mechanical, enhancing resistance to water‑related damage and providing improved fatigue resistance under repeated loading; it can be combined with granular materials, cement or lime for additional stability (section 5.15 INTENTIONALLY BLANK) [1]. In a cold‑recycled asphalt base course, the behavior of an emulsified‑asphalt or foamed‑asphalt stabilizing agent is controlled simultaneously by physical dosing and mixing actions, chemical coating and curing processes, and mechanical compaction requirements. Physically, the mix must be placed at the optimum moisture level prescribed in the approved mix design, and the water or additive must be injected uniformly into the hot binder; equipment used for this purpose must meter liquids with a variation of not more than ± 2.0 % by weight and must volumetrically control delivery according to milling depth, width and machine speed so that foamed asphalt, reclaimed‑asphalt pavement (RAP) material and water are blended consistently (section 341.2 MATERIAL—; section 341.3 CONSTRUCTION—(a) Equipment) [2]. Chemically, the asphalt binder must coat the reclaimed aggregates sufficiently; proper coating and the resulting strength depend on adhering to the approved emulsion or foam content of the mix design, and field adjustments may be made as needed to obtain satisfactory coating (section 341.3 CONSTRUCTION) [2]. Mechanically, the placed layer must be compacted to at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density, after which it is cured until the average moisture content falls below 2 %; for emulsified‑asphalt mixes a minimum curing period of 2 weeks before overlay is required (section 341.3 CONSTRUCTION) [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–128); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 220–222)

Durability and deterioration

In full‑depth reclamation projects, increasing the amount of chemical stabilizer generally raises strength, but excessive amounts may result in brittleness, which can compromise durability of the stabilizing agent. [1] Likewise, excess moisture can lead to loss of pavement material integrity and weaken the structural capacity of the treated layer. [1] Calcium or magnesium chloride as stabilizing agents lower the freezing temperature of reclaimed material, thereby improving resistance to freeze‑thaw cycling. [1] Emulsified asphalt stabilization enhances resistance to water‑related damage and provides better fatigue resistance under traffic loading. [1] In cold‑recycled asphalt base courses, physical damage from construction equipment can mar, distort, or otherwise damage the treated layer, so protection measures are required. The same section notes that susceptibility also rises under moisture‑related exposure since excess water in the subgrade or pavement layers can degrade material integrity and weaken structural capacity. Conversely, keeping the stabilizer content at levels that provide strength without inducing brittleness and providing proper drainage to eliminate excess moisture therefore reduces deterioration. The same section further states that emulsified asphalt stabilization reduces susceptibility by enhancing resistance to water‑related damage and by providing better fatigue resistance under loading. Material interactions and compatibility

In full‑depth reclamation, chloride stabilizers such as calcium chloride or magnesium chloride increase strength by cementing particles together and lower the freezing point, thereby improving freeze‑thaw resistance; calcium chloride is typically applied as a minimum 35 % solution at 0.1 to 0.15 gal/yd² for each inch of depth reclaimed. [1] Emulsified asphalt used as a stabilizer improves resistance to water‑related damage and fatigue cracking, and it can be combined with granular aggregates, cement, and lime without adverse effects on the mix. [1] In a cold‑recycled asphalt base course, the stabilizing agents—either emulsified asphalt or foamed asphalt—are introduced to the on‑grade mix and thoroughly blended with reclaimed asphalt pavement, reclaimed asphalt material, cement, or virgin aggregates, creating a cohesive matrix that binds the recycled and fresh components together before placement, compaction and testing. [2] The stabilizing agent is added at the mix‑design‑specified optimum moisture level and in the required emulsion or foam content so that the asphalt coats the particles; any variation in RAP gradation or composition may be corrected on site through field adjustments detailed in the quality‑control plan to obtain satisfactory coating and the specified compaction. [2] The equipment used for cold‑in‑place construction injects the emulsified or foamed asphalt volumetrically and mixes it uniformly with reclaimed pavement material and aggregate while maintaining adequate total liquids, and water may be added at the mill head if needed to ensure thorough coating. [2] After placement, the stabilized base is cured—emulsified asphalt requires at least two weeks or until the average moisture content is less than 2 %, and foamed asphalt is cured until the average moisture content is less than 2 %—to allow the stabilizing agent to develop its binding action before subsequent layers are placed. [2] In a central‑plant mixed cold recycled asphalt base course, the same emulsified or foamed asphalt stabilizing agent is blended with a combination of RAP, RAM, virgin aggregates, or cement inside a continuous or batch type mixer, distributing the binder throughout the aggregate and reclaimed material to create a cohesive base layer for the pavement structure. [2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 126–128); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 220–223)

The specification treats moisture susceptibility by requiring that both emulsified‑asphalt and foamed‑asphalt stabilized cold‑recycled base courses be cured until the average moisture content drops below 2 % before any wearing or binder course is placed, indicating that excess moisture can impair compatibility. It also limits placement of CRABC to periods when ambient temperatures are not too low—prohibiting installation from September 1 to April 30 in certain districts and from October 1 to April 30 elsewhere—showing a temperature‑related compatibility concern. [2]

Influence of production and placement

Construction‑related moisture conditions are identified as primary factors influencing long‑term performance of stabilizing agents, with excess water from uncorrected drainage problems degrading material integrity and weakening structural capacity. [1] The specifications also require that the cured base retain an average moisture content below 2 % before overlay placement, and for emulsified asphalt a minimum two‑week cure is mandated; failure to meet these curing limits constitutes a defect that can compromise strength and durability. [2] The amount of chemical additive applied likewise governs performance; while increasing the stabilizer generally raises strength, applying an excessive quantity may render the treated layer brittle and reduce durability. [1] Under the cold‑recycled asphalt base course specifications, liquid‑additive metering must be controlled within a variation of not more than ± 2.0 % by weight; deviations beyond this range are identified as defects that can affect the stabilizing agent’s effectiveness. [2] Proper sizing of reclaimed material is required so that at least 95 % passes the 2‑inch sieve; failure to reduce oversized material is listed as a variation capable of dominating long‑term performance. [2] Significant changes in RAP gradation or composition must be addressed through field adjustments detailed in the quality‑control plan to obtain satisfactory coating and the specified compaction, and neglecting such adjustments is considered a defect. [2] Uniform milling depth and width are required, with any section deficient by 1/2‑inch or more to be corrected; uneven milling is cited as a construction defect that can compromise the stabilizing agent’s strength. [2] Compaction must achieve at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density; insufficient compaction is identified as a defect affecting durability. [2]

Selection, specification, and improvement

In Section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES > B. Selection of Projects, the selection of a stabilizing agent begins with a thorough evaluation of the existing pavement that includes traffic level, condition‑survey results, in‑situ strength testing and proper sampling to establish the structural needs and drainage conditions of the project [1]. If the reclaimed material alone has sufficient strength potential, pulverization stabilization, which uses only the in‑place material and moisture, may be selected; when gradation improvement or volume increase is required, mechanical stabilization adds aggregate or RAP during recompaction; and when additional strength is needed, chemical stabilization employs additives such as Portland cement, lime or fly ash, with the specific type and dosage set by laboratory testing to achieve the desired strength without causing brittleness from excessive stabilizer [1]. Generally, increasing the amount of chemical stabilizer increases strength, but excessive amounts of stabilizing agent may result in brittleness [1]. The selection of a stabilizing agent is also driven by the full‑depth reclamation mix‑design procedure prescribed in Publication 27, which must incorporate the applicable test procedures and specify the type of emulsified asphalt approved for PennDOT projects; for chloride stabilization the mix design requires calcium chloride to be introduced as at least a 35 % solution applied at 0.1–0.15 gal/yd² per inch of reclaimed depth, providing particle cementing, strength gain and lower freezing temperature [1]. When emulsified asphalt is used, the agent is chosen for its ability to improve resistance to water‑related damage and fatigue loading and may be combined with granular material, cement or lime as allowed by the design specifications [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification further states that selection of a stabilizing agent is driven by the need to develop an approved mix design based on laboratory testing of each reclaimed material sample; for emulsified asphalt the mix design must follow Bulletin 27 while using one of the emulsified types listed in Bulletin 25, and for foamed asphalt it must be prepared according to AASHTO MP 38 [2]. The chosen agent also has to be compatible with a performance‑graded binder that conforms to AASHTO M 332 and is obtained from a source listed in Bulletin 15, and the mix design must specify the optimum moisture and emulsion or foamed asphalt content; if the asphalt material source or grade changes during construction, a new mix design must be prepared [2]. Specification of the stabilizing system includes equipment and performance criteria that ensure consistent mixing and curing: machinery must be capable of automatically metering liquids within a variation of not more than ± 2.0 % by weight, and the pugmill mixer’s liquid‑additive system and spray bar must be able to volumetrically control additive delivery according to milling depth, width and speed [2]. For foamed asphalt, the equipment must provide accurate and uniform water injection into hot binder and achieve a homogeneous blend of foamed asphalt, reclaimed pavement material and water that satisfies the mix design, with temperature gauges on the hot‑asphalt supply tankers continuously displaying asphalt temperature [2]. Finally, the cured base must be allowed to cure for at least 2 weeks or until the average moisture content falls below 2 % before any wearing or binder course is placed [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–128); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 220–222)

In Full‑Depth Reclamation projects the reclaimed base may be upgraded by adding a stabilizing material, and the type of additive determines the magnitude of structural capacity improvement [1]. Basic pulverization alone yields support comparable to a standard 2A subbase, whereas calcium chloride or similar additives provide modest improvement, asphalt binders give performance akin to cold‑recycled layers, and chemical stabilization with cement, lime, fly ash, lime‑kiln dust or Portland cement can achieve stiffness equal to or slightly better than that of asphalt‑stabilized material [1]. Mechanical modification can be achieved by incorporating additional aggregate or reclaimed asphalt pavement after pulverization to improve gradation, while chemical modification is accomplished by mixing and reacting agents such as cement, fly ash, lime‑kiln dust, calcium chloride or magnesium chloride [1]. The dosage of each stabilizer must be established through laboratory testing; generally increasing the amount of chemical stabilizer raises strength, but excessive amounts may result in brittleness, so the mix design must balance these effects [1]. When chloride additives are used, calcium chloride is applied as a minimum 35 % solution at a rate of 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of depth reclaimed, providing particle cementing and lowering the freezing temperature of the reclaimed material [1]. Emulsified asphalt may also be employed, improving resistance to water‑related damage and fatigue while remaining compatible with granular materials, cement or lime [1]. The appropriate type and quantity of stabilizer must be selected through the mix‑design process outlined in Publication 27, and the design procedure and quality‑control requirements specified in Bulletin 27 are required for PennDOT full‑depth reclamation projects [1]. For cold‑in‑place recycling the specification recommends that equipment be capable of automatically metering liquids with a variation of not more than ± 2.0 % by weight, and that a pugmill mixer equipped with a liquid‑additive system and spray bar volumetrically control the additive according to milling depth, width and machine speed [2]. Adequate total liquids must be maintained for thorough mixing of reclaimed material and aggregate with the emulsified asphalt, and water may be added at the mill head using a calibrated meter when necessary [2]. Quality control is achieved by measuring in‑place density of each 3,000 sq yd area in accordance with AASHTO T 310 and maintaining at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density; if densities fall short the surface is scarified and moisture content adjusted [2]. The stabilized base must be cured for at least two weeks or until the average moisture content is less than 2 %, with moisture verified by random core samples, and any remaining voids may be back‑filled with either CRABC material or an approved alternate material [2]. For material preparation reclaimed aggregate should be reduced until at least ninety‑five percent passes the 2‑inch sieve, after which a binder selected from performance‑graded PG asphalt, listed emulsified types such as MS‑2 or CMS‑2, or foamed asphalt is added together with Portland cement and the specified aggregate grades [2]. Mix designs must be derived from laboratory testing of each reclaimed component and applied using the optimum moisture and binder contents indicated in the design, while field adjustments to coating and compaction are permitted when RAP gradations vary [2]. A comprehensive quality‑control plan reviewed before work begins, together with required binder certification, bill‑of‑lading documentation and ongoing testing per Sections 106.03(b) and 702.1(b)1, provides the control needed to mitigate variability and achieve durable pavement performance [2]. In a central‑plant mixed cold‑recycled asphalt base course performance of the stabilizing agent can be improved by selecting an emulsified or foamed form of the asphalt stabilizer, adding cement to supply additional binding strength, and processing a blend of RAP, RAM, virgin aggregates and cement in either a continuous or batch type mixer to promote uniform distribution and enhance durability of the base layer [2].

Performance evaluation and implications

The evaluation of a stabilizing agent used in full‑depth reclamation depends on performance data obtained through laboratory verification of structure‑layer coefficient values that were collected from industry literature and existing FDR practices in other states and then verified by testing in Pennsylvania [1]. The type and content of the stabilizer shall be determined by laboratory testing, and laboratory mix‑design testing as defined by the PennDOT design procedure outlines applicable test procedures and quality‑control requirements that must be used for FDR projects [1]. Evaluation of a stabilizing agent begins in the laboratory where samples of reclaimed asphalt pavement and any recycled aggregate are obtained and tested to develop a mix design; emulsified asphalt follows Bulletin 27 while foamed asphalt follows AASHTO MP 38, and the reclaimed material must be screened so that at least 95 % passes the 2‑inch sieve [2]. Once the laboratory mix design is approved, a project‑specific Quality‑Control Plan required by Section 106 is prepared and reviewed before construction to provide field verification of coating quality and target compaction [2]. Field evidence is provided by non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures that determine the need for additional structural layer thickness to increase pavement capacity, and the in‑situ strength of the reclaimed layer is verified with field tests such as Falling Weight Deflectometer testing before and after construction together with supplemental Dynamic Cone Penetrometer or Light Weight Deflectometer measurements [1]. In the field the cold‑recycled asphalt base course must be compacted to a minimum density of 96 % of the PTM 402 target density, with in‑place density determined for each 3,000‑square‑yard area according to AASHTO T 310 [2]. After a minimum two‑week cure, or until the average moisture content is less than 2 %, at least three random cores of at least 1,200 g each are taken per PTM No. 1 and their moisture content determined in accordance with PTM No. 749 [2]. Equipment used to meter liquids must maintain a variation not exceeding ± 2.0 % by weight [2]. Neither source describes any separate long‑term performance monitoring of the stabilizing material [1][2].

When additional stabilizing material may be added in Full Depth Reclamation, differences in structural capacity and consequently the structure layer coefficient associated with different stabilization materials mean that the choice of agent directly influences how well the pavement can support loads and resist deterioration. Basic pulverization will produce a product similar in support characteristics to a standard 2A subbase material, while chemical stabilization using cement, lime or similar additives will provide support stiffness equivalent to or slightly better than that achieved from asphalt material stabilization, affecting design thicknesses and expected durability. Because the stabilizing agent improves the reclaimed base’s integrity it can reduce future maintenance requirements and guide construction practices that incorporate appropriate mechanical, chemical or asphalt‑based processes. Chemical stabilizers such as Portland cement, lime and fly ash raise the strength of the reclaimed base when used in full‑depth reclamation, but if too much is added the material can become brittle, potentially compromising durability and long‑term performance; therefore the type and dosage must be set by laboratory testing and applied in a controlled manner—either dry or as a slurry before the reclaimer or introduced through a spray bar in the mixing chamber—to ensure consistent mix quality that influences design thickness, structural capacity and construction quality control. Calcium or magnesium chloride additives provide particle cementing that yields a modest strength gain and also lower the freezing temperature of reclaimed material, thereby improving resistance to freeze‑thaw cycles. For full‑depth reclamation the specification requires a minimum 35% calcium chloride solution applied at 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each 1 inch of depth reclaimed, which directly influences mix design and construction rates. Emulsified asphalt stabilization further enhances performance by improving resistance to water‑related damage and increasing fatigue resistance under traffic loading, and it can be combined with granular materials, cement or lime as part of the prescribed mix‑design procedures. For cold recycled asphalt base courses, equipment capable of automatically metering liquids within ± 2.0% by weight is required, so the amount of emulsified or foamed asphalt added to the recycled base is tightly controlled, which helps achieve the designed mix proportions and thus consistent strength and durability. Accurate and uniform injection of water into hot binder and uniform mixing of foamed asphalt with RAP are mandated to meet mix‑design requirements, ensuring the stabilized layer develops the intended stiffness and resistance to raveling. The cured base must remain at a moisture content below 2%—for emulsified asphalt after at least 2 weeks and for foamed asphalt as soon as that level is reached—before any wearing or binder course is placed, a condition that protects against premature deterioration. Compacting the stabilized base to a minimum of 96% of the PTM 402 target density provides the necessary structural stability, reducing future maintenance needs. [1][2]
