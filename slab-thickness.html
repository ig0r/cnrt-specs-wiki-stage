Slab Thickness

Part of: Rigid Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Slab Thickness is a topic under Rigid Pavement Design.
slab-thickness
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Slab thickness is the vertical dimension of a concrete pavement slab that is prescribed in design to guarantee sufficient structural capacity, durability and ride‑ability for traffic loads, with an absolute minimum of eight inches and never reduced below that limit[1]. It is derived from a full pavement‑design analysis (e.g., DARWin) and must fall within the minimum and maximum depths listed in Table 8.3, often being increased when full‑width reconstruction exceeds 500 ft or when additional reserve is needed for reduced subgrade support such as spring thaws[1]. For precast and full‑depth slabs a sacrificial quarter‑inch of concrete is added over the nominal design thickness—excluded from structural calculations but retained to allow post‑placement diamond grinding and to meet joint‑tolerance requirements[2]. The specified thickness also governs related practices such as the allowable depth of partial‑depth repairs (no more than one‑half the slab thickness) and, in roller‑compacted concrete, the minimum lift thickness of four inches with single lifts limited to nine inches before multiple equal lifts are required[2].

Purpose and design objectives

Slab thickness is specified to ensure that concrete pavements possess sufficient structural capacity and long‑term performance, especially when large sections are being reconstructed; for any full‑width replacement exceeding 500 feet across all lanes a pavement design analysis shall be performed to determine whether a thicker slab depth is required, and in no case may the reduction in slab thickness result in a thickness less than 8 inches [1]. In rehabilitation work the depth of a concrete overlay serves to add structural capacity and correct surface deficiencies that compromise performance; for bonded overlays a thickness of 2.5 to 4 inches should be considered to eliminate surface distress such as spalling or rutting, while a thickness of 4 to 8 inches should be considered to add structural capacity and minimize maintenance cycles, and unbonded overlays rely on sufficient thickness to restore structural capacity in more deteriorated pavements [1]. The slab thickness is chosen to give a rigid pavement enough structural capacity to carry traffic loads and survive periods of reduced subgrade support such as spring thaws while still meeting required durability and ride‑ability standards, and the designer must determine the combination of design variables that results in the most economical rigid pavement design; mitigation of differential frost heave through increased structural strength design (thickness) has limited success for rigid pavements [1]. In full‑depth precast concrete pavement systems the slab is required to be one‑quarter inch thicker than the nominal design thickness so that a sacrificial layer of concrete is available for post‑placement trimming; this extra material can be removed by diamond grinding up to the sacrificial amount, allowing the finished slab surface and joint elevations to meet prescribed vertical differential tolerances and thereby achieve the intended load‑transfer and structural performance [2]. For partial‑depth repairs slab thickness governs how deep a repair may be cut into an existing concrete pavement, with the design calling for the repair depth to be no greater than half the slab thickness so that sufficient un‑damaged concrete remains to carry traffic loads; when a required repair exceeds that half‑thickness threshold the pavement must instead receive a full‑depth patch or corner repair to preserve structural integrity [2]. In roller‑compacted concrete pavements the slab‑thickness (lift‑thickness) requirement is intended to ensure that the pavement can meet its required field density and compaction, which are essential for structural performance; accordingly, a minimum lift thickness of 4 inches is prescribed, single lifts may be between 4 inches and less than or equal to 9 inches, and any pavement thicker than 9 inches must be placed in multiple lifts of equal thickness so that the density requirements can be achieved [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 8 (p. 164); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 370–435)

The calculated slab thickness must provide sufficient structural reserve to endure the reduced subgrade support that occurs during spring thaws while also meeting the required durability and rideability (serviceability) qualities. In addition, the designer should select a thickness that yields the most economical rigid pavement design yet remains within the minimum and maximum depth limits given in Table 8.3. Slab Thickness shall incorporate an additional 1/4 inch over the nominal design thickness, designated as sacrificial slab thickness; this extra concrete is not counted in the structural design calculations or reinforcement cover and must remain no more than 1/4 inch after any diamond grinding performed to achieve surface tolerance. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 8 (p. 164); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Scope and applicability

Slab Thickness is employed as a design parameter for jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) and for precast concrete paving systems, both non‑prestressed and prestressed, when reconstructing lanes of 500 feet or more or when a precast system is selected for high average daily traffic or time‑critical repairs; the required thickness for non‑prestressed precast slabs shall be taken from the conventional JPCP design and shown on plans, while prestressing may be used to achieve a thinner cross‑section with reductions of up to 4 inches relative to non‑prestressed designs but never resulting in a thickness less than 8 inches, and the designer must specify the final slab thickness for the prestressed pavement within those limits [1]. Partial‑depth repairs of existing concrete slabs are appropriate for transverse or longitudinal joints, cracks and interior spalling provided that the repair depth does not exceed one half of the slab thickness; when the required repair depth is greater than one half of the slab thickness a full‑depth concrete pavement patch must be used [1][2]. For Portland cement concrete (JPCP) overlays placed over existing asphalt or composite pavements, thin‑bonded overlays are specified at 2.5 to 4 inches depth to correct surface deficiencies and eliminate rutting, whereas standard bonded overlays are specified at 4 to 8 inches depth to add structural capacity and reduce maintenance cycles, with the choice of bonded or unbonded treatment depending on whether the existing pavement is in good‑to‑fair condition or moderately to significantly deteriorated [1]. When the DARWin program calculates a required slab thickness, the resulting value is rounded according to Section 8.11: for pavements thicker than 7 inches and with a quantity less than 30,000 square yards the thickness is rounded to the nearest inch, otherwise it is rounded to the nearest half‑inch while remaining within the minimum and maximum depths of Table 8.3, and the required slab thickness may not be reduced by one inch if tied concrete shoulders are used because their effect is accounted for in the load transfer coefficient J [1]. For plain or reinforced cement‑concrete pavements covered by Section 500, the finished concrete thickness of each lot not exceeding 3,000 square yards must be confirmed either by core drilling in accordance with PTM No. 606 or by non‑destructive testing per PTM No. 605; any measurement that is more than ¼ inch below the specified depth is considered deficient and a deficit greater than ½ inch renders the slab defective [2]. In roller‑compacted concrete pavements, slab thickness expressed as lift thickness is appropriate when the design calls for a minimum lift of 4 inches; single lifts may be placed from 4 inches up to and including 9 inches, while any required thickness greater than 9 inches must be built in multiple equal lifts, and lifts less than 6 inches may be placed with either a conventional or high‑density paver whereas lifts of 6 inches to less than 9 inches require a high‑density paver equipped with compaction devices capable of achieving the specified density before rolling [2]. In pervious concrete pavement systems, slab thickness is used for the required test sections that verify mix design and performance; each test section must have an area of at least 225 square feet and its thickness may not be more than ¼‑inch less than the design thickness prescribed in Section 501.3(s) [2]. Long‑life concrete pavement projects apply slab‑thickness measurement after any surface corrective work and before opening to traffic, with depth testing performed in each sublot by a core or non‑destructive test as directed by PTM No. 1; cores are taken at the sublot locations per PTM No. 606 and the average lot pavement thickness is obtained by averaging the individual sublot core thicknesses [2]. When a bonded concrete overlay of Class AA cement‑concrete is placed over an existing asphalt‑surfaced pavement, slab thickness serves as the reference dimension for joint layout and panel sizing such that maximum panel dimensions may not exceed 18 times the concrete overlay thickness and the ratio of the longer to the shorter panel dimension must not exceed 1.5 [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 8 (p. 164); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–448)

When a project’s geometric constraints dictate a cross‑section that is thinner than what conventional non‑prestressed concrete slabs can accommodate, designers should turn to prestressed precast pavement because it can reduce slab thickness by up to four inches while still meeting the minimum eight‑inch thickness requirement [1]. If more than five hundred feet of full‑width pavement must be replaced for all lanes, a pavement design analysis is required and often leads to reconstructing the section with jointed concrete rather than relying on the standard slab‑thickness assumption [1]. In cases where an early opening to traffic is essential and the use of high‑early‑strength concrete would risk excessive shrinkage cracking, accelerated concrete or precast concrete paving systems are preferred alternatives to simply increasing slab thickness [1]. When a pavement defect requires a repair depth greater than half the slab thickness, partial‑depth repair is no longer suitable and a full‑depth concrete pavement patch or corner repair must be used [1][2]. The specification also limits removal of unsound concrete to a minimum of 2 inches and a maximum of half the slab thickness; if removal exceeds this maximum or damages dowel bars, misaligns them, or encounters extensively deteriorated dowels, the design shifts to a full‑depth patch as prescribed in Section 516 [2]. If additional slab thickness cannot be provided because it is unnecessary or because raising the roadway grade is restricted, a bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) inlay is preferred; thin‑bonded overlays of 2.5 to 4 inches are applied when the existing pavement is in fair to excellent condition, while standard bonded overlays of 4 to 8 inches are selected for similar conditions, and unbonded overlays are used for moderately to significantly deteriorated pavements where slab thickness alone would not achieve the needed performance [1]. When differential frost heave is expected, relying on increased slab thickness provides only limited success, so designers should employ alternative frost‑design measures rather than depend solely on slab thickness [1].

Design inputs and requirements

The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10) states that slab thickness for rigid‑pavement repairs is governed by the design analyses and PennDOT publications cited in the project specifications, and that when a full‑depth replacement exceeds 500 feet on all lanes designers must perform a pavement design analysis to determine whether a thicker slab depth is required and then follow Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards RC‑21M for jointed reinforced concrete pavement or RC‑27M for jointed plain concrete pavement [1]. Both the Pavement Policy Manual and Publication 408 require that shop and working drawings show a sacrificial thickness of at least ¼ inch, which is excluded from structural thickness calculations; Publication 408 further specifies in Section 529.3 that each full‑depth precast or prestressed slab be cast ¼ inch thicker than the nominal design thickness as a sacrificial layer that may be milled off after delivery [1][2]. The Pavement Policy Manual adds that any reduction of a prestressed slab may not produce a final slab thinner than eight inches [1]. For conventional plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements, Publication 408 Section 501.3(s) governs acceptance and defect criteria, requiring thickness determination in the presence of the Representative by drilling cores according to PTM No. 606 or by non‑destructive measurements per PTM No. 605; a core measurement deficient by more than ¼ inch triggers additional coring, a deficiency greater than ½ inch declares the slab defective, and one retest is permitted if an average sublot thickness is deficient by more than ¼ inch [2]. The same tolerance of not being more than ¼ inch less than design thickness applies to pervious concrete test sections under Section 520.3 [2]. Construction lift requirements in Publication 408 Section 502.3 set a minimum lift thickness of four inches, allowing pavements between four inches and nine inches inclusive to be placed in a single lift, while any pavement greater than nine inches must be placed in multiple lifts of equal thickness to satisfy density requirements [2]. Partial‑depth repair provisions are consistent across both documents: the Pavement Policy Manual limits the allowable depth of a partial‑depth repair to no greater than half the existing slab thickness per Publication 408 Section 525, and Publication 408 Section 525 further requires that concrete be removed to a minimum depth of two inches but shall not exceed one‑half of the slab thickness [1][2]. When the required repair depth exceeds this half‑thickness limit, the work must be performed as a full‑depth patch in accordance with Publication 408 Sections 516 and 519 [1]. For concrete overlays, the Pavement Policy Manual governs bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays on existing asphalt or composite pavements to be four to eight inches thick (Section 540) and thin‑bonded JPCP overlays to be two and a half to four inches thick (Section 541); unbonded concrete overlays must include a fine‑graded one‑inch minimum asphalt separation layer or an equivalent geotextile as stipulated in Section 548 [1]. Publication 408 Section 540 also imposes dimensional constraints on bonded overlay panels, limiting the maximum panel dimension to eighteen times the overlay thickness and requiring the ratio of the longer side to the shorter side not to exceed 1.5, which constitutes a project constraint governing slab thickness for this treatment [2]. The Pavement Policy Manual further directs that calculated slab thickness be rounded to the nearest half inch, except when the pavement is thicker than seven inches and the total area is less than 30 000 square yards, in which case rounding shall be to the nearest whole inch; the final thickness must also fall within the minimum and maximum depths listed in Table 8.3 [1]. The effect of tied concrete shoulders is already incorporated through the load‑transfer coefficient J, so slab thickness cannot be reduced by one inch as was allowed in earlier PennDOT procedures [1]. Frost design requirements are not applied to rigid pavements unless differential frost heave is anticipated, at which point Section 6.2.B must be consulted and the designer should select variable combinations that achieve the most economical solution while meeting durability and rideability criteria [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 8 (p. 164); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–448)

Design methods and criteria

In section 4.2 (Concrete Pavement Patching), slab thickness for concrete pavement repairs is established through a full pavement‑design analysis (per Chapters 6, 8 and 9); when reconstructing all adjacent traffic lanes for 500 ft or more, a pavement design analysis should be performed to determine if a thicker slab depth is required. The resulting design thickness from a conventional jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) analysis is then applied to non‑prestressed precast slabs, while prestressed precast units may reduce the required thickness by up to four inches; as a rule of thumb each 50 psi of mid‑slab prestress can be considered equivalent to a one‑inch reduction in thickness, provided the final slab does not fall below eight inches and the base condition (granular or stabilized) permits the corresponding reduction. In section 8.11 (Calculated Design Thickness), the designer enters all required input values into DARWin and activates its Calculate button to obtain a slab‑thickness result; the tied concrete shoulders are already accounted for through the load transfer coefficient J, so no separate one‑inch reduction is applied. After the software provides the thickness, it must be rounded to the nearest half inch, except when the pavement is thicker than 7 inches and the total area is less than 30,000 square yards, in which case rounding is to the nearest inch, and the final value must fall within the minimum and maximum depths listed in Table 8.3. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89), Ch. 8 (p. 164)

Slab thickness for concrete pavement patches shall be set by the conventional jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) design procedures described in Chapter 8, and the structural thickness used for calculations must exclude any sacrificial ¼‑inch layer provided for diamond grinding [1]. When a prestressed precast slab is employed, the designer may reduce the non‑prestressed thickness by up to 4 inches, but the resulting slab shall never be less than 8 inches thick, and each 50 psi of mid‑slab prestress is considered equivalent to a 1‑inch reduction; the required thickness and any permitted reduction must be shown on the typical sections and conform to the maximum allowable reduction and minimum slab‑thickness limits specified in the precast design guidelines [1]. Under Section 529—Full Depth Precast/Prestressed Concrete Pavement Slabs, each concrete pavement slab shall be fabricated ¼ inch thicker than the nominal design thickness as a sacrificial layer; this extra ¼ inch is excluded from the structural calculations, does not count toward cover over reinforcement, and may be milled off after delivery, with any grinding beyond ¼ inch prohibited [2]. For partial‑depth repairs, a patch may be placed only when its depth does not exceed half the slab thickness, and any condition requiring a repair deeper than that must be addressed with a full‑depth concrete pavement patch as specified in Publication 408; designers must first determine the existing slab thickness, often by coring, before limiting the partial‑depth repair [1]. Under Section 525, any partial‑depth repair of an existing concrete slab shall be limited to a depth that does not exceed half the slab thickness, and the unsound concrete must be removed to a minimum depth of 2 inches but may not exceed half of the slab thickness; if the required removal exceeds this limit, the work shall be performed as a full‑depth patch according to Section 516 [2]. Thin‑bonded JPCP overlays intended solely to correct surface deficiencies are specified at 2.5 to 4 inches in depth, while bonded JPCP overlays meant to add structural capacity are specified at 4 to 8 inches in depth; these limits apply when the existing pavement is in good to fair condition, the subgrade is stable, and drainage is functional [1]. Unbonded concrete overlays can be used to correct a range of pavement deficiencies, and because they are treated as new pavements on a stable platform, their slab thickness follows normal joint‑spacing tables rather than a prescribed minimum or maximum [1]. Under Section 540.3(i), slab thickness for a bonded concrete overlay is indirectly constrained by joint layout rules that require each panel’s longer side to be no more than eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and the ratio of the longer to shorter panel dimension should not exceed 1.5, thereby limiting panel size to avoid premature cracking [2]. The calculated design thickness must be rounded after computation to the nearest half‑inch, and if the pavement is thicker than 7 inches and the total area is less than 30,000 square yards the result is rounded to the nearest inch; the final thickness also has to fall within the minimum and maximum depths listed in Table 8.3, and because tied concrete shoulders are accounted for through the load‑transfer coefficient J, the required slab thickness may not be reduced by 1 inch [1]. Under Section 501.3(s), slab thickness of a rigid pavement must be verified by coring (PTM No. 606) or non‑destructive testing (PTM No. 605) in the presence of the Representative, and any measurement that is deficient by more than ¼ inch from the required depth requires additional cores at 100‑foot intervals until the deficiency is reduced to no more than ¼ inch; a pavement is considered defective if any measurement is deficient by more than ½ inch [2]. In the pervious concrete pavement system, each test‑section slab shall be poured to a thickness that cannot be more than ¼‑inch less than the design thickness, establishing an upper tolerance for the as‑built slab, and construction joints shall be cut to approximately ¼ slab thickness, using the slab thickness as the reference dimension for joint depth [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete pavement, lifts shall be at least 4 inches thick; single‑lift placements may not exceed 9 inches, and any required thickness greater than 9 inches shall be built in multiple equal‑thickness lifts, with a minimum compaction of 85 % of the maximum wet density for lifts less than 6 inches and 90 % for lifts of 6 inches or more before rolling, and after final rolling all lifts must reach greater than 96 % of the maximum wet density [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 8 (p. 164); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–448)

Structural section and dimensions

In concrete pavement patching the slab thickness must be controlled so that any prestress‑induced reduction never results in a slab thinner than 8 inches [1]. For intermittent repairs using precast slabs the designer shall limit slab widths to no more than one lane width and specify lengths of at least 6 feet and not greater than 15 feet, with a maximum length of 20 feet permitted for prestressed units [1]. In thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays placed on existing asphalt or composite pavements the slab depth is required to be between 2.5 inches and 4 inches [1]. For standard bonded JPCP overlays on those same pavements the slab thickness shall range from 4 inches to 8 inches [1]. When an unbonded concrete overlay is used a fine‑graded separation layer of at least 1 inch of asphalt must be placed between the old concrete and the new overlay [1]. If rubblizing is performed prior to an overlay, the existing pavement is broken into pieces no larger than 12 inches for Type 1 or 8 inches for Type 2, and these pieces become part of the base support for the new slab [1]. In full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs the installed thickness shall be ¼ inch greater than the nominal design thickness; slab lengths are limited to a maximum of 15 feet (20 feet if prestressed) and a minimum of 6 feet, while widths are determined by the longitudinal joint layout shown on the shop drawings [2]. The transverse joints in those slabs may not exceed ½ inch in width and shall be at least ¼ inch, and longitudinal joints may not exceed ¾ inch [2]. The size and position of reinforcement for these precast slabs shall be indicated on the shop drawings; no explicit dimensional values are prescribed [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements the minimum lift thickness is 4 inches; single lifts may be placed with a thickness between 4 inches and ≤9 inches, and any pavement thicker than 9 inches must be constructed in multiple lifts of equal thickness [2]. In pervious concrete pavement systems the slab thickness may not be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness, and the exterior formed surfaces that serve as edge‑support shall be rounded to a radius of at least ¼ inch [2]. For bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements the dimensions of each overlay panel shall not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness, and the ratio of the longer side to the shorter side shall not exceed 1.5; joints are to be sawed to a depth equal to one‑third of the slab thickness with a minimum width of ⅛ inch [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 370–448)

When a full‑depth reconstruction exceeds 500 feet in length the designer must run a pavement design analysis and may increase slab depth if the analysis shows a thicker slab is required [1]. For precast concrete units the specified thickness follows conventional jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) values, but prestressing can reduce the slab thickness by up to 4 inches provided the final slab does not fall below 8 inches, and the nominal slab thickness is increased by a sacrificial ¼ inch over the design thickness [1][2]. The as‑delivered elevation of precast slabs must be at least ¼ inch higher than the surrounding pavement so that after placement the surface can be diamond‑ground to restore the intended cross‑slope and profile [1]. The bedding layer and subbase are graded to the required cross‑slope with a screeding device so that surface variation does not exceed 1⁄8 inch over any slab length, and any permanent vertical differential between new and existing pavement is limited to ¼ inch; where temporary drops greater than 2 inches occur an asphalt wedge transition is required [2]. Jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) calls for perpendicular joints spaced at 30 feet with coated dowel bars, whereas jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) requires perpendicular joints spaced at 15 feet with similar dowels [1]. Transverse joint gaps between slabs must not exceed ½ inch and may be no less than ¼ inch along their entire length, while longitudinal joint gaps may not exceed ¾ inch; the slab width follows the longitudinal joint layout shown on the shop drawings [2]. Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only for concrete patches longer than 65 feet because shorter patches gain little benefit, and tie‑bars are installed along longitudinal joints for continuous sections greater than 65 feet as indicated in the accepted fabricator shop drawings [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Materials and mixture design

For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529—Full Depth Precast/Prestressed Concrete Pavement Slabs, 529.3 Construction), the slab‑thickness specification shall require that the concrete mix achieve a compressive strength of at least 4,925 psi and a flexural strength of at least 650 psi; it shall also require incorporation of the standard aggregate expansion‑coefficient values appropriate to the project’s ambient temperature conditions, and each slab shall be cast with an additional sacrificial thickness of 1/4 inch over the nominal design thickness. For long‑life concrete pavement (Section 530—Long‑Life Concrete Pavement, 530.3 Construction, q Opening to Traffic), the slab‑thickness specification shall require that the concrete type called for in Section 530.3(u) be used; the concrete shall achieve a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi before any traffic or heavy equipment is placed on it, and the hardened mix shall have an entrained‑air content that does not fall below 3.5 % nor exceed 8.5 %; thickness shall then be verified by core or non‑destructive testing as directed in Section 530.3(s). [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–439)

Slab thickness for concrete overlays is chosen according to the rehabilitation goal and existing pavement condition; thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavements of 2.5 to 4 inches are used when only surface deficiencies such as spalling or rutting need correction on good‑to‑fair pavements, while bonded jointed plain concrete overlays of 4 to 8 inches are specified to add structural capacity on pavements that require increased load‑bearing ability, and if additional slab thickness is not needed or grade limits prevent raising the roadway, a bonded JPCP inlay may be employed instead of a full‑depth overlay [1]. In the full‑depth precast concrete pavement slab system, the specified slab thickness is increased by a sacrificial layer of one‑quarter inch that is added to the nominal design thickness but excluded from structural calculations; this extra concrete provides allowance for diamond grinding and protects durability [2]. The concrete mix used for the slab must be submitted at least 28 days before production and demonstrated through trial batches to achieve at least 4,925 psi compressive strength and 650 psi flexural strength, thereby satisfying structural and performance criteria [2]. Additionally, the mix design shall incorporate the site’s average daily temperature and exposure conditions so that the thermal expansion coefficient used for panel sizing and joint placement is appropriate, supporting constructability and long‑term performance [2].

Structural details and interfaces

In the Pavement Policy Manual, when a thicker slab depth is required for reconstruction of adjacent lanes extending more than five hundred feet, the design shall follow the joint and load‑transfer requirements of Publication 72M using either RC‑21M or RC‑27M; RC‑21M specifies thirty‑foot joint spacing, perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, whereas RC‑27M specifies fifteen‑foot joint spacing, perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, and the reinforcement in JRCP does not add any additional strength [1]. Load transfer at transverse patch joints shall be provided by dowelling all patch joints and fully bonding the dowels to the existing pavement with anchoring materials such as epoxy, polyester binders or vinylester binders [1]. Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are only required in concrete patches more than sixty‑five feet long [1]. For unbonded concrete overlays the joints are installed in the same way as in new concrete pavements and joint spacing is set based on the slab’s type and thickness, with no particular reinforcement, load‑transfer devices, transitions or connections prescribed that depend on slab thickness [1]. In the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications for full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, each slab shall be cast one‑quarter inch thicker than the nominal design thickness, and the size and position of reinforcement must be shown on the shop drawings together with dowel bars, tie bars and their slots, which must be cast within ±1/8 inch of the indicated locations; the dowels shall be coated with a bond‑breaker [2]. Transverse joints between slabs shall not exceed one‑half inch in width and shall be at least one‑quarter inch along their entire length, while longitudinal joints shall not exceed three‑quarters inch; load transfer across transverse joints is provided by the cast dowel bars or by an approved load‑transfer method shown on the fabricator’s shop drawings, and tie bars are installed in longitudinal joints for continuous sections greater than 65 feet [2]. Optional structural details such as a wide foam gasket attached to the underside edge of the slab and slab setting bolt hardware may be placed as required by the manufacturer and must be clearly indicated on the shop drawings [2]. For rigid concrete slabs constructed by the slip‑form method, transverse joints must run the full slab width and be positioned over dowel‑bar or load‑transfer‑unit (LTU) assemblies that are staked before paving; each end of a dowel may not be displaced more than 0.25 inch vertically or horizontally relative to the midpoint of the dowel and longitudinal translation of an LTU may not exceed one inch [2]. Type B coated dowel bars shall receive bond‑breaker lubricant on at least one‑half of their length, applied at least one hour before concrete placement, whereas Type A dowels require no lubricant [2]. Saw cuts for contraction joints shall be made to a depth equal to one‑third the slab thickness and a width of one‑eighth inch, centered within ±1 inch of the dowel or LTU centreline [2]. Partial‑depth repairs are limited to transverse or longitudinal joints, cracks, or interior slab spalls provided that the repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness; no reinforcement, load‑transfer systems, transitions, connections, or other structural details are specified for these repairs, although when a repair abuts an existing transverse joint a one‑half inch expansion joint material shall be installed extending at least one‑half inch below the bottom of the repair and when it borders an existing longitudinal joint a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene shall be placed with the same minimum embedment [2]. In roller‑compacted concrete pavement, any fresh vertical joint between lanes or horizontal joint between lifts must be placed within 60 minutes of the adjacent lane or lift to maintain a continuous bond, and control joints shall be saw‑cut to one‑quarter depth of the compacted RCC pavement; no reinforcement or load‑transfer systems are called for [2]. For pervious concrete slabs, joints must be provided at the locations shown on the plans either by tooling into the fresh concrete immediately after final finishing to a depth of approximately one‑quarter slab thickness or by saw‑cutting once sufficient compressive strength has been attained; exterior edges are required to be rounded to a radius of not less than one‑quarter inch, and no reinforcement, load‑transfer systems, transitions, or other connections are prescribed for slab thickness in this section [2]. In bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavement, panels may not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and their aspect ratio shall not exceed 1.5; saw cuts are the only acceptable method for joints, using a green‑cut saw to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness and at least one‑eighth inch wide, performed as soon as the concrete is hard enough to prevent raveling, and transitions shall be constructed at the overlay limits as shown on the plans; no reinforcement is required for this overlay [2]. The long‑life concrete pavement specification requires that before any traffic is permitted all joints must be sealed (or an approved joint‑protection method used), a ramp shall be constructed when moving on and off the slab, protective mats are required after sweeping debris, and equipment operation shall avoid damaging the pavement surface or joints; this section does not prescribe specific reinforcement, load‑transfer systems, connections, or other structural details [2].

The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) states that in concrete pavement patching the interfaces between the new slab and adjacent layers or features are governed by joint and load‑transfer requirements; transverse joints must provide load transfer, which is achieved by dowelling all patch joints and securely anchoring the dowels to the existing pavement with epoxy, polyester binders, or vinylester binders. The manual further specifies that new pavement joints may be either skewed or non‑skewed baskets according to design need, and for full‑width reconstructions Publication 72M RC‑21M calls for 30‑foot joint spacing with perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, while RC‑27M calls for 15‑foot spacing with similar provisions. When only a single lane is reconstructed for 500 feet or more the new joints must be aligned with existing joints and an intermediate joint added if existing spacing exceeds 20 feet. Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only for patches longer than 65 feet, and slab thickness may be increased after a pavement design analysis determines that a thicker slab depth is needed for sections longer than 500 feet. In a partial‑depth repair the interface between the new patch and the surrounding concrete is controlled by limiting the repair depth to no greater than half the slab thickness and by inserting a rigid polystyrene board for longitudinal joints or a premolded expansion joint material for transverse joints that is equal in width to the existing joint. These materials keep the patch from contacting the adjacent slabs, create sufficient space for movement of the surrounding slabs, and thereby prevent compression or point‑loading failures, especially when repairs are placed while the pavement is contracted. When slab thickness is used to define the interface between existing and new concrete pavements the design follows either a bonded or an unbonded approach. A bonded overlay treats the underlying pavement as part of the structural system, so the new slab is typically 2.5 to 4 inches thick for thin‑bonded JPCP overlays or 4 to 8 inches thick for standard bonded JPCP overlays, allowing the two layers to act together. An unbonded overlay isolates the new slab with at least a 1‑inch fine‑graded asphalt separation layer (or a non‑woven geotextile), so its thickness can be selected without requiring structural continuity, and joint spacing is based on the type and thickness of the concrete placed rather than matching existing joints. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that in full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs the interface to adjacent layers and features is defined by dimensional and material requirements, and the slab is cast 1/4 inch thicker than the nominal design thickness, providing sacrificial concrete that can be milled off to meet joint tolerances; this extra thickness is not included in structural calculations. Joint geometry is limited so that transverse joints are no more than 1/2 inch wide and at least 1/4 inch long, longitudinal joints may not exceed 3/4 inch, and the finished vertical differential across any joint must not exceed 1/4 inch (up to 1/2 inch before grinding). Load‑transfer elements are placed in specified slots with bond‑breaker coating, slab elevation is achieved using setting bolts, polyurethane leveling or strongback beams while the bedding material is graded within ±½ inch of design grade. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements a lift must be at least 4 inches thick; single lifts may be from 4 up to 9 inches, and any slab thicker than 9 inches shall be placed in multiple equal‑thickness lifts. The second lift must follow the first within 60 minutes, otherwise the interface is treated as a cold joint and must be prepared according to the cold‑joint procedures of Section 502.3(i)2. Adjacent lanes must also be placed within 60 minutes or their vertical joint becomes a cold joint requiring the same preparation; joints placed within this window are considered fresh joints with moist surface maintenance for horizontal joints and cleaning/moistening for vertical joints. In pervious concrete pavement construction joints are created by tooling or saw‑cutting to a depth of approximately 1/4 slab thickness and any saw‑cut joint must have fines removed to prevent clogging; exterior formed edges should be rounded with a radius not less than 1/4‑inch. Test sections used for verification must have a thickness that cannot be more than 1/4‑inch less than the design thickness, ensuring the installed slab meets the required thickness before these interfaces are established. During a concrete pavement partial‑depth repair unsound concrete may be removed only to a maximum of half of the slab thickness, and when the repair meets an existing transverse joint a 1/2 inch expansion joint material must be placed while a longitudinal joint requires 1/4 inch preformed cellular polystyrene; both fillers should extend at least 1/2 inch below the bottom of the repair whenever possible and the joint can be re‑established by sawing through the full depth of the new concrete. When a bonded concrete overlay is placed over an asphalt‑surfaced pavement the joint layout must be tied to the slab thickness; panel dimensions are limited to no more than 18 × the concrete overlay thickness and the longer side of any panel may not be more than 1.5 times the shorter side. At the overlay limits transitions are constructed and, where existing treated base course or concrete pavement lies directly beneath, a geotextile debonding layer is installed as required by Section 212. Prior to placement cracks wider than 3/4 inch are cleaned and filled with approved non‑shrink grout to ensure proper bonding of the new slab to the prepared surface. [1][2]

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

For full‑depth repairs the slab thickness must respect the geometric limits established for precast and prestressed concrete, allowing a prestressed reduction of up to 4 inches but never permitting the resulting thickness to be less than 8 inches. When replacement exceeds 500 feet on all lanes a pavement design analysis should be performed to determine whether a thicker slab depth is required, and reconstruction of full‑width sections must follow the joint spacing rules of 30‑foot joints for JRCP or 15‑foot joints for JPCP, which indirectly govern slab dimensions. Accelerated concrete patching intended to open to traffic early may involve material choices that affect safety and user impact, yet those choices do not alter the minimum thickness requirement. A partial‑depth repair is permissible only when the required repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness; if the needed depth is greater than half the slab thickness the work shall be performed as a full‑depth concrete pavement patch in accordance with Publication 408 Section 516. The patch must not be placed in contact with adjacent slabs and must provide adequate space for movement of those slabs, which is achieved by installing a rigid polystyrene board or premolded expansion joint material equal in width to the existing joint. For precast concrete pavement slabs the slab thickness shall incorporate a sacrificial layer that makes each slab 1/4 inch thicker than the nominal design thickness, with this extra concrete excluded from structural calculations and cover depth. The finished slab surface must be placed so that vertical differentials across any joint do not exceed 1/4 inch, transverse joint widths are kept between a minimum of 1/4 inch and a maximum of 1/2 inch, and longitudinal joint widths are limited to no more than 3/4 inch, all of which support safe vehicle operation and proper geometric continuity. Slab thickness shall be verified either by coring in accordance with PTM No. 606 or by non‑destructive testing per PTM No. 605; any measured depth that is deficient by more than 1/4 inch triggers additional coring until the deficiency is reduced to no more than 1/4 inch, and a core that is deficient by more than 1/2 inch renders the pavement defective. Lift‑thickness limits require a minimum lift thickness of 4 inches; single lifts may be between 4 inches and less than or equal to 9 inches, while slabs greater than 9 inches are placed in multiple lifts of equal thickness to achieve density requirements, and the finished surface tolerance is limited to a variation of no more than 1/4 inch, with high points removed by grinding when this limit is exceeded. Before any traffic or heavy equipment may be placed on a new concrete slab the Representative must verify that the pavement has attained a minimum compressive strength of 3,000 pounds per square inch and that all joints are sealed unless an approved protective method is used. Accessibility is addressed by requiring a ramp to be constructed whenever personnel move on and off the slab to avoid damage, and safety further requires positive actions to prevent surface or joint damage during operations, with work suspended and corrective measures taken if damage occurs. Geometric control of slab thickness is achieved by having the Representative determine the average lot pavement thickness through averaging individual sublot core (or non‑destructive) measurements as specified in the PTM procedures. For concrete overlays the maximum panel dimensions shall not exceed 18 times the concrete overlay thickness and the ratio of the longer to the shorter panel dimension shall not exceed 1.5, joint layout must follow the project plans, and longitudinal joints are required to be avoided in wheel paths to protect users. Verification and expected performance

After entering all inputs, the designer runs the calculation in DARWin and then applies the rounding rules: the computed thickness is rounded to the nearest half‑inch, except when the slab is thicker than 7 inches and the total area is less than 30,000 square yards, in which case it is rounded to the nearest inch. The resulting value must be checked that it lies between the minimum and maximum depths listed in Table 8.3. Because tied concrete shoulders are already accounted for through the load‑transfer coefficient J, no additional one‑inch reduction is applied. The designer also confirms that the thickness provides sufficient durability and rideability and, if differential frost heave could occur, refers to the frost‑design guidance in Section 6.2.B. Finally, the design should be reviewed with the District Pavement Management Engineer or Pavement Manager to verify economic feasibility and overall compliance with project requirements. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529) the finished slab is inspected by measuring its overall thickness and all other dimensions against the authorized shop‑drawing values and the tolerances listed in Table A; any slab whose fabricated dimensions fall outside those tolerances must be rejected. The slab must be fabricated one‑quarter inch thicker than the nominal design thickness to provide the required sacrificial layer for later diamond grinding, and this extra concrete is not counted toward cover or design calculations but must still meet the dimensional tolerance limits. For slip‑form placed concrete pavements (Section 501) the completed slab thickness must be verified after placement by measuring the concrete depth either with core drilling in accordance with PTM No. 606 or with an approved non‑destructive method in accordance with PTM No. 605 while a Representative is present. Any individual core that is more than one‑quarter inch short of the required thickness triggers additional cores at 100‑foot intervals until all measurements are within one‑quarter inch, and a deficiency greater than one‑half inch declares the pavement defective; likewise, if an average sublot thickness from non‑destructive testing exceeds the one‑quarter‑inch limit, a confirmatory core is required and, if it confirms a shortfall over one‑quarter inch, further cores are drilled to delineate the extent of the deficiency. For pervious concrete pavement systems (Section 520) the completed slab thickness is first verified by measuring the test‑section thickness and confirming that it is not more than one‑quarter inch less than the design thickness as required in Section 501.3(s). After placement, three four‑inch diameter cores are taken for each 5,000 ft² and the core lengths are compared to the specified limits; the average core length must fall between minus three‑eighths inch and plus three‑quarters inch of the required thickness and no individual core may differ by more than one‑half inch. For long‑life concrete pavement (Section 530) after any surface corrective work the Representative conducts a depth‑testing program defined in Section 530.3(s); one core or non‑destructive test is obtained in each sublot, cores are cleaned of laitance and filled with the original mix or non‑shrink grout, measured according to PTM No. 614, and the average lot pavement thickness is calculated by averaging the individual sublot core thicknesses to assess compliance with the project’s thickness requirements.
