Load Transfer Coefficient (J)

Part of: Rigid Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Load Transfer Coefficient (J) is a topic under Rigid Pavement Design.

Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

The Load Transfer Coefficient (J) is a parameter defined in the AASHTO Rigid Pavement Design Procedures that quantifies how efficiently loads are transferred across transverse joints and lateral support at concrete slab corners[1]. Designers select J from prescribed ranges based on observed joint performance—lower values for good to normal load‑transfer efficiency indicated by deflection testing, higher values when efficiencies are low or when subgrade modulus is low or truck traffic is high[1]. By incorporating the effects of doweled joints and tied concrete shoulders, J ensures that calculated slab thickness accurately reflects the actual support conditions rather than relying on generic reductions, thereby helping meet durability and rideability requirements[1].

Purpose and design objectives

The Load Transfer Coefficient (J) is introduced in the AASHTO Rigid Pavement Design Procedures to address the problem of insufficient lateral support at concrete slab corners, which can generate excessive corner stress when transverse joints are not dowelled or tied shoulders are absent. By quantifying how efficiently loads are transferred across these joints, J lets designers compensate for reduced support and keep pavement stresses within acceptable limits; when field deflection testing indicates low load‑transfer efficiency a higher J value is used, while good efficiencies allow a lower J value. In addition, J captures the contribution of tied concrete shoulders to load transfer across joints, ensuring that the calculated slab thickness reflects this effect rather than permitting an automatic one‑inch reduction when such shoulders are present, thereby providing an accurate assessment of required pavement thickness while still meeting durability and rideability requirements. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 163–164)

Scope and applicability

Load Transfer Coefficient (J) is employed as an input variable when applying the 1993 AASHTO rigid‑pavement design procedures to projects that use a rigid pavement surface, specifically Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement or Roller Compacted Concrete, and therefore it applies to designs of these concrete pavement types using the DARWin software and the associated design inputs listed for rigid pavements. The coefficient is used to represent the lateral support at concrete slab corners, so it is appropriate for rigid (concrete) pavements that have transverse joints—especially when those joints are dowelled or when tied concrete shoulders provide additional support; its value should be chosen based on observed joint performance, using lower J values when past deflection testing indicates good to normal load‑transfer efficiency and higher J values when the testing indicates low efficiency, and higher J values are recommended for conditions with lower subgrade modulus (k‑values) or a higher percentage of truck traffic, while lower J values are appropriate for higher k‑values or lower truck percentages. When tied concrete shoulders are incorporated, the effect on slab behavior is captured through J during thickness calculations, and the resulting calculated slab thickness—adjusted for J—is rounded to the nearest half‑inch, or to the nearest inch for pavements thicker than 7 inches with a quantity less than 30,000 square yards, ensuring compliance with the minimum and maximum depths in Table 8.3. Load Transfer Coefficient (J) is also used when designing a widened concrete slab that does not exceed 14 feet in width and includes a partial asphalt or Roller Compacted Concrete right shoulder; this design approach is intended for low‑volume rural Interstates and Expressways with two‑way average daily traffic less than 10,000 where the need for an incident‑management lane is low, and the tables specifically call for J in the “widened lane with asphalt or RCC shoulders” case. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 160–165)

Design inputs and requirements

The 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures for rigid pavements govern the Load Transfer Coefficient (J), and when a pavement structure is designed with the AASHTOWare® DARWin® 3.01 software the designer must supply J as an input variable. Under the AASHTO Rigid Pavement Design Procedures designers are required to select a J value from the ranges shown in Table 8.2 for all Department designs; the appropriate end of the range is determined by performance evidence, so that if deflection testing of transverse joints indicates good to normal load‑transfer efficiency a lower J value is used, whereas low efficiency indicated by testing leads to selection of a higher value. The same procedures also require that the selection must reflect project constraints, with higher J values applied when subgrade k‑values are low or truck percentages are high, and lower J values applied when k‑values are high or truck percentages are low. For widened concrete lanes that incorporate an asphalt or roller‑compacted concrete shoulder the design must employ the Load Transfer Coefficient given in Table 8.2 of Chapter 8; this requirement applies to slabs whose overall width does not exceed 14 feet, that are striped at 12 feet, and that serve low‑volume rural Interstates or Expressways with two‑way ADTs less than 10,000 where an incident management lane is not needed, and when a tied concrete shoulder is used a thirteen‑foot‑wide outside lane may be considered to reduce slab deflections. [1]

Design methods and criteria

The load transfer coefficient J is incorporated into the AASHTO Rigid Pavement Design Procedures to represent the lateral support at slab corners; its value is selected from the ranges given in Table 8.2 based on observed load‑transfer efficiency from deflection testing and on pavement parameters such as subgrade modulus (k) and truck traffic percentage. When past testing shows good to normal transfer efficiencies a lower end of the range is chosen, whereas low efficiencies call for values toward the upper end, and higher J‑values are used with lower k‑values or higher truck percentages. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (p. 163)

The design of the load transfer coefficient (J) in the AASHTO Rigid Pavement Design Procedures requires selecting a value from the range given in Table 8.2 that reflects the estimated lateral support at slab corners; when past deflection testing shows good to normal joint efficiency, the lower end of the range is appropriate, whereas low measured efficiencies call for values toward the upper end. Additionally, the selection must be adjusted upward if the pavement has a low subgrade modulus (k) or a high proportion of truck traffic, and downward if k is high or truck percentages are low. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (p. 163)

Structural details and interfaces

The Load Transfer Coefficient J reflects the lateral support available at slab corners and therefore requires transverse joints that are doweled and, when present, tied concrete shoulders to provide effective load‑transfer. If these features give good or normal transfer efficiency, a lower J is appropriate; conversely, poor lateral support—such as missing dowels or absent shoulder ties—will cause the J value to increase. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (p. 163)
