Cross-Stitching

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Cross-Stitching is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
cross-stitchingcross-stitching
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Cross‑stitching is a pavement preservation technique applied to longitudinal cracks or joints in plain cement‑concrete slabs, including lane‑to‑shoulder and centerline joints that are still structurally sound but showing early signs of distress such as slab migration or emerging faulting [1][2]. The method restores near‑full (approximately 100 %) load transfer by installing epoxy‑coated deformed steel tie bars at a prescribed angle across the discontinuity after a full‑depth saw cut, allowing the bars to reestablish aggregate interlock and arrest further crack propagation [1][2]. When performed under the required temperature minimum of 40 °F and with the specified drilling, epoxy bonding, routing, and sealing controls, cross‑stitching effectively prevents slab migration, improves structural condition, and extends the service life of the concrete pavement [1][2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Cross‑stitching is applied to pavement surfaces that exhibit longitudinal cracks or separations in the joints, whether they are plain cement‑concrete slabs or asphalt layers.[1] It is also specified for concrete pavements where opened longitudinal construction joints create a pathway for slab movement and loss of structural continuity.[2] These linear distresses reduce the ability of the pavement to transfer loads across the crack, which can lead to slab migration, lane or shoulder separation, early faulting of centerline joints, increased water infiltration, and overall premature deterioration.[1][2] By installing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle across the affected line and sealing the seam, the method restores near‑full load transfer and reestablishes aggregate interlock.[1][2] The restored continuity arrests further crack propagation and extends the service life of the pavement.[1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 202–203); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 424–425)

Cross‑stitching is applied to longitudinal cracks or joints in plain cement concrete pavements by installing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars across the defect, which repairs the damaged crack and restores near‑full (approximately 100 %) load transfer. By reestablishing aggregate interlock and preventing slab migration, the technique halts further deterioration of the pavement, maintains its serviceability, and thereby extends the pavement’s useful life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90)

Distress characterization and causes

The distress addressed by cross‑stitching consists of longitudinal cracks or joints in concrete pavement—such as the cracks between traffic lanes, lane‑to‑shoulder joints, and centerline joints—that are still relatively early in condition but can lead to slab migration, lane or shoulder separation, and faulting if left untreated. Because these cracks are typically only partially developed (“reasonably good condition” or “starting to fault”), their extent is limited to the longitudinal direction and they have not yet caused severe structural failure, though the potential for progressive widening exists. The operational impact includes loss of load transfer, possible lane separation, and a maintenance problem that can affect safety if the cracks progress. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90)

The distress that cross‑stitching seeks to remediate stems from slab migration and faulting caused by inadequate load transfer across longitudinal cracks or joints, which often results from omitted tie bars during construction, weak subgrade support, or drainage failures that allow water infiltration; heavy traffic loads further aggravate these mechanisms. By installing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle, cross‑stitching restores near‑full load transfer and restrains slab movement, thereby mitigating the underlying causes. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90)

Applicability and treatment selection

Cross‑stitching is indicated for rigid pavements—specifically plain cement concrete slabs—that exhibit longitudinal cracks or joints that are still structurally sound but showing early signs of distress such as slab migration, emerging faulting, missing tie bars, or separating lanes and shoulders [1][2]. The method is limited to longitudinal features; it must not be applied to transverse joints or cracks [2]. Prior to installation a full‑depth saw cut must be made along the entire length of the existing longitudinal joint to provide a clean edge for the stitching equipment [2]. Installation can proceed only when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at least 40 °F, ensuring that the epoxy anchoring material will achieve proper flow and cure [2]. The equipment used must be capable of maintaining the prescribed drill angle throughout the operation to achieve the intended near‑100 % load transfer across the repaired crack or joint [1][2]. When applied under these conditions the treatment restores aggregate interlock and delivers almost complete load transfer, thereby mitigating further slab migration and preserving pavement performance [1]. Traffic may be reopened to normal loads only after the epoxy anchoring material has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 424–425)

Cross‑stitching is intended for longitudinal cracks or joints that remain in reasonably good condition, providing near‑full load transfer across the fracture [1]. The technique becomes unsuitable when the crack exhibits extensive spalling in the upper half of the slab; such surface damage must be addressed with partial‑depth spall repairs rather than stitching [1]. If spalling extends beyond the mid‑slab depth, a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair is required instead of cross‑stitching [1]. When the joint condition has deteriorated to the point that load transfer cannot be restored with tie bars, other rehabilitation measures such as partial‑depth or full‑depth joint replacement must be employed [1]. Cross‑stitching is also inappropriate for cracks or joints oriented transverse to the pavement direction [2]. Ambient or surface temperatures below forty degrees Fahrenheit preclude its use because drilling and bonding performance would be compromised [2]. Situations that would cause drilling through the slab bottom or otherwise damage the structure likewise mandate selection of an alternative maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair method [2]. In any of these conditions, the guides advise choosing a different treatment rather than cross‑stitching to meet acceptance criteria [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 424–425)

Condition assessment and timing

Cross‑stitching should be employed on plain cement‑concrete pavements when a longitudinal crack or joint has formed but is still in an early, reasonably good condition—essentially at the point where the fissure is just beginning to fault or separate lanes or shoulders and aggregate interlock remains intact. At this stage the repair involves installing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars diagonally across the crack or joint, which restores near‑full (approximately 100 %) load transfer and prevents slab migration. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90)

Materials and repair details

Cross‑stitching repairs longitudinal cracks or joints in plain cement‑concrete pavements by inserting epoxy‑coated deformed steel tie bars set at an angle across the opening, which enables nearly full load transfer between adjacent slabs and prevents slab migration [1][2]. The technique is applied when the crack or joint remains in reasonably good condition, with the epoxy coating protecting the steel from corrosion while the deformed surface provides mechanical interlock with the surrounding concrete [1]. The repair sequence begins with a full‑depth saw cut through the discontinuity followed by drilling angled holes on each side of the crack using a hydraulic‑powered drill or gang‑mounted version, as specified in drawing RC‑26M [2]. The drilled holes are sized no more than three‑eighths of an inch larger in diameter than the tie bar that will occupy them [2]. After cleaning and drying, each hole is filled with an epoxy bonding compound and an epoxy‑coated deformed steel tie bar is inserted diagonally across the crack; the bar is rotated several times to purge air and the epoxy is left to cover at least one inch of the bar at the pavement surface [2]. The epoxy is allowed to cure in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations before any further work proceeds [2]. Subsequently, the cracked area is routed to a uniform width of three‑quarters of an inch and a depth of one inch, then flushed, dried, and sealed with a joint‑sealing material placed between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the surface, provided ambient and pavement temperatures exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit [2]. Traffic may be restricted until full cure of the epoxy and sealant [2]. The complete repair package therefore comprises hydraulic drilling equipment, epoxy‑coated steel tie bars, an epoxy bonding compound, and a joint‑sealing material, with all associated operations—drilling, epoxy application, bar placement, routing, and sealing—accounted for on a per‑tie‑bar basis [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 424–425)

Cross‑stitching can restore near‑full load transfer across longitudinal cracks or joints in plain cement concrete pavements when epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars are installed at an angle, provided the bar coating and geometry are compatible with the concrete substrate both chemically and mechanically, and sufficient aggregate interlock remains to allow the bars to re‑establish continuity [1]. The method is limited to cracks or joints that are still in reasonably good condition; excessive deterioration or loss of aggregate interlock would prevent the tie bars from achieving effective load sharing [1]. Successful installation also requires that ambient air and pavement surface temperatures be at least 40 °F before any drilling, sealing, or placement of the tie bars, because lower temperatures can impair epoxy curing and bond strength [2]. Holes must be drilled to a diameter only slightly larger than the tie bar—no more than three‑eighths of an inch greater—and the boreholes must be cleaned, dried, and free of oil or moisture, typically using compressed air at a minimum pressure of 100 psi to ensure proper epoxy adhesion [2]. After drilling, the longitudinal crack or joint is sawed full depth, routed to the prescribed width and depth, flushed with water and allowed to dry before epoxy bonding compound is injected; the tie bar is then rotated while being withdrawn to purge air voids and to leave approximately an inch of epoxy coating on its surface, which secures mechanical interlock and chemical bond [2]. The combined requirements of material compatibility (epoxy‑coated deformed bars with concrete), temperature thresholds, precise hole sizing, cleanliness, and proper joint preparation govern the durability and performance of the cross‑stitch repair, allowing it to prevent slab migration, maintain aggregate interlock, and extend pavement service life in applications such as lane separations, missing original tie bars, or centerline joints that are just beginning to fault [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 424–425)

Treatment procedures

Prior to any cross‑stitching activity the contractor must develop a quality‑control plan in accordance with Section 106.03 and submit it for review at least fourteen days before work begins. The plan must be approved by the Representative after the contractor demonstrates the stitching procedure on a longitudinal joint. Work may only commence when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are equal to or exceed 40 °F, and after a full‑depth saw cut has been made along the entire length of the crack.

The preparation continues with drilling holes across the longitudinal crack on each side of the joint using a hydraulic drill. The holes are placed at the angles, offsets and spacing shown on Standard Drawing RC‑26M, and each hole is sized no more than three‑eighths of an inch larger in diameter than the tie bar that will be installed. After drilling, the holes are flushed with water, cleaned and dried before the epoxy bonding compound is introduced.

Epoxy is then injected into the cleaned holes and a steel tie bar is inserted. While the tie bar is being placed it is rotated three to five full turns so that air voids are expelled as completely as possible. The epoxy is left to cover the tie bar one inch above the pavement surface, and the excess material is troweled smooth so that the surface of the epoxy bond is flush with the surrounding pavement.

The joint is routed to a width of three‑quarters of an inch and a depth of one inch, cleaned again after routing, and sealed so that the sealant finishes between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface without spilling onto the slab. Traffic may not be allowed on the repaired section until the epoxy anchoring material has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations.

Payment for the work is measured per tie bar installed at each location, and all items such as drilling, epoxy application, tie‑bar placement and crack sealing are considered incidental to that measurement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 424–425)

Cross‑stitching of longitudinal cracks in pavement is performed by installing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle across the crack, a practice described in both sources [1][2]. The implementation requires a hydraulic‑powered or gang‑mounted drill mounted on a rig to maintain the prescribed drilling angle and to create the angled holes for the tie bars [2]. A full‑depth saw cut is made along the joint before drilling, and the sequence proceeds with drilling appropriately sized holes, cleaning them, injecting epoxy adhesive, inserting and rotating the tie bars, routing and cleaning the crack, applying a sealant, and finally permitting traffic only after the anchoring material has fully cured in accordance with manufacturer recommendations [2]. The guides also require that work not be undertaken when either ambient air temperature or pavement surface temperature is below 40 °F, establishing this as the minimum temperature condition for successful placement [2]. Traffic is restricted throughout the operation and remains closed until the epoxy cure period is complete, ensuring that vehicles are not exposed to unfinished repairs [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 424–425)

Quality and workmanship

Acceptance of a cross‑stitch repair is confirmed only after the contractor submits a QC plan and obtains Representative approval of the demonstrated procedure, and when all field tolerances are met: work may proceed only if ambient and pavement temperatures are at least 40 °F; each drill hole must be no larger than 3/8 in. beyond the tie‑bar diameter and positioned within ±1 in. of the specified pattern on drawing RC‑26M, with angled holes intersecting the crack near mid‑depth without penetrating the slab bottom; the epoxy must provide at least a 1‑in. surface cover over each bar; the routed joint must be cut to ¾ in. wide by 1 in. deep and sealed to a level 1/8–1/4 in. below the pavement surface, with no sealant spilling onto the slab; finally traffic may be allowed only after the anchoring epoxy has fully cured per the manufacturer’s recommendations. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 424–425)

Performance and service life

Cross‑stitching reinforces longitudinal cracks or joints by installing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle, which restores nearly 100 % load transfer across the defect when it is still in reasonably good condition. By preventing slab migration and preserving aggregate interlock, the treatment improves the pavement’s structural condition and functional performance, enhances durability against further cracking, and thereby helps preserve or extend the service life of the concrete pavement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90)

Common cross‑stitching failures arise when the prescribed construction controls are not met: drilling too deep can breach the slab bottom and requires full‑depth patch replacement; surface spalling during hole preparation indicates excessive stress or improper drill angle; low ambient or pavement temperatures below forty degrees prevent proper epoxy cure and sealant bonding, leading to premature loss of the tie bar connection; and insufficient cleaning of drilled holes leaves air voids that weaken the bond. These distresses are directly linked to temperature limits, drilling technique (angle, depth, spacing), hole cleanliness, and adherence to sealing procedures. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 424–425)
