Bonded Concrete Overlay

Part of: Concrete Overlay is-subtopic-of
Bonded Concrete Overlay is a topic under Concrete Overlay.
bonded-concrete-overlaybonded-concrete-overlaysbonded-overlaysbonded-overlaybonded-pcc-overlay
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-30

A bonded concrete overlay is a rehabilitation technique applied to an existing Portland‑cement concrete pavement (or compatible composite deck) that remains structurally sound but exhibits deficiencies such as failed transverse joints, surface cracking, insufficient load‑carrying capacity, low friction, or uneven slab support [1]. By placing a new thin Portland‑cement concrete slab directly on the old surface and creating a durable bond, the two act as a monolithic unit that restores uniform support, adds flexural strength, narrows crack widths, improves joint load‑transfer efficiency, and can incorporate macrofibers to achieve the required residual flexural strength while meeting low chloride‑ion penetration criteria for durability [1][2]. The overlay thickness is limited (typically 2½–4 inches for thin sections and up to 8 inches for structural upgrades), and its design follows Chapter 10 requirements, including verification of a stable subgrade, functional drainage, and a falling‑weight deflectometer test of the existing slab before calculating the bonded layer thickness [1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Bonded Concrete Overlay” also include the following subtopics: Debonding.

Debonding represents a failure mode where the loss of adhesion at the layer interface prevents the bonded concrete overlay from functioning as a structural unit. [1][2] The condition arises from construction deficiencies such as improper tack-coat application or surface contamination, requiring full-depth removal when bond-strength tests fall below specified limits. [1][2]

Purpose and design objectives

The bonded concrete overlay is employed whenever an existing concrete pavement, although still serviceable, fails to provide adequate structural capacity or ride performance. Typical deficiencies include failed transverse joints, localized distress such as cracking or faulting, insufficient load‑carrying ability for anticipated traffic, low surface friction, excessive rutting, and uneven slab support caused by variable subgrade conditions. By bonding a new concrete layer directly to the old slab so that the two act as a monolithic unit, the overlay restores uniform support, adds flexural strength, narrows crack widths, and improves joint load‑transfer efficiency. The technique also permits patching of distressed areas based on field measurements, provides slab stabilization where needed, and can be reinforced with macrofibers to further reduce slab movement and misalignment. In cases where only surface performance is deficient, an ultra‑thin (approximately 2 in.) non‑structural wearing course may be applied to raise skid resistance, smooth the riding surface, and fill ruts without substantially increasing thickness. The design follows the requirements of Chapter 10 and is limited to pavements that do not exhibit extensive cracking or faulting, ensuring that the combined slab thickness remains within prescribed limits while extending service life and meeting ride‑quality and friction standards. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

The bonded concrete overlay shall be designed in accordance with the prescribed pavement design chapter and must satisfy a full spectrum of functional, structural, durability, safety, and serviceability objectives. [1][2] Functionally it is intended to rehabilitate a deteriorated surface by correcting any failed transverse joints, eliminating existing surface distress, and improving ride quality, with particular attention to low‑friction conditions that could compromise vehicle stability. [1] The overlay also serves to restore proper lateral support by providing concrete shoulders where the existing shoulders are concrete or by adding a lane widening of one to two feet together with asphalt shoulders as required. [1] Structurally the new layer must increase the pavement’s load‑carrying capacity and behave as a single structural unit with the underlying slab, a performance achieved through a durable bond between the two layers and, where necessary, slab stabilization around patches or other weak zones to obtain uniform support. [1] In addition, the concrete mix is required to contain synthetic or steel macrofibers at a dosage that attains the design residual strength value (f150) specified for the project, thereby contributing to the required structural capacity. [2] When the overlay thickness is less than three inches, No. 67 coarse aggregate must be used in place of No. 57 to ensure proper bonding and performance of the thin layer. [2] Durability objectives include provision of a continuous pavement‑base drain or a composite base with integrated drainage to protect against moisture intrusion, as well as strict control of joint and crack openings to no more than about one millimeter—typically limited to the range of 0.5 to 1.0 mm—to preserve high load‑transfer efficiency and limit crack widening. [1] The concrete mixture must also be designed to achieve a low level of chloride ion penetration, classified as Low, Very Low, or Negligible after a curing period of fifty‑six days or less, which is verified by AASHTO T 277 or T 358 testing and may not exceed 2,800 coulombs, with resistance thresholds of 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4×8‑in cylinders and 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6×12‑in cylinders. [2] These durability measures collectively address the need to reduce slab movement, misalignment, plastic shrinkage cracking, and crack widening over the service life of the overlay. [1] Safety requirements call for inspection and, if necessary, adjustment or replacement of guide rails and drainage structures after the overlay is placed to maintain safe operating conditions, while the provision of adequate friction and proper shoulder configurations further supports vehicle safety. [1] The serviceability goals are met by delivering a smooth ride, maintaining sufficient surface friction, and ensuring that the pavement remains functional for its intended design life, which shall not be less than eight years nor exceed twenty years based on projected average daily traffic, growth rates, and truck‑percentage data. [1] Overall, the bonded concrete overlay must rehabilitate failed joints, provide uniform support, increase structural capacity, control cracking, ensure effective drainage, accommodate appropriate shoulder arrangements, address safety features, and sustain acceptable ride quality throughout its eight‑ to twenty‑year design life. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 463)

Scope and applicability

Bonded concrete overlay is suitable when the existing surface is a Portland‑cement concrete slab that remains in good to fair structural condition, exhibits only limited cracking or faulting, and rests on a stable subgrade with functional drainage [1]. It may also be applied over asphalt‑surfaced decks or AC/PCC composite pavements that are in fair to good condition, free of extensive stripping, and supported adequately, with the guidance emphasizing asphalt‑surfaced pavements as a common substrate [1][2]. The technique is appropriate for rehabilitation projects that require repair of failed transverse joints, slab stabilization to restore uniform support, or correction of surface distress such as spalling and rutting [1]. For thin overlays ranging from about 2½ to 4 inches, the primary purpose is a structural upgrade or preservation wearing course, and the new concrete is bonded directly to the existing layer so that the two act as a monolithic slab [1][2]. When the overlay thickness exceeds three inches, thicker sections of 4‑8 inches (or 3‑6 inches when fiber‑reinforced concrete is used) are employed to provide additional structural capacity for high traffic loads or variable support conditions [1]. For overlays less than three inches thick, the mix design must incorporate No. 67 coarse aggregate rather than No. 57 and include macrofibers at a rate sufficient to achieve the prescribed residual strength value (f150) [2]. The concrete mixture also has to satisfy chloride‑ion penetration criteria of Low, Very Low, or Negligible after 56 days (or Moderate with specified charge and resistivity limits) to ensure durability [2]. Bonded overlays can be used to add concrete shoulders or widen a lane by one to two feet, typically accompanied by asphalt shoulders, provided continuous pavement‑base drainage is maintained [1]. For flexible‑pavement preservation where only a non‑structural wearing surface is needed, an ultra‑thin bonded overlay limited to about 2 inches may be employed; any thickness greater than this falls outside the preservation scope [1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 463)

A bonded concrete overlay should be limited to situations where the existing slab is essentially sound and the supporting layers are stable. The pavement must be in fair‑to‑excellent condition, with a subgrade that shows no signs of instability and drainage that functions properly. When the underlying concrete exhibits extensive cracking, faulting, or other distress, those defects will transmit through the bonded layer unless they are fully repaired; under such circumstances an unbonded overlay or another rehabilitation technique is preferred. The same restriction applies to composite pavements in which the lower asphalt course shows stripping or severe deterioration—these conditions preclude bonding and favor an unbonded design. Structural inadequacy of the substrate also governs thickness limits: if the required bonded overlay thickness exceeds 4½ inches with a 9.5‑mm Superpave wearing course, or more than 5 inches with a 12.5‑mm mix, the project no longer qualifies as a preservation overlay and must be designed using full reconstruction criteria. Likewise, when concrete patching is needed on more than about ten percent of the slab area, a simple bonded overlay is not appropriate unless a cost‑effectiveness justification can be demonstrated. Finally, thin bonded overlays are only justified when field‑load testing (e.g., FWD) confirms that the existing pavement is structurally sound. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (pp. 183–188), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Design inputs and requirements

Design of a bonded concrete overlay begins with structural testing—specifically an FWD test on the existing PCC pavement—to quantify its capacity, and it requires a detailed inventory of the current pavement condition, including any slab movement, faulting, spalling or joint integrity that may demand full‑depth patching, slab stabilization or jacking. The subgrade must be confirmed stable and drainage functional, with adequate subdrainage provided where subsurface moisture problems exist, while traffic considerations such as high average daily traffic guide the selection of overlay thickness. Material information on the existing concrete (type, joint condition) and any required repair materials (PCC or asphalt base course) is also collected to ensure compatibility. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

The design of bonded concrete overlays follows the requirements set out in Chapter 10 of the PCCP Guidelines, which prescribe rehabilitation of failed transverse joints on composite bases, slab stabilization to achieve uniform support, and provision of continuous pavement‑base drainage where needed. Overlay geometry may include concrete shoulders that match existing types or a lane widening of one to two feet accompanied by asphalt shoulders, with adjustments to guide‑rail and drainage structures and fill widening to retain proper shoulder widths and bridge clearances. Thickness limits are defined in AASHTO Publication 408, where standard bonded overlays are specified between four and eight inches (Section 540) and thin‑bonded overlays are limited to a depth of two‑point‑five to four inches (Section 541), while ultra‑thin applications used as non‑structural wearing courses are capped at two inches of asphalt. Scratch overlays on existing concrete that incorporate a Superpave wearing course may not exceed four‑and‑a‑half inches with a 9.5 mm mix or five inches with a 12.5 mm mix, and any excess must satisfy the 3R criteria. When an overlay depth is less than three inches, the coarse aggregate specification changes from No. 57 to No. 67, and macrofibers must be added at a dosage that achieves the residual flexural strength (f150) indicated in Table B, with verification by ASTM C1609‑12 testing at 14 days. All thin‑bonded overlays require macrofibers to meet the residual strength requirement, and the fibers are quantified according to ASTM C1609, typically providing f150 values between 100 and 225 psi for fiber contents of 0.2 to 0.5 percent by volume. The concrete mix must also satisfy chloride‑ion penetration limits, targeting Low, Very Low, or Negligible ratings after no more than 56 days of curing in accordance with AASHTO T277 or T358, although a Moderate rating is permissible provided the measured charge does not exceed 2,800 coulombs (AASHTO T277) or the specified kΩ‑cm limits (AASHTO T358). Design life requirements call for a minimum of eight years and a maximum of twenty years, and the mechanistic‑empirical BCOA‑ME procedure developed under FHWA’s TPF 5‑165 study is used because conventional AASHTO methods do not account for bonding. Traffic inputs for the analysis must include average daily traffic, growth forecasts, and truck percentages, with ESAL calculations based on the rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1. Existing pavement thickness is obtained through the condition‑survey method of Table 10.9, future‑traffic thickness from Table 10.8 in DARWin, and the selected overlay depth is rounded up to the nearest half‑inch while being checked against the minimum and maximum limits listed in Table 10.10. Prior to design a falling weight deflectometer test must be performed on the existing concrete pavement; any slab rocking, pumping, or faulting identified must be stabilized by jacking or comparable measures, and adequate subdrainage must be ensured. The overlay is permissible only when the underlying pavement is in good‑to‑fair structural condition and the subgrade is stable; otherwise an unbonded solution is recommended. Concrete patching may not exceed ten percent of the total area unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided, and any leveling courses required to meet minimum cross‑slope are excluded from thickness calculations. All design submissions require approval of concrete paving job‑mix formulas by the District Materials Engineer or Manager, and documentation is completed on Form D‑4332 for PDAU concurrence and FHWA approval when necessary. [1][2]

Design methods and criteria

The design process begins by confirming that the existing concrete pavement is suitable for bonding, which requires coring representative transverse joints and performing a falling‑weight deflectometer test to obtain structural capacity data [1]. In parallel, the maturity method may be employed to relate degree‑hours of cure to compressive strength at specified ages (8 h, 1 day, 7 days, 28 days and 56 days) in accordance with PTM No. 640 [2]. Once the existing slab is deemed acceptable, the required overlay thickness is determined using mechanistic‑empirical procedures such as the bonded‑concrete‑overlay‑of‑asphalt (BCOA‑ME) method, which was developed under FHWA pooled‑fund study TPF 5‑165 and is also available in software packages like ACPA Pavement Designer, AASHTOWare Pavement ME, or DARWin [1]. For overlays placed over asphalt or composite bases the BCOA‑ME model calculates an effective flexural strength as the sum of the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture and the residual fiber strength (f₁₅₀), the latter being obtained from ASTM C1609 testing on macro‑fibred concrete [1][2]. The residual strength f₁₅₀ is estimated for a given fiber volume fraction by consulting Table B and, when needed, using the CP‑Tech Center Residual Strength Estimator spreadsheet to translate fiber content into f₁₅₀ values [1][2]. When DARWin is used, the analyst first computes the future‑traffic pavement thickness from Table 10.8, then evaluates the effective existing thickness through the Condition Survey method of Table 10.9, applies average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 to obtain ESALs, and finally invokes the Calculate function to produce a required overlay thickness that is rounded up to the nearest half‑inch and checked against the minimum‑maximum limits in Table 10.10 [1]. For thin bonded overlays over asphalt or composite pavements the prescribed thickness range is 2.5 to 4 inches, while thicker overlays may be specified from 4 to 8 inches, with the exact value emerging from the mechanistic‑empirical analysis [1]. All failed transverse joints must be repaired prior to overlay placement, and any concrete patching is based on field measurements of the existing pavement condition [1]. If a composite base underlies the slab, slab stabilization measures such as jacking or supplemental support are provided to achieve uniform support before the new concrete is placed [1]. Continuous base drainage may be incorporated into the design, and where geometric improvements are required concrete shoulders or lane widenings of one to two feet—often accompanied by asphalt shoulders—are added [1]. The concrete mix for the overlay must also satisfy a chloride‑ion penetration target (Low, Very Low, or Negligible) within 56 days, evaluated using AASHTO T 277 or T 358 criteria with limits of 2,800 coulombs or 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4×8 in. cylinders and 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6×12 in. cylinders [2]. The required residual strength f₁₅₀ is achieved by adding macro‑fibers at a rate determined from the fiber volume fraction–strength relationship obtained in the ASTM C1609‑12 test conducted at 14 days [2]. Once the mix design meets both the structural flexural requirement and the chloride penetration criteria, the overlay thickness computed from the mechanistic‑empirical model is adopted for construction [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 463)

The design of a bonded concrete overlay follows the structural‑design procedures outlined in Chapter 10, requiring that the existing pavement be evaluated with a falling‑weight deflectometer to verify good‑to‑fair structural condition, stable subgrade and functional drainage before any calculations are performed [1]. Only pavements exhibiting limited cracking or faulting and whose core samples show no stripping of asphalt bases are considered eligible for bonding so that the new concrete can act monolithically with the old slab [1]. All failed transverse joints must be rehabilitated, concrete patches prepared based on field measurements, and slab stabilization—by jacking or other means—applied where necessary to restore uniform support [1]. The overlay design also mandates a continuous base drain, properly sized concrete shoulders that match the existing geometry, and any required adjustments to guide rails, drainage structures, or shoulder widening to preserve bridge clearances [1]. Thickness limits are prescribed by PennDOT tables; the calculated depth, rounded up to the nearest half‑inch, must fall within the minimum and maximum values of Table 10.10, with thin‑bonded sections limited to 2.5–4 inches, standard bonded sections ranging from 4 to 8 inches, and fiber‑reinforced overlays typically using 3–6 inches [1]. For ultra‑thin preservation overlays the maximum depth is two inches over asphalt and such layers are treated as non‑structural wearing courses, while scratch‑type overlays may not exceed 4½ inches with a Superpave 9.5 mm mix or five inches with a Superpave 12.5 mm mix; leveling courses required for cross‑slope are excluded from the depth count [1]. When the selected overlay thickness is less than three inches, the concrete mix must employ No. 67 coarse aggregate rather than No. 57 to satisfy gradation requirements [2]. Macro‑fibers are incorporated at a dosage that provides the design residual flexural strength designated as f150; this dosage is verified by testing 14‑day specimens in accordance with ASTM C1609‑12 and correlating the measured residual strength with fiber volume fraction as given in Table B [2]. The typical fiber content for concrete overlays ranges from 0.2 % to 0.5 % by volume, delivering residual flexural strengths between 100 and 225 psi for the fiber‑reinforced sections described in the thickness tables [1][2]. The mix must also satisfy stringent permeability criteria so that after no more than 56 days of curing the chloride ion penetration is classified as Low, Very Low, or Negligible according to AASHTO T277 or T358; if a moderate level is permitted, the charge measured in an AASHTO T277 test may not exceed 2,800 coulombs and resistance values from AASHTO T358 must remain below 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑by‑8‑in cylinders and below 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑by‑12‑in cylinders [2]. All overlay designs are required to provide a minimum design life of eight years and may not exceed twenty years, with accurate traffic data—including current AADT, projected growth and truck percentages—used to compute design ESALs employing the average initial truck factors for rigid pavements from Table 7.1 [1]. Because conventional AASHTO 1993 procedures do not account for the bond between overlay and existing pavement, thicknesses for bonded overlays over asphalt or composite bases are determined using the bonded‑concrete‑overlay mechanistic‑empirical (BCOA‑ME) method developed at the University of Pittsburgh [1]. The design package must include the required Form D‑4332 for PennDOT concurrence and FHWA approval where applicable, thereby ensuring that all safety margins, factors of safety and reliability requirements are satisfied before construction begins [1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 463)

Structural section and dimensions

The bonded concrete overlay is placed as a thin Portland‑cement concrete slab whose thickness is limited to the range of 2.5 inches to 4 inches [2]. When the selected overlay depth falls below three inches, the mix design calls for a finer No. 67 coarse aggregate in place of the standard No. 57 aggregate [2]. The overlay mixture must also contain macro‑fiber at a rate sufficient to achieve the prescribed residual strength value (f150) as shown in the applicable tables [2]. In addition to the slab, the design calls for concrete shoulders or a widened lane that are one to two feet wide and are typically paired with asphalt shoulders [1]. The guides do not prescribe specific dimensions for the existing pavement, base, subbase, treated‑soil, edge support, or reinforcement, leaving those parameters to be established by the detailed design process [1][2].

Materials and mixture design

The specification for a bonded concrete overlay must identify the cementitious material as Portland‑cement concrete and indicate whether synthetic or steel macrofibers are incorporated. If macro‑fiber reinforcement is called for, the engineer shall state the fiber type, provide the required residual flexural strength measured at 150 psi (f150) which for overlays is limited to a range of 100 to 225 psi, and calculate an effective flexural strength as the sum of the concrete’s modulus of rupture and this residual value for use in structural calculations. The dosage of macrofibers shall be sufficient to achieve the design f150 value according to ASTM C1609‑12 and Table B, while typical volumes for overlays are reported between 0.2 % and 0.5 % by volume; individual fibers are generally one to two and a half inches long with an aspect ratio of 30 to 100. Any adjustments to workability‑enhancing admixtures required to meet fresh‑property limits must be noted in the specification. The overlay thickness and aggregate size are linked; for overlays less than three inches thick the mix shall use No. 67 coarse aggregate rather than No. 57, whereas Superpave mixes may employ nominal maximum aggregate sizes of 9.5 mm, 12.5 mm, or 19.0 mm with corresponding layer thicknesses such as 1½ inches for a 9.5 mm mix, two inches for a 12.5 mm mix placed over a 2½‑inch base of 19.0 mm aggregate, and an optional scratch course not exceeding one‑half inch using the 9.5 mm size. The total bonded concrete system shall not exceed four and a half inches when a 9.5 mm wearing course is used or five inches when a 12.5 mm wearing course is employed, and ultra‑thin non‑structural wearing courses are limited to an asphalt depth of two inches. The mixture must be designed to achieve a chloride‑ion penetration classification of Low, Very Low, or Negligible after no more than fifty‑six days of curing as measured by AASHTO T 277 or T 358; a Moderate rating is permissible only if the charge passed does not exceed 2,800 coulombs or the resistivity remains below 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4×8 in. cylinders and 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6×12 in. cylinders. The complete mix design shall be submitted at least two weeks prior to placement, receive approval from the District Materials Engineer, and be accompanied by appropriate concrete curing materials as prescribed in Sections 711.1 and 711.2(b). The design process begins by establishing the residual flexural strength (f150) required to achieve target structural capacity, crack‑width limits and joint load transfer efficiency, which is then added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength that governs thickness selection. To reach the specified f150, a macrofiber—synthetic or steel—is selected and dosed, typically between 0.2 % and 0.5 % by volume, with the exact content calibrated through ASTM C1609‑12 testing at 14 days to relate fiber volume to residual strength. When the overlay thickness is less than three inches, the coarse aggregate size is limited to No. 67 rather than No. 57 to aid workability and meet constructability constraints. Because macrofibers reduce slump, water‑reducing admixtures and adjustments to air‑entraining agents are incorporated into trial mixes so that fresh‑property specifications for workability, finishability, air content and durability are satisfied without compromising performance. Trial batches must verify that the mix meets the effective flexural strength, specified air content, and durability targets before full production is authorized. Durability requirements include achieving low to negligible chloride ion penetration after no more than 56 days of curing, as measured by AASHTO T 277 or T 358, with only moderate values permitted within defined coulomb or resistivity limits. For ultra‑thin bonded concrete wearing courses intended as non‑structural layers, the overlay is limited to a two‑inch asphalt depth; such configurations are excluded from structural design considerations. Scratch overlays that do not include leveling must not exceed four‑and‑a‑half inches when a Superpave 9.5 mm wearing‑course mix is used, or five inches with a Superpave 12.5 mm mix; exceeding these limits disqualifies the treatment from pavement‑preservation eligibility. When greater structural capacity is required, prescribed layer stacks are employed, for example a 1½‑inch layer of a 9.5 mm design over a 2½‑inch base of a 19.0 mm mix, or a 2‑inch layer of a 12.5 mm design over the same base, thereby satisfying structural demands while remaining within constructability limits. The selection of the Superpave gradation and adherence to the thickness thresholds ensure that the overlay meets the combined goals of structural performance, durability, constructability, and overall service life. Structural details and interfaces

Before placing a bonded concrete overlay, any failed transverse joints in the existing slab must be repaired, and where a composite base or uneven support exists, slab stabilization is required to provide uniform backing. A continuous drainage system within the pavement‑base should be installed when conditions dictate to prevent water entrapment beneath the overlay. Where the original pavement includes concrete shoulders, the overlay must extend with matching concrete shoulders; if the existing shoulders are asphalt, an adjacent asphalt shoulder may be used and a lane widening of one to two feet is permissible. Guide‑rail and drainage structures intersecting the overlay area need adjustment or replacement as necessary, and a leveling course is added only when surface irregularities demand it. Joint design for the overlay relies on saw‑cut contraction joints placed at appropriate depth and spacing so that load transfer efficiency develops early in service; dowel bars are specified in joints that must control faulting because fibers do not replace conventional dowel‑bar systems. Reinforcement of the overlay slab is achieved by incorporating synthetic or steel macrofibers into the concrete mix, typically ranging from 0.2 % to 0.5 % by volume, with the fiber rate selected to achieve the required design residual strength (f150). The macrofibers provide residual strength and enhance flexural capacity while helping maintain load‑transfer efficiency under repeated loading, but they are not considered a substitute for dowel bars where fault control is required. When the overlay thickness is less than three inches, the mix shall use No. 67 coarse aggregate rather than No. 57 coarse aggregate to meet material specifications. The sources do not prescribe additional load‑transfer devices, transition treatments, or connection details beyond those already described for joints and reinforcement. [1][2]

Design begins with a thorough condition assessment of the existing pavement, including coring of the old slab or any underlying asphalt to verify absence of stripping and excessive cracking or faulting; distress identified must be repaired before overlay placement.[1] All failed transverse joints in a composite base are rehabilitated and slab stabilization is provided where needed to achieve uniform support across the bond line.[1] A continuous pavement‑base drain is installed at the interface to control water infiltration between the existing surface and the new concrete.[1] The overlay is placed in direct contact with the existing surface, without any separation layer such as asphalt or geotextile, so that the two act as a monolithic slab sharing load.[1][2] The overlay thickness is limited to a thin Portland cement concrete layer typically ranging from about 2½ inches to 4 inches, and the required thickness is calculated by adding the effective existing thickness to future traffic demands, then rounding up to the nearest half‑inch within prescribed minimum–maximum limits.[1][2] Concrete shoulders are provided when the existing shoulders are concrete; otherwise the shoulder type matches the existing configuration, usually extending one to two feet beyond the lane and may include fill widening to maintain proper widths and bridge clearances.[1] Guide‑rail and drainage structures at the overlay edges are adjusted or replaced as necessary to preserve functional continuity across the new interface.[1]

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

When a bonded concrete overlay is specified, the design process begins with verification that the existing pavement’s drainage system is functioning properly and that the subgrade is stable. If subsurface water infiltration is likely or has been observed, a continuous pavement‑base drain is incorporated to convey moisture away from the new concrete layer, thereby protecting it from excess moisture under varying environmental conditions. All guide‑rail and other drainage structures intersecting the overlay are examined and, where necessary, adjusted or replaced so that runoff paths remain uninterrupted. In cases where the existing system exhibits deficiencies, adequate subdrainage must be installed before the overlay is placed to correct those problems. Consequently, bonded concrete overlays are limited to sections where the underlying pavement is in relatively good condition, the subgrade is stable, and the drainage is functional; they should not be used on alignments with unresolved drainage issues. The source provides no additional guidance regarding climate or other environmental factors beyond these water‑management requirements. [1]

The bonded concrete overlay shall be designed in accordance with Chapter 10 and must incorporate a comprehensive set of accessibility, safety, geometric, and user‑related provisions. All failed transverse joints in the existing pavement are to be rehabilitated, and any slab stabilization required to restore uniform support shall be provided. A continuous base drainage system shall be installed wherever water accumulation could occur. The overlay shall include concrete shoulders that match the existing shoulder type; where additional width is needed, a lane widening of one to two feet may be added together with asphalt shoulders, and any fill widening must preserve the required shoulder widths while being verified against bridge clearances. All guide‑rail and drainage structures intersecting the overlay are to be adjusted or replaced as necessary to maintain safe and accessible travel for all users. The geometric design shall satisfy minimum cross‑slope criteria so that drainage and vehicle stability are assured, and any leveling course needed to achieve this slope may be placed without being counted toward the total overlay thickness. To meet user expectations for skid resistance and ride quality, the surface may be treated with diamond grinding, micro‑surfacing, or an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course, and when an asphalt layer is placed over concrete, the transverse joints should be sawed and sealed to preserve joint integrity. Verification and expected performance

The bonded concrete overlay is expected to raise the pavement’s load‑bearing capacity while eliminating existing surface distress, which translates into fewer cracks or spalling, smoother ride quality and longer intervals between maintenance actions. Prior to placement, the structural adequacy of the underlying slab is confirmed with a Falling Weight Deflectometer test; this baseline informs the design thickness of the overlay. When macro‑fibers are incorporated, the mix provides a measurable residual flexural strength (f150) obtained in accordance with ASTM C1609. Adding this residual value to the plain concrete modulus of rupture yields an effective flexural strength that is entered into mechanistic‑empirical design tools such as BCOA‑ME or the ACPA Pavement Designer to predict service life and required overlay thickness. Post‑construction performance is verified through laboratory slab tests that record load‑transfer efficiency gains of roughly thirty percent over plain concrete, reductions in crack width—particularly when initial cracks exceed one millimetre—and a lower count of cracks after restrained shrinkage or freeze–thaw cycling. In the field, ongoing monitoring of distress reduction and maintenance records provides additional evidence of extended serviceability and improved rideability. [1]
