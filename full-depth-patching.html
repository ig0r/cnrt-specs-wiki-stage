Full-Depth Patching

Part of: Concrete Patching is-subtopic-of
Full-Depth Patching is a topic under Concrete Patching.
full-depth-patchingfull-depth-patchingfull-depth-repairfull-depth-repairsfull-depth-repair
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Full‑depth patching is a concrete‑pavement repair applied when distress—such as major working cracks, crushed or severely deteriorated joints, and spalling that extends beyond the mid‑slab depth—penetrates the entire slab thickness, rendering partial repairs ineffective [1][2]. The method removes the full depth of the defective concrete—including all compromised cracks, joint material, and broken slabs—and replaces it with new Portland cement concrete whose texture and profile match the surrounding pavement, while installing fully bonded dowels to restore load‑transfer capability [1][2]. By eliminating the underlying distress and reestablishing structural integrity, full‑depth patching restores functionality, extends the service life of the existing structure, and provides a sound base for traffic or subsequent overlay when the original slab can no longer support such treatments [1][2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Full‑depth patching is applied to concrete pavements where the distress extends through the entire slab thickness, making partial repairs ineffective [1][2]. Typical conditions include badly deteriorated or broken slabs, major working cracks that penetrate beyond the top half of the slab, and joints that have become crushed, severely deteriorated, or exhibit loss of load transfer and faulting [1]. The method also addresses spalling that reaches past the mid‑slab depth as well as localized break‑back or other damage that renders a portion of the slab unsound [1][2]. When such full‑depth distress is present, the only effective remedy is to remove the entire depth of the defective concrete—including all compromised cracks and joint material—and replace it with new concrete that restores structural integrity and load‑transfer capability [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–402)

Full‑depth patching removes the entire deteriorated slab or joint section and replaces it with new concrete, directly repairing the damaged pavement [1]. By eliminating broken slabs, badly deteriorated areas, and spalls that extend beyond mid‑slab depth, the method restores load‑transfer capability and overall functionality of the structure [1]. The treatment also corrects subbase surface irregularities greater than one inch and repairs any shoulder damage caused by the work, ensuring the repaired portion can carry traffic under normal conditions [2]. By placing new concrete whose texture and profile match the surrounding pavement, the patch re‑establishes a structurally sound surface capable of long‑term performance, thereby preventing further deterioration of the existing pavement [1][2]. Consequently, full‑depth patching not only repairs damage but also extends the service life of the pavement, allowing the existing structure to remain in service without requiring an overlay [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–408)

Distress characterization and causes

Full‑depth patching is required when concrete pavement distress has progressed to affect the entire thickness of the slab at critical locations such as longitudinal joints between traffic lanes, transverse joints, cracks and especially the outer corners of slabs. The deterioration often begins with small voids beneath these joints and cracks—created by pumping action, subgrade consolidation or settlement—and expands into excessive slab deflection, joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately complete breakup of the slab. When spalling extends beyond the mid‑slab depth, the loss of support is severe enough that the joint’s load‑transfer capability is compromised, producing faulting and poor ride quality. In many cases the affected area can be extensive; if intermittent patches less than ten feet apart are needed over a stretch of roughly five hundred feet, the entire length should be removed and reconstructed to restore structural integrity. The condition is highly progressive: leaving deep spalls or void‑induced deflections unattended leads to increasingly severe deterioration that eventually demands more extensive repairs. These defects create unsafe riding conditions, increase the risk of sudden slab failure, and often force lane closures or traffic detours. Consequently, early‑opening methods such as accelerated concrete placement or precast units are employed to limit work‑zone impacts and avoid operational penalties. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92)

Full‑depth patching is required when the distress originates from mechanisms that undermine both the structural capacity of the pavement and its resistance to environmental attack. Heavy traffic loads that exceed the design capacity generate load‑related failures, while climate‑driven processes such as water infiltration through joints or cracks, drainage deficiencies, frost action, aggregate deterioration and the general aging of concrete erode material durability. These factors often act together: infiltrating water carries chlorides and other incompressible particles into the pavement structure, accelerating pumping action that creates voids beneath the slab. Subgrade consolidation and settlement further reduce support, producing excessive slab deflections that manifest as transverse joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately slab breakup. When joint performance deteriorates—evidenced by faulting or poor load transfer—the underlying subgrade must be stabilized and the drainage system restored to prevent future infiltration. Stabilization typically involves filling the voids with cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing or high‑density polyurethane while simultaneously sealing joints and cracks; guidelines advise treating at least 25 % of transverse joints (and all patch joints) when no prior testing has confirmed adequate support. Addressing these underlying mechanisms—insufficient structural capacity, weak subgrade support, water ingress, drainage failure, material aging and excessive loading—is essential before or alongside the full‑depth replacement to ensure a durable repair. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92)

Applicability and treatment selection

Full-depth patching is the preferred repair when a concrete slab is badly deteriorated or broken, when joint deterioration extends beyond the mid‑slab depth or loss of load transfer occurs, and when corner joints are damaged past the slab’s midpoint. It is also required where the existing pavement cannot provide a uniform, stable base for an overlay, such as crushed or severely cracked joints that must be removed. The method is applied on two‑lane facilities unless traffic safety or lack of detour routes makes it infeasible, and poor subgrade conditions may call for additional undercutting. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89)

Full‑depth patching becomes unsuitable when the portion of pavement that must be removed exceeds roughly ten percent of the surface because a cost‑effectiveness analysis typically shows that another treatment would be more economical.[1] It also loses suitability when the distress originates from load‑related structural deficiencies or from climate and material durability problems, situations that require structural improvement, protective measures, or complete slab replacement rather than simple patching.[1] When damage is limited to shallow surface spalling that does not affect load transfer, partial‑depth repairs are preferred, while extensive surface spalling may be more efficiently addressed by full pavement replacement or the construction of a thin‑bonded concrete inlay.[1] Full‑depth patches should not be placed on areas narrower than one lane width, as patches less than a lane wide fall outside acceptable dimensions for this technique.[2] Likewise, sections shorter than six feet or longer than five hundred feet are better served by alternative maintenance or rehabilitation methods rather than isolated full‑depth patches.[1][2] For lengths between six and five hundred feet, the guides define acceptable patch length intervals—six to twenty feet, twenty‑point‑one to sixty‑five feet, and sixty‑five‑point‑one to five hundred feet—and recommend using full‑depth patches only within those ranges; if intermittent patches would be spaced less than ten feet apart over a stretch of about five hundred feet, removal and reconstruction of the entire segment is advised.[1][2] When many intermittent patches would be required within a relatively short distance, the logical alternative is to remove and reconstruct the whole section instead of applying isolated full‑depth repairs.[1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 399)

Condition assessment and timing

Determining the need for full‑depth patching begins with a systematic visual inspection of the concrete slab in which every type of distress—spalling, cracking, joint faulting, corner breaks, settlement, fines accumulation, blow‑holes or depressions—is recorded and quantified. The quantities are tabulated so that the total area (or volume) requiring replacement can be expressed as a percentage of the slab, and when this calculated patching percentage exceeds ten percent a cost‑effectiveness analysis is required before authorizing any full‑depth repair. When visual cues suggest loss of support, inspectors measure the depth of spalls relative to slab thickness; spalling that penetrates beyond the mid‑slab depth signals that surface resealing will not suffice and that a full‑depth patch or longitudinal joint repair is indicated. Transverse joint faulting and poor load transfer are quantified with fault gauges or similar faulting measurements to confirm inadequate support at joints. To verify the condition of underlying layers, cores are extracted from deteriorated joints as well as from randomly selected locations across the slab, providing direct information on subbase or subgrade quality and any hidden deterioration. The core program required for full‑depth patch justification calls for at least one core per traffic lane at each proposed repair location when the distressed segment is less than 100 ft long, and for longer segments cores are spaced no farther than every 100 ft; additional cores may be requested by the representative to confirm existing pavement conditions before slab design. Complementary non‑destructive testing is employed to map subsurface distress without further damage; deflection testing quantifies stiffness loss, ground‑penetrating radar detects voids or delamination, and friction and roughness surveys assess surface texture. Advanced methods such as the Modular Impact Resonance Analyzer (MIRA), MIT Scan 2/T2, and tensile bond tests are also used to evaluate interlayer bonding and overall structural integrity. The integration of visual observations, measured spall depths, faulting data, core results, and non‑destructive test outputs enables the engineer to pinpoint which joints or slab sections lack adequate support and therefore require full‑depth patching, ensuring that repairs are limited to areas where structural integrity is compromised. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 426)

Full‑depth patching is indicated only when a concrete pavement has progressed to a stage of severe distress that cannot be remedied by surface or partial‑depth repairs. This condition includes slabs that are badly deteriorated or broken, showing major working cracks, and joints that are crushed, severely deteriorated, or otherwise damaged to a depth that extends beyond the mid‑slab level, causing loss of load transfer. When spalling penetrates past the midpoint of the slab thickness, or when joint damage is extensive enough that resealing or partial repair would be inadequate, the entire affected portion must be removed and replaced with new concrete in order to restore structural integrity. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90)

Materials and repair details

Full‑depth patching replaces the deteriorated concrete slab with new Portland cement concrete, using dowel bars that are bonded along their full length to the existing pavement with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders for load transfer [1][2]. Dowel bars are installed in the transverse faces of the existing pavement and may be coated with a bond‑breaker lubricant on at least half their length to facilitate placement, while they are anchored using the specified epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders [2][1]. Standard transverse joint spacing is about thirty feet for reinforced sections and fifteen feet for plain concrete, and longitudinal tie bars are introduced only when a patch extends beyond roughly sixty‑five feet in length [1][2]. The concrete placed is a single‑course cement concrete of normal strength, though accelerated or rapid‑set mixes may be used when an opening time of four to seven hours is required; high‑early‑strength concrete is avoided because of shrinkage cracking, and standard mixes allow traffic after twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours [1][2]. The required slab thickness is obtained by taking core measurements at the four corners of the existing pavement, averaging those depths and adding one inch to provide the additional concrete needed, with the extra volume priced on a cubic‑yard basis [2]. Patches are required to cover at least one full lane width and are categorized by length—Type A patches range from roughly four to twenty feet when welded splices are used or six to twenty feet with tied splices, Type B patches span about twenty to sixty‑five feet, and Type C patches may extend up to five hundred feet; for intermittent repairs precast concrete slabs limited to a single lane width and sized between six and fifteen feet in length (up to twenty feet for prestressed units) are permissible [2][1]. The preparation begins with a quality‑control plan and the execution of full‑depth perimeter saw cuts using a diamond‑tipped blade equipped with a guard, alignment guides, water cooling and cut‑depth controls; each cut is limited to a maximum width of one‑half inch and may be extended two inches into the adjoining pavement as long as existing dowel bars are not compromised, and any cuts opened to traffic must be closed within seventy‑two hours [2]. The deteriorated slab is then removed with hand tools, typically a jackhammer not exceeding thirty‑five pounds and operated at a shallow angle while avoiding drop or hydraulic hammers, and the removal must be finished within forty‑eight hours to prevent prolonged exposure of the subgrade over weekends or holidays [2]. The underlying subbase is inspected, any unsuitable material is removed and replaced, and the new subbase is placed and compacted in accordance with standard specifications, with quantities measured and billed by the cubic yard [2]. Load‑transfer dowels are installed across the transverse faces of the existing pavement, their surfaces coated with a bond‑breaker lubricant on at least half their length and anchored using epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders; for Type A and Type B patches a quarter‑inch full‑depth polystyrene board serves as a longitudinal bond breaker, whereas Type C patches do not require this board, and a sealant reservoir is constructed at each joint, sealed prior to concrete placement and resealed with an approved liquid membrane after curing [2][1]. When the repair involves replacement of an existing expansion joint, a three‑quarter‑inch expansion‑joint filler is placed in the joint nearest to the remaining joint, while corner repairs employ a half‑inch filler material [2]. The patch area is subsequently grouted through a two‑hole pattern drilled into the adjacent concrete, using one of three materials—cement‑based grout at roughly one cubic foot per hole, hot liquid asphalt pumped at thirty to fifty gallons per hole, or high‑density polyurethane applied at approximately 0.4 pound per square foot of slab surface—with traffic withheld until the chosen material cures, which may require up to twelve hours for cement grout and a minimum of thirty minutes for asphalt or polyurethane [1]. The new concrete is placed using suitable equipment by trained crews, finished and textured to replicate the surrounding pavement cross‑section, and allowed to cure until a minimum compressive strength of one thousand psi is confirmed by cylinder testing before reopening to traffic [2]. After curing, all joints are resealed with an approved liquid‑membrane sealant to restore water tightness [2]. For intermittent repairs, precast concrete slabs that incorporate the required dowel bars, reinforcing steel and tie bars may be used as full‑depth patches, provided they are limited to one lane width, sized between six and fifteen feet in length (twenty feet for prestressed units), have their transverse joints aligned with adjacent lanes, and are set on a properly prepared bedding course [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–426)

Full‑depth patch performance depends on the condition of the existing pavement, the properties of the repair concrete and associated materials, and the degree of compatibility achieved at the joint interface [1][2]. The surrounding slab should be inspected for compressive strength, air content, and signs of durability problems such as weak aggregate or frost damage, because these influence load transfer and long‑term serviceability [1]. When more than ten percent of the surface requires replacement, a cost‑effectiveness analysis is required since extensive removal can jeopardize the pavement’s remaining life [1]. The concrete mix used for the patch must be normal‑strength or accelerated‑strength cement concrete that meets the applicable specifications and attains at least 1,000 psi compressive strength as confirmed by cylinder testing before traffic is permitted [2]. Early‑strength cements should be avoided because their rapid shrinkage can induce cracking; instead a standard pavement concrete meeting Publication 408 with an optimized aggregate gradation provides adequate early strength within 24 to 48 hours, while accelerated mixes may be employed when earlier opening is needed [1][2]. Reinforcing steel, dowel bars and tie bars incorporated in the patch must satisfy certification and quality‑control requirements, and high‑performance dowels are recommended for repairs expected to remain in service longer than twenty years [1][2]. Load transfer at transverse joints is achieved by installing full‑depth dowels that are anchored with epoxy, polyester, vinylester, or other fast‑setting binders, and the dowels must be aligned correctly, coated with a bond‑breaker lubricant, and protected during construction [1][2]. For patches longer than sixty‑five feet longitudinal tie bars become necessary to maintain structural continuity [1]. Joint preparation requires full‑depth saw cuts no wider than one‑half inch, clean and seal all adjacent joints and cracks with a rubberized joint‑sealing material, and construct a sealant reservoir that is sealed to prevent moisture intrusion [1][2]. Surface tolerances of the repaired area are limited to three‑sixteenths of an inch, and the patch must extend at least one lane width and fall within prescribed length categories (6–20 ft, 20.1–65 ft, or 65.1–500 ft) to ensure adequate behavior [2]. The subgrade beneath the patch must be stable and well‑drained; if the native subgrade is deficient it should be undercut, and a fine‑graded bedding material placed over a properly prepared subbase to provide a uniform foundation [1][2]. Subbase irregularities greater than one inch must be corrected by loosening, adding or removing material, and any disturbance caused by excavation should be recompacted with small vibratory compactors [2]. The underlying causes of distress—such as pumping action, subgrade consolidation, settlement, or voids beneath the slab—must be identified and remedied, often by grouting the voids with cement slurry, hot asphalt, high‑density polyurethane, or a two‑hole drilling pattern to improve penetration [1]. Excavated repair areas should not remain unpatched for more than forty‑eight hours, including weekends and holidays, to preserve bond continuity [2]. Proper drainage must be maintained to keep water, chlorides, or incompressible materials from infiltrating the repair zone, which otherwise would degrade performance [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–426)

Treatment procedures

Preparation begins with the development and submission of a quality‑control plan at least two weeks before work starts, together with surface marking of the repair zone and verification that the required equipment, trained crew, and hauling capacity are available.[2] The designer then establishes the patch boundaries so that all deteriorated pavement, major working cracks and crushed joints will be removed, ensuring that the area to be excavated fully encloses the damaged slab.[1][2] Traffic protection measures are installed and, if traffic must remain on the remaining lanes, it may continue for up to 72 hours while the patch is being prepared, provided that the excavated area is not left unpatched for more than 48 hours.[2] A diamond‑tipped saw equipped with water cooling, blade guard and depth controls is used to cut full‑depth lines around the intended perimeter, making longitudinal cuts first followed by transverse cuts to create a sealable joint interface.[2][1] The damaged concrete is then removed in one or more pieces using hand tools or light equipment rather than drop‑hammers or hydro‑hammers so as to avoid disturbing the subbase, and any subbase that has been displaced more than an inch is either re‑compacted or replaced with suitable material before a fine‑graded bedding layer is placed if required.[2] New load‑transfer dowel bars are installed across each transverse joint, and where continuous reinforcement is specified tie bars may also be placed; the dowels are positioned using bond‑breaker lubricant and secured with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders as appropriate.[1][2] A sealant reservoir is constructed at the interface and sealed with expansion‑joint material to provide a proper joint before concrete placement, and for continuously reinforced patches a partial‑depth cut is provided at the interface prior to sealing.[2] The project‑specified concrete mix—standard portland cement concrete unless an accelerated mix is required for early opening—is placed to fill the void, consolidated as needed, and finished and textured to match the existing cross‑section and surface texture.[1][2] After placement the fresh concrete is cured either with an approved membrane‑forming compound or by following the rapid‑set manufacturer’s method, protecting the patch from rain until the required compressive strength is achieved; typical curing times are 24 to 48 hours for a normal mix and 4 to 7 hours for an accelerated mix.[1][2] Once the concrete has attained strength, the patch is grouted, all joints—including any saw‑cut extensions—are sealed, surface tolerances are checked with a straightedge and corrected if necessary, and the repaired section, which must be at least one lane wide, is then opened to traffic.[2][1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–426)

The implementation sequence for full‑depth patching is positioned after any cross‑stitching, slab stabilization, partial‑depth repairs, load‑transfer retrofits, or diamond grinding and before the installation of base drains [1]. Prior to physical work a quality‑control plan is prepared and the defect is surface‑marked, establishing the documentation framework required by the guides [2]. A diamond‑tipped saw equipped with a blade guard, alignment guides, water‑cooling system and depth‑control is then used to cut a full‑depth perimeter whose cuts are limited to one‑half inch in width; this cutting operation constitutes Class 1 work and may be followed by removal with either a wheel saw or a 35‑lb jackhammer held at an angle of forty‑five degrees or less [2]. The deteriorated slab is completely excavated and the void is immediately backfilled with small vibratory compactors to restore the subbase, ensuring that no excavated area remains unfilled for more than forty‑eight hours, a limit that also applies over weekends or holidays [2]. When precast concrete units are specified, coring tools are first employed in traffic lanes to verify existing slab thickness, after which heavy lifting equipment and special hauling permits are required to move the large fabricated slabs onto a prepared bedding; erection equipment is then used to set the slab before undersealing and joint work [1][2]. For patches longer than sixty‑five feet longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are installed, and load‑transfer dowels are placed in the transverse faces of the existing pavement with a sealant reservoir formed so that the joint can be sealed after concrete placement or slab erection [1][2]. All full‑depth patches must be completed before any adjacent longitudinal joint repairs commence, and when a patch intersects an existing joint the patch is finished prior to proceeding with the joint repair [2]. Immediately before the stabilization crew arrives, drilling equipment creates a two‑hole pattern of holes adjacent to the patch so that grout—whether cement slurry, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane—can be pumped beneath the slab; performing drilling and stabilizing in the same lane allows both crews to share a single set of Maintenance and Protection of Traffic devices and confines activities to one lane at a time [1]. The traffic‑control sequence is further governed by the concrete mix selected: accelerated mixes permit reopening to traffic within four to seven hours after placement, whereas standard mixes require twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours to achieve the necessary compressive strength [1]. After saw cutting, traffic may travel on the repaired portion of existing pavement for up to seventy‑two hours provided that drop‑off conditions are protected with temporary separators or bond breakers when work abuts live lanes and that work is restricted to one lane while adjacent lanes remain open [2]. On two‑lane facilities full‑depth repairs are generally executed under lane closures unless a safe detour can be provided, and high‑average‑daily‑traffic routes may be subject to liquidated‑damage provisions if opening is delayed beyond the stipulated time frames [1]. The guides do not impose specific temperature or humidity limits, but if rain occurs while a patch remains open an outlet is excavated through the shoulder at the lowest point of the patch and any resulting shoulder damage is repaired; curing then follows the concrete manufacturer’s recommendations together with appropriate rain‑protection measures [2]. No additional environmental constraints are stipulated beyond the rain‑outlet requirement, allowing work to proceed under typical weather conditions provided that the rain‑mitigation steps are observed [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–426)

Quality and workmanship

The concrete mix used for a full‑depth patch must be accepted on a lot‑by‑lot basis and shown to meet the specified mix design, with all material certifications for dowel bars, tie bars, bedding and any precast slab verified before placement【2】. In addition, inspectors confirm that the mix conforms to the concrete specifications outlined in Publication 408, ensuring consistency with the required composition【1】. Dowel‑bar anchors are required to be bonded along their entire embedded length using approved epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders, and the anchorage is examined by core drilling to verify that any void surrounding a dowel does not exceed one‑half inch in any core【1】【2】. Preparation begins with complete removal of all deteriorated joints and major cracks, including full excavation of the damaged slab and an undercut where the subgrade is deficient【1】. The saw‑cutting operation that defines the patch boundaries must be performed with a diamond‑tipped blade equipped with water cooling and verified for correct dimensions and alignment before any concrete is placed【2】. Existing pavement thickness at the repair location is checked by coring each lane, providing a baseline for the required patch depth【2】. The exposed subbase and bedding are inspected to confirm they are fine‑graded and properly compacted; if removal of the slab disturbs the subbase, it must be re‑compacted with small vibratory compactors and any surface irregularities greater than one inch corrected【2】. A contractor’s quality‑control plan that details equipment, trained personnel, concrete testing frequencies and hauling capacity is reviewed prior to work commencement to ensure continuous placement of the fresh concrete【2】. During installation the new concrete is placed in accordance with the design details, maintaining uniform support and avoiding workmanship defects, while load‑transfer units such as dowel bars are installed at transverse joints per the approved specifications【1】【2】. If a flexible base is incorporated, density acceptance sampling and testing are performed on the compacted material before concrete placement【1】. After curing, cylinders taken from the patch are tested for compressive strength, and traffic may be permitted only when a minimum of one thousand pounds per square inch has been achieved within the time limits prescribed by the specifications【1】【2】. The cured surface is examined with a twelve‑foot straightedge both longitudinally and across the patch joints; any high or low spots exceeding three‑sixteenths of an inch are required to be ground out, and the final texture and cross‑section must match that of the surrounding pavement【2】. Original joints are resealed after any necessary diamond grinding, joint sawing and sealing, completing the patch’s finish work【2】. The completed repair is finally inspected for uniform support, absence of poor‑quality workmanship and compliance with all material, preparation, installation and post‑cure criteria before it is opened to traffic【1】【2】. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–426)

A satisfactory full‑depth patch requires that every major working crack and any crushed or deteriorated joint be completely removed and replaced with new concrete placed in accordance with the specified mix and curing requirements, and that load‑transfer dowels be fully bonded along their entire embedded length to ensure proper load transfer. The installed concrete must attain the minimum compressive strength prescribed by the specifications within the required time period, otherwise the work is considered defective. All saw cuts that will be opened to traffic must extend through the full pavement thickness and their width must not exceed one‑half inch, with any narrower cut prohibited. The interface between existing pavement and new concrete is prepared by providing full‑depth saw cuts, installing transverse load‑transfer dowels, forming a sealant reservoir and sealing the joint. Dowel anchorage is verified by core drilling and the total void around each dowel perimeter on any core must be no greater than one‑half inch. The subbase beneath the patch may not contain depressions deeper than one inch; such irregularities must be removed or filled and compacted before concrete placement. Patch length must correspond to the prescribed type ranges—between six and twenty feet for Type A, between 20.1 and 65 feet for Type B, or between 65.1 and 500 feet for Type C—and the repaired area must be at least one lane wide. Excavated sections must be refilled within forty‑eight hours and cannot remain unpatched over weekends or holidays. After placement, the surface is ground and textured to match the existing cross‑section, with flatness required to be within three‑sixteenths of an inch measured over a twelve‑foot straightedge; any high points or depressions exceeding this limit must be corrected before acceptance. The concrete must achieve a compressive strength of at least one thousand pounds per square inch as determined by cylinder testing before the patch can be opened to traffic, and all original longitudinal and transverse joints are resealed at that time. Any evidence of poor workmanship such as inadequate consolidation or surface defects is unacceptable and must be remedied prior to acceptance. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–408)

Performance and service life

Full‑depth patching removes the entire deteriorated slab—including all major cracks and crushed joints—and replaces it with new concrete designed to restore proper load transfer through fully bonded dowels, which improves the pavement’s structural condition and provides a uniform support layer for traffic and any subsequent overlay. By eliminating the underlying distress and ensuring correct joint design, the treatment enhances functionality and durability, avoiding premature cracking or loss of load transfer that would otherwise reduce service life. PennDOT’s experience shows that such repairs, when built to specification, extend the useful life of concrete pavements. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89)

Full-depth patching can fail when the original causes of distress are not addressed, allowing the same load‑related cracking or climate‑and‑material problems such as poor drainage to reappear. The probability of recurrence is linked to the condition of the existing slab—particularly the proportion of area being patched, its compressive strength and air content—as well as the quality of the subgrade or subbase and the provision of adequate friction and drainage. Inadequate subgrade or subbase preparation can also produce surface irregularities greater than one inch, which if not re‑compacted create a weak foundation that promotes settlement and edge cracking. Material selection influences distress development; high early‑strength concrete is prone to excessive shrinkage cracking, while premature traffic loading on a partially set patch can cause tie bars or bolts to shift, especially on short patches. Proper load transfer at transverse joints requires dowel anchorage that is fully bonded along its embedded length, with voids around each dowel limited to less than 0.50 inch; insufficient lubrication or poor placement creates gaps that lead to localized spalling and joint failure. Cuts that do not extend through the full pavement thickness, overly wide transverse saw cuts, or prolonged exposure of the excavated face increase susceptibility to cracking or spalling of the new material before final inspection. Use of aggressive removal tools such as drop hammers or hydro‑hammers can damage adjacent slabs and exacerbate edge break‑back. Premature exposure of the fresh concrete to traffic, inadequate curing, or insufficient rain protection also contributes to early cracking or spalling. Non‑uniform support resulting from poor placement practices creates localized stress concentrations that can manifest as edge cracking or settlement after the patch hardens. The finished surface must meet a straight‑edge tolerance of three‑sixteenths of an inch; high points or depressions beyond this limit indicate inadequate compaction or uneven backfill and lead to premature distress. Collectively, these failures stem from inappropriate material choices, insufficient attention to load‑transfer detailing, subgrade deficiencies, premature traffic loading, and workmanship shortcomings, any of which can render the full‑depth patch ineffective. [1][2]

Monitoring and follow-up

After the full‑depth patch has been placed and cured, the contractor must scribe the placement date on the fresh concrete edge, then a post‑construction inspection is performed by testing the patched surface with a 12‑foot straightedge; any high spots or depressions exceeding three‑sixteenths of an inch are ground smooth and rechecked. The Representative also verifies dowel‑bar anchorage by coring random locations and accepts only when peripheral voids are less than half an inch, and will not open the patch to traffic until compressive‑strength cylinders show at least 1,000 psi as required. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–402)

After a full‑depth patch is placed, the pavement must be monitored for several performance trends that signal insufficient repair. Intermittent patches over roughly 500 feet with less than about ten feet of original concrete remaining between successive patches indicate that the entire section is compromised and reconstruction of the whole length is warranted [1]. When the cumulative length of pavement that must be replaced exceeds 500 feet in any lane, a design analysis should be undertaken to determine whether a thicker slab or other structural modifications are required [1]. Individual concrete patches longer than 65 feet trigger the requirement for longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts, suggesting that the original patch depth may be inadequate [1]. Persistent extensive surface spalling after the patch is another indicator that replacement of the patched area or construction of a thin‑bonded inlay is needed [1][2]. The reappearance of cracking or spalling within the repaired slab after placement signals that the patch has become defective and must be removed and replaced [2]. Surface testing that reveals high points or depressions equal to or greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch after grinding is considered out of tolerance and requires corrective grinding work [2]. Dowel bar anchorage that leaves perimeter voids larger than one‑half inch on any core also mandates additional repair [2]. Observable distress of the underlying slab, such as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks on either the traffic lane or shoulder, small blow‑hole depressions in the shoulder adjacent to a joint, corner breaks, or measurable slab settlement, all point to ongoing loss of support beneath the patched area and call for actions such as slab stabilization, repeat full‑depth patching, or broader rehabilitation [1]. Failure to achieve the minimum compressive strength before opening the pavement to traffic is defined in specifications as grounds for further maintenance or rehabilitation [2].
