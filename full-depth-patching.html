Full-Depth Patching

Part of: Concrete Patching is-subtopic-of
Full-Depth Patching is a topic under Concrete Patching.
full-depth-patchingfull-depth-patchingfull-depth-repairfull-depth-repairsfull-depth-repair
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Full‑depth patching is a concrete‑pavement rehabilitation technique used when distress—such as broken slabs, major working cracks, or spalling that penetrates beyond the mid‑slab depth—and joint deterioration have compromised the entire thickness of the slab and its load‑transfer capability[1][2]. The treatment removes the unsound concrete (and any deficient subbase) with full‑depth saw cuts, replaces it with new Portland‑cement concrete, and installs fully bonded dowel bars to re‑establish structural continuity and long‑term performance[1][2]. By excising all compromised material and restoring a sound foundation, full‑depth patching restores load transfer, structural integrity, and service life without the need for an overlay[1][2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Full‑depth patching is applied when a rigid concrete pavement exhibits distress that extends through the entire thickness of the slab. [1][2] The distress includes badly deteriorated or broken slabs, major working cracks, and spalling that reaches beyond the mid‑slab depth. [1][2] Break‑back of the surface and other unsound sections are also cited as conditions requiring removal of the full slab thickness. [2] Joint problems such as crushed or severely deteriorated joints, loss of load transfer, faulting, or generally poor joint performance are likewise addressed because partial repairs cannot restore structural continuity. [1][2] When the underlying subbase is deemed unsuitable, the treatment calls for removal of that material together with the concrete to re‑establish a sound foundation. [2] By removing all compromised concrete and any deficient base and replacing it with new concrete or precast units that meet current specifications, full‑depth patching restores structural integrity, load‑transfer capability, and long‑term performance. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–417)

Full‑depth patching is employed to excise the unsound concrete slab or joint section and replace it with new material, thereby directly repairing the damaged pavement and re‑establishing its structural function[1][2]. By removing broken slabs, badly deteriorated areas, and spalls that extend beyond mid‑slab depth, the treatment restores load‑transfer capability and overall functionality of the structure[1]. The specifications further require prompt replacement and prohibit leaving excavated areas open, emphasizing that the purpose is to fix existing deterioration rather than merely slow its progression[2]. In addition to restoring performance, the new concrete creates a structurally sound surface capable of long‑term service, which prevents further deterioration and extends the pavement’s service life without an overlay[1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–412)

Distress characterization and causes

Full‑depth patching is required when concrete pavement distress has progressed to affect the entire thickness of the slab at critical locations such as longitudinal joints between traffic lanes, transverse joints, cracks and especially the outer corners of slabs. The deterioration often begins with small voids beneath these joints and cracks—created by pumping action, subgrade consolidation or settlement—and expands into excessive slab deflection, joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately complete breakup of the slab. When spalling extends beyond the mid‑slab depth, the loss of support is severe enough that the joint’s load‑transfer capability is compromised, producing faulting and poor ride quality. In many cases the affected area can be extensive; if intermittent patches less than ten feet apart are needed over a stretch of roughly five hundred feet, the entire length should be removed and reconstructed to restore structural integrity. The condition is highly progressive: leaving deep spalls or void‑induced deflections unattended leads to increasingly severe deterioration that eventually demands more extensive repairs. These defects create unsafe riding conditions, increase the risk of sudden slab failure, and often force lane closures or traffic detours. Consequently, early‑opening methods such as accelerated concrete placement or precast units are employed to limit work‑zone impacts and avoid operational penalties. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92)

Full‑depth patching is required when the distress originates from mechanisms that undermine both the structural capacity of the pavement and its resistance to environmental attack. Heavy traffic loads that exceed the design capacity generate load‑related failures, while climate‑driven processes such as water infiltration through joints or cracks, drainage deficiencies, frost action, aggregate deterioration and the general aging of concrete erode material durability. These factors often act together: infiltrating water carries chlorides and other incompressible particles into the pavement structure, accelerating pumping action that creates voids beneath the slab. Subgrade consolidation and settlement further reduce support, producing excessive slab deflections that manifest as transverse joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately slab breakup. When joint performance deteriorates—evidenced by faulting or poor load transfer—the underlying subgrade must be stabilized and the drainage system restored to prevent future infiltration. Stabilization typically involves filling the voids with cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing or high‑density polyurethane while simultaneously sealing joints and cracks; guidelines advise treating at least 25 % of transverse joints (and all patch joints) when no prior testing has confirmed adequate support. Addressing these underlying mechanisms—insufficient structural capacity, weak subgrade support, water ingress, drainage failure, material aging and excessive loading—is essential before or alongside the full‑depth replacement to ensure a durable repair. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92)

Applicability and treatment selection

Full-depth patching is the preferred repair when a concrete slab is badly deteriorated or broken, when joint deterioration extends beyond the mid‑slab depth or loss of load transfer occurs, and when corner joints are damaged past the slab’s midpoint. It is also required where the existing pavement cannot provide a uniform, stable base for an overlay, such as crushed or severely cracked joints that must be removed. The method is applied on two‑lane facilities unless traffic safety or lack of detour routes makes it infeasible, and poor subgrade conditions may call for additional undercutting. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89)

Full‑depth patching is unsuitable when the portion of pavement that must be removed exceeds roughly ten percent of the surface, because at that scale a cost‑effectiveness analysis typically shows that another treatment would be more economical. It also loses suitability when the distress originates from load‑related structural deficiencies or from climate and material durability problems; in those cases a structural improvement, protective measures, or complete slab replacement are required rather than simple patching. When damage is limited to shallow surface spalling that does not affect load transfer, a partial‑depth repair is preferred, while extensive surface spalling may be more efficiently addressed by full pavement replacement or the installation of a thin‑bonded concrete inlay. Moreover, if a roadway segment about five hundred feet long contains many intermittent patches spaced less than ten feet apart, the logical alternative is to remove and reconstruct the entire section instead of applying isolated full‑depth patches; projects that involve reconstruction of all lanes over lengths exceeding five hundred feet also demand a comprehensive design analysis for slab thickness and jointing rather than piecemeal patching. In such situations alternatives such as concrete restoration, a concrete overlay, or an asphalt overlay should be evaluated. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–89)

Condition assessment and timing

The determination begins with a systematic visual survey of the concrete slab in which every type of distress—spalling, cracking, joint faulting, corner breaks, settlement, accumulation of fines, blow‑holes or depressions—is recorded and quantified. The measured quantities are tabulated so that the total area (or volume) of pavement that would require replacement can be expressed as a percentage of the slab; if this calculated patching percentage exceeds ten percent, a cost‑effectiveness analysis must be performed before any full‑depth repair is authorized. Where visual cues suggest loss of support, inspectors measure the depth of spalls relative to the overall slab thickness; spalling that penetrates beyond the mid‑slab depth signals that surface resealing will not suffice and that a full‑depth patch or longitudinal joint repair per Publication 408 Section 519 is indicated. Faulting at transverse joints and poor load transfer are also measured, typically with fault gauges, to confirm inadequate support. To verify the condition of the underlying layers, core samples are extracted from deteriorated joints as well as from randomly selected locations across the slab. The cores provide direct information on subbase or subgrade material quality and any hidden deterioration. In addition to coring, a suite of non‑destructive tests is employed to map the extent of subsurface distress without further damage. Deflection testing quantifies stiffness loss; faulting measurements locate differential settlement at joints; ground‑penetrating radar detects voids or delamination; friction and roughness surveys assess surface texture; MIRA, MIT Scan 2/T2, and tensile bond tests evaluate interlayer bonding. The combined results pinpoint which joints or slab sections lack adequate support and therefore require full‑depth patching. Only after the visual, destructive, and non‑destructive data have been integrated does the engineer decide where a full‑depth concrete pavement patch is warranted, ensuring that every identified area truly lacks structural integrity and that the extent of repair remains economically justified. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92)

Full‑depth patching is applied only when a concrete pavement has progressed to a stage of severe distress that cannot be remedied by surface or partial‑depth repairs, such as badly deteriorated or broken slabs exhibiting major working cracks and joints that are crushed or severely damaged extending beyond the mid‑slab level [1]. When spalling penetrates past the midpoint of slab thickness, or when joint damage is extensive enough that resealing or partial repair would be inadequate, the entire affected portion must be removed and replaced with new concrete to restore structural integrity [1]. The guides also require full‑depth patching whenever a crack or spall appears in the concrete slab before final acceptance, mandating removal of the damaged section and replacement at no additional cost, which indicates that such early severe surface failures trigger the treatment [2]. In those instances the specification calls for a new full‑depth transverse saw‑cut beyond the area of break back or spalling prior to placing the replacement material [2]. Consequently, across sources the condition for applying full‑depth patching includes severe distress beyond mid‑slab depth with major cracks and heavily damaged joints, as well as any crack or spall detected before final inspection that cannot be addressed by lesser repairs [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–412)

Materials and repair details

Full‑depth patching replaces the deteriorated concrete slab with a new Portland cement concrete slab that may be of normal strength or an accelerated rapid‑setting mix when early traffic reopening is required, while high‑early‑strength concrete is avoided because of shrinkage cracking [1][2]. The replacement slab extends across the full lane width and its length is classified by type: Type A patches range from six to twenty feet (or four to twenty feet when welded splices are used), Type B patches cover lengths just over twenty‑one feet up to sixty‑five feet, and Type C patches exceed sixty‑five feet but not more than five hundred feet [2]. When a patch exceeds roughly sixty‑five feet longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are incorporated, whereas shorter patches rely only on transverse dowel bars for load transfer [1][2]. Transverse dowel bars are coated and bonded along their full length to the existing pavement with epoxy, polyester, or vinylester binders, and a bond‑breaker lubricant is applied to at least half of each bar’s length before placement in fast‑setting anchoring grout that achieves an initial set within five minutes [1][2]. The spacing of transverse joints follows standard practice—approximately thirty feet for reinforced concrete sections and fifteen feet for plain concrete—and longitudinal tie bars are introduced only when the patch length approaches sixty‑five feet [1]. For Type A and B patches a one‑quarter‑inch full‑depth polystyrene board is installed as a bond breaker in the longitudinal joint, while Type C patches do not require this additional layer [2]. At any joint that abuts an existing expansion joint a three‑quarter‑inch (or half‑inch for corner transverse joints) expansion‑joint filler is placed, and all joints are provided with a sealant reservoir before sealing with appropriate sealant material [2].

The damaged slab is removed in whole pieces without the use of drop hammers, after which the subbase is inspected; any disturbed surface is recompacted with small vibratory compactors and subbase material deeper than one inch or otherwise unsuitable is replaced according to specifications [2]. Saw cuts defining the patch perimeter are made through the full depth using a diamond‑tipped blade equipped with a guard, alignment guides, water cooling, and cut‑depth controls; the cut width is limited to one‑half inch and may extend transversely into adjacent pavement up to the full depth plus two inches provided neighboring dowel bars remain intact [2]. All sides of the patch that do not adjoin existing pavement are formed, longitudinal cuts run parallel to existing joints, and a transition taper of at least twenty feet is required for patches longer than sixty‑five feet [2].

After placement, the new slab may be cured with an approved membrane former; traffic on the sawed area can be permitted after up to seventy‑two hours, while the excavated area must not remain unpatched for more than forty‑eight hours [2]. To improve support beneath the patch, a two‑hole grout pattern is drilled into the adjoining concrete and filled with either a cement‑based grout (approximately one cubic foot per hole), hot liquid asphalt (thirty to fifty gallons per hole), or high‑density polyurethane (about 0.4 pound per square foot of slab surface); curing times are up to twelve hours for cement grout and a minimum of thirty minutes for asphalt or polyurethane before traffic is restored [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–417)

The performance of a full‑depth patch is governed by the properties of the repair concrete, which must be placed in accordance with manufacturer or Publication 408 specifications and achieve a compressive strength of at least 1,000 psi before traffic is allowed, as verified by cylinder testing [1][2]. Standard pavement concrete meeting the optimized aggregate gradation requirements provides adequate early strength within 24 to 48 hours, while rapid‑set or accelerated mixes may be used when earlier opening is required, but high early‑strength cement should be avoided because of its tendency to shrink and crack [1][2]. Air content and overall quality of the existing slab are also evaluated; compressive strength and air content of the surrounding concrete influence the durability of the repair and trigger a cost‑effectiveness analysis when more than ten percent of the surface requires replacement [1]. Load transfer across the repaired joint is achieved by installing dowel bars that run the full embedded length of each transverse joint, anchoring them with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders that set within five minutes, and applying bond‑breaker lubricant to at least half the length of coated dowels unless pre‑lubricated [1][2]. When the repaired section is expected to remain in service for more than twenty years, high‑performance dowel bars are recommended, and longitudinal tie bars become necessary for patches longer than sixty‑five feet [1]. Compatibility between new and existing pavement requires true full‑depth saw cuts that are no wider than one‑half inch, with the cuts extending completely through the slab so that original longitudinal and transverse joints can be re‑established and a sealant reservoir and appropriate joint filler installed to provide smooth load transfer and prevent water intrusion [2]. All surrounding joints and cracks must be cleaned and sealed with rubberized joint‑sealing material before placement of the patch, creating a sound bond between old and new concrete [1]. The underlying subgrade must be stable and its drainage system functional; if the native subgrade is poor it should be undercut, and any disturbed subbase deeper than one inch must be removed and replaced, while shallower disturbances are re‑compacted with vibratory equipment [1][2]. Existing slabs adjacent to the repair area must be free of break‑back, spalling, or other damage, and surface tolerances should be kept within three‑sixteenths of an inch before cutting [2]. The patch must extend the full width of a lane and meet prescribed length limits; undersized or partial‑width repairs compromise structural continuity, and excavated areas may not remain unpatched for more than forty‑eight hours to avoid loss of moisture and strength development [2]. Grouting of voids beneath the slab can be accomplished with cement slurry, hot asphalt, or high‑density two‑component polyurethane selected for low viscosity and resistance to water and chemicals; a two‑hole drilling pattern adjacent to the patch improves grout penetration and overall stability [1]. By observing these material properties, compatibility measures, and existing pavement condition requirements, full‑depth patches can achieve durable performance and maintain structural continuity of the pavement system [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–417)

Treatment procedures

The first step is to establish the exact limits of the repair so that every deteriorated slab, major working crack and crushed joint within those limits will be removed; designers therefore define patch boundaries before any cutting begins [1]. Concurrently, contractors prepare a quality‑control plan as required by Section 106.03(a)2.a and submit it for review at least two weeks prior to mobilizing crews, while also installing traffic protection measures and surface‑marking the repair limits [2]. All necessary equipment, a trained crew and adequate hauling capacity are verified to allow continuous placement of concrete once the patch is ready [2]. The perimeter of the patch is cut with full‑depth diamond‑tipped saw blades equipped with guards, alignment guides, water‑cooling systems and depth controls; longitudinal cuts are made first followed by transverse cuts to create a series of narrowly spaced edges that define the removal area [2]. These saw cuts follow the previously defined boundaries so that every compromised concrete is isolated, and the damaged material is then lifted out in one or more pieces, avoiding drop hammers or hydro‑hammers to minimize disturbance to the subbase, subgrade and adjoining pavement [1][2]. If any unsuitable subbase material is exposed during removal, it is excavated and either replaced with approved fill or re‑compacted to meet design specifications [2]. Load‑transfer dowels are installed across each transverse face of the existing slab, and they are bonded in place with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders to provide proper load transfer at the new joint [1][2]. A sealant reservoir is formed at each joint and the joints are sealed; when the adjacent portion is a new‑pavement joint the same load‑transfer unit and sealant preparation are provided [2]. Forms are set on all sides that do not abut existing pavement, and the specified rapid‑set concrete mix—standard portland cement concrete unless an accelerated mix is required for early opening—is placed to the full depth of the removed section [1][2]. The fresh concrete is struck off, consolidated and finished so that its surface texture matches the surrounding slab, with original longitudinal and transverse joints re‑established and sealed, and any saw‑cut edges extending beyond the patch also sealed [1][2]. Grout is placed around the perimeter of the patch before the final joint sealing to ensure a sound bond between new and existing material [2]. Curing is accomplished with an approved membrane‑forming compound; a Type 2 white pigmented liquid membrane‑forming curing compound may be used in place of the standard poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene (PAMS) product [2]. For a normal concrete mix the cure period typically ranges from 24 to 48 hours, while an accelerated mix can achieve traffic‑opening strength in 4 to 7 hours; the repaired area may be opened to traffic only after the curing period has elapsed and test cylinders indicate a compressive strength of at least 1,000 psi [1][2]. The patch is then released to traffic once these criteria are satisfied, completing the full‑depth patching process [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–417)

The equipment required for full-depth patching includes a diamond‑tipped saw equipped with a blade guard, alignment guides, water‑cooling system and cut‑depth controls to make the initial longitudinal and transverse cuts, as well as wheel saws with carbide tips or a 35‑lb jackhammer held at no more than a 45° angle for removal of the deteriorated concrete [2][1]. When precast concrete units are used, heavy‑lifting machinery and special haul‑route permits are needed to position the large slabs onto a prepared bedding, and patches longer than sixty‑five feet require longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts to be installed [1]. Prior to stabilization, drilling equipment creates a two‑hole pattern adjacent to the patch so that grout such as cement slurry, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane can be pumped beneath the slab [1]. Load‑transfer dowels are installed in the transverse faces of the existing pavement, a sealant reservoir is constructed, and the joint is sealed before concrete placement [2]. The construction schedule positions full-depth patching after cross‑stitching, any required slab stabilization, partial‑depth repairs, load‑transfer retrofits, or diamond grinding, and before the installation of base drains, ensuring that all preceding rehabilitation activities are completed first [1]. Across sources, the sequence begins with a quality‑control plan, surface marking, then full-depth longitudinal saw cuts followed by transverse cuts that define the patch perimeter; all patches must be placed before any longitudinal joint repairs, and when a patch intersects a joint the two operations are coordinated as a combined repair [2]. After cutting, the damaged concrete is removed, disturbed subbase is re‑compacted with small vibratory compactors, dowels and the sealant reservoir are installed, and finally the new concrete mix is placed, cured according to specifications, and surface tolerances verified before traffic is restored [2][1]. The selection of concrete mix determines the reopening time, with accelerated mixes allowing traffic within four to seven hours after placement whereas standard mixes require twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours to achieve sufficient compressive strength [1]. Guidelines impose strict limits on exposure time: an excavated area may not remain open longer than forty‑eight hours and work cannot be left unfinished over weekends or holidays, requiring that the patch be completed within this window [2]. Rainfall during exposure necessitates an outlet cut in the shoulder and repair of any resulting damage, while temperature and moisture conditions must conform to standard site practices for concrete placement [2]. The first guide does not specify particular weather thresholds, but together the sources emphasize that work should be conducted under conditions that will not compromise curing or grout placement [1][2]. Both guides require one‑lane‑at‑a‑time operations with drop‑off protection, and confine drilling and stabilizing to a single lane so that the same set of MPT devices can protect both crews, simplifying traffic management [1][2]. Saw‑cut sections of existing pavement may support traffic for a maximum of seventy‑two hours after cutting, provided the cuts are no wider than one‑half inch; wider cuts must remain closed until the patch is completed [2]. On two‑lane facilities full-depth repairs are generally executed under lane closures unless a safe detour exists, and high‑average‑daily‑traffic routes may be subject to liquidated-damage provisions for delayed opening [1]. The patch is opened to traffic only after joint sealing, surface tolerances, and concrete curing have been confirmed, completing the traffic-control sequence [2][1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–417)

Quality and workmanship

Inspectors must confirm that the concrete used for the patch conforms exactly to the specified mix design, as required by the specifications. The dowel bars that provide load transfer are required to be anchored with approved epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders and must remain fully bonded along their entire embedded length. When a flexible base is incorporated beneath the new concrete, density acceptance sampling and testing of the compacted material must be performed in accordance with the applicable sections of Publication 408. Prior to any field work, a detailed quality‑control plan that describes the equipment, trained crew, mixing, curing, joint sawing, sealing procedures and testing frequencies must be prepared, submitted for review at least two weeks before construction, and approved. The plan also identifies the points at which corrective actions are triggered. Full‑depth saw cuts are required to be no wider than one‑half inch, and the appropriate saw tools, water‑cooling systems and cut‑depth controls must be verified on site before cutting begins. After the cuts are placed traffic is restricted for a minimum of seventy‑two hours to allow safe completion of the removal work. The excavated area may not remain unpatched for more than forty‑eight hours, and any delay beyond that must be avoided even over weekends or holidays. Removal of the deteriorated slab and subbase is inspected to ensure that adjacent pavement is not damaged and that no subbase irregularities exceed one inch; any such irregularity must be corrected and the subbase re‑compacted with vibratory equipment before new concrete placement. During placement, continuous hauling of the approved concrete mix is observed and the mix is verified against lot specifications while proper compaction of a flexible base, when used, is monitored. Dowel bars are set in a rapid‑set material and their positions are confirmed by core drilling; acceptance requires that the void surrounding each bar be less than half an inch. Concrete strength is evaluated on cylinders prepared according to PTM No. 604, and traffic may be opened only after the measured compressive strength reaches at least one‑thousand pounds per square inch, which must be achieved within the time limits specified in Publication 408. After curing, the surface texture of the patch is finished to match the existing cross‑section and a twelve‑foot straightedge is used both longitudinally and across joints to detect any high or low spot greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch, which must be ground off. All original longitudinal and transverse joints are resealed in accordance with the joint details, and the completed patch is inspected for uniform support and absence of poor‑quality workmanship before it is released to traffic. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–417)

A satisfactory full‑depth patch requires that all major working cracks and any crushed or deteriorated joints be completely removed before new concrete is placed according to the specified mix design and curing regime [1]. The interface must be prepared with full‑depth saw cuts whose width does not exceed one‑half inch, and these cuts may only be opened to traffic after verification of that dimension [2]. Load‑transfer dowels are installed in the transverse faces of the existing concrete, extending fully into the new material so that they are bonded along their entire embedded length, and a sealant reservoir is constructed to allow resealing of all intersecting longitudinal and transverse joints [1][2]. A verification core taken around each dowel must show that the total voids do not exceed one‑half inch, ensuring proper load transfer [2]. The subbase beneath the repair zone must be free of depressions deeper than one inch; any such irregularities must be corrected by loosening and adding or removing material before placement of the patch [2]. Backfilling of the excavated area must be completed within forty‑eight hours, not counting weekends or holidays, and traffic on the sawn sections must be removed no later than seventy‑two hours after cutting [2]. The placed concrete must achieve a compressive strength of at least one thousand pounds per square inch before any traffic is permitted, and this strength must be attained within the time frame prescribed by the specifications; failure to meet either condition renders the work defective [1][2]. Surface workmanship must be free of inadequate consolidation, surface defects, high points or depressions greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch, any of which must be ground away so that the finished texture matches the existing pavement cross‑section [1][2]. The repaired segment must conform to length categories—between six and twenty feet for Type A, between twenty‑point‑one and sixty‑five feet for Type B, and up to five hundred feet for Type C—and it may not be narrower than a single lane width [2]. All original longitudinal and transverse joints intersecting the patch are resealed, completing the acceptance criteria for a full‑depth repair [2][1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–417)

Performance and service life

Full‑depth patching removes the entire deteriorated slab—including all major cracks and crushed joints—and replaces it with new concrete designed to restore proper load transfer through fully bonded dowels, which improves the pavement’s structural condition and provides a uniform support layer for traffic and any subsequent overlay. By eliminating the underlying distress and ensuring correct joint design, the treatment enhances functionality and durability, avoiding premature cracking or loss of load transfer that would otherwise reduce service life. PennDOT’s experience shows that such repairs, when built to specification, extend the useful life of concrete pavements. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89)

Full‑depth patching can fail when the original causes of pavement distress are left unaddressed, allowing the same load‑related cracking or climate‑ and material‑driven problems such as inadequate drainage to reappear [1]. The probability of recurrence is closely linked to the condition of the existing slab, including how much of the surface is being patched, its compressive strength and air content, as well as the quality of the subgrade or subbase and the provision of sufficient friction and drainage [1]. Material and construction choices also introduce new distresses; for example, using high early‑strength concrete tends to produce excessive shrinkage cracking, while installing tie bars or bolts before the patch has attained adequate strength can cause them to shift under traffic, especially on short patches [1]. Proper load transfer at transverse joints is essential, and inadequate dowel anchorage—whether from insufficient bonding along the embedded length or from poor handling during placement—prevents effective load sharing and can lead to joint failure; this issue is highlighted in both guides [1][2]. Non‑uniform support resulting from poor placement practices creates localized stress concentrations that further compromise performance [1].

Edge spalling or break‑back cracking at the saw‑cut margins is one of the most frequent failures, arising when cuts exceed the permitted half‑inch width, are not made through the full pavement thickness, or damage the surrounding slab during cutting [2]. Surface unevenness that exceeds the allowable 3/16 in tolerance signals another failure mode and typically results from inadequate compaction of the subbase, settlement of fresh concrete, or insufficient curing before traffic is permitted [2]. Voids larger than 0.5 in around dowel bars develop when dowels are not properly anchored or lubricated, compromising load transfer at joints [2]; this aligns with concerns about dowel anchorage raised in the other guide [1][2]. Opening the patch to traffic before the concrete reaches at least 1,000 psi compressive strength can cause premature cracking and loss of structural integrity, a risk that is amplified by premature loading of tie bars or bolts as described earlier [1][2].

The underlying factors influencing these distresses include improper saw‑cut dimensions, use of prohibited equipment that over‑penetrates the wheel‑saw, disturbance of the subbase during slab removal, exposure of fresh concrete to rain or prolonged open periods beyond 48 hours, and failure to protect openings immediately after placement [2]. Inadequate drainage, subgrade deficiencies, and workmanship shortcomings such as insufficient compaction or curing also contribute to the development of failures across both sources [1][2]. Adhering strictly to specifications—ensuring full‑depth cuts, timely placement, proper dowel handling, surface testing with a straightedge, verification of concrete strength, and provision of adequate drainage and friction—mitigates the risk of these recurring distresses.

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–417)

Monitoring and follow-up

After the patch has cured, the Representative must verify that the finished surface meets tolerance limits by testing it with a 12‑foot straightedge and grinding any high or low spots exceeding three‑sixteenths of an inch; the concrete’s compressive strength is confirmed through cylinder testing and traffic may only be opened when the material reaches at least 1,000 psi; dowel bar anchoring is also inspected by random core samples to ensure proper load transfer, and the placement date is scribed on the shoulder edge for future reference. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–412)

After a full‑depth patch has been installed, the guides require a thorough inspection for several distress indicators that suggest the repair is inadequate.[1][2] If intermittent patches are needed over a stretch of roughly 500 feet and less than about ten feet of original concrete remains between successive patches, the entire section is considered compromised and reconstruction of the whole length is warranted.[1] Likewise, when the cumulative length of pavement that must be replaced exceeds 500 feet in any lane, a design analysis should be performed to determine whether a thicker slab or other structural modifications are needed.[1] Individual patches that extend beyond 65 feet also trigger the requirement for longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts, indicating that the original patch depth may be insufficient.[1] Persistent extensive surface spalling after the patch is another indicator that replacement or a thin‑bonded inlay is required.[1] In addition, the appearance of cracks or spalling at any location and time after the patch, high points or depressions exceeding three‑sixteenths of an inch when measured with a straightedge, or failure of the concrete to reach the specified one‑thousand‑psi compressive strength before traffic is allowed all denote a defective repair that must be remedied.[2] Observable distress of the underlying slab provides early warning as well; transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks on either the traffic lane or shoulder, small blow‑hole depressions in the shoulder adjacent to a joint, corner breaks, and measurable slab settlement each point to ongoing loss of support beneath the patched area.[1] When any of these symptoms are detected, additional actions such as slab stabilization, repeat full‑depth patching, installation of tie bars or bolts, or broader rehabilitation should be considered.[1][2]
