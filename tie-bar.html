Tie Bar

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Tie Bar is a topic under Rigid Pavement.
tie-barsepoxy-coated-deformed-tie-barsepoxy-coated-deformed-tie-barslongitudinal-tie-bars
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Tie bars are epoxy‑coated deformed steel members installed at an angle across longitudinal joints or cracks in rigid Portland‑cement concrete pavements, especially where continuous slabs exceed about sixty‑five feet [1][2]. They provide essentially full (near‑100 %) longitudinal load transfer, preserve aggregate interlock, and prevent slab migration, differential settlement, or lane separation while still permitting the designed joint movement [1][2]. Placement follows approved shop drawings, with each bar positioned square to the slab edge within ±1⁄8 in., spaced at least 24 in from dowelled contraction joints and 30 in from transverse joints, omitted when a joint has opened ≥1⁄8 in.; installation requires epoxy bonding under ambient and pavement temperatures of 40 °F or higher and is verified by pull‑out testing after the concrete reaches 3,500 psi [2][1].

Purpose and design objectives

Tie bars are incorporated in concrete pavements to provide a structural link between adjacent slabs, addressing the problem of slab movement that can degrade load transfer and overall pavement performance [1][2]. In continuous paving sections longer than about sixty‑five feet, the bars connect longitudinal joints so that loads can be transferred across slab boundaries while still permitting the designed joint movement, thereby preventing differential settlement and maintaining slab alignment [2]. For patch repairs exceeding roughly 65 feet, tie bars restrain the newly placed slab relative to the existing lane, counteracting traffic‑induced displacement that would otherwise compromise the repair; for shorter patches the bar’s own movement can outweigh any benefit, so they are not required [1]. The guides also specify that when a longitudinal crack or joint has opened to one‑eighth inch or more, a tie bar adjacent to that joint should be eliminated to minimize shear cracking and limit differential motion [2]. In situations where original tie bars were omitted or joints are beginning to fault, cross‑stitching repairs install epoxy‑coated deformed bars at an angle across the crack, restoring near‑complete load transfer, strengthening the slab, halting migration, and preserving aggregate interlock [1]. Installation is directed by approved shop drawings and may be performed with a mechanical device that accurately spaces and positions each bar, allowing either mechanical or manual insertion, and the reinforcement is firmly supported at every intersection, spliced by tied or welded connections and lapped with existing bars according to standard splice details [2]. Collectively these measures satisfy the performance need for reliable load transfer, crack mitigation, and preservation of structural continuity in concrete pavements [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–450)

Tie bars are epoxy‑coated deformed steel members installed at an angle across longitudinal cracks or joints to achieve essentially full load transfer, thereby preserving structural continuity and preventing slab migration while maintaining aggregate interlock [1][2]. Functionally, they must provide reliable longitudinal load transfer between adjacent concrete slabs, especially in continuous precast sections longer than 65 feet, to maintain joint alignment and prevent differential settlement [2][1]. Structurally, the bar shall conform to the geometry and deformation criteria for deformed or deformed‑bent steel as specified in Section 709.1, be epoxy coated for corrosion protection, and be placed square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface within a tolerance of ±1⁄8 inch, while still being installed at an angle across the joint or crack as required by cross‑stitching practice [2][1]. Placement must respect clearances of at least 24 inches from any dowelled contraction joint and no closer than 30 inches to transverse joints, with spacing and location controlled by a slip‑form paver equipped with a non‑oscillating extrusion plate whose adjustable entry angle allows the required angular installation while stopping vibration when motion ceases for safety [2][1]. Durability requirements dictate that holes be drilled no more than 3⁄8 inch larger than the bar, never penetrating the slab bottom, filled with epoxy bonding compound, and that the bar be rotated three to five times to expel air before being covered by at least one inch of epoxy before troweling flush [2]. Installation may proceed only when ambient and pavement temperatures are 40 °F or higher, and the anchoring epoxy must be allowed to cure fully before traffic is permitted; any exposed bars at the end of a setting period must be protected, and holes that breach the slab bottom require removal and replacement of the affected slab [2]. Safety objectives are met by ensuring that tie bars do not impede the natural movement of existing joints, by maintaining the prescribed clearances from other reinforcement, and by verifying structural capacity through pull‑out testing after the concrete reaches a compressive strength of 3 500 psi using a center‑pull hydraulic jack capable of applying up to 12 000 lb or detecting slippage of 1⁄32 inch; bars failing to meet the resistance values in Table B must be replaced or supplemented while respecting clearance limits, and only approved manufacturers listed in Bulletin 15 may be used [2][1]. Serviceability is achieved by preserving continuous reinforcement across patches through firm support at every intersection, employing tied or welded splices lapped according to standard drawings with weld laps located at the centre of a patch to accommodate slab expansion, thereby ensuring that the pavement remains safe for traffic, retains its load‑distribution capability over its design life, and exhibits minimal shear cracking or differential settlement [2][1].

Scope and applicability

Tie bars are employed in rigid Portland‑cement concrete pavements, including both jointed reinforced slabs and plain slabs, where longitudinal continuity and load transfer are required [1][2]. They are routinely specified for precast concrete pavement systems when continuous slab lengths exceed about 65 feet, with the bars cast into slots according to the fabricator’s shop drawings to provide connection without restraining joint movement [2]. Slip‑form placed pavements also call for tie bars, using a mechanical device on the paver that accurately spaces and positions the reinforcement to meet line‑and‑grade control and positive interlock requirements [2]. For full‑depth patch repairs, longitudinal tie bars (or tie bolts) become necessary when the repair length is greater than roughly 65 feet because maintaining near‑full load transfer across such long patches is critical; for shorter patches the movement of a bar in partially set concrete can outweigh any benefit [1]. In retrofit or patch work on existing longitudinal joints, tie bars may be installed provided ambient and pavement surface temperatures are at least 40 °F, with installation involving a full‑depth saw cut, drilling holes no larger than three‑eighths of an inch beyond the bar diameter, inserting epoxy‑bonded steel bars while rotating to purge air, leaving about one inch of epoxy coverage at the surface, and sealing the joint after routing [2]. The material requirements call for deformed or deformed‑bent steel bars that satisfy Section 709.1 criteria and are epoxy coated as prescribed, sourced from a manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15 and shown on the standard drawings [2]. When a longitudinal crack or joint is in reasonably good condition, epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars placed at an angle across the discontinuity provide near‑100 % load transfer, strengthen the crack to prevent slab migration, and preserve aggregate interlock; this cross‑stitching technique also compensates for omitted tie bars in contraction joints and can be used to tie separating lanes or shoulders and to secure centerline longitudinal joints that are beginning to fault [1]. Tie bars are not employed on transverse joints or cracks, and placement is avoided within 24 inches of a dowelled contraction joint for non‑standard slab lengths; additionally, one bar adjacent to any joint where cracks have opened at least 1/8 inch may be omitted, and during the October 1–April 1 paving season an extra bar next to dominant joints is eliminated as specified [2]. After placement, pull‑out resistance testing is required once the concrete reaches the specified strength before traffic may be permitted, ensuring the intended load transfer performance [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–450), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 684)

Tie bars are limited to longitudinal joints in continuous concrete paving sections that extend beyond roughly sixty‑five feet; when a segment is shorter or the design intent is to allow natural joint movement, designers turn to other load‑transfer methods such as dowel slots or dowel‑bar retrofits. For full‑depth patches longer than about sixty‑five feet the use of tie bars may be justified, but for patches equal to or shorter than that length the risk of bar movement in still‑setting concrete—especially from traffic on an adjacent lane—outweighs any load‑transfer benefit, prompting reliance on alternatives like dowel bars, slab jacking, or other devices. Even when the length criterion is met, tie bar placement must respect several conflict conditions, including keeping a minimum distance of twenty‑four inches from any dowelled contraction joint on non‑standard slab lengths and avoiding installation within thirty inches of a transverse joint. If a nearby joint exhibits shear cracking of one‑eighth inch or greater, the guidelines call for omitting one tie bar per adjacent slab, effectively favoring other repair strategies. Seasonal temperature restrictions also limit tie bar use; work should not be performed when ambient air or pavement surface temperatures fall below forty degrees Fahrenheit, and during the cold season from October 1 through April 1 tie bars next to dominant joints are eliminated in favor of alternative measures. Additional constraints arise when drainage is deficient, the subgrade is unstable, or the joint distress has progressed beyond early‑stage cracking to severe spalling or faulting; under those conditions tie bars cannot adequately address the problem and full‑depth patching, slab stabilization, jacking, dowel‑bar retrofit, or joint rehabilitation become preferable. Cross‑stitching of tie bars is prohibited on transverse joints or cracks, reinforcing the need to select other strengthening techniques when such features are present. Finally, if slabs have drifted and would require forcing back against adjacent slabs, tie bars should not be installed, and alternative repair or load‑transfer approaches are recommended. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–447)

Design inputs and requirements

Tie bars are required in concrete pavement repairs when a full‑depth patch or continuous precast section exceeds sixty‑five feet in length, as stipulated by the federal Publication 408 specifications and reinforced by the rigid‑pavement Section 529.3 requirements [1][2]. For patches longer than this threshold the design must follow the PCC patching provisions of Publication 408 Sections 516 and 519, which call for removal of major working cracks and replacement with new concrete before tie‑bar installation [1]. When a longitudinal joint or crack is to be cross‑stitched, Publication 408 Section 528 prescribes the use of epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars placed at an angle across the joint, while the Roadway Construction Standards (RC‑26M) supply detailed geometry, material, and placement criteria for the technique [1]. The same standards require that aggregate interlock be essentially intact so that the tie‑bar system can achieve near‑100 % load transfer across the repaired joint [1]. Tie bars must be fabricated from manufacturers listed in Bulletin 15 and conform to the deformed bar categories of Section 709.1(a)1, (b)1, (d)1 or (e), with an epoxy coating as specified in Section 709.1(c) [2]. All size, location, and detailing information for each tie bar is documented on approved shop drawings, and the bars must be cast within plus‑or‑minus one‑eighth inch of the indicated slot locations [2]. Placement rules prohibit installing a tie bar within twenty‑four inches of any dowelled contraction joint or within thirty inches of a transverse joint, and a bar shall be omitted adjacent to cracks larger than one‑eighth inch or near dominant joints during the October‑April paving window [2]. The installation must not impede movement of existing adjacent joints, and each bar is required to be firmly supported and tied at every intersection using either tied splices or welded splices as shown on the standard drawings and in accordance with Section 1105.03(m) [2]. Holes for the bars are drilled with a hydraulic drill to a diameter no more than three‑eighths of an inch larger than the bar, must be dry, clean, and shall not cut through the slab bottom; after drilling, epoxy bonding compound is injected, the bar rotated three to five times to purge air, and at least one inch of epoxy coverage is provided on the surface before troweling flush [2]. The joint is then routed to a width of three‑quarters inch and a depth of one inch, and sealant may be applied only after the faces are dry and the epoxy has cured per the manufacturer’s recommendations prior to traffic exposure [2]. A quality‑control plan prepared in accordance with Section 106.03 must be submitted at least fourteen days before work begins, and tie‑bar activities are prohibited when ambient or pavement temperatures fall below forty degrees Fahrenheit [2]. After the concrete attains a compressive strength of three‑thousand five hundred psi—or when the epoxy anchoring meets the manufacturer’s requirements—a representative selects fifteen bars from the first day’s placement for pull‑out testing using a calibrated center‑pull hydraulic jack capable of twelve thousand pounds with a slip limit of one‑thirty‑second inch; the average resistance divided by spacing must satisfy the values prescribed in Table B, and any bar that fails is replaced or supplemented at no additional cost [2]. Slip‑form paving operations must employ a mechanical device capable of accurately spacing and positioning tie‑bar reinforcement to meet the placement tolerances indicated on Standard Drawing RC‑26M [2].

Design methods and criteria

The guides specify that longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are employed only in concrete patches or continuous precast sections whose length exceeds 65 feet, because shorter repairs risk detrimental movement of a bar embedded in partially set concrete [1][2]. When a longitudinal crack or joint remains in acceptable condition, the recommended repair method is cross‑stitching, which aims to restore nearly complete (approximately 100 %) load transfer across the defect by inserting epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle through slots cut across the crack [1][2]. Installation of tie bars follows the fabricator’s approved shop drawings and may be accomplished either by drilling slots that align with the bottom slots of precast slabs or by applying the cross‑stitching technique illustrated on those drawings, with both approaches required to preserve freedom of movement at adjacent joints [2]. Bars are placed along longitudinal joints but must be located at least 24 inches away from any dowelled contraction joint, and a bar is omitted where a crack equal to or greater than one‑eighth inch has opened or when dominant joints occur during the winter paving window [2]. Work may commence only after ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature have reached a minimum of 40 °F, ensuring adequate conditions for epoxy curing and concrete handling [2]. A full‑depth saw cut defines the joint, after which holes are drilled on each side of the joint to a diameter no more than three‑eighths of an inch larger than the tie bar; the holes are cleaned, filled with epoxy, and the bar is inserted while being rotated three to five complete turns to expel air voids, leaving approximately one inch of epoxy covering the bar surface [2]. The crack is routed to a width of three‑quarters of an inch and a depth of one inch, then flushed, dried, and sealed slightly below the pavement surface before traffic is allowed, with traffic prohibited until the anchoring material has fully cured in accordance with manufacturer instructions [2]. After the concrete attains a compressive strength of 3,500 psi—or when the epoxy meets its specifications—a quality‑control representative selects fifteen tie bars from the first day’s pour for pull‑out testing in accordance with PTM No. 1, using a calibrated center‑pull hydraulic jack and load gauge to apply up to 12,000 lb or until a slip of one‑thirty‑second inch occurs [2]. The average load obtained from the pull‑out tests is divided by the tie‑bar spacing to produce a pull‑out resistance expressed in pounds per cubic foot, which is then checked against Table B for compliance; bars that fail are replaced or additional bars are installed following an approved pattern while maintaining at least a 30‑inch distance from transverse joints [2]. For pavement patches, reinforcement is placed as specified in Section 1002.3 and the standard drawings, with tie bars firmly supported and tied at every intersection to re‑establish continuity; splices may be either tied or welded, and when welding is used the bars are lapped at the centre of the patch to accommodate expansion [2]. No explicit design equations, empirical relationships, or specialized software tools beyond the pull‑out resistance calculation and the referenced tables and testing procedures are prescribed for tie‑bar design in the guides [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–447)

Tie bars are mandated only when a concrete slab or patch extends beyond sixty‑five feet in length, a threshold that appears uniformly across the guides. For shorter repairs the use of longitudinal tie bars is discouraged because movement of the bar in partially set concrete under traffic can degrade performance more than it benefits the joint. The design assumes strict adherence to the manufacturer’s specifications for size, material and coating, with deformed (or deformed‑bent) bars conforming to Section 709.1(a)1, (b)1, (d)1 or (e) and receiving the epoxy coating required by Section 709.1(c). Placement must follow the approved shop drawings, with the slot positioned within plus‑or‑minus one‑eighth inch of the indicated location and oriented square to the slab edge. Additional geometric restrictions require that a tie bar be at least twenty‑four inches from any dowelled contraction joint on non‑standard slabs, that no bar be installed within thirty inches of a transverse joint, and that bars be omitted adjacent to joints where cracks exceed one‑eighth inch or, during the October‑to‑April season, next to dominant joints. Installation may proceed only when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at least forty degrees Fahrenheit. Holes for the bars are drilled no more than three‑eighths of an inch larger than the bar diameter on each side of the longitudinal joint, and the drilling must not penetrate the slab bottom; after placement a minimum of one inch of epoxy‑coated bar must remain exposed at the surface. Quality assurance calls for selecting fifteen tie bars for pull‑out testing once the concrete has attained a compressive strength of three‑thousand five hundred psi, using a calibrated center‑pull hydraulic jack no older than six months to apply up to twelve thousand pounds or until a slip of one‑thirty‑second inch occurs; the average load divided by spacing must satisfy the values prescribed in Table B, and any bar that fails this test is replaced or supplemented with additional bars while observing the distance restrictions. No explicit numerical safety factor or reliability index beyond these placement tolerances, material specifications and pull‑out performance criteria is stipulated; compliance with the listed thresholds constitutes the required level of reliability for tie‑bar performance. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–450), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 684)

Structural section and dimensions

Tie bars are required only on the longitudinal joints of precast concrete slabs that form a continuous paving section longer than 65 feet. Their locations and sizes must follow the approved shop drawings, with the slots for the tie bars cast to within plus‑or‑minus one‑eighth inch of the drawing coordinates. Design directions further require that each tie bar be positioned at least 24 inches away from any dowelled contraction joint and that no additional tie bar be placed within 30 inches of a transverse joint. When a joint has opened to one‑eighth inch or more—especially dominant joints during the October‑to‑April period—a single tie bar is omitted in each adjacent slab. No other dimensions for pavement, base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab thickness, shoulder width, edge support, or reinforcement are prescribed for tie bars. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–447)

Materials and mixture design

Tie bars are required to be steel bars that are deformed and receive an epoxy coating according to the specifications [1][2]. The deformation and coating requirements are linked to the provisions identified in Section 709.1 (a)1, (b)1, (d)1 or (e), which also govern bent tie bars [2]. No additional material property values such as yield strength, tensile capacity, or concrete mixture proportions are prescribed for the tie bar itself [1]. Installation must employ a compatible epoxy bonding compound as the anchoring material, and the drilled hole must be dry and clean before the epoxy is introduced by an approved method such as a caulking gun [2]. After the epoxy is placed, the steel bar is inserted and rotated several times to purge air from the joint [2]. The epoxy coating must extend at least one inch beyond the bar at the pavement surface to ensure proper load transfer [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 434–435), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 684)

Structural details and interfaces

Tie bars are installed in longitudinal joints or cracks of concrete pavements, especially where precast slabs extend continuously for more than sixty‑five feet, to provide near‑full load transfer and to preserve aggregate interlock while preventing slab migration, lane separation, or faulting of centerline joints [1][2]. The bars are deformed (or deformed‑bent) steel members that conform to the dimensional and deformation requirements of Section 709.1(a)1, (b)1, (d)1, or 709.1(e) and are epoxy‑coated in accordance with Section 709.1(c), becoming part of the longitudinal joint material system together with tiebolts and key formers [2]. Placement follows approved fabricator shop drawings; bars must be positioned exactly as shown and must not impede the intended movement of adjacent joints, with a minimum clearance of twenty‑four inches from any dowelled contraction joint on non‑standard slab lengths and no bar located within thirty inches of a transverse joint [2]. Where a longitudinal crack wider than one‑eighth inch has opened, one tie bar adjacent to that joint is omitted, and during the October‑to‑April period an additional bar is excluded next to dominant joints to reduce shear cracking potential [2]. Installation may be performed by drilling directly into the existing pavement or by the cross‑stitching technique; in either case a full‑depth saw cut is first made along the entire length of the joint, after which holes are drilled on each side of the joint at the prescribed angle, offset distance, and spacing shown on Standard Drawing RC‑26M [2]. Each hole is sized no more than three‑eighths of an inch larger than the bar, stopped short of the slab bottom, cleaned, dried, and filled with epoxy before the bar is inserted; the bar is rotated several times to purge air, leaving approximately one inch of epoxy at the surface which is then troweled flush with the pavement [2]. After the bar sets, the joint is routed to a width of three‑quarters of an inch and a depth of one inch, flushed, dried, and sealed to a level slightly below the pavement surface, with all work performed only when ambient and pavement temperatures are at least forty degrees Fahrenheit [2]. Pull‑out testing is required once the concrete has reached a compressive strength of 3,500 psi; any bar that fails this test is replaced, and extra bars may be added in an approved pattern provided they respect the thirty‑inch limitation from transverse joints [2]. Slip‑form paving equipment must include a mechanical device capable of accurately spacing and positioning the required tie‑bar reinforcement so that insertion can be performed either mechanically or manually [2]. When tie bars are incorporated into reinforced patches, reinforcement is placed according to Section 1002.3 and the standard drawings, with each bar firmly supported and tied at every intersection to re‑establish continuity; connections may be made by tied splices or welded splices, the latter requiring a lap of the tie bar at the centre of the patch as shown on the drawings to accommodate expansion [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–450), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 684)

Tie bars are employed at pavement interfaces primarily when longitudinal continuity over a long repair length is required, typically for concrete patches exceeding about 65 ft. [1][2] For shorter repairs the potential movement of a bar in partially set concrete under traffic outweighs any load‑transfer benefit, so conventional dowelled joints are retained. [1][2] When a long patch or continuous paving section is present, the preferred treatment is cross‑stitching, which embeds epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an oblique angle across the longitudinal joint or crack to achieve near‑complete load transfer and prevent slab migration. [1][2] The tie bars are positioned according to approved shop or design drawings, set square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface within a tolerance of ± 1/8 in., and the corresponding slots are cast to the same tolerance to align reinforcement with the intended load‑transfer path. [2] Installation must respect clearances from other joints; at least 24 in. from any dowelled contraction joint and 30 in. from transverse joints, and a tie bar is omitted on each side of a crack that has opened to 1/8 in. or greater, as well as adjacent to dominant joints during winter paving. [2] The bars are placed so they do not restrict movement of existing adjacent joints, and for precast slabs in sections longer than 65 ft the same longitudinal placement rules apply. [2][1] Holes are drilled on each side of the longitudinal joint no more than 3/8 in. larger than the bar diameter, cleaned, dried and filled with epoxy bonding compound, leaving at least one inch of epoxy exposed at the surface before excess is troweled flush. [2] The joint is then routed to a width of ¾ in. and depth of 1 in., sealed only when ambient and pavement temperatures are at least 40 °F, and traffic may not be reopened until the epoxy has fully cured per manufacturer recommendations. [2] After the concrete reaches a compressive strength of 3,500 psi (or when epoxy meets its specifications), fifteen representative tie bars are pull‑out tested; any that fail to meet resistance criteria are replaced or supplemented while maintaining the required clearances before adjacent lanes are placed. [2] In patch areas the new bars are firmly supported and tied at every intersection, spliced to existing reinforcement by either tied or welded laps, with weld splices located at the center of the patch to accommodate expansion, thereby establishing a proper load‑transfer interface among the new concrete, underlying base, and adjacent slabs. [2] Overall, these practices create a continuous reinforcing network across the interface between pavement layers, materials, structures, and adjoining features, ensuring effective load transfer while allowing necessary joint movement. [1][2]

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Tie bar installation must be timed for suitable climate conditions, prohibiting any drilling, epoxy injection or sealant placement when the ambient air or pavement surface temperature is below 40 °F. All drilled holes must be completely dry and free of debris before epoxy is injected, and any compressed‑air cleaning must use a moisture‑free stream. After routing the joint, it is flushed with water then dried thoroughly prior to applying sealant, ensuring that moisture does not become trapped in the bond. [2]

Tie bars are mandated only for longitudinal joints of continuous precast concrete sections that extend beyond approximately sixty‑five feet, making the length of the pavement segment the primary trigger for their inclusion [1][2]. When required, the reinforcement must be a deformed steel bar—either straight or bent—that receives an epoxy coating in accordance with the specified material sections and is installed at an angle across the longitudinal joint or crack to achieve near‑complete load transfer and preserve aggregate interlock [1][2]. Geometrically, each bar is positioned square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface within a tolerance of plus or minus one‑eighth inch, following the angle, offset distance and spacing illustrated on the approved shop drawing (RC‑26M) and must be placed at least twenty‑four inches from any dowelled contraction joint and no closer than thirty inches to a transverse joint; bars are omitted adjacent to joints where cracks have opened one‑eighth inch or greater [2]. The installation procedure requires drilling holes on each side of the longitudinal joint that are no more than three‑eighths of an inch larger in diameter than the bar, routing the joint to a width of three‑quarters inch and a depth of one inch, flushing and cleaning the hole, drying it, and filling it with epoxy bonding compound that provides at least one inch of surface coverage before rotating the bar to purge air voids and troweling the epoxy flush with the pavement surface [2]. Work may proceed only when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at least forty degrees Fahrenheit, and traffic must be prohibited until the anchoring material has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations, because movement of a bar or bolt in partially set concrete caused by adjacent‑lane traffic can degrade the performance of short repairs more than it benefits them [2][1]. After the concrete attains a compressive strength of three thousand five hundred psi, each tie bar’s pull‑out capacity is verified with calibrated equipment capable of applying up to twelve thousand pounds or detecting slip of one‑thirty‑second inch; any bar that fails to meet the prescribed criteria is replaced or supplemented until compliance is achieved [2]. For operator accessibility and safety, the slip‑form paver must incorporate a mechanical device that accurately spaces and positions the reinforcement while allowing both mechanical and manual insertion, and it must feature a non‑oscillating extrusion plate with an adjustable entry angle to ensure precise geometric placement of the tie bars [2]. Throughout, installation practices are required not to impede the intended movement of existing joints adjacent to the precast slabs, thereby preserving joint functionality while providing the necessary load transfer [2].

Verification and expected performance

The completed tie bar is verified through a sequence that confirms compliance with the design drawings, constructability standards, performance criteria, and overall project requirements. First, the contractor must demonstrate that each bar has been cast square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface within a plus‑or‑minus one‑eighth inch tolerance as shown on the approved shop drawings, and that the slot for the longitudinal tie bar is likewise positioned within that same tolerance. After placement, an inspection confirms that the bond‑breaker coating is intact, the bar aligns correctly with the longitudinal joint, and it can move freely without impeding existing joints; any exposed bar that does not meet these conditions triggers removal and replacement of the slab before adjacent slabs are placed or traffic is permitted. Constructability is assured by submitting a quality‑control plan at least fourteen days prior to work and obtaining the Representative’s sign‑off after a live cross‑stitching demonstration on a longitudinal joint, which verifies that the proposed procedures and equipment satisfy project specifications. During installation, the approved hydraulic drill creates correctly sized, angled holes as detailed in drawing RC‑26M; the holes are kept dry and clean, filled with epoxy bonding compound using an approved method, and each tie bar is inserted while being rotated three to five times to purge air voids, leaving a one‑inch epoxy cover that is troweled flush with the pavement surface. Sealing of the joint proceeds only when ambient and surface temperatures exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit, and traffic is not allowed until the epoxy has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidance; any hole that penetrates the slab bottom must be patched at no cost to the department. Performance verification occurs after the concrete reaches a compressive strength of at least 3,500 psi (or when the epoxy meets the manufacturer’s requirements). The Representative selects fifteen tie bars from the first day’s placement and subjects each to a pull‑out test using a calibrated center‑pull hydraulic jack capable of applying up to 12,000 lb or detecting slip of one‑thirty‑second inch. The load at slippage—or the full 12,000 lb—is recorded, an average is calculated, and this value is divided by the bar spacing to obtain pull‑out resistance in pounds per cubic foot, which is then compared with Table B. If the result does not satisfy Table B, the failing bars are replaced or additional tie bars are installed in an approved pattern while maintaining a minimum thirty‑inch distance from any transverse joint, and no adjacent lane construction proceeds until compliance is demonstrated. This integrated verification process ensures that the tie bar installation meets all dimensional tolerances, material specifications, functional performance thresholds, and contractual obligations.
