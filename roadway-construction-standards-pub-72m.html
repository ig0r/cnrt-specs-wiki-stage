Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M)

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of

The Roadway Construction Standards (Publication 72M) are PennDOT’s comprehensive set of design‑construction‑quality‑control specifications that govern the planning, execution, testing, and acceptance of both rigid (concrete) and flexible (asphalt) pavement projects across the Commonwealth. The standards provide detailed procedures for activities such as slab fracturing, dowel‑retrofit, slab stabilization, precast concrete installation, subbase preparation, material placement, compaction, density verification, joint construction, and related quality‑assurance testing, while also defining boundary conditions, climate limits, traffic‑control requirements, and performance criteria that must be met before a pavement can be placed or opened to traffic.

Purpose and intended use

Pub 72M is used by engineers when deciding the design details for rehabilitation treatments on Portland cement concrete pavements, providing the RC‑26M drawing set that defines required materials and installation procedures for dowel‑bar retrofits and cross‑stitching. It also guides designers of new or reconstructed rigid‑pavement projects to include Portland Cement Concrete shoulders, while flexible‑pavement projects must be built two feet wider into the shoulder area before applying the designated shoulder type; full‑depth concrete shoulders are required on Interstate and other limited‑access freeways, Type 2 shoulders on arterials, collectors and local roads, and Type 1, 1‑F, 1‑S or 1‑SP shoulders adjacent to flexible pavements when drainage problems are anticipated, when truck traffic exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150 on two‑lane roads, or when specific intersection geometry calls for shoulder use. For concrete‑pavement repairs and full‑width reconstructions the standard specifies patch boundaries, joint type selection (skewed or non‑skewed as shown on RC‑26M), and joint spacing criteria—30‑foot spacing for jointed reinforced concrete pavement per RC‑21M or 15‑foot spacing for jointed plain concrete pavement per RC‑27M when reconstructing lanes longer than about 500 feet—and requires that these elements be identified as separate pay items during preliminary engineering. When performing slab‑stabilization repairs, the standard mandates execution in accordance with RC‑26M, defines the number and pattern of holes to drill at joints or cracks, and states that for estimating purposes at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized if no preliminary testing has been performed. In urban interstate or expressway projects the standard directs construction of full‑depth cement‑concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints, requiring shoulder materials to match the mainline pavement from subgrade to surface. Pub 72M also supplies detailed quality‑control criteria that enable engineers to decide whether a pavement construction activity may continue or be opened to traffic; for slip‑form concrete pavements it limits edge slump to 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes and lanes and ramps, and to 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders, and requires suspension of paving after 200 feet if those limits cannot be maintained, while surface flatness after hardening must not vary more than 3/16 inch when measured parallel or perpendicular to the centreline. For plant‑mixed asphalt courses the standard supports the decision on mat density by requiring acceptance according to one of the levels in Table F, determining density with an approved nuclear gauge or electrical impedance gauge in the presence of the Representative and the contractor’s certified field technician, and demanding pavement cores for RPS construction; if cores are not taken within one day after placing the mixture, paving operations must be stopped. The standard also guides engineers in installing pipe underdrains and pavement‑base drains by specifying placement after fine grading, limiting fitting deflection angles to 45 degrees or less, requiring trench widths shown on Standard Drawings with a depth extending at least 2 inches into the subgrade, backfilling with Type A fine aggregate in hydraulically compacted lifts not greater than 9 inches while spraying water at one gallon per linear foot, and mandating flat placement of Class 1 geotextile with a minimum six‑inch overlap and submission of the installation method two weeks before work begins. For flexible asphalt pavements it directs spreading and striking off the mixture across the entire lane width or as much as practical, requires courses deeper than 6 inches to be placed in two or more layers each between three and six inches thick, treats quantities greater than 500 tons but less than 2,500 tons as a lot divided into five equal sublots, declares pattern segregation unacceptable when the difference in average pavement texture depth exceeds 0.024 inch, and defines flushing as defective when the average texture depth is ≤ 0.006 inch. Concrete‑pavement patching is governed by a QC plan that limits exposure of excavated patches to no more than 48 hours, permits traffic on saw‑cut areas for a maximum of 72 hours, prohibits opening to traffic any saw cuts greater than ½ inch in width or less than full depth, requires the summation of voids around the perimeter of each dowel to be less than 0.50 inch on any core, and allows opening to traffic only after cylinders reach at least 1,000 psi compressive strength. In subgrade construction the standard requires formation of the roadbed to the established top‑of‑subgrade elevation and compaction as specified in Section 206.3(b), sealing all constructed or disturbed surfaces each workday with a smooth‑drum steel‑wheel roller operated in static mode, checking grade and slope using a string line stretched transversely at intervals not more than 25 feet (or up to 50 feet or 500 feet when GPS/laser control is used), correcting any irregularities exceeding ½ inch before acceptance, and submitting a machine‑control grading QC plan at least one week prior to the preconstruction conference together with electronic data files for fine grading. For slab fracturing of existing concrete pavements the standard requires submission of a detailed fracturing plan and equipment list for pre‑construction approval, establishment of a minimum 1,000‑foot‑long test section to define the optimal fracture pattern, verification that the fractured layer’s average elastic modulus falls within the range given in Table A by conducting falling‑weight deflectometer testing at intervals of 50 feet or less with a 9,000‑pound drop weight, and specification of roller passes and equipment such as a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller before proceeding with production fracturing. Continuously reinforced concrete pavement patching is supported by requirements for full‑depth transverse saw cuts from the outside edge toward the longitudinal cut, a second cut of at least 1 inch depth at each end, prohibition of cuts extending into adjacent pavement or shoulders, removal of concrete between the two full‑depth cuts before making shallow cuts, limitation of wheel‑saw penetration to ½ inch and jackhammer size to 30 pounds, excavation and replacement of any disturbed subbase deeper than 1 inch, and installation of reinforcement bars tied or welded per standard drawings with splices lapped as shown, ensuring continuous reinforcement across the patch. Long‑life concrete pavement projects are guided by requirements for slip‑form construction, daily aggregate‑gradation monitoring by sieve analysis for projects exceeding 15,000 square yards of mainline concrete, prohibition of placement when evaporation exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·hr unless mitigated, discontinuation of concreting when descending air temperature falls to 40°F (45°F for accelerated‑strength concrete), a concrete temperature control plan if temperatures exceed 80°F at placement, a first‑truck plastic‑air content of 7.0 % ± 1.5 %, and verification of LTU alignment within 18 hours after completing placements greater than 700 SY. Stone‑matrix asphalt safety edges must be constructed as the standard edge treatment with an angle between 26° and 40° measured from the pavement cross‑slope extension, and paving must cease if the angle is out of range until corrective action restores compliance; lot acceptance is required for RPS construction. Full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete slabs are covered by minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and flexural strength of 650 psi, dimensional tolerances shown in Table A, and a detailed installation plan covering lifting, moving, protecting, lowering, adjusting, bedding, dowel‑bar and grout placement, and joint‑gap control. Rubblizing of concrete pavements is supported by a requirement for a pre‑construction rubblizing plan and equipment list, use of mechanical or sonic vibratory equipment capable of producing fragments of the specified size throughout the full depth, excavation of two 3 ft × 12 ft full‑depth sections within the first half mile to verify fragment dimensions, final seating satisfactory when vertical deflection under a roller is less than ¾ inch after no more than four passes, backfilling with No. 2A subbase in lifts not exceeding six inches, completion of any overlay within sixty calendar days while avoiding freezing conditions, and provision of a power‑driven self‑propelled saw capable of making two parallel cuts per dowel‑bar slot. Dowel‑bar retrofit work is directed by the standard to use a power‑driven saw that makes two parallel cuts per slot, a jackhammer weighing not more than 30 pounds (or 15 pounds if damage occurs), cleaning of slots within 24 hours of concrete placement and no later than 30 minutes before pour, alignment tolerances of 0.25‑inch maximum displacement, 1 inch maximum longitudinal translation, and 1 inch maximum horizontal translation, use of 1½‑inch high‑expansion caps leaving a ¼‑inch clearance, pre‑formed polystyrene joint material, bond‑breaker lubricant applied at least one hour before concrete, non‑metallic chairs providing at least ½‑inch clearance, epoxy bonding per manufacturer recommendations, and subsequent concrete patching, surface finishing, curing, sealing and traffic reopening. Pervious asphalt pavement systems are supported by a requirement for two test sections of at least 225 sq ft, an average infiltration rate exceeding 200 in/hr, compacted thickness not more than ¼ inch less than design, prohibition of placement when air or surface temperature is below 50°F, placement in lifts not greater than four inches, and limitation of roller passes to four to retain porosity. Subbase work is guided by a requirement for a Subbase Delivery Plan when quantities exceed 1,500 tons, layer thickness not exceeding eight inches (four inches when granulated slag is used), compaction from sides toward the centre with overlapping passes, remedial scarifying, remixing and recompaction if depth deficiency is ½ inch or more, and prohibition of any traffic on subbase constructed of No. OGS aggregate. Next‑generation concrete surface grinding and grooving is supported by a requirement for a self‑propelled diamond‑blade machine weighing at least 40,000 lb with a grinding head at least four feet wide, texture differentials not exceeding 1/16 inch at joints, a feather pass extending at least two feet into shoulders, texturing of at least 98 % of the surface, and an IRI not greater than 70 in/mile for posted speeds above 45 mph or 90 in/mile for speeds of 45 mph or less. Evaluation of asphalt longitudinal joint density is directed by a definition of lots of twelve‑thousand‑five‑hundred‑foot length composed of five 2,500‑ft sublots, requirement of one core per sublot tested according to PTM 715 or PTM 716 within 24 hours, a lower specification limit of 91.0 %, and authority for the Representative to determine any payment addition or deduction. Polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing is supported by a requirement for aggregate sampling at least two weeks before work, testing according to PTM 607/616/100 with specified lot sizes and percent‑within‑limits thresholds, approval of a test strip that demonstrates set time not exceeding ten minutes and traffic opening within one hour, and cessation of placement if the mixture takes longer than one hour and twenty minutes to set. Asphalt rich base courses are guided by prohibitions on placement on wet surfaces or when air or surface temperature is 35°F or lower, a requirement that each compacted layer be placed in a single lift between three and six inches thick, a limit on surface irregularities to one‑quarter inch, and a definition of the pavement as defective if depth deficiency of one‑half inch or more cannot be corrected. Superpave asphalt base courses impose similar temperature restrictions, require two or more layers of approximately equal compacted depth when total depth exceeds six inches for a 25 mm mix (or eight inches for a 37.5 mm mix) with each layer between three and six inches (or four and eight inches), define defects as surface irregularities over one‑quarter inch, and consider the pavement defective if depth deficiency of one‑half inch or more cannot be corrected. Pavement relief joints in rigid pavements require saw‑cutting directly above the interface within ±¼ inch tolerance, performance of saw cutting within seven days after joint placement, prohibition of sealing material when air temperature is less than 40°F or greater than 90°F unless allowed by the Representative, and filling the reservoir with sealing material to a level one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface. Unbonded concrete overlays require stabilization or repair of any unstable slabs or slab fragments before placement of the interlayer material, sweeping debris prior to installing a separation layer, placing geotextile flat with an overlap of 8 inches ± 2 inches, limiting the number of geotextile layers to three, and ensuring fabric temperature does not exceed 120°F before overlay placement. Concrete pavement cross‑stitching is supported by a requirement for a QC plan submitted at least fourteen days before work, prohibition of work when ambient air or pavement surface temperature is below 40°F, drilling of holes no more than 3/8 inch larger in diameter than the tie bar on each side of the joint or crack, and restriction of traffic opening until the anchoring material has fully cured per manufacturer recommendations. Ride‑quality evaluation is guided by a requirement that transverse profile deviation greater than 1/8 inch shall not be produced, any individual bump exceeding ¼ inch must be ground, verification MRI shall not exceed acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile on at least 90 % of the 528‑foot lots for each lane, and average verification MRI shall not exceed average acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile for all lots tested in each lane. Asphalt treated permeable base courses are prohibited from placement on surfaces that are unstable, frozen, or below 35°F, must be produced at a temperature below 320°F, placed within eight hours of manufacture, and compacted with steel‑wheel power rollers having a certified metal weight of eight to ten tons. Bonded concrete overlays limit concrete placement temperature to between 65°F and 90°F, prescribe scarification, shot blasting or water blasting for surface preparation, require joint backing installed within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface, mandate application of curing materials within thirty minutes after bleed‑water dissipation, set maximum edge slump at ¼ inch next to shoulders and ⅛ inch next to adjacent lanes, and allow opening to traffic only when compressive strength reaches at least 3,600 psi and bond strength meets ASTM C1583 requirements of an average of at least 175 psi with each individual test not less than 160 psi. Stone‑matrix asphalt mix design calls for preparation of a paving operation QC plan on Form CS‑413, scheduling of an asphalt preplacement meeting at least two weeks before placement, use of a minimum of three steel‑wheeled rollers each weighing at least ten tons, placement of a minimum of 100 tons at a trial location identified in the QC plan, and prohibition of placement when surfaces are wet or when air or surface temperature is 50°F or lower. Superpave thin asphalt overlay courses similarly require a paving operation QC plan on Form CS‑413, an asphalt preplacement meeting at least two weeks before placement, prohibition of placement on wet surfaces or when air or surface temperature is 50°F or lower, and use of steel‑wheeled rollers each weighing a minimum of ten tons. Measurement and payment of constructed asphalt pavement lots are supported by equations that calculate pay factors when percent‑within‑limits (PWL) is greater than or equal to 90 % (PF_X = 100 + 0.4 (PWLx − 90)) or between 50 % and less than 90 % (PF_x = 70 + 0.75 (PWLx − 50)), and define a lot as defective if the payment factors for asphalt content, percent passing the 75‑µm sieve, and percent passing the 2.36‑mm sieve are all 85 % or if density cores yield a percent within limits less than 50. The Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) are directed primarily to pavement design and construction personnel—including the project review team, contractors, and the District’s design and construction staff—who are responsible for preparing specifications and drawings; these users consult the manual during the design and execution phases of concrete‑pavement rehabilitation treatments such as dowel‑bar retrofit or cross‑stitching because it supplies the standard drawing set for those repairs (section 2.1 GENERAL) [1]. When new‑construction or reconstruction projects involve rigid or flexible pavements, the District Executive may decide to apply the standards and the design and construction team uses them to specify shoulder types (Types 1‑F, 1‑S, 1‑SP, 6‑F, 6‑S, 6‑SP) during the design phase of any Change in Road Type or No Change in Road Type work (section 2.6 SHOULDERS) [1]. Design engineers developing concrete‑pavement plans and specifications rely on Pub 72M in the preliminary engineering/design stage to define patch boundaries, select appropriate joint types—skewed or non‑skewed—and determine quantities that must appear on contract drawings and pay items (section 4.2 CONCRETE PAVEMENT PATCHING) [1]. For urban Interstate or Expressway projects that require full‑depth cement concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints, the District’s design and construction staff refer to Pub 72M during the construction phase to detail and build those shoulders as shown in the standard (section 8.15 SHOULDERS FOR JOINTED PLAIN CONCRETE PAVEMENT) [1]. Slip‑form paving of plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 501.3(g)) is intended for the contractor who performs the concrete work; the standard is applied during construction when a fully energized, self‑propelled slip‑form machine equipped with side forms must be used and continuous forward movement maintained, and opening to equipment or traffic may occur only before second‑stage sawing [2]. For rigid pavement projects exceeding 15 000 sq yd of mainline concrete, the contractor must submit a comprehensive paving‑operation quality‑control plan at least two weeks before paving begins, monitor aggregate gradations, and avoid placement when the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·h or air temperature falls below 40°F (45°F for accelerated‑strength mixes) unless mitigation measures are employed [2]. Subgrade work (Section 210.3) is directed to both the contractor and the Department Representative; the standard guides preparation, compaction, proof‑rolling, geotextile installation and quality‑control testing during earthwork, requiring the contractor to submit a machine‑control grading QC plan at least one week before the preconstruction conference, provide control points and grade stakes at critical locations, and re‑compact and proof‑roll after the subgrade has remained in place for more than 60 days or at least one winter season, while the Representative may require additional staking if more than 10 % of acceptance tests exceed ½ inch [2]. Subbase construction (Section 350.3) is intended for the contractor, who must prepare a Subbase Delivery Plan when the subbase quantity exceeds 1 500 tons and submit it to the Representative for review before any work begins; subbase operations may not start until the plan is approved, with the subgrade prepared as specified in Section 210 prior to placing the subbase [2]. Mat density acceptance of plant‑mixed asphalt courses (Section 413.3(j)) is the responsibility of the Department’s field inspector—especially the Inspector‑in‑Charge—working together with the Representative and the contractor’s certified asphalt field technician; during construction the inspector must approve density acceptance, determine density with an approved nuclear gauge in the presence of the technician, and accept the mat density according to Table F, while pavement cores are required for accepting the density of RPS construction [2]. Spreading and finishing of asphalt placement (Section 413.3(h)) is directed to the contractor’s paving crew led by a certified asphalt field technician as defined in Publication 351; during construction the crew must control mixture laying, screeding, compaction, edging and safety‑edge installation, perform lot acceptance sampling, observe temperature limits, layer‑thickness limits and segregation controls, and may elect to expedite delivery of acceptance samples to the LTS at no additional cost [2]. Stone‑matrix asphalt courses (Section 419.3) are intended for the contractor who will place SMA mixtures; the standard is applied during pre‑construction activities when a paving‑operation QC Plan must be prepared, an asphalt preplacement meeting with the Representative scheduled at least two weeks before any paving, and a demonstration that the proposed mix can be produced, placed and compacted to meet the specification, continuing to guide field control throughout the actual paving operation [2]. Plant‑mixed thin‑asphalt overlay courses (Section 412.3) are intended for the contractor; the standard is useful during pre‑construction planning when a paving‑operation QC Plan is prepared and submitted to the Representative before or at the pre‑construction conference, and an asphalt preplacement meeting with the Representative scheduled at least two weeks prior to placement [2]. Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (Section 530.3) is directed to the contractor’s construction team—particularly the Quality Control Supervisor, the Representative, DME/DMM and other QA/QC personnel; the standard guides daily concrete production during placement, requiring aggregate gradation checks, a temperature control plan when concrete exceeds 80°F at placement, sampling of the first truck in each lot for plastic air with acceptance of 7.0 % ± 1.5 %, verification of LTU alignment within 18 hours after any day’s placement exceeding 700 SY or per 2 640 LF intervals, and a QC Supervisor must be on site at all times or reachable within one hour [2]. Evaluation of asphalt pavement longitudinal joint density and payment (Section 405.3) is intended for the agency Representative who oversees pavement construction; during the active paving phase when longitudinal joints are being formed the Representative applies the standards to select sampling locations, receive core samples, compute percent‑within‑limits density values and determine incentive or disincentive payments, while the Contractor follows the same procedures for quality‑control cores [2]. Asphalt Rich Base Course construction (Section 314.3) is directed to the contractor under oversight by the Representative and Inspector; the standard is applied during construction, prohibiting placement on wet surfaces or when air or surface temperature is 35°F or lower, requiring each compacted layer to be laid in a single lift between 3 inches and 6 inches thick, acceptance based on Optimum‑Rolling Pattern density criteria, and deeming pavement defective if deficient in depth by ½ inch or more that cannot be satisfactorily corrected [2]. Superpave asphalt base course construction (Section 313.3) is intended for the contractor and the Representative or Inspector; the standard is applied during construction, prohibiting placement on wet surfaces or when air or surface temperature is 35°F or lower, requiring layered placement if the indicated compacted depth exceeds six inches for a 25‑mm mix (layers between three and six inches) or eight inches for a 37.5‑mm mix (layers between four and eight inches), declaring pavement defective if irregularities are greater than ¼ inch, and prescribing core‑drilling quality checks where the inspector measures depth and flags any section half an inch or more below design depth [2]. Concrete pavement cross‑stitching (Section 528.3(d)) is intended for the construction contractor; the standard requires preparation of a QC Plan as specified in Section 106.03 and submission at least fourteen days before work begins, demonstration of the operation to the Representative for final approval, avoidance of work if ambient or pavement surface temperature is below 40°F, and keeping the pavement closed to traffic until the anchoring material has fully cured [2]. Corrective action on concrete pavements (Section 507.3) is directed to both the contractor who performs corrective procedures and the Representative; before pavement‑depth test and smoothness acceptance testing the contractor must eliminate any ridge or valley exceeding ¼ inch on the transverse profile, use carbide grinding for defects up to 15 feet long with a maximum single‑pass depth of ¼ inch and groove widths between 3/32 inch and ¼ inch, or apply diamond grinding or removal‑and‑replacement as specified, and coordinate with the Representative at least fourteen days before the Department’s smoothness verification testing where the verification MRI must not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile on at least 90 % of the 528‑foot lots for each lane [2]. Asphalt Treated Permeable Base Course (Section 360.3) is primarily intended for the contractor acting under the Representative’s oversight; the standard is applied during construction when mixing, placing, spreading, compacting and testing the ATPBC, prohibiting placement on unstable, frozen or surfaces below 35°F, requiring production at a temperature below 320°F, placement within eight hours of production, compaction with steel‑wheel rollers weighing 8 to 10 tons in four passes, and one six‑inch diameter depth test hole per 3 000 square yards (with additional holes as required) [2]. Bonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545.3) is intended for the contractor and associated inspector/Representative who execute the work during construction; the standard prescribes surface preparation such as scarifying and shot blasting, placement when concrete temperature is between 65°F and 90°F, curing within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation, and mandatory bond‑strength testing at seven days per ASTM C1583 before opening to traffic [2].

Scope and applicability

Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) provide guidance for both asphalt‑concrete and Portland‑cement concrete pavements, requiring evaluation of drainage, base repair, leveling or milling, binder‑course thickness, surface friction, and shoulder condition for asphalt surfaces while PCC surfaces are assessed for drainage, patching, slabjacking, slab stabilization, dowel‑bar retrofit, diamond grinding, joint and crack rehabilitation, shoulder support, friction improvement, and cross‑stitching of longitudinal cracks [1]. The publication defines a Restricted Performance Specification that may be applied to Hot Mix Asphalt or Warm Mix Asphalt when Superpave wearing and binder courses meet minimum thickness requirements and the existing pavement is stable, leveled, sealed and patched before resurfacing [1]. Percent Within Limits requires contractors to achieve field densities near the middle of a 92.0 % to 98.5 % range for standard and RPS wearing‑course construction, and recycling provisions mandate retaining at least 15 % of milled material while allowing Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement in HMA, WMA, or cold‑recycled base courses [1]. Full‑depth reclamation is addressed as a sustainable rehabilitation technique that may use mechanical, chemical, asphalt, or other stabilizers to produce a stabilized base with structure‑layer coefficients ranging from subbase to cement‑type values [1]. For new construction or reconstruction projects with a rigid pavement structure, Portland Cement Concrete shoulders shall be provided, while flexible‑pavement projects shall have the pavement constructed 2 feet wider into the shoulder area (the 2‑foot widening does not apply to inside shoulders on Interstate and Other Limited Access Freeways) [1]. The standards require concrete full‑depth shoulders on Interstates and other limited‑access freeways, Type 2 concrete shoulders on arterials, collectors, and local roads, and they call for various Type 1, 1‑F, 1‑S, or 1‑SP shoulder treatments adjacent to flexible pavements when serious drainage problems are anticipated, when truck traffic exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150 on two‑lane roadways, or where intersection geometry such as curves sharper than 12°‑30′ or tight ramp radii would cause vehicles to use the shoulder [1]. For collectors and local roads, Type 3 shoulders may be provided using the same surface material as the pavement structure, and bicycle‑friendly rumble strips are addressed through referenced publications [1]. Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) provides detailed guidance for full‑depth repair and reconstruction of Portland cement concrete pavements, specifying that jointed reinforced concrete pavement must be built to RC‑21M with 30‑foot joint spacing, perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and seals, while jointed plain concrete pavement follows RC‑27M with 15‑foot joint spacing and the same joint treatment; patches longer than 65 feet require longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts, all patch joints must be dowelled and fully bonded with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders to ensure load transfer, and when a continuous section of about 500 feet or more is being reconstructed the design must address slab thickness, joint alignment and spacing, including an intermediate joint if existing spacing exceeds 20 feet [1]. The standards also restrict the use of high‑early‑strength concrete because of shrinkage cracking, recommending accelerated concrete for early traffic opening, which can achieve required strength in four to seven hours versus twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours for standard concrete [1]. Precast concrete paving systems are covered as an alternative to cast‑in‑place PCC; precast slabs may be no wider than a single lane and must be six feet minimum up to fifteen feet long (twenty feet maximum for prestressed units), with prestressing allowing up to four inches of thickness reduction but never less than eight inches [1]. Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) are applied to Portland‑cement concrete pavement slab stabilization, a process that addresses voids created by pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement which manifest as transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks, small depressions, corner breaks or overall slab settlement; the standard prescribes drilling holes at joints or cracks and injecting one of three grout materials—cement grout (approximately 1 cubic foot per hole), hot asphalt (30 to 50 gallons per hole) or high‑density polyurethane (about 0.4 lb per square foot)—to refill the voids, while also requiring joint and crack sealing and proper subsurface drainage; at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized when no testing has been done [1]. The procedures must follow RC‑26M together with specifications in Publication 408 Section 679 and traffic‑control guidance in Publication 213 [1]. Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) specifies design for full‑depth plain cement concrete shoulders that are tied to jointed plain concrete pavements, requiring dowelled transverse joints on urban Interstates/Expressways and allowing undowelled joints on rural routes when a high probability of incident‑management or work‑zone traffic control exists; if the two‑way average daily traffic is less than 10 000, widened slabs with asphalt or RCC shoulders may be considered [1]; the standard also mandates that all materials of a full‑depth cement concrete shoulder match those of the mainline pavement from subgrade to surface, and it addresses engineering problems such as edge stresses, differential heaving, settlement, and horizontal separation between pavement and shoulder [1].

The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242) further details slip‑form construction for plain or reinforced cement‑concrete pavements, requiring reinforcement free of rust, dirt or oil, limiting edge slumping to 1⁄8 inch between adjacent lanes and ramps and to 1⁄4 inch between lanes and shoulders, prohibiting concrete temperature during curing from exceeding 160°F, mandating a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 inches, and requiring accelerated‑strength pavements to achieve a compressive strength of at least 1,200 psi before traffic opening; concrete mix control is limited by an evaporation rate not to exceed 0.20 lb/ft²·h, concreting must cease when descending air temperature falls to 40°F (45°F for accelerated‑strength concrete), hot‑weather plastic concrete temperatures must be maintained between 50°F and 90°F, and form tolerances may not exceed 1⁄8 inch in ten feet [2]. Full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slab systems are covered in Section 529, requiring a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and flexural strength of 650 psi, a sacrificial thickness of one‑quarter inch, slab lengths not to exceed fifteen feet (twenty feet if prestressed), transverse joint widths between one‑quarter and one‑half inch, longitudinal joints no more than three‑quarters inch, vertical differentials across any joint limited to one‑quarter inch, and grout strength of at least 2,500 psi before traffic may be opened [2]. Rubblizing of concrete pavements is governed by Section 526, which mandates a pre‑approved equipment plan, removal of the asphalt overlay, verification of full‑depth breakage by excavating two 3‑ft × 12‑ft sections within the first one‑half mile, backfill placed in lifts not exceeding six inches and compacted until non‑movement, and final seating acceptable only when vertical deflection under a 50‑ton roller is less than three‑quarters inch after no more than four passes [2]. Dowel retrofit of rigid pavements falls under Section 527, requiring two parallel slots per wheel path cut to the slab mid‑depth with a power‑driven saw, concrete removal using a jackhammer not exceeding 30 lb (or 15 lb if damage occurs), cleaning of exposed surfaces within twenty‑four hours and final cleaning within thirty minutes before concrete placement, dowel positioning on plastic chairs with at least a one‑half inch clearance, and limits on dowel displacement of no more than 0.25 inch relative to the midpoint and translation not exceeding one inch [2]. Next‑generation concrete surface treatment by diamond grinding and longitudinal grooving is specified in Section 509, requiring a self‑propelled machine weighing at least 40,000 lb with a grinding head of minimum four feet width; grooves are spaced approximately one‑half inch center‑to‑center and cut to depths between one‑eighth and three‑sixteenths inch; joint or crack differentials must not exceed one‑sixteenth inch; cross‑slope deviation may not exceed one‑eighth inch in twelve feet; ridges are limited to one‑eighth inch; at least ninety‑eight percent of the pavement must be textured, and ride quality shall meet an IRI limit of seventy inches per mile for speeds over forty‑five miles per hour or ninety inches per mile for lower speeds [2]. Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (LLCP) is addressed in Section 530, adopting slip‑form as the standard construction method, requiring plant‑mixed concrete except where truck‑mixed concrete is approved for shoulders, closure pours or projects under 15,000 sq yd per year; daily sieve analysis of stockpiled aggregate must keep combined percent retained gradation within PTM No. 529 limits (deviation less than five percent if permitted); a concrete temperature control plan manages temperatures that exceed 80°F at placement; the QC Plan requires plastic‑air testing of the first truck in each lot to meet 7.0 % ± 1.5 %; alignment of LTU assemblies must be verified within eighteen hours after any day’s placement exceeding 700 sq yd by a nondestructive method across at least the first five transverse joints and one joint per 2,640 linear feet; a QC supervisor must remain on site during LLCP placement or be able to arrive within one hour [2]. Concrete pavement patching is covered in Section 516, permitting conventional concrete or rapid‑set patching material together with expansion‑joint filler, coated dowel bars, anchoring grout and bond‑breaker board; full‑depth saw cuts may not exceed one‑half inch in width; excavated areas must be patched within forty‑eight hours; surface tolerance is checked with a twelve‑foot straightedge and any high points or depressions of three‑sixteenths inch or more must be ground smooth; traffic may resume only after cylinders reach a compressive strength of at least 1,000 psi, and dowel‑bar voids shall be less than 0.50 inch [2]. Continuously reinforced concrete pavement patching (Section 518) requires patches constructed using concrete, reinforcement bars and two layers of polyethylene sheeting at least 4.0 mil thick as a new bond breaker; full‑depth transverse saw cuts with a minimum one‑inch partial depth cut are required at each end of the patch area; wheel‑saw penetration into the subbase is limited to a maximum of ½ inch; jackhammer size may not exceed thirty pounds; if the subbase is disturbed more than one inch it shall be re‑compacted before placement of reinforcement that must be tied or welded in accordance with the Standard Drawings [2]. Slab fracturing and seating (Section 522) requires a test section at least 1,000 ft long to establish an optimal fracture pattern and verify elastic modulus limits; fracturing shall not be performed within ten feet horizontally or vertically of culverts, cross pipes or utility sleeves, nor within forty feet of bridge approaches or three feet of fixed objects; sonic vibratory equipment must be avoided where subgrade liquefaction could occur; falling‑weight deflectometer testing is prohibited when the pavement surface is frozen or air temperature falls below 45°F or exceeds 90°F; seating shall use a minimum fifty‑ton pneumatic tire roller with tires capable of at least one hundred psi inflation, and traffic may not resume until an asphalt overlay is placed within one week of seating [2]. Subgrade work (Section 210) requires placement to the established top of subgrade elevation and compaction as specified in Section 206.3(b); sealing of all constructed or disturbed subgrade surfaces at the end of each workday with a static smooth‑drum steel wheel roller promotes continuous positive drainage and minimizes water infiltration; when the completed subgrade has been in place for more than sixty days or at least one winter season it shall be compacted and proof‑rolled before installing a Class 4, Type A geotextile separator and subbase; surface irregularities exceeding one‑half inch must be corrected by loosening the surface and adding or removing material as required; random quality‑control checks every five hundred feet and additional verification if more than ten percent of tests exceed limits are mandated [2]. Pipe underdrain and pavement base drain installation (Section 610) requires trench excavation to extend at least two inches into the subgrade but not disturb the subbase, use of Class 1 geotextile, perforated or semicircular pipe, prefabricated drainage cores and fine‑aggregate Type A backfill placed in hydraulically compacted lifts no more than nine inches thick while water is sprayed at a rate of one gallon per linear foot; fittings must have deflection angles of forty‑five degrees or less; geotextile overlap shall be at least six inches; open trenches may not exceed two inches depth overnight [2]. Subbase construction (Section 350) mandates a Subbase Delivery Plan when quantities exceed 1,500 tons, maintenance of aggregate moisture, installation of Class 4 Type A geotextile, placement of No. 2A or No. OGS aggregate to minimum compacted depths of three inches (four inches for No. OGS), layer limits of eight inches in compacted depth (reduced to four inches when granulated slag is used, or six inches if the full layer is compacted as required), and keeping the subbase at least 1,500 linear feet ahead of the succeeding pavement course; a finished depth deficient by one‑half inch or more shall be considered defective and must be scarified to three inches, blended with additional material, recompacted and retested; exposure to weather for more than sixty calendar days or traffic on No. OGS aggregate also triggers retesting; proof rolling is required before placing the asphalt base course for flexible pavements or cement concrete for rigid pavements, and any unstable subbase must be removed, the subgrade repaired and the subbase replaced at no additional cost [2]. Crushed‑aggregate base course type DG (Section 312) requires a mix of proportioned coarse and fine aggregates together with water measured by weight or volume; calcium chloride may be added at seven pounds per ton for Type 1 or 5.7 pounds per ton for Type 2 before water is added; placement in layers not thinner than three inches and not thicker than six inches, each layer compacted to not less than one hundred percent of the maximum density according to PTM No. 106 Method B; surface irregularities greater than one‑half inch are corrected by blading, reshaping or replacement; moisture control during transport, use of adjustable self‑propelled spreaders to avoid segregation, depth testing at intervals up to three thousand square yards, a prime coat not exceeding 0.15 gallon per square yard, and placement limited to temperatures above forty°F on unfrozen surfaces are required [2]. General crushed‑aggregate base course (Section 310) specifies layer thickness limited to no less than four inches and no more than eight inches for Method 1 (or five to ten inches for Method 2); surface irregularities tested at intervals not exceeding twenty‑five feet must be corrected if they exceed one‑half inch; depth measurement with a test hole for each three thousand square yards is required; any area deficient by one‑half inch or more must be replaced; the base must be protected from traffic until the surface course is placed, placement on wet, frozen or otherwise unsuitable subgrade is avoided, material segregation removed and damage repaired before final use [2]. Superpave asphalt mixture design for standard and RPS plant‑mixed courses (Section 413) covers wearing, binder and tack‑coat layers; mixture acceptance must be demonstrated by certification and density acceptance may be shown either through a non‑movement/optimum‑rolling pattern or by pavement cores; mat‑density acceptance includes a non‑movement or optimum‑rolling pattern method; scratch or leveling courses less than one inch in depth or equal to or less than 110 lb/yd² are covered; materials placed not exceeding five hundred tons in a continuous run are addressed; mixtures placed on unstable or non‑uniform bases are included; longitudinal joints for surface courses must be sealed with hot PG‑64S‑22 asphalt binder maintained between 265°F and 320°F, and sealant may not be placed when the air temperature is below forty°F unless allowed by the Representative [2]. Any course deeper than six inches in compacted depth shall be constructed in two or more layers of approximately equal depth, with no layer less than three inches or greater than six inches; a Safety Edge shall be built to a depth of at least one and a half inches and shall not be placed where the total pavement depth exceeds five inches; an average texture depth of less than or equal to 0.006 inch renders the pavement flushed and defective; pattern segregation is unacceptable when the difference in average pavement texture depth between segregated and non‑segregated areas exceeds 0.024 inch [2]. Stone Matrix Asphalt (SMA) wearing courses require a safety edge on the outside edge of SMA courses when the course depth is one and a half inches or greater; the edge must be formed with the same material and shaped to an angle of twenty‑six to forty degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope; mixture acceptance is organized in lots, each lot requiring a loose mixture sample and Gmm verification; acceptance is contingent on asphalt content, percent passing the No. 200 sieve and density meeting single‑sample tolerances; compaction must begin immediately after placement and achieve required density without flushing, roller marks, displacement, shoving, cracking or aggregate breakage; rolling speed, pattern, roller size or vibration may be adjusted as approved; areas inaccessible to rollers are compacted with mechanical vibrating hand tampers; paving operations must be stopped if cores are not taken within one day, if density or asphalt content fails for two consecutive lots (or three total), if the sieve‑passing percentage is out of tolerance, or if flushing occurs, at which point a cause analysis and proposed solution must be submitted before work can resume [2]. Pervious asphalt pavement systems (Section 420) require test sections of at least 225 sq ft each to achieve an average infiltration exceeding 200 inches per hour and a compacted thickness no more than one quarter inch below design; the subgrade shall remain undisturbed and permeable with eroded fine material removed; a detention basin is constructed on a minimum two‑inch layer of fine aggregate topped by coarse aggregate placed in lifts not exceeding eight inches; placement of the pervious mix is permitted only when air or surface temperature is fifty °F or higher; the mixture must be spread using smooth steel‑wheeled rollers (no pneumatic tires) and may involve no more than four static passes with a ten‑ton roller to retain porosity; existing vertical surfaces are conditioned with an emulsified asphalt tack coat; the mix is laid in lifts up to four inches thick using a track paver or mechanical spreader; storage of equipment or soils on pervious beds during construction is prohibited [2]. Asphalt rich base course (Section 314) may not be placed on wet prepared surfaces or when air or surface temperature is thirty‑five °F or lower; it shall be spread to a compacted depth of one layer between three and six inches; density acceptance follows the optimum‑rolling pattern specified in Section 413.3(j)3; surface irregularities greater than one quarter inch render the pavement defective; full‑depth cores are taken each three thousand square yards, and any core deficient by one half inch or more that cannot be satisfactorily corrected classifies the pavement as defective [2]. Superpave asphalt base courses (Section 313) require a twenty‑five millimeter mix placed in two or more layers when indicated compacted depth is greater than six inches, each layer between three and six inches; a thirty‑seven point five millimeter mix follows analogous limits of four to eight inches for depths over eight inches; placement on wet prepared surfaces or when air or surface temperature is thirty‑five °F or lower is prohibited; pavement is defective if irregularities exceed one quarter inch; core testing controls depth, and any pavement deficient in depth by one half inch or more that cannot be satisfactorily corrected is deemed defective [2]. Evaluation of asphalt pavement longitudinal joint density (Section 405) defines a full lot as twelve‑thousand five‑hundred feet of longitudinal joint divided into five sublots of two‑thousand five‑hundred feet; the contractor shall obtain five random six‑inch diameter core samples on the first day of joint construction for quality‑control density testing in accordance with PTM 715 or PTM 716; joints adjacent to existing pavement, within one foot of obstructions or where density was accepted by means other than cores are excluded; the lower specification limit for in‑place compaction shall be ninety‑one percent when computing percent‑within‑limits for each lot [2]. Polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing (Section 483) specifies a mixture of polymer‑modified emulsion, aggregates and water that must be spread to seal cracks, fill voids and produce a uniform surface while avoiding excess crowning; the material shall set within one hour of placement and operations shall be stopped immediately if setting exceeds one hour and twenty minutes; compaction before opening to traffic is performed with a pneumatic‑tire roller having tire pressures of forty to sixty psi; aggregate sampling and testing must achieve at least ninety percent within limits or the stockpile shall be rejected; any blending of aggregates requires written DME/DMM approval and on‑site laboratory verification; defective work is identified when excess flushing exceeds five percent of the finished surface, tear or drag marks exceed size limits, clumps larger than one and a half inches are present, washboard ripples extend over one hundred feet, longitudinal joint bumps exceed one quarter inch, edge variance exceeds plus or minus three inches per one hundred feet, or rut depressions measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge are observed [2]. Asphalt‑treated permeable base course (ATPBC) (Section 360) requires the aggregate to be dried as necessary, the asphalt material heated and mixed to produce ATPBC at a temperature below three hundred twenty °F, placement within eight hours from when it is made, prohibition of placement on surfaces that are unstable, frozen or below thirty‑five °F, and prohibition during rain; after placement the mat must be allowed to cool to one hundred °F before subsequent layers are placed; the mixture shall be spread in maximum four‑inch compacted lifts using a slip‑form paver or mechanical spreader and compacted with steel‑wheel power rollers weighing eight to ten tons applying four roller passes; longitudinal joints must overlap the edge of the lane by one to two inches; transverse joints formed after any interruption longer than thirty minutes shall be created by installing a temporary vertical bulkhead or by sawing; surface irregularities greater than one half inch are corrected by loosening or adding ATPBC without grinding or milling; depth is verified by digging one six‑inch diameter test hole per three thousand square yards [2]. Bonded concrete overlays (Section 545) require fresh concrete placed when the concrete temperature is between sixty‑five °F and ninety °F and the existing surface is below one hundred twenty °F; edge slump may not exceed one quarter inch next to shoulders or one eighth inch next to adjacent lanes; insulating blankets are required if forecast air temperature is forty‑five °F or less and may be removed once temperature exceeds forty‑five °F or compressive strength reaches three thousand six hundred psi; the process includes scarifying, shot‑blasting or water‑blasting the existing surface, cleaning within thirty minutes of paving, marking and cutting transverse and longitudinal joints to full overlay thickness plus one half inch (intermediate panels not longer than fifteen feet), applying curing compound at a minimum rate of one gallon per one hundred twenty‑five square feet, bond‑strength testing per ASTM C1583 on five random locations per lot up to three thousand square yards, and declaring the area defective if the average test result is below one hundred seventy‑five psi or any individual result is under one hundred sixty psi, which then requires removal and replacement of work deficient in depth, air content, bond strength, rain damage, honeycombing or surface defects [2]. Unbonded concrete overlays (Section 548) address unstable slabs and slab fragments that move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads, joint faulting greater than one quarter inch, surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch, and debris removal; pre‑overlay repairs such as concrete patching, slab stabilization, slabjacking or flexible base replacement are required; shallow repairs may use an asphalt, cementitious or similar pavement repair material before the interlayer is placed; the interlayer may be a geotextile laid flat with eight‑inch ± two‑inch overlaps, no more than three layers, edges extending at least four inches beyond the pavement and kept clean, damp but not saturated, or an asphalt interlayer of nominal one inch depth of Superpave mix; joint faulting over one half inch requires a scratch course or milling; surface temperature must not exceed one hundred twenty °F before overlay placement; debris must be swept away before installation [2]. Slab‑jacking (Section 681) applies to reinforced concrete pavement, jointed slabs of concrete pavement and bridge‑end slabs, specifying cement grout or high‑density polyurethane as the lift material; holes are drilled, grout mixed and jacked under the slab while controlling rise so that continuously reinforced concrete is raised within 0.02 foot of a string line grade and jointed or bridge‑end slabs within 0.03 foot; continuous injection pressures may not exceed two hundred psi, pressures up to three hundred psi are allowed only for short periods as directed, brief rises to six hundred psi are permitted when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade; if the lift exceeds tolerance by more than one tenth foot the over‑jacked portion must be removed and replaced per Section 516 [2]. Concrete pavement cross‑stitching (Section 528) requires a QC plan submitted at least fourteen days before work and a demonstration to the Representative, ambient air temperature or pavement surface temperature of forty °F or higher, drilling holes no more than three eighths inch larger in diameter than the tie bar, cleaning of the holes, injection of epoxy bonding compound, insertion and rotation of tie bars to achieve about one inch surface coverage, routing the crack to a width of three quarters inch and a depth of one inch, flushing with water, resealing to a level one eighth‑to‑one quarter inch below the pavement surface, and opening to traffic only after full cure; any hole that extends through the slab bottom shall be patched [2]. Ride‑quality evaluation for reinforced cement concrete and plain cement concrete pavements (Section 507) defines defects as ridges or valleys exceeding one eighth inch on the transverse profile, bumps larger than one quarter inch measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge, or an IRI above the maximum acceptable value in Table A; corrective actions may include carbide grinding for sections up to fifteen feet long with a single‑pass depth not exceeding one eighth inch, groove dimensions between three‑thirty‑seconds inch and one quarter inch wide, diamond grinding applied to the full lane width, removal and replacement of at least ten feet of pavement between transverse joints of reinforced cement concrete pavements or an entire panel of plain cement concrete pavement, and smoothness verification testing using an inertial profiler that allows a verification IRI to exceed the acceptance IRI by no more than six point one inches per mile on at least ninety percent of the five hundred twenty‑eight foot lots and in average across all lots [2]. Pavement relief joints (Section 504) require painting the joint faces of adjacent pavement with a thin coating of PG‑64S‑22 before placing fresh asphalt mixtures, making saw cuts directly above the interface between the asphalt courses within plus or minus one quarter inch, keeping the asphalt wearing course below one hundred forty °F before sawing, cutting a reservoir one inch deep by one half inch wide, not placing sealing material when air temperature is less than forty °F or greater than ninety °F unless otherwise permitted, controlling sealant temperature according to manufacturer limits and not holding it at pouring temperature for more than six hours, cleaning and drying the reservoir faces, using oil‑free compressed air, placing a tape bond breaker no more than one eighth inch up the face, filling the reservoir with sealing material to a level one eighth‑to‑one quarter inch below the pavement surface while avoiding overflow [2].

Key content and structure

Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) provide a comprehensive set of evaluation items and procedural criteria for both asphalt‑concrete and Portland cement concrete pavements, covering drainage improvement, base repair, surface friction upgrades, shoulder rehabilitation, dowel‑bar retrofit or cross‑stitching with epoxy‑coated tie bars, and they establish performance thresholds such as permitting diamond grinding only when wheel ruts are less than 0.5 inches deep, requiring surface‑course thicknesses to meet the minimums in Table 9.5, and mandating a uniform nominal depth of not less than 5,600 square yards with continuous placement; quality measures include a Percent Within Limits tie field density target range of 92.0 %–98.5 % for graduated pay factors, recycling practices that require the contractor to retain at least 15 % of milled material or provide all material when total milled quantity is under 200 tons, and full‑depth reclamation guidance that uses structure‑layer coefficients varying with the type of stabilization used[1]. For new construction or reconstruction projects having a rigid pavement structure, Portland Cement Concrete shoulders shall be provided, while flexible pavements shall be constructed two feet wider into the shoulder area (excluding inside shoulders on Interstates and other limited‑access freeways) and the remainder of the shoulder shall use the designated type; full‑depth concrete shoulders are required on Interstates and other limited‑access freeways, Type 2 concrete shoulders on arterials, collectors and local roads, and higher‑type paving is specified for roadways with serious drainage problems, for two‑lane roads where truck traffic exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150, and where traffic is anticipated to use the shoulder at intersections with ramps having radii sharper than 12°‑30′ or where left‑turn vehicles may force through traffic onto the right shoulder, in which case the higher‑type paving shall start 250 ft before the intersection and stop 150 ft downstream; collectors and local roads may receive either a Type 3 shoulder of the same surface material as the pavement structure or one of the Type 1 families, and bicycle‑compatible rumble strips are referenced to Publication 13 with standard strip details found in RC‑22M[1]. Concrete pavement patching must achieve load transfer at transverse joints by dowels bonded along their entire embedded length, designers must define patch boundaries so that all deteriorated material is removed, joint details follow RC‑26M with separate items for skewed or non‑skewed baskets, and when reconstructing 500 feet or more of adjacent lanes with Portland cement concrete the work shall follow RC‑21M for jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) requiring 30‑foot joint spacing, perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, or RC‑27M for jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) requiring 15‑foot joint spacing with the same joint features; accelerated concrete pavement patching may be used where opening to traffic is controlled by the patch operation, providing early traffic opening in four to seven hours, and for intermittent repairs using precast slabs slab widths shall not exceed one lane width and slab lengths shall be at least six feet but no more than 15 feet (20 feet for prestressed slabs)[1]. Slab stabilization is required when voids under concrete cause transverse joint faulting, fines near joints, small depressions, corner breaks or settlement; the work follows RC‑26M for the number and pattern of holes and may use cement grout estimated at one cubic foot per hole (0.25 bag of cement) with traffic reopening only after the grout cures (which can be 12 hours), hot asphalt typically 30 to 50 gallons per hole with traffic reopening after a minimum of 30 minutes once the asphalt cools, or high‑density polyurethane at 0.4 lb per square foot with traffic reopening after a minimum of 30 minutes once the foam expands and hardens; for estimating purposes at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized if no preliminary testing has been performed, joint and crack sealing must accompany stabilization, subsurface drains must be kept in good condition, and drilling and stabilizing are confined to a single lane using the same traffic control devices as required by Publication 213[1]. Pub 72M contains no construction details for continuously reinforced concrete pavement (CRCP); any CRCP work must be governed by special provisions inserted into the contract, may proceed only after approval from the Director of BODD, structural design should follow the 1993 AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures using DARWin software, and guidance on appropriateness is obtained from HDTD; an illustration (RC‑25M) showing full‑depth cement concrete shoulders constructed with dowelled transverse joints is required for all urban Interstate or Expressway projects that must use tied concrete shoulders[1]. The construction process begins with subgrade formation, requiring the roadbed to be formed to the established top‑of‑subgrade elevation and compacted as specified, then sealed at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel‑wheel roller in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage; after the completed subgrade has been in place for more than 60 days or at least one winter season it must be re‑compacted and proof rolled before placing a Class 4, Type A geotextile separator and subbase, and any surface irregularities exceeding ½ inch must be corrected by loosening, adding or removing material and re‑compacting[2]. Pipe underdrain is installed after fine grading is completed; trenches are excavated to the dimensions shown on the Standard Drawings and extend at least 2 inches into the subgrade, then backfilled with Type A fine aggregate compacted in lifts not exceeding 9 inches while each lift is sprayed with water at a rate of 1.0 gallon per linear foot, providing a minimum of 6 inches of geotextile overlap, using fittings with deflection angles of 45 degrees or less, and pipe joints follow the manufacturer’s recommendations, perforations face down, ends are sealed, and backfilling is completed only after inspection[2]. Plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements placed by slip‑form use a fully energized, self‑propelled slip‑form paving machine equipped with side forms and automatic alignment controls; longitudinal joints must be placed parallel to the final markings within 12 inches, edge slump may not exceed 1⁄8 inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and 1⁄4 inch between lanes and shoulders as measured with a 12‑foot straightedge before the concrete loses plasticity, dowel bars must be positioned so that vertical tilt or horizontal skew does not exceed 0.25 inch at each end while longitudinal translation is limited to 1 inch[2]. Transverse joints are sawed to a depth of one‑third slab thickness and a width of 1⁄8 inch with oversized backer rod, and tie bars selected for testing shall resist pull‑out up to 12,000 lb or 1⁄32 inch slip after the concrete reaches 3,500 psi[2]. Surface finish criteria require that after floating and straightedge testing the pavement not vary more than 3⁄16 inch parallel or perpendicular to the centreline, macrotexture shall meet a minimum mean texture depth of 0.040 inches (ASTM E965), curing temperature must be kept below 160 °F while maintaining concrete temperature above 40 °F using specified membranes or compounds, and compressive strength shall be at least 1,200 psi for accelerated mixes before opening to traffic; any defective work such as excessive edge slump, out‑of‑tolerance joints, or insufficient depth must be removed[2]. Concrete pavement patching requires a contractor‑prepared quality‑control plan that describes actions for concrete mixing, curing, joint sawing, sealing and testing prior to commencement; saw cuts shall be full depth, not wider than ½ inch, performed with a diamond‑tipped blade equipped with water cooling, traffic may be allowed on saw‑cut areas for no more than 72 hours, and unpatched excavations must be repaired within 48 hours unless graded fill or other approved methods are used; removal of existing pavement shall avoid drop hammers or hydro‑hammers, using a maximum 35‑pound jackhammer at an angle of 45 degrees or less for corner repairs, and any subbase disturbance deeper than 1 inch must be removed and replaced[2]. Dowel bar anchoring is verified by coring a 4‑inch diameter hole and is acceptable only if voids around the dowel are less than 0.50 inch; concrete mix design, placement and finishing follow Section 501.3, surface tolerance remains limited to 3⁄16 inch measured with a 12‑foot straightedge, and the pavement may be opened to traffic after the concrete attains a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi as measured by cylinder testing[2]. Continuously reinforced concrete pavement patching follows precise saw‑cutting procedures: an initial full‑depth transverse cut is made from the pavement edge toward the longitudinal cut, followed by a second transverse cut of at least 1 inch depth at each end, with all cuts kept at least 24 inches from existing cracks and never extending into adjacent pavement or shoulders; wheel saws may be used for the full‑depth cuts but must not penetrate the subbase more than ½ inch, the concrete between the cuts is lifted out without disturbing the remaining subbase, and any concrete strips at the patch ends are broken with jackhammers limited to 30 pounds and without harming existing reinforcement bars, while if break back exceeds 2 inches or bars are cut additional full‑depth cuts are required; when a bond breaker is present it is removed and replaced by two layers of polyethylene sheeting at least 4.0 mil thick[2]. Plant‑mixed asphalt courses have mat density acceptance rules that allow courses less than 1 inch deep or equal to or less than 110 lb/yd², as well as placements not exceeding 500 tons in a continuous placement, to be accepted by non‑movement; otherwise an optimum‑rolling pattern must be established with an approved nuclear or electrical impedance gauge operated by a licensed operator and documented on Form TR‑4276B or C[2]. Pavement cores are required within one day of placement, labeled with project and location data, and tested to determine bulk specific gravity and density; the theoretical maximum specific gravity must agree with the contractor’s daily value within ± 0.030 before calculating percent of theoretical density, and paving is suspended if cores are not obtained in time or if two consecutive lots or three total lots fail to meet a density payment factor percentage of at least 100[2]. During spreading and finishing of asphalt mixtures the material shall be delivered, placed and compacted during daylight at the laying temperatures listed in Table A, spread across the full lane width and built in layers no deeper than 6 inches; any layer exceeding 6 inches must be constructed in two or more lifts each between 3 and 6 inches thick, a certified asphalt field technician with a valid certification card shall be onsite, and for projects of 2,500 tons or greater the lot is defined as 2,500 tons divided into five 500‑ton sublots with strict sampling, labeling and security procedures; acceptance testing of asphalt content, sieve passing and density follows Table A and Table E tolerances[2]. The standards also prescribe construction of a Safety Edge of at least 1.5 inches depth (not exceeding 5 inches total) with an angle of 26 to 40 degrees, evaluation of pattern segregation by macrotexture differences greater than 0.024 inch and flushing by texture ≤ 0.006 inch, and adjustment of compaction practices to meet density requirements without roller marks[2]. Measurement and payment for Superpave mixtures use Percent Within Limits (PWL) thresholds: when PWL is ≥ 90 the pay factor for a parameter is PF = 100 + 0.4(PWL‑90); when 50 ≤ PWL < 90 the factor is PF = 70 + 0.75(PWL‑50); any PWL below 50 makes the lot defective, and the overall lot pay factor is OLPF = 0.5·PFD + 0.3·PFAC + 0.1·PF200 + 0.1·PFPCS rounded in accordance with ASTM E29; a defective lot or an OLPF below 90 permits the contractor to request a written retest within three weeks using prescribed core locations and methods (PTM 715/716), with cost deductions applied as outlined in Table J[2]. Crushed‑aggregate base courses are constructed after acceptance testing of the processing and mixing plant; water is added after proportioning and uniformly blended, directed calcium chloride is added at 7 lb per ton for Type 1 or 5.7 lb per ton for Type 2 prior to water; placement requires that the subbase or subgrade be prepared at least 1,500 ft ahead of the spreader, shoulders built up to the layer elevation, and if the compacted depth exceeds six inches the course is placed in two or more layers each between three and six inches; surface tolerance is controlled with templates cut to the required cross‑section and a 10‑foot straightedge, any irregularities over one‑half inch are corrected by blading, reshaping or reconstruction, each layer must be compacted to not less than 100 % of maximum density as determined according to PTM No. 106, Method B, with depth verified by a test hole for each 3,000 sq yd; joints are scarified and moistened, a prime coat is applied at a rate not to exceed 0.15 gallon per square yard, and the completed base course is protected from traffic until the surface course is placed[2]. Long‑Life Concrete Pavement uses slip‑form as the standard construction method; plant‑mixed concrete shall be used unless the Representative approves truck‑mix for limited areas, aggregate gradations are monitored daily by sieve analysis of stockpiled material and compared to the cumulative percent retained chart in PTM 529, any deviation greater than five percent triggers mix proportion adjustments and written notification to CMD QA, a concrete temperature control plan is required when placement temperatures exceed 80 °F, and the first truck in each lot is sampled for plastic‑air content (7.0 % ± 1.5 %) with trucks not meeting this range excluded from incorporation[2]. Longitudinal joint density evaluation defines a full longitudinal‑joint lot as 12,500 feet divided into five sublots of 2,500 feet; one core of six‑inch diameter is taken per sublot according to PTM 1 and PTM 729, and the lower specification limit for density is 91.0 %[2]. Polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt (micro‑surfacing) must be spread uniformly to seal cracks, fill voids and leave a uniform surface while maintaining sufficient material in the spreader box; the mixture must properly set within one hour of placement, and if setting exceeds one hour and twenty minutes the placement shall be stopped[2]. Asphalt Rich Base Course may not be placed on wet surfaces or when air or surface temperature is 35 °F or lower; it must be placed in one lift between three and six inches thick, and surface irregularities greater than one‑quarter inch render the pavement defective, while a depth deficiency of one‑half inch or more that cannot be satisfactorily corrected also classifies the work as defective; Superpave base courses follow similar guidance with specific thickness limits for 25 mm and 37.5 mm bases[2]. Pavement relief joints are constructed after the subbase is placed by excavating, building the sleeper slab, installing polyethylene sheeting, painting joint faces with PG 64S‑22 and placing the asphalt base and wearing courses; a saw‑cut must be made within ± ¼ inch directly above the interface between the asphalt courses after the wearing course has cooled below 140 °F, the cut forms a reservoir one inch deep by one‑half inch wide, and the reservoir is filled with sealing material to a level one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface[2]. Slabjacking requires a field quality‑control plan that provides existing roadway profiles showing depressions and proposed grade line; the slab must be raised to within 0.02 foot (or 0.03 foot for jointed slabs) of the string‑line grade, continuous pressures may not exceed 200 psi, short periods may reach 300 psi, spikes to 600 psi are permitted only when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade, and overjacking greater than 0.10 foot requires removal and replacement of the pavement in accordance with Section 516 at no additional cost[2]. Unbonded concrete overlay mandates that before placement the existing surface be prepared by repairing any unstable slabs or slab fragments, performing concrete patching, slab stabilization, slabjacking, flexible‑base replacement, or shallow repairs with approved asphalt or cementitious material for defects larger than 4 square inches and 1 inch deep; after debris is swept away a separation layer is installed, and if a geotextile interlayer is used joints with more than ¼‑inch faulting are milled, the fabric is laid flat, edges overlapped by 8 inches ± 2 inches, limited to three layers, extended at least 4 inches beyond the pavement edge, kept clean, dampened but not saturated, and protected from traffic while its surface temperature is kept below 120 °F; if an asphalt interlayer is specified a nominal 1‑inch depth of Superpave mix is placed, joints with faulting exceeding ½‑inch are treated by a scratch course or milling, the material is compacted with a rubber tire roller, and the pavement surface temperature must also stay below 120 °F before overlay placement[2]. Concrete pavement cross‑stitching requires submission of a quality‑control plan at least fourteen days before work begins, demonstration approval, ambient air and pavement surface temperatures not below 40 °F, a full‑depth saw cut along the entire length of the slab, and hydraulic‑powered drilling that creates holes no more than 3⁄8 inch larger in diameter than the tie bar at the angles, offsets and spacings shown on drawing RC‑26M; each hole is cleaned with a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi free of oil or moisture, tie bars are installed by injecting epoxy into the dry hole, rotating the bar three to five times to purge air, providing one inch of epoxy coverage at the surface and troweling flush, after which the routed joint (¾ inch wide by 1 inch deep) is flushed with water, cleaned and sealed only when the faces are clean, dry and temperature is at least 40 °F, filling to a level .125–.250 inch below the pavement surface without spreading over the slab; traffic may not be allowed until the anchoring material has fully cured, and any slab in which a hole penetrates the bottom must be removed and patched with a full‑depth repair at no additional cost[2]. Evaluation of concrete pavement ride quality defines that any ridge or valley measured on the transverse profile may not exceed 1⁄8 inch, bumps may not exceed ¼ inch on a 12‑foot straightedge, and a lot is defective if its MRI exceeds the maximum in Table A, if adjacent ridges/valleys exceed 1⁄8 inch, or if other specified thresholds are violated; incentive payment is based on verification MRI not exceeding acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches per mile for at least 90 % of the 528‑foot lots, and defective areas must be removed, replaced and retested unless an exception is agreed[2]. Asphalt treated permeable base course may not be placed on unstable, frozen surfaces or when air temperature is below 35 °F or during rain, the material must be placed within eight hours of production at a mixing temperature below 320 °F, it is laid in compacted lifts not exceeding four inches using a slip‑form paver or mechanical spreader, steel‑wheel rollers weighing eight to ten tons are applied in exactly four passes after the mat has cooled to 100 °F, joints are formed by overlapping longitudinal edges one to two inches and full‑depth transverse bulkheads installed after daily or thirty‑minute interruptions; surface quality is verified with a ten‑foot straightedge moved no more than five feet at a time, any irregularities greater than half inch are corrected by adjusting the ATPBC, depth control requires drilling a six‑inch test hole for each 3,000 square yards of completed base and replacing sections that are deficient by one‑half inch or more[2]. Bonded concrete overlay may be placed only when a concrete temperature is between 65 °F and 90 °F and the prepared surface temperature is less than 120 °F; fog‑misting or protective covers may be used to achieve these conditions, surface preparation calls for scarifying equipment to remove old material to the specified depth, removal of loose reinforcement, shot‑blasting or water‑blasting to a roughened finish, final cleaning no more than thirty minutes before placement, protection from oil and grease, and installation of joint backing within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface that cannot be dislodged by paving; bond strength testing is performed at seven days in accordance with ASTM C1583 for each day’s placement up to 3,000 square yards, the average must be at least 175 psi and each individual test a minimum of 160 psi; curing materials are applied within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates, insulating blankets are used when air temperature is forecasted to be 45 °F or less and removed once the temperature exceeds 45 °F or compressive strength surpasses 3,600 psi, temperature change is limited to no more than forty degrees per hour and curing compound is applied at a minimum of one gallon per 125 square feet; joint cutting specifications call for transverse cuts equal to the full overlay thickness plus ½ inch with widths matching underlying joints, intermediate cuts so panel length does not exceed fifteen feet, longitudinal sealant reservoirs ¼ inch wide, edge slump limits of ¼ inch next to shoulders and ⅛ inch next to adjacent lanes; traffic may be opened only when compressive strength reaches 3,600 psi and bond strength meets Section 545.3(e) criteria, and defective overlay sections are removed and replaced according to depth, air‑content, bond‑strength or surface‑defect thresholds with a minimum replacement length of six feet between transverse joints[2]. Stone matrix asphalt construction mandates that the contractor prepare a paving‑operation QC Plan on Form CS‑413 and submit it for Representative review before the pre‑construction conference, hold an asphalt preplacement meeting at least two weeks before placing mixtures to confirm specifications, sequence, mixture acceptance and sample custody; placement of SMA mixtures is prohibited from October 1 to March 31 in specified districts and may not occur when surfaces are wet or air/surface temperature is fifty degrees Fahrenheit or lower without written permission; the mixing plant must be fully automated, listed in Bulletin 41, provide a minimum 120‑ft² office, keep aggregates and mix free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture, incorporate any WMA additives or stabilizer systems calibrated to ±10 % of the required fiber weight with low‑level, no‑flow indicators and flow‑rate printouts, ship SMA material within two hours of mixing, and a HOLA laboratory that has passed an AASHTO re:source On‑Site Laboratory Assessment within the past two years must be approved; before full production a demonstration strip of at least one hundred tons is placed, sampled and tested to meet all specification limits, and vibratory rolling is allowed only after successful demonstration[2]. Superpave thin asphalt overlay construction requires the contractor to submit a paving‑operation QC Plan on Form CS‑413 before any work begins and to hold a preplacement meeting at least two weeks prior to placing the 6.3 mm wearing course; steel‑wheeled rollers of at least ten tons, equipped with watering or soapy systems and operated per the manufacturer’s recommendations, must be used, while pneumatic rollers are prohibited unless aggregate breakage can be demonstrated not to occur; mixture lot acceptance is based on individual samples tested by LTS according to PTM No. 757 (minimum sample size one thousand grams for a 6.3 mm mix) with asphalt content and 75‑µm sieve results evaluated against the single‑sample tolerances in Table B, and density must be verified with an approved nuclear or electrical impedance gauge using a control‑strip technique and documented calibration certificates[2].

Required inputs and assumptions

To apply Pub 72M the user must first provide a project‑specific assessment of drainage needs, base failures, surface levelness, required binder‑course thickness (derived from NDT or other design methods), desired friction improvements and shoulder condition for asphalt pavements; similarly for Portland‑cement concrete pavements the user must identify drainage issues, patching requirements, slabjacking or stabilization needs, whether dowel‑bar retrofit, diamond grinding, joint repair, crack sealing, shoulder upgrades or cross‑stitching are required [1]. If a Superpave Restricted Performance Specification is to be used, the user must confirm that the existing pavement is stable, properly leveled, sealed and patched, and provide clear plans showing the State Route limits of paving for both surface courses and structural overlays [1]. The user must identify whether the project is new construction or reconstruction (with or without a change in road type) and indicate whether the pavement structure is rigid or flexible because rigid projects require Portland Cement Concrete shoulders while flexible projects require a 2‑foot widening into the shoulder area before applying the designated shoulder type [1]. The effective shoulder width taken from design criteria charts must be supplied together with the roadway classification (Interstate, Other Limited Access Freeways, Arterials, Collectors or Local Roads) and any anticipated drainage problems because Type 1, 1‑F, 1‑S or 1‑SP shoulders are required on all Interstates and other limited‑access freeways and arterials, on roads where serious drainage problems are expected, on two‑lane roads where truck traffic exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150, and on two‑lane roads where traffic is expected to use the shoulder for left‑turn or right‑turn movements at intersections with tight ramp radii or curves sharper than 12°‑30’ [1]. When defining patch work the user must define the patch boundaries and indicate whether the affected length exceeds about 500 feet, because lengths of that magnitude trigger full‑width reconstruction criteria; they also have to supply quantities for new pavement joints and state explicitly if skewed or non‑skewed baskets are required per RC‑26M [1]. The user must provide excavation and sawcutting limits (Class 1), any “As Directed” undercut quantity for poor subgrade, joint spacing details—30‑foot spacing for JRCP or 15‑foot spacing for JPCP, with an intermediate joint added when existing spacing exceeds 20 feet on a single‑lane rebuild—and indicate whether longitudinal tie bars are needed for patches longer than 65 feet [1]. For precast options the inputs include slab dimensions (width no more than one lane, length from six to fifteen feet, up to twenty feet for prestressed slabs), any required thickness reduction and associated prestress forces, and the subgrade and subbase strength parameters that must be incorporated into the design calculations [1]. The user must specify whether the project is an urban or rural Interstate/Expressway, indicate if there is an anticipated need for the shoulder to support traffic during work‑zone control or incident‑management activities on a high‑volume corridor, and provide the two‑way average daily traffic so that the condition of less than 10,000 can be evaluated; these inputs dictate whether full‑depth cement concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints (required for urban projects and for rural projects with anticipated shoulder use) or tied shoulders with undowelled joints are to be designed, and when full‑depth shoulders are selected the user must also match all materials of the mainline pavement structure from subgrade to surface [1]. For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements the contractor must submit a complete field‑control quality‑control plan at least two weeks before paving begins that identifies personnel and responsibilities, specifies sampling and testing frequencies with action points, describes methods for determining opening‑to‑traffic strength and pavement depth, and outlines procedures for weather placement, evaporation control, subgrade/subbase maintenance, joint construction, edge‑slump prevention, grade and depth control, straightedge testing, curing temperature monitoring, timely joint sawing and sealing, damage avoidance and concrete‑pavement joint wipe testing [2]. The contractor must also supply daily aggregate‑gradation data obtained by sieve analysis of plant stockpiled aggregate before production and when bins are restocked, generate a cumulative percent retained chart and verify that it falls within the limits of the Combined Gradation Percent Retained Chart per PTM No. 529; any required adjustments to aggregate proportions must be accompanied by new mix designs and trial batches [2]. For slip‑form paving the user must provide measured edge‑slump values taken with a 12‑foot straightedge so that slippage does not exceed 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and does not exceed 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders, precise dowel‑bar and LTU placement markings so that transverse joints are sawed within ±1 inch of the dowel centreline, tie‑bar pull‑out test results after the concrete attains a compressive strength of 3,500 psi using a hydraulic jack capable of 12,000 lb or 1/32 inch slippage, macrotexture measurements according to ASTM E965 showing a Mean Texture Depth of at least 0.040 inches for radii less than 50 feet, and compressive‑strength test data from cylinders (or maturity values) that demonstrate the pavement meets the minimum strength required for opening to traffic while confirming that concrete temperature never exceeds 160°F during curing; edge‑slump limits of a maximum ¼ inch next to a shoulder and a maximum ⅛ inch next to an adjacent lane must also be supplied [2]. For superpave mixture design, standard and RPS construction the user must provide mat‑density acceptance information that includes the ECMS project number together with lot and sub‑lot identifiers, the exact station and offset of each density‑acceptance core, the placement date, the mixture type classified as Sample Class AS, the daily theoretical maximum specific gravity value to be used in density calculations, a current certificate of annual calibration for the nuclear gauge per ASTM D2950 and documentation of the gauge operator’s training; the chosen density‑acceptance level (non‑movement or optimum‑rolling pattern) must be justified by indicating whether the course is a scratch or leveling layer less than 1 inch in depth or ≤110 lb/yd², width under 4 ft, quantity not exceeding 500 tons in continuous placement, or placed on an unstable or non‑uniform base [2]. For spreading and finishing of asphalt the contractor must supply the project’s planned tonnage so that a normal lot size of 2,500 tons with five 500‑ton sublots can be established unless operational conditions dictate otherwise, provide a certified asphalt field technician holding a valid Publication 351 certification who remains onsite during all placement operations, label each mixture acceptance or Gmm verification sample with the ECMS project number, lot and sublot numbers, station and offset location, date of placement and mixture type, and include expedited‑delivery details in the quality‑control plan for discussion at pre‑construction meetings [2]. For stone matrix asphalt mixtures the contractor must submit a paving operation quality‑control plan on Form CS‑413 that details the specific construction equipment and methods to be used for placing and testing the mix, identify a laboratory that will perform local acceptance testing together with a current AASHTO re:source On‑Site Laboratory Assessment report not older than two years, provide any corrective‑action documentation for deficiencies noted in that assessment, and submit these items at the pre‑construction meeting for Representative approval [2]. For pervious asphalt pavement systems the user must supply two test sections each at least 225 sq ft, report infiltration measurements so that their average exceeds 200 in/hr, verify that the compacted thickness is not more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness, provide subgrade information confirming that excavated soil remains undisturbed, that any fine material or ponding has been removed and scarified to a minimum depth of 6 in, confirm that ambient and surface temperatures are above 50°F before placing pervious mixtures, and document the selection of smooth steel‑wheeled rollers limited to one‑to‑four static passes [2]. When subbase quantities exceed 1,500 tons the user must provide a Subbase Delivery Plan that details construction equipment and placement methods that will not damage the subgrade, confirm that the subgrade has been prepared and any deficiencies corrected as required by Section 210, verify that the Class 4, Type A geotextile specified in Section 210.3(b) has been installed, and supply material information showing sufficient moisture to prevent segregation together with the aggregate type (No. 2A or No. OGS) having minimum compacted depths of 3 inches for No. 2A and 4 inches for No. OGS while ensuring that layer thickness does not exceed 8 inches (or 4 inches when granulated slag is used) [2]. The Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) embed a series of assumptions, limitations, and boundary conditions that guide their application. Section 2.1 GENERAL assumes that a project’s scope should be in accordance with the Federal policy on pavement management and design and that each project presents unique conditions that must be thoroughly evaluated using engineering judgment [1]. Use of the Superpave Restricted Performance Specification is limited to overlays where the existing pavement must be stable, properly leveled, sealed and patched before the resurfacing course is placed, and where surface‑course thicknesses meet the minimum requirements in Table 9.5, with plans required to show State Route limits for the RPS portion [1]. Percent Within Limits density acceptance must consider the condition of the base, and contractors are expected to achieve densities near the middle of the target field range (e.g., 92.0% to 98.5% for Standard and RPS construction of Wearing Courses) [1]. Recycling provisions assume that at least 15 % of the estimated total milled material must be retained by the contractor, and if the total milled quantity is less than 200 tons the entire amount is provided to the contractor [1]. Section 2.6 SHOULDERS assumes that every new construction or reconstruction project is classified by pavement type, so rigid‑pavement projects shall have Portland Cement Concrete shoulders while flexible‑pavement projects shall extend the pavement 2 feet into the shoulder area, except for inside shoulders on Interstate and Other Limited Access Freeways where this widening does not apply [1]. Shoulder material and design are then limited by road classification and anticipated conditions: full‑depth concrete shoulders are required on Interstates and other limited access freeways, Type 2 concrete shoulders on arterials, collectors and local roads, and Type 1 (or its variants) when serious drainage problems are expected, truck traffic exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150, or the geometry anticipates shoulder use such as curves sharper than 12°‑30′ or specific intersection turning movements; for collectors and local roads with flexible pavements either Type 3 shoulders of the same surface material or the Type 1 family may be provided [1]. Section 4.2 CONCRETE PAVEMENT PATCHING assumes that full‑depth patching is limited to sections where the deteriorated slab can be completely removed; if a significant length of roadway (±500 feet) requires intermittent patches leaving ten feet or less of original pavement between them, the entire section must be reconstructed [1]. Joint design is bounded by RC‑21M which mandates thirty‑foot joint spacing with perpendicular joints and coated dowel bars, while RC‑27M limits spacing to fifteen feet; longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are only required in patches longer than 65 feet [1]. The use of high early strength concrete should be avoided because of shrinkage cracking, accelerated concrete may be used only when opening to traffic within four to seven hours and must meet the minimum compressive‑strength schedule in Publication 408 Section 501, and precast concrete paving should not be selected where reasonable detour routes exist or MPT is not a concern [1]. Load transfer devices must be bonded along the full embedded length of dowels, and any design for patches longer than 500 feet on all lanes requires a pavement‑design analysis to verify slab thickness [1]. Section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION assumes that loss of support is caused by voids beneath the concrete, especially near transverse joints and corners, and it limits the treatment to restoring sub‑base support without correcting surface depressions, increasing structural capacity, or eliminating faulting; the procedure must be accompanied by joint and crack sealing and functional subsurface drains [1]. When no preliminary testing has been performed at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints shall be stabilized, with traffic reopening only after the specified cure times for each grout material [1]. Section 8.13 CONTINUOUSLY REINFORCED CONCRETE PAVEMENT (CRCP) assumes that CRCP will be employed only in special cases and that its structural design must follow the 1993 AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures using DARWin; because Pub 72M no longer includes detailed construction specifications for CRCP, any CRCP project must contain special provisions in the contract documents, and its use is permitted only after obtaining approval from the Director of BODD [1]. Section 8.15 SHOULDERS FOR JOINTED PLAIN CONCRETE PAVEMENT assumes that shoulder design must reflect corridor classification and expected traffic conditions: urban Interstates/Expressways are assumed to require full‑depth PCC shoulders with dowelled transverse joints, while rural routes may use tied concrete shoulders without dowels only when no need exists for work‑zone or incident‑management traffic support; if the two‑way ADT is less than 10,000, widened slabs with asphalt or RCC shoulders become an allowable alternative [1]. When a full‑depth cement concrete shoulder is specified—either on urban interstates or rural high‑volume corridors anticipated to carry work‑zone traffic—the standard assumes the shoulder materials must match those of the mainline pavement from subgrade through surface, and it limits the use of dowelled joints to these defined situations [1]. The slip‑form concrete pavement construction assumptions require a fully energized self‑propelled slip‑form machine equipped with side forms, automatic alignment and grade controls and vibration/tamping across the full slab thickness; longitudinal joints are assumed to be placed within 12 inches of final pavement markings and not cross a driving lane unless drawings specify otherwise [2]. Edge slumping is limited to no more than 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders, measured over the 6‑inch edge zone with a 12‑foot straightedge, and paving must be halted after any 200‑ft segment that cannot meet this limit [2]. Joint placement tolerances restrict dowel misalignment to a maximum displacement of each end relative to its midpoint of 0.25 inch, longitudinal or horizontal translation of no more than 1 inch, and transverse saw cuts must be within ±1 inch of dowel centers, cut to a depth of one‑third slab thickness and a width of 1/8 inch [2]. Concrete temperature may not exceed 160°F; cold‑weather curing requires surface temperatures stay at or above 40°F (below 35°F renders the work defective) and temperature changes must not exceed 40°F per hour, while accelerated pavements cannot be opened to traffic until a compressive strength of 1,200 psi is achieved and macrotexture must reach a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 inch [2]. For mat‑density acceptance the standards assume density can be verified by non‑movement, optimum‑rolling pattern or pavement cores according to course type and conditions; non‑movement acceptance may be used only for scratch or leveling courses less than 1 inch deep or ≤110 lb/yd², for paving areas under 4 ft wide, for placements not exceeding 500 tons continuous, or for mixtures on unstable bases [2]. Optimum‑rolling pattern acceptance may also be used for courses ≥1 inch deep or ≥110 lb/yd² and for mixes placed at less than the minimum compacted depths in Table G, provided a certified field technician and an approved nuclear or electrical gauge are present, the optimum pattern is documented on Form TR‑4276B/C and the Representative verifies target density [2]. Pavement cores are required for RPS construction and standard extended‑season paving unless waived, but only when materials are placed at compacted depths ≥the minimum in Table G on stable uniform bases; a maximum of one core per sublot may be taken, drilled within one day after placement, and if cores are not obtained or density falls below the payment factor for two consecutive lots or three total, paving stops as directed [2]. Longitudinal joint construction assumes joints offset about 6 inches, located near the pavement centreline or within 12 inches of lane lines, with joint faces painted with PG‑Binder and sealant applied only when binder temperature is between 265°F and 320°F and air temperature is not below 40°F unless the Representative permits it [2]. Spreading and finishing provisions assume paving is performed in daylight and that mixture temperatures remain within the limits shown in Table A; any material outside those bounds must be excluded, and courses exceeding six inches compacted depth must be placed in two or more layers each not less than three inches and not more than six inches [2]. The Safety Edge is limited to a minimum depth of 1.5 inches, never greater than five inches, with a required angle of 26 to 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope extension [2]. Lot definition assumes a normal lot of 2,500 tons divided into five 500‑ton sublots; if work stops for five calendar days or more the lot must be reset, and only one loose acceptance sample per sublot may be taken, packaged within the specified box dimensions [2]. Acceptance criteria dictate that pattern segregation is unacceptable when the macrotexture depth difference exceeds 0.024 inch and flushing is defective when the macrotexture depth is ≤0.006 inch, triggering mandatory suspension of paving and corrective actions [2]. Pipe‑underdrain installation assumes it occurs only after fine grading and that pavement base drain is placed after the adjacent roadway pavement except in curb sections; open trenches are prohibited during precipitation or overnight, fitting deflection angles are limited to 45 degrees or less, backfill must be placed in hydraulically compacted lifts no greater than nine inches with each lift sprayed at one gallon of water per linear foot, trench depth must extend at least two inches into the subgrade while maintaining the width shown on the Standard Drawings, geotextile use is limited to Class 1 material for all base‑drain construction and prefabricated drains must be stored on an elevated platform protected from weather and UV exposure [2]. Subgrade preparation assumes the subgrade must be formed to the established top‑of‑subgrade elevation and compacted as prescribed, with daily sealing using a static steel‑wheel roller to promote drainage; any subgrade that has remained in place for more than 60 days or at least one winter season must be re‑compacted and proof rolled before placing the Class 4, Type A separator [2]. Inspection intervals are bounded by the grading method: manual checks cannot exceed 25 ft, automatic grading machines allow a longitudinal interval of not more than 50 ft, and GPS or laser‑controlled equipment may increase the interval to not more than 500 ft provided Section 210.3(d) requirements are met; surface irregularities exceeding ½ inch must be corrected, and acceptance testing imposes a threshold that if more than 10 % of random tests exceed this limit the Representative may require additional staking and string‑line verification [2]. Concrete pavement patching assumes a quality‑control plan will be prepared and approved before construction, that traffic protection follows Section 901.3(j), and that excavated patch areas may not remain un‑patched for more than 48 hours unless they are graded, compacted or otherwise protected; traffic may be allowed on saw‑cut patches for a maximum of 72 hours and saw cuts must not exceed ½ inch in width nor be less than full depth [2]. Concrete placement is limited to equipment that can maintain continuity, removal methods are restricted (no drop hammers or hydro‑hammers, jackhammer angle ≤45°, wheel‑saw penetration minimized) and subbase disturbance is limited to 1 inch; joint tolerances are bounded by a surface deviation limit of 3/16 inch measured with a 12‑foot straightedge and opening to traffic requires the concrete to attain at least 1,000 psi compressive strength as verified by cylinder testing [2]. Continuously reinforced concrete pavement patching assumes work will be confined to an area bounded by full‑depth transverse saw cuts that must not extend into adjacent pavement or shoulders; a second cut must be at least 1 inch deep and placed outside the initial cut as shown on the drawings, no saw‑cut may be located within 24 inches of an existing crack nor cut reinforcement bars in partial‑depth cuts [2]. Equipment limits require wheel‑saw penetration into the subbase to not exceed ½ inch, jackhammer weight to be no more than 30 pounds, and break back greater than 2 inches or damage to reinforcement bars triggers a new full‑depth cut; if subbase material is disturbed beyond 1 inch the disturbed material must be removed and replaced with concrete at no additional cost to the Department, and when a bond breaker is present it must be replaced by two layers of polyethylene sheeting of minimum 4.0 mil thickness [2]. Slab fracturing assumes equipment can apply variable force without causing extensive surface spalling, punching holes or shattering, and that sonic vibratory equipment may only be used when the fracturing will not cause soil liquefaction; unsuitable subgrade must be replaced at no additional cost, fracture locations are limited to at least 10 feet horizontally and 10 feet from the bottom of the slab for culverts, cross pipes and utility sleeves, at least 40 feet from bridge approaches or tunnels, and at least 3 feet from fixed objects; unfracturable panels must be saw‑cut into 5‑foot longitudinal by 6‑foot transverse segments [2]. Seating must be performed with a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller having four to seven wheels, tires capable of a minimum inflation pressure of 100 psi, and at least three passes for the Crack and Seat method or five passes for the Break and Seat method; vibratory rollers are prohibited, the roller may cross bridges only with bridge engineer approval, operations cannot occur in wet conditions, and performance verification relies on a minimum 1,000‑foot test section and subsequent falling weight deflectometer testing at intervals of 50 feet using a 9,000‑pound drop weight, which should not be performed when the pavement surface is frozen or when air temperature falls below 45°F or exceeds 90°F; the average elastic modulus must fall within the range specified in Table A [2]. Slabjacking assumes contractors will provide existing roadway profiles and surveyed profile drawings before work acceptance, using these documents to compare the existing grade, proposed grade and achieved grade after stabilization; vertical tolerances are limited to 0.02 ft for continuously reinforced slabs and 0.03 ft for jointed or bridge‑end slabs, any overjacking greater than 0.10 ft must be corrected by removal and replacement of the pavement at no additional cost [2]. Applied pressure is bounded to a continuous 200 psi, short‑term spikes are permitted up to 300 psi and brief peaks to 600 psi only when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade; the specified equipment such as grout plants, water tankers, drills and pump units must be used before opening to traffic [2]. Unbonded concrete overlay assumes any slab instability or surface defect larger than four square inches and one inch deep will be repaired before an interlayer is placed, and preparation is limited to a single sweep of debris removal; for a geotextile interlayer the material must overlap eight inches ± 2 inches, extend at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, not exceed three layers in any location, and the surface temperature of the fabric may not exceed 120°F before concrete placement [2]. Cross‑stitching assumes work can proceed only when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at least 40°F, is limited to longitudinal joints or cracks after a full‑depth saw cut, and expressly excludes transverse joints; a quality‑control plan must be submitted for review at least fourteen days before work begins and demonstrated to the Representative, drilling is constrained to holes no larger than three‑eighths inch beyond the tie‑bar diameter, shall not penetrate the slab bottom, and must be cleaned with a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi that is free of oil or moisture, and sealing may only occur under the same temperature condition and the sealant must be placed one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the surface without spilling onto the pavement [2]. Corrective‑action rules assume any ridge or valley may not exceed one‑eighth inch when measured on the transverse profile and must be corrected before pavement‑depth testing; carbide grinding is limited to areas fifteen feet long or less, each pass being limited to one‑eighth inch depth, and grooves must be between 3/32 inch and 1/4 inch wide with land areas between 1/16 inch and 3/16 inch [2]. Measurement and payment provisions assume pavement quality can be judged by percent‑within‑limits (PWL) values for in‑place density, asphalt content, the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve and the primary control sieve, limiting acceptable performance to PWL greater than or equal to 50 for each of those parameters and declaring a lot defective when either PWLD or PWLAC is less than 50; for 9.5 mm and 12.5 mm courses with an average density below 93.0 the maximum density pay factor is capped at 100, payment adjustments are calculated from the PWLs using equations supplied for PWL ≥ 90 and for PWL < 90 and ≥ 50, and the overall lot pay factor is formed as a weighted sum of the individual pay factors (0.5 PFD + 0.3 PFAC + 0.1 PF200 + 0.1 PFPCS) rounded in accordance with ASTM E29; retesting is bounded by a requirement that written retest requests be submitted to the District Executive within three weeks of the LTS test release, that samples are taken 24 inches from the original locations, and retests are not permitted when density cores were coated with paraffin wax [2].

Method and workflow

The application of Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) begins with the identification of a new construction or reconstruction project and the classification of its pavement structure; if the structure is rigid, Portland Cement Concrete shoulders shall be provided, whereas if the structure is flexible the pavement shall be constructed two feet wider into the shoulder area [1]. For flexible pavements the appropriate shoulder type is then selected: concrete full‑depth shoulders are required for Interstates and other limited‑access freeways, Type 2 concrete shoulders are used on arterials, collectors, and local roads, and Type 1, 1‑F, 1‑S, or 1‑SP shoulders adjacent to flexible pavements are called for when the road is an Interstate or arterial, drainage problems are serious, truck traffic on a two‑lane roadway exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150, or shoulder use is anticipated at intersections with tight radii or turning movements; alternatively Type 3 shoulders may be used on collectors and local roads when the same surface material as the pavement structure is desired [1]. When the project is an urban Interstate or Expressway, full‑depth plain cement concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints are constructed exactly as illustrated in Publication 72M RC‑25M and all such projects must use tied concrete shoulders; for a rural Interstate or Expressway where there is no anticipated need to use the shoulders for work‑zone traffic control or incident management on a high‑volume corridor, tied shoulders with undowelled transverse joints are placed using jointed plain concrete pavement, and when the two‑way ADT is less than 10 000 widened slabs with asphalt or RCC shoulders may be considered [1]. The application of Pub 72M also requires the preparation of a quality‑control (QC) plan as required by Section 106.03 and Form CS‑413, which must be submitted for review at least two weeks before any work commences and shall describe equipment, materials, methods and testing procedures for the specific pavement type [2]. Subgrade work follows Pub 72M guidance: the roadbed is formed to the established top of subgrade elevation and compacted as specified in Section 206.3(b), fill materials are placed and compacted to that elevation while sealing each day’s work with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller in static mode, and after the completed subgrade has been in place for more than 60 days or at least one winter season it is re‑compacted and proof rolled before installing a Class 4, Type A geotextile separator as specified in Section 212.3(d)1 [2]. Pipe underdrain and pavement‑base drain installation requires the contractor to submit the proposed method in writing at least two weeks before work begins, ship drainage materials protected from damage, excavate a trench of the width and depth shown on the Standard Drawings without disturbing the subbase, place Class 1 geotextile as specified, install the pipe with perforations down and semicircular side down, backfill using nine‑inch maximum hydraulically compacted lifts of fine aggregate Type A sprayed at one gallon per linear foot while maintaining a minimum six‑inch geotextile overlap, and obtain Inspector‑in‑Charge approval before completing backfilling [2]. For plant‑mixed asphalt courses the Inspector‑in‑Charge first determines whether density may be accepted by non‑movement or requires an optimum‑rolling pattern based on course depth, weight per square yard and placement size; courses less than one inch deep or ≤110 lb/yd², or placements not exceeding 500 tons may be approved for non‑movement, otherwise an optimum‑rolling pattern is required, established with the Representative and a certified asphalt field technician present, measured with an approved nuclear gauge per PTM No. 402 or an electrical impedance gauge per PTM No. 403, documented on Form TR‑4276B/C, and applied until target density is met [2]. When concrete pavement is required, the contractor mobilizes a fully energized self‑propelled slip‑form machine equipped with side forms, automatic alignment and grade controls and vibrating/tamping capability as described in Section 501.3(g)1, sets line and grade so that longitudinal joints are parallel to final markings and within 12 inches unless drawings require otherwise, then places concrete in continuous forward motion while mixing, delivery and spreading are coordinated; if the paver stops the vibration and tamping functions are disengaged (Section 501.3(g)3) [2]. Edge slumping is continuously monitored with a 12‑foot straightedge before the concrete loses plasticity and must not exceed 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders; paving shall be suspended after 200 ft if those limits cannot be met (Section 501.3(g)5.a) [2]. Load‑transfer units or dowel bars are installed in advance, their locations being marked so that vertical tilt or horizontal skew does not exceed 0.25 inch per end and longitudinal and horizontal translation do not exceed 1 inch; transverse joints are then sawed over the centre of the dowels as specified (Section 501.3(i)) [2]. Fifteen tie‑bars are tested at a concrete compressive strength of 3,500 psi using a hydraulic jack up to 12,000 lb or 1/32 inch slip and spacing is adjusted until the average pull‑out resistance meets Table B (Section 501.3(i)) [2]. After hardening edge slump is re‑tested and correction methods are submitted if needed (Section 501.3(g)5.b) [2]. Final strike‑off, consolidation, floating and straightedge surface checks are performed with deviation not greater than 3/16 inch; a broom texture is applied and curing begins within 30 minutes of bleed water dissipation, temperature being kept at or below 160 °F until the compressive strength required for traffic opening is reached, the latter being verified by cylinder testing per PTM No.604 or the maturity method AASHTO T413; only after joint, surface‑tolerance, depth and macro‑texture (MTD ≥ 0.040 inch) criteria are satisfied may the pavement be opened to traffic (Section 501.3(q)) [2]. If concrete pavement patching is required, the contractor prepares a QC plan as required by Section 106.03(a)2.a describing actions for mixing, curing, joint sawing and sealing and submits it for review; the designer first determines the patch boundaries so that all deteriorated pavement is removed, and when the affected length approaches ±500 feet a full‑width reconstruction may be required instead of intermittent patches (Section 4.2) [1]. The patch procedure then calls for making all required full‑depth longitudinal saw cuts along the perimeter of the patch before making any full‑depth transverse saw cuts, keeping cuts in an adjacent lane no more than 1 foot from the existing joint and a temporary separator not greater than 1/8 inch thick (Section 516.3) [2]. Unsound concrete is removed using a maximum 35‑pound jackhammer at a 45‑degree angle for corner repairs while avoiding drop or hydraulic hammers; the subbase is re‑compacted if disturbed, dowel bars are installed in holes drilled up to 1/8 inch larger than the bar and anchored with material that reaches initial set within five minutes, lubricated on Type B bars at least one hour before concrete placement, and verified by core drilling where voids must be less than 0.50 inch (Section 516.3) [2]. New concrete is placed, finished to match existing texture, cured with an approved membrane‑forming compound, grout‑filled around the patch and surface tolerance checked with a 12‑foot straightedge, grinding any high points or depressions of 3/16 inch or more; traffic may be opened when cylinder tests show a compressive strength of at least 1,000 psi as measured by PTM No.604 (Section 516.3) [2]. For any patch longer than 65 feet longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts must be included (Section 4.5) [1]. Slab stabilization begins with identifying loss‑of‑support signs such as transverse joint faulting or corner breaks and then selecting a grout material—cement, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane—based on site conditions; holes are drilled according to the number and pattern specified in RC‑26M using an estimate of one cubic foot of cement grout per hole (0.25 bag), 30–50 gallons of hot asphalt per hole, or 0.4 pounds of polyurethane per square foot of slab (Section 8.15) [1]. The chosen material is pumped, traffic and crew are protected during pumping, and the slab remains closed to traffic until the material cures—12 hours for cement grout, a minimum of 30 minutes for hot‑asphalt cooling, or a minimum of 30 minutes for polyurethane expansion (Section 8.15) [1]. Finally, joints are sealed and subsurface drains maintained, ensuring that at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints are stabilized when no preliminary testing has been performed (Section 8.15) [1].

Criteria, outputs, and interpretation

The Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) define the detailed geometry and installation of load‑transfer devices for concrete pavements by reference to the standard drawing set RC‑26M, which specifies how dowel bar retrofits and cross‑stitching are placed across transverse cracks or joints [1][2]. In shoulder design the standards classify treatments according to pavement type and roadway function: any new construction or reconstruction project with a rigid pavement structure shall provide Portland Cement Concrete shoulders, while projects with flexible pavements must be widened two feet into the shoulder area before applying the designated shoulder type [1]. Concrete full‑depth shoulders are mandated on Interstate and other limited‑access freeways; Type 2 concrete shoulders are required on arterials, collectors and local roads; and Type 1 shoulders—including variants 1‑F, 1‑S and 1‑SP—are prescribed adjacent to flexible pavements on all Interstates, arterials, on roadways where the Design Hourly Volume of truck traffic exceeds 150, or where anticipated vehicle movements require shoulder use such as tight‑radius ramp intersections, curves sharper than 12°‑30′, and left‑turn situations that start 250 ft before an intersection and stop 150 ft downstream [1]. For collectors and local roads the standard also permits Type 3 shoulders of the same surface material as the pavement or the use of the aforementioned Type 1 variants, and bicycle‑friendly shoulder types (Types 1‑F, 1‑S, 1‑SP, 6‑F, 6‑S, 6‑SP) may be provided at the District Executive’s discretion [1]. In concrete pavement patching, full‑depth patches shall follow RC‑26M and designers must select joint spacing according to the pavement type: reconstruction of segments 500 feet or longer with Portland cement concrete requires thirty‑foot joint spacing for jointed reinforced concrete pavement (RC‑21M) or fifteen‑foot joint spacing for jointed plain concrete pavement (RC‑27M); if existing joint spacing exceeds twenty feet an intermediate joint is required, and longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are only required in concrete patches more than 65 feet long [1]. Accelerated concrete pavement patching may be opened to traffic as early as four to seven hours after placement, whereas standard concrete patches achieve opening strength in twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours [1]. Precast slabs shall not exceed one lane width and shall be six feet minimum up to fifteen feet maximum (twenty feet for prestressed units), with transverse joints aligned to adjacent lanes, a delivered elevation at least one‑quarter inch higher, and a minimum thickness of eight inches; prestressed units may reduce slab thickness by up to four inches provided the final thickness does not fall below eight inches [1]. When full‑depth cement concrete shoulders are used, all materials of the mainline pavement structure from the subgrade to the surface must match, and the appropriate specifications from Publication 408 (Sections 501/506 for dowelled shoulders, Section 658 for non‑dowelled/RCC shoulders) shall be applied [1]. In slip‑form paving the edge slumping limit is one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and one‑quarter inch between a lane and a shoulder; if this cannot be maintained after two hundred feet of paving, work must be halted [2]. Dowel‑bar end displacement may not exceed 0.25 inch, longitudinal translation may not exceed one inch, and horizontal translation may not exceed one inch; each selected tie bar shall be tested for pull‑out resistance after the concrete has attained a compressive strength of 3,500 psi using a center‑pull hydraulic jack capable of testing to 12,000 pounds or to a 1/32‑inch slip, with results evaluated against Table B [2]. Dowel‑retrofit work shall use a power‑driven self‑propelled saw capable of two parallel cuts, three dowel slots (six cuts) per wheel path with the center of each slot cut at mid‑depth of the slab, and jackhammers weighing not more than thirty pounds (a fifteen‑pound hammer shall replace any damaged equipment); slot cleaning must be completed within twenty‑four hours of concrete placement and final cleaning no later than thirty minutes before placement, air‑blasting shall use a compressed‑air stream of at least one hundred psi free of oil, moisture or other contaminants, dowel bars receive bond‑breaker lubricant on Type B coatings at least one hour before concrete placement, are seated in chairs providing a minimum half‑inch clearance, may not exceed a quarter‑inch vertical tilt or horizontal skew, nor more than one inch longitudinal or horizontal translation, and require one‑and‑a‑half‑inch non‑metallic expansion caps with a quarter‑inch clearance from the dowel end [2]. Pay factors for each parameter are derived from the measured PWL values using the piece‑wise equations (100 + 0.4(PWL‑90) for PWL ≥ 90, or 70 + 0.75(PWL‑50) for 50 ≤ PWL < 90), so the OLPF is sensitive to small changes in those inputs through the weighted sum 0.5·PF_D + 0.3·PF_AC + 0.1·PF_200 + 0.1·PF_PCS, rounded per ASTM E29; a contractor may request retesting when the OLPF is below 90.00 or when any individual pay factor falls below 80.00, providing an implicit uncertainty that can be resolved by additional testing. Authority and technical basis

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) the Roadway Construction Standards is referenced in section 2.6 Shoulders to define shoulder types, construction details and standard rumble‑strip designs, which indicates that within that portion it functions as a reference manual providing agency guidance rather than a governing specification or test method. The same manual states in section 4.2 Concrete Pavement Patching that concrete pavement patches must be performed with PCC in accordance with Publication 72M, Roadway Construction Standards, RC‑26M, thereby treating the source as a governing specification for design and construction of those patches. In addition, section 4.5 Slab Stabilization requires slab stabilization shall be done according to Publication 72M, Roadway Construction Standards, RC‑26M and refers to it for the number and pattern of holes to use at a joint or crack, further reflecting its role as an agency‑issued manual that provides required procedures. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 Specifications describes the Roadway Construction Standards as an agency‑issued manual that functions as a governing specification for construction of both rigid and flexible pavements, prescribing mandatory construction actions, equipment approvals, material limits, quality‑control plans and acceptance criteria that must be followed. For example, slip‑form concrete paving in Section 501.3 is required to use a fully energized, self‑propelled slip‑form machine, limit edge slump to not exceed 1/8 in between adjacent lanes or 1/4 in between lane and shoulder, and suspend paving after 200 ft if those limits cannot be maintained, illustrating the mandatory nature of the specification. The mat‑density acceptance procedures in Section 413.3 mandate acceptance according to the levels in Table F, require use of one of the listed gauges or an approved equivalent, and call for submission of a nuclear‑gauge calibration certificate per ASTM D2950 together with operator‑training documentation, further confirming its status as a governing specification. Additional sections such as Section 610.3 pipe underdrain, Section 210.3 subgrade grading, Section 516.3 concrete pavement patching, and many others each contain prescriptive equipment specifications, timing constraints and acceptance thresholds that must be met before work may proceed, reinforcing the characterization of the standard as a governing specification rather than a voluntary guide, test method, research report, manufacturer recommendation or illustrative case study. Practical application and case studies

In concrete pavement rehabilitation projects the presence of active transverse cracks triggers a dowel‑bar retrofit that follows the standard drawings in Publication 72M RC‑26M to restore load transfer across the slab [1]. When longitudinal cracks or joints are identified, cross‑stitching is performed by installing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle as prescribed in Publication 72M RC‑26M, which improves crack control and joint integrity [1]. For any new construction or reconstruction that employs a rigid pavement, the standards require full‑depth Portland cement concrete shoulders to be provided, while flexible pavements are widened two feet into the shoulder area and built with the designated shoulder type [1]. Type 1 (or its variants 1‑F, 1‑S, 1‑SP) shoulders shall be placed on all freeways and arterials, on roadways where serious drainage problems are anticipated, or on two‑lane roads where truck traffic exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150, thereby ensuring smooth surfaces for bicyclists and accommodating expected shoulder use [1]. When curves are sharper than 12°‑30′ or left‑turning vehicles are likely to force through traffic onto the right shoulder, the pavement structure may be extended into the shoulder beginning 250 ft before the intersection and ending 150 ft downstream [1]. If a deteriorated concrete slab exceeds roughly ±500 feet in length, designers follow Pub 72M RC‑21M or RC‑27M to reconstruct the entire width, selecting jointed reinforced concrete pavement with 30‑foot perpendicular joints or jointed plain concrete pavement with 15‑foot joints; the choice is documented in the contract and influences pay items [1]. For full‑depth patches shorter than 500 feet, the patch must be designed per Pub 72M RC‑26M, using dowelled load‑transfer at all transverse joints and specifying high‑early‑strength concrete only when accelerated opening of 4–7 hours is required, otherwise standard concrete achieves traffic strength in 24–48 hours; rapid lane reopening may trigger liquidated damages based on user delay calculations [1]. Precast concrete paving systems are permitted as an alternative to cast‑in‑place or accelerated concrete when work‑zone safety and MPT concerns dominate, provided the project justifies the higher cost [1]. Slab‑stabilization follows Pub 72M RC‑26M by diagnosing loss of support indicated by transverse joint faulting, fines near joints, small depressions, corner breaks or settlement; when voids are confirmed the crew drills holes in the pattern prescribed by Pub 72M and selects a grout—cement mix (≈1 cubic foot per hole), hot asphalt (30‑50 gallons per hole) or high‑density polyurethane (0.4 lb per square foot)—based on site conditions, then pumps the material under the slab, with traffic not reopened until the cement cures (up to 12 hours), the asphalt cools (minimum 30 minutes) or the polyurethane expands and hardens (minimum 30 minutes) [1]. For estimating purposes at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints are stabilized if no preliminary testing has been performed, restoring structural integrity by filling voids, reducing slab deflections and thereby lowering the potential for future pumping, faulting and cracking [1]. In shoulder design for jointed plain concrete pavement, urban Interstates/Expressways are required to use tied concrete shoulders with full‑depth PCC shoulders and dowelled transverse joints as shown in Pub 72M RC‑25M; rural Interstates/Expressways without anticipated work‑zone or incident‑management uses tied shoulders with undowelled JPCP joints, while those that anticipate such uses must employ full‑depth dowelled shoulders; when the two‑way ADT is less than 10,000 designers may consider widened slabs with asphalt or RCC shoulders [1]. Installation of pipe underdrain and prefabricated pavement base drain follows a sequence where fine grading is completed first, the proposed installation method is submitted in writing at least two weeks before work begins, the trench is backfilled with Type A fine aggregate using hydraulically compacted lifts not exceeding 9 inches, and each lift is sprayed with water at a rate of 1.0 gallon per linear foot; compliance is verified by inspection prior to final backfill [2]. For Superpave asphalt mixtures the contractor spreads and strikes off the material for the entire lane width or as much lane width as practical, adjusts screed assemblies to provide the required cross‑section and depth, and monitors pavement texture so that when the difference in average texture depth between non‑segregated and segregated areas exceeds 0.024 inch the pavement is deemed defective, prompting suspension of paving, placement of a test section not to exceed 200 tons after corrective actions, and removal of any lane segment where the average texture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 inch, which must be replaced with a new wearing course extending at least five feet beyond each end [2]. In concrete highway projects exceeding fifteen thousand square yards of mainline paving, daily sieve analyses of plant‑stockpiled aggregate are performed before beginning daily production and when restocking bins, a cumulative percent retained chart is generated and verified to plot within the limits defined by the Combined Gradation Percent Retained Chart according to PTM No. 529; a comprehensive paving‑operation quality‑control plan must be submitted at least two weeks before any placement for Representative review, and concrete may not be placed when the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·h unless mitigation measures are employed, which dictates mix proportion adjustments or trial batch development whenever gradations fall more than five percent outside the limits [2]. Construction of a Safety Edge on Stone Matrix Asphalt courses that are 1.5 inches or deeper requires the contractor to attach an adjustable device to the paver, measure the completed edge angle each day and stop paving if the angle falls outside 26 to 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope extended; acceptance of SMA mixtures follows a lot‑by‑lot system in which a normal lot size is 2,500 tons with five 500‑ton sublots, and payment at 100 % of the contract unit price is made when samples meet the n=1 criteria for asphalt content, sieve passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve and density criteria [2]. Full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs illustrate another application: manufacturers are qualified through Bulletin 15, mix designs are submitted at least twenty‑eight days before production to verify that a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and flexural strength of 650 psi are achievable, shop drawings must show slab dimensions within the tolerances shown in Table A or the slabs are rejected, transverse joint widths are limited to no more than ½ inch with a minimum of ¼ inch along their entire length, longitudinal joints may not exceed ¾ inch, vertical differentials across any joint may not exceed ¼ inch, and traffic is not permitted until dowel and bedding grout have attained a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi [2]. The dowel‑retrofit procedure mandates the use of a power‑driven, self‑propelled saw capable of making two parallel cuts per dowel bar slot, removal of concrete between cuts with a jackhammer weighing not more than 30 pounds (reduced to 15 pounds if pavement damage occurs), cleaning of all exposed slot surfaces within twenty‑four hours and final cleaning within thirty minutes before concrete placement using air blasting at a pressure of at least 100 psi, and placement tolerances that limit vertical tilt or horizontal skew to a maximum displacement of 0.25 inch for each end of the dowel relative to its midpoint; any defective work must be removed and replaced at no additional cost [2]. When subbase quantities exceed 1,500 tons the contractor prepares and submits a Subbase Delivery Plan describing equipment and placement methods before construction can begin; if density testing shows the subbase depth is deficient by ½ inch or more the subbase is classified as defective, requiring scarifying of the top three inches, blending in additional material, recompacting, and retesting until the depth falls within the ½‑inch tolerance; traffic, including construction traffic, is prohibited on a subbase constructed of No. OGS aggregate, and if a subbase of No. 2A aggregate remains exposed for more than 60 calendar days the Representative requires a re‑test for surface irregularities and depth before pavement placement [2]. The next‑generation concrete surface specification calls for construction of a 300‑foot test section in a single lane to demonstrate that the prescribed diamond‑grinding and grooving equipment produce an acceptable surface, and work on the full pavement does not proceed until this test section receives written approval, although the District Executive may waive the requirement by granting written approval [2]. In crushed‑aggregate base course projects described in Section 312, the contractor first obtains acceptance of the processing and mixing plant before work begins, measures coarse and fine aggregates separately, adds calcium chloride at 7 lb per ton for Type 1 or 5.7 lb per ton for Type 2 prior to water, spreads the mixture in layers no thinner than 3 inches and no thicker than 6 inches, and compacts each layer to not less than 100 % of maximum density as determined by PTM No. 106 Method B; any surface irregularity exceeding ½‑inch is immediately corrected by blading, reshaping or replacement, and frequent moisture control tests are performed at delivery while adjustable self‑propelled spreaders and string lines maintain elevation tolerances, with a depth deficiency of ½‑inch or more triggering corrective work before the surface course is placed [2]. The crushed‑aggregate base course example in Section 310 adds that adjustable self‑propelled mechanical spreaders are used to place and screed material without segregation, templates cut to the final cross‑section are employed at least every 25 feet, a 12‑foot straightedge is moved along the base in 5‑foot increments to detect longitudinal irregularities, and when the compacted depth exceeds 8 inches (or 10 inches for Method 2) the base is constructed in two or more layers of roughly equal depth with no layer less than 4 inches nor more than 8 inches (or between 5 and 10 inches under Method 2); after compaction a test hole is cut for each 3,000 square yards to verify overall depth and any area deficient by ½‑inch or more must be replaced [2]. Long‑life concrete pavement projects employ the slip‑form method as the prescribed construction technique, with plant‑mixed concrete required except where the Representative approves truck‑mixed concrete for shoulders, closure pours, or projects under fifteen thousand square yards of mainline paving per year; daily aggregate gradation is monitored by sieve analysis and plotted against PTM No. 529, adjustments are permitted when the combined gradation stays within five percent of required limits while deviations beyond those limits must be reported in writing, a concrete temperature control plan is activated for placement temperatures exceeding 80°F, the first truck of each lot is tested for plastic‑air content and must fall between 7.0 % plus or minus 1.5 %, and after any day’s placement exceeding 700 square yards the LTU vertical and horizontal alignment is verified within 18 hours by a nondestructive method acceptable to the Representative, with a QC Supervisor continuously on‑site or reachable within one hour [2]. Evaluation of asphalt pavement longitudinal joint density defines longitudinal‑joint lots of 12,500 ft consisting of five sublots of 2,500 ft; five random 6‑inch cores are collected on the first day for density testing according to PTM 715 or 716 and results submitted within 24 hours so the Representative can adjust mixture design or placement if needed, and after each sublot a core is taken for the incentive/disincentive program; the Representative computes the percent‑within‑limits using a lower specification limit of 91.0 % to decide any payment addition or deduction [2]. In polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt (micro‑surfacing) projects the contractor must construct a test strip on the first day that is opened to traffic within one hour and achieve a set time of ten minutes; if the average texture depth exceeds the limit, the Representative gives a warning with an opportunity to correct, while a set time longer than one hour and twenty minutes results in immediate stoppage of placement; aggregate sampling performed at least two weeks before placement that yields a percent‑within‑limits less than 90 leads the Representative to reject the stockpile and require re‑blending or replacement, and when application rates fall outside the ranges in Table C the work is deemed defective, requiring either an additional minimum of 15 lb/yd² or payment of only fifty percent of the contract unit price for the defective lot [2]. The Superpave asphalt base‑course example states that placement must be halted when the prepared surface is wet or when air or surface temperature falls to 35°F or lower; for a Superpave 25‑mm mix with a compacted depth greater than six inches the material is placed in two or more layers each no thinner than three inches and no thicker than six inches (similarly a 37.5‑mm mix deeper than eight inches is placed in layers between four and eight inches); after construction full‑depth cores are drilled at one random location per 3,000 sq yd under inspector supervision, any irregularity exceeding one‑quarter inch or depth shortfall of half an inch renders the pavement defective and triggers immediate corrective work [2]. Pavement relief joint construction requires that after constructing the subbase, sleeper slab and polyethylene sheeting as shown on standard drawings, base‑course joint faces are painted before laying the asphalt base course, and a saw‑cut is made directly above the asphalt–concrete interface within ±¼ inch only after the wearing course has cooled below 140°F and completed within seven days; the cut forms a reservoir one inch deep by half an inch wide, which is sealed under air temperatures between 40°F and 90°F using material kept at the manufacturer’s recommended pouring temperature and not held longer than six hours per batch, with a tape bond breaker placed no more than⅛ inch up the face and the reservoir filled to ⅛–¼ inch below the pavement surface [2]. Slab‑jacking projects begin with the contractor providing existing roadway profiles and proposed grade lines for the contract area before work acceptance; during cement or high‑density polyurethane grout stabilization the slab is raised within 0.02 foot (or jointed slabs within 0.03 foot) of the string line grade using continuous pressures up to 200 psi and short‑term pressures up to 300 psi (briefly up to 600 psi when bonded), and any overjacking greater than 0.10 foot requires grinding or full pavement removal at no additional cost [2]. Concrete pavement cross‑stitching work must be preceded by a quality‑control plan submitted at least fourteen days before work begins, demonstration on an existing longitudinal crack or joint and Representative approval, ambient air temperature or pavement surface temperature not below 40°F, drill holes no more than 3/8 inch larger in diameter than the tie bar, maintenance of the prescribed angle, cleaning with compressed air at a stream measuring at least 100 psi free of oil or moisture, rotation of the tie bar several times after epoxy injection, troweling flush, sealing the routed joint only when dry and clean and filling to within one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the surface, and traffic may not be allowed until the anchoring material has fully cured per the manufacturer’s recommendations; any slab in which a hole penetrates the bottom must be removed and patched at no additional cost [2]. Asphalt‑treated permeable base course projects require that placement be avoided on surfaces that are unstable, frozen, or when air temperature is below 35°F, and also avoid placement during rain; the mix must be produced at a temperature below 320°F, placed within eight hours of production, and not stockpiled; compaction is performed with an 8‑ton to 10‑ton steel‑wheel roller in exactly four passes using only static vibratory operation to preserve drainage and prevent aggregate crushing, longitudinal joints are overlapped by one to two inches and transverse joints require a full‑depth vertical bulkhead or saw cut after any interruption longer than thirty minutes, while depth verification is performed by drilling a 6‑inch test hole for each three thousand square yards of completed base [2]. Bonded concrete overlay projects limit placement to ambient concrete temperatures between 65°F and 90°F; the existing slab is scarified and shot‑blasted before applying a fresh overlay, bond strength is verified at seven days using ASTM C1583 with an average of three tests required to be at least 175 psi and each individual test not less than 160 psi, any area failing those limits is deemed defective and must be retested, curing begins within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates, insulating blankets are used when forecast air temperatures are 45°F or lower and are removed only after concrete strength exceeds 3,600 psi while keeping temperature change under forty °F per hour, and the pavement is released to traffic only after compressive‑strength cylinders show at least 3,600 psi and the bond‑strength criteria of Section 545.3(e) are satisfied [2]. Stone Matrix Asphalt projects require a preplacement meeting and QC plan two weeks before placing SMA mixtures, with weather limits that prohibit placement when air or surface temperature is 50°F or lower and during specified October‑March windows in certain districts unless a written exception is granted; the contractor must place a minimum of one hundred tons at an approved demo location, obtain and test three loose plant samples for asphalt content, gradation and draindown and three pavement cores for density, and if any result falls outside specification tolerances another demonstration is required; successful completion authorizes full production, whereas failure triggers repeat trials or material rejection with no payment for the demo strip [2]. The 6.3 mm thin‑asphalt overlay project mandates that a paving operation QC plan be submitted on Form CS‑413 before Representative review and a preplacement meeting be scheduled at least two weeks before placing asphalt mixtures to review specifications, sequence of paving operations, mixture acceptance, density acceptance and the care and custody of asphalt samples; placement is prohibited from October 1 to March 31 in listed districts unless a written exception is granted, and even then the minimum surface temperature of 50°F and an air temperature of 40°F are strictly enforced; lot acceptance requires LTS testing of asphalt content and percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve according to Table B, and if two consecutive lots or three total fail these tolerances production must stop until a corrective solution is approved, with any defective material required to be removed and replaced [2]. Measurement and payment provisions demonstrate how performance metrics affect outcomes: for each asphalt lot the Department measures in‑place density, asphalt content and sieve gradations to obtain percent‑within‑limits (PWL); when all PWL values are at least fifty the pay factor is derived from equations that increase the factor when PWL is greater than or equal to ninety or apply a lower slope when fifty ≤ PWL < 90, and the overall lot pay factor is calculated as a weighted sum of the individual factors; if any PWL falls below fifty the lot is declared defective, leading either to a leave‑in‑place decision with an OLPF of seventy or to full removal and replacement at no additional cost, while lots meeting higher thresholds may qualify for incentives or avoid disincentives [2].

Transfer of the documented results from Pub 72M may be limited when the traffic environment of a new project differs from that assumed in the standards [1]. For two‑lane roadways where truck traffic exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150, the standards are considered inappropriate because they assume lower truck volumes [1]. Rural routes with a two‑way average daily traffic less than 10,000 may be allowed to use widened slabs with asphalt or RCC shoulders and tied shoulders without dowels, whereas urban interstates and expressways are expected to employ full‑depth PCC shoulders with dowelled transverse joints; high‑volume work‑zone traffic would therefore preclude the latter approach [1]. Section 522 of the Pennsylvania specifications requires that all traffic be prohibited after a concrete slab has been fractured and seated until an asphalt surface is placed, limiting applicability where continuous traffic cannot be halted [2]. The construction drawings for plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements prescribe one‑ or two‑lane widths as shown on the Standard Drawings, and a one‑lane method must be used when two‑lane work would interfere with traffic flow [2]. Traffic may not be allowed on a full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slab until the grout has reached a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi, and slabs that lack effective load transfer at transverse joints or are placed without undersealing must not be exposed to traffic for more than three days [2]. Climate conditions also restrict direct transfer. Section 522 specifies that falling‑weight‑deflectometer testing shall not be performed when the pavement surface is frozen or when air temperature is below 45 °F or exceeds 90 °F, and rollers for seating operations may not be operated during wet conditions [2]. Concrete placement in Section 501 is prohibited when the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·h unless mitigation measures are employed; concreting must be discontinued when the descending air temperature away from artificial heat falls to 40 °F (or 45 °F for accelerated‑strength concrete), and if ambient temperature near the concrete rises to 85 °F, plastic concrete temperatures must be monitored at half‑hour intervals and operations stopped if the temperature exceeds 90 °F after mixing [2]. The mix design for full‑depth precast or prestressed slabs (Section 529) must account for ambient average daily temperatures and exposure conditions at the project site to determine appropriate panel size and expansion‑joint locations, tying applicability to local climate [2]. Pervious asphalt mixtures may not be placed when air or surface temperature is lower than 50 °F (Section 420), and Section 312 requires that crushed‑aggregate base courses not be placed on a frozen surface, when air temperature is below 40 °F, or when freezing temperatures are forecast within the next 24 hours [2]. Placement of Superpave asphalt base courses (Sections 313 and 314) is prohibited on wet prepared surfaces or when air or surface temperature is 35 °F or lower, and the compacted depth must be placed in a single layer between 3 inches and 6 inches; any irregularities greater than ¼ inch or depth deficiencies of ½ inch or more that cannot be satisfactorily corrected render the pavement defective [2]. Asphalt treated permeable base courses (Section 360) may not be placed on unstable, frozen surfaces, when air temperature is below 35 °F, or during rain, and the mix must be produced at a temperature below 320 °F and placed within eight hours of manufacture [2]. Sealing material for pavement relief joints (Section 504) may only be placed when air temperature is between 40 °F and 90 °F, saw‑cutting cannot begin until the asphalt wearing course has cooled below 140 °F, and a single batch of sealing material must not remain at the recommended pouring temperature for more than six hours [2]. Concrete overlays (Section 545) may be placed only when concrete temperature is between 65 °F and 90 °F and surface temperature is kept below 120 °F; if forecast air temperature is 45 °F or less insulating blankets must be used, and the rate of temperature change at the concrete surface may not exceed 40 °F per hour [2]. Stone matrix asphalt mixtures (Section 419) are restricted to periods outside October 1 through March 31 in many districts and may not be placed when surfaces are wet or when air or surface temperature is at or below 50 °F, linking applicability to seasonal climate conditions [2]. Subgrade characteristics further limit transferability. Section 529 mandates that any soft, wet, or unsuitable subbase or subgrade material be removed and replaced with compacted No. 2A coarse aggregate, and the finished surface must be shaped within a tolerance of ±½ inch of grade and contour [2]. For pervious asphalt systems (Section 420) the subgrade must be excavated to undisturbed soil without compaction so that it remains as permeable as possible, and haul trucks and other heavy equipment should avoid driving through the installation area [2]. Section 350 requires retesting of a No. 2A aggregate subbase if it is used as a haul road or exposed for more than 60 calendar days, prohibits traffic on a subbase constructed of No. OGS aggregate, and mandates proof rolling before placement of flexible or rigid pavement when the subbase remains over winter without a protective asphalt or cement‑concrete layer [2]. Prior to placing a crushed‑aggregate base course (Section 312) the subbase or subgrade must be completed and maintained at least 1,500 feet ahead of the leading spreader, kept free of ruts, irregularities, and loose material, and only necessary local traffic and essential construction equipment may use the completed base until directed otherwise [2]. Drainage conditions also affect applicability. The Roadway Construction Standards call for Type 1 or similar shoulders where serious drainage problems are anticipated; more severe drainage conditions than those assumed therefore limit direct transfer [1]. Section 522 requires that base drains be provided and damaged drainage facilities repaired before concrete slab fracturing, restricting use of the documented results in locations with inadequate drainage [2]. Workmanship requirements impose additional constraints. Section 522 permits the use of sonic vibratory equipment only when fracturing will not cause soil liquefaction of the subgrade, and rollers for seating operations may not be operated during wet conditions [2]. Concrete placement (Section 501) is prohibited when evaporation rates exceed 0.20 lb/ft²·h unless mitigation measures are employed, and temperature monitoring must be performed as specified to ensure quality [2]. Dowel slot locations in precast or prestressed slabs must be within ±⅛ inch and slab dimensions must conform to Table A limits (Section 529), while sealing material for pavement relief joints must be placed at temperatures between 40 °F and 90 °F, with each cut required to fall within ±¼ inch [2]. Asphalt placement procedures in Sections 313, 314, 360, 419, and 412 all prescribe specific compaction depths, equipment types (e.g., steel‑wheel rollers of at least 8–10 tons or three rollers each weighing a minimum of 10 tons), number of passes, and temperature windows, linking successful transfer to adherence to these workmanship standards [2]. Maintenance practices further restrict the reuse of results. After slab fracturing and seating, traffic must be prohibited until an asphalt surface is placed (Section 522) and concrete overlays may be opened to traffic only after the concrete reaches a compressive strength of 3,600 psi and meets a minimum bond strength of 175 psi (average of three tests, each not less than 160 psi) [2].

Limitations and cautions

When Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) are applied, the guidance in the General provisions is deliberately generalized, so project‑specific conditions such as existing pavement stability, drainage adequacy and traffic‑control options introduce uncertainty and require professional judgment; the standard also simplifies design by using uniform thickness assumptions and minimum surface‑course thicknesses that are only valid if the overlay base is stable, properly leveled, sealed and patched [1]. The need to coordinate detours with the District Traffic Unit when feasible, retain at least 15 % of the estimated total milled material (or provide all material when the estimate is less than 200 tons), and achieve a target field density range of roughly 92.0 % to 98.5 % for Percent Within Limits contracts are explicit boundary conditions that, if ignored, can lead to misuse such as applying RPS criteria without confirming pavement stability or pursuing PWL density targets despite base variability [1]. For shoulder design the standard sets a boundary condition that Type 1‑type shoulders on two‑lane roads are required only when truck traffic exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150, that the 2‑foot pavement widening does not apply to inside shoulders on Interstate and other limited‑access freeways, and that special shoulder extensions are permitted only where curve radii are sharper than 12°‑30′ or within distances starting 250 ft before an intersection and stopping 150 ft downstream; the discretionary nature of smooth‑surface bicycle shoulders means misuse can occur if the discretion or anticipated drainage problem criteria are ignored [1]. In concrete pavement patching, uncertainties arise from length and joint‑spacing limits: full‑depth patches longer than about ±500 ft trigger a design analysis for slab thickness and when intermittent patches leave 10 ft or less of original pavement between them the entire section should be removed and reconstructed; simplifications include assuming JRCP joints may be spaced at 30 ft and JPCP joints at 15 ft, while tie bars are required only for patches more than 65 ft long. Potential misuse includes relying on reinforcement in JRCP despite guidance that it does not add strength, using high‑early‑strength concrete because of its shrinkage‑cracking potential, or substituting a flexible base material for full‑depth concrete patches [1]. Slab stabilization is bounded by the fact that it only restores support by filling voids and does not correct surface depressions, increase structural capacity or eliminate faulting; because void locations are uncertain, the guidance simplifies field implementation by recommending that at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints be stabilized when no preliminary testing has been performed, with additional requirements for joint sealing, functional subsurface drains and traffic‑control compliance before reopening pavement, and traffic may not be opened until cement grout cures (up to 12 hours), asphalt cools (minimum 30 minutes) or polyurethane expands and hardens (minimum 30 minutes). Misuse can result if stabilization is treated as a cure for faulting or if traffic is reopened prematurely [1]. For continuously reinforced concrete pavement the standard no longer contains construction details, limiting designers to the 1993 AASHTO Guide using DARWin and requiring special contract provisions; CRCP may be used only in special cases with approval of the Director, BODD, so misuse occurs if CRCP is employed without those approvals or contractual provisions [1]. Jointed plain concrete pavement shoulder design imposes boundary conditions that urban Interstates/Expressways must use full‑depth PCC shoulders with dowelled transverse joints, while rural Interstates/Expressways may use tied shoulders with undowelled joints unless a high probability of incident‑management traffic exists; when the two‑way ADT is less than 10 000 widened slabs with asphalt or RCC shoulders may be considered, and full‑depth concrete shoulders require that all materials from subgrade to surface match those of the mainline pavement. Misuse includes applying the wrong shoulder type for the corridor classification, ignoring the ADT threshold, failing to match material properties, or not following Publication 408 specifications for dowelled or non‑dowelled shoulders [1].

The Specification (Publication 408/2026) adds further uncertainties and simplifications. Mat‑density acceptance may be simplified to non‑movement or optimum‑rolling pattern methods only for scratch or leveling courses less than 1‑inch deep or equal to or less than 110 pounds per square yard, for paving areas less than 4 feet in width, and for material placements not exceeding 500 tons in a continuous run; reliance on the Inspector‑in‑Charge’s judgment and a certified field technician introduces uncertainty, while use of uncalibrated or damaged nuclear gauges, taking cores later than one day after placement, or compromising sample security may trigger a stop‑paving order [2]. Section 610.3 creates boundary conditions that trenches greater than 2 inches in depth shall not be left open overnight, fittings must have deflection angles of 45 degrees or less, damaged drainage material will not be accepted and homemade connections are prohibited, so misuse can arise if these requirements are ignored [2]. The spreading and finishing provisions set temperature limits, require mixture integrity without cold lumps or segregation, limit work to daylight hours, and for courses deeper than 6 inches mandate construction in two or more layers of approximately equal depth with no layer less than 3 inches or more than 6 inches; the Safety Edge must be at least 1.5 inches deep but not placed at total depths greater than 5 inches, and normal lot size is 2,500 tons split into five 500‑ton sublots with adjustments after work stoppages of five calendar days or more, while macrotexture differences exceeding 0.024 inch are unacceptable and an average texture depth less than or equal to 0.006 inch renders the pavement flushed and defective [2]. Concrete pavement patching (Section 516.3) requires a contractor‑prepared quality‑control plan, prohibits excavated patch areas from remaining un‑patched for more than 48 hours or over weekends/holidays, mandates excavation of an outlet through the shoulder at the lowest point when rain occurs on an open patch, limits saw‑cut width to a half‑inch, forbids cooling water, slurry and dust from entering traffic lanes, bans drop hammers and hydro‑hammers, accepts dowel bar anchoring only when voids around the dowel are less than 0.50 inch on any core, and permits opening to traffic only after a compressive strength of 1,000 psi is demonstrated by cylinder testing; deviation from these steps introduces uncertainty and potential misuse [2]. Subgrade construction (Section 210.3) allows the longitudinal interval for cross‑section checks to be increased to not more than 500 feet when GPS or laser equipment satisfies performance requirements, otherwise the default interval is 25 feet (or 50 feet with automatic grading machines); any surface irregularity exceeding 1/2 inch must be corrected and if more than 10 percent of acceptance tests exceed this limit the Representative may require stakes and stringline as specified, while Class 4, Type A geotextile must be placed continuously with transverse limits extending to within a minimum of 6 inches and a maximum of 12 inches from the interior edge of the pavement base drain, creating clear boundaries for material handling and potential misuse if ignored [2]. Slab fracturing (Section 522.3) prohibits equipment that will cause extensive surface spalling, punch holes or shatter the pavement, permits sonic vibratory tools only when subgrade liquefaction will not occur, requires clearances of at least 10 feet horizontally and vertically from culverts, cross‑pipes and utility sleeves, 40 feet from structures, bridge decks or tunnels, and 3 feet from fixed objects; falling weight deflectometer testing shall not be performed on frozen pavement or when air temperature falls below 45°F or exceeds 90°F, seating rollers may not operate under wet conditions or over bridges without bridge‑engineer approval, and a test section must be established to verify fracturing pattern and modulus before production with cessation of work if results are unsatisfactory [2]. Continuously reinforced concrete pavement patching (Section 518.3) limits saw‑cuts to at least 24 inches from an existing crack, restricts wheel penetration into the subbase to a maximum of 1/2 inch, caps jackhammer weight at a maximum of 30 pounds, and requires a new full‑depth transverse saw‑cut beyond the affected area when break back exceeds 2 inches or reinforcement bars are damaged; if subbase disturbance exceeds 1 inch the disturbed material must be removed and replaced [2]. Concrete placement (Section 501.3) may not occur when the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 pounds per square foot per hour unless mitigation measures are employed, concreting shall be discontinued when ambient temperature drops to 40°F (45°F for accelerated‑strength concrete) unless a written exception is granted, and form construction must not exceed 1/8 inch in ten feet from the true plane while resisting a 1,700‑pound center load with deflection no greater than 0.01 inch; failure to meet these thresholds may result in misuse [2]. Stone matrix asphalt (Section 419.3) requires safety‑edge geometry measured at an angle of 26 to 40 degrees from the pavement cross‑slope each day after final compaction, a normal lot size of 2,500 tons divided into five 500‑ton sublots with redefinition after stoppages of five calendar days or more, asphalt content and percent passing No. 200 sieve must meet single‑sample criteria, density results outside individual mat limits are subject to extreme‑value analysis at the 5 percent significance level, and Gmm verification samples must be within ±0.030 of department values otherwise department values are used; deviation can lead to misuse [2]. Full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slabs (Section 529.3) base acceptance on trial‑batch test data demonstrating minimum compressive strength, impose dimensional limits of maximum slab length 15 feet (20 feet if prestressed), exclude a sacrificial thickness of 1/4 inch from design calculations, restrict joint widths to ¼–½ inch transversely and ≤¾ inch longitudinally, limit vertical differential across joints to ¼ inch, require grout to achieve at least 2,500 psi compressive strength before traffic is permitted and ungrouted slabs may not remain exposed to traffic longer than three days; survey or fabrication tolerances of ±1⁄8 inch for dowel placement can cause misuse [2]. Dowel retrofit (Section 527.3) limits jackhammer weight to not more than 30 pounds, mandates a power‑driven self‑propelled saw capable of making two parallel cuts per dowel bar slot, requires slot cleaning within 24 hours before concrete placement and final cleaning within 30 minutes before concrete placement, and allows only a maximum displacement of 0.25 inch at each end for vertical tilt or horizontal skew and no more than 1 inch longitudinal or horizontal translation; exceeding these limits constitutes misuse [2]. Pervious asphalt pavement (Section 420.3) requires verification sections of at least 225 square feet whose average infiltration must exceed 200 in/hr, compacted thickness may not be more than ¼ inch less than design thickness, placement is prohibited when air or surface temperature is lower than 50°F, compaction must be performed with smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode for one to four passes (pneumatic‑tire rollers may not be used nor exceed four passes), storing equipment, materials or soils on the pervious beds during construction is prohibited, subbase quantities exceeding 1 500 tons require a Subbase Delivery Plan, material may not be placed on soft, muddy or frozen areas, moisture must be sufficient to prevent segregation, layers may not exceed 8 inches in compacted depth (or 4 inches when granulated slag is used unless a 6‑inch exception applies), and abrupt turns with tracked equipment that could pull or damage the geotextile are prohibited; violation of any of these conditions creates potential misuse [2]. Asphalt‑treated permeable base course (Section 360.3) prohibits placement on unstable or frozen surfaces, when air temperature is below 35°F, or during rain; mix must be produced at a temperature below 320°F and placed within eight hours of manufacture; steel‑wheel power rollers certified at eight to ten tons are required, lifts may not exceed four inches, the mat must be cooled to 100°F before subsequent layers, no more than four roller passes are allowed unless surface irregularities demand additional passes, and stockpiling, grinding or over‑compaction that would eliminate drainage is prohibited; quality control is simplified to one six‑inch diameter test hole per three thousand square yards of completed base and corrective action is required for depth deficiencies of a half inch or greater [2]. Bonded concrete overlay (Section 545.3) mandates concrete temperature between 65°F and 90°F, surface temperature less than 120°F before placement, final cleaning no more than 30 minutes prior to overlay placement, compressed‑air streams free of oil, moisture and other contaminants with a minimum pressure of 100 psi at the source, removal of standing water before concrete is placed, bond‑strength testing averaging at least 175 psi with each individual test not less than 160 psi, compressive strength of 3 600 psi before traffic opening, curing insulation removal only when air temperature exceeds 45°F or concrete strength exceeds 3 600 psi while surface‑temperature change does not exceed 40°F per hour, and dimensional tolerances including a maximum edge slump of ¼ inch next to shoulders (or ⅛ inch next to an adjacent lane) and panel‑length limits for intermediate joints; failure to meet these limits can result in defective work that must be removed and replaced [2]. Concrete pavement ride quality (Section 507.3) requires corrective work before any pavement‑depth testing, limits allowable surface deviation to a ridge or valley of no more than 1/8 inch measured on the transverse profile and any individual bump greater than ¼ inch must be ground, demands smoothness verification covering at least 10 percent of lane miles and an acceptance MRI not exceeded by more than 6.1 inches/mile on at least 90 percent of the 528‑foot lots; otherwise incentive payments may be forfeited and the Department’s smoothness results used [2]. Thin asphalt overlay (Section 412.3) prohibits paving from October 1 to March 31 and when surface temperature falls to 50°F or lower unless written District Executive approval is obtained, requires air temperature of at least 40°F, permits only steel‑wheeled rollers each weighing a minimum of 10 tons (pneumatic rollers are forbidden), allows vibratory rolling only after the Representative is satisfied that no aggregate breakage or binder flushing occurs, bases acceptance of mixture lots on single‑sample tolerances for asphalt content and the 75 µm sieve as defined in Table B, requires density verification with a calibrated nuclear gauge operated by a licensed operator using the control‑strip technique, and triggers a stop‑production order when these criteria are not met for two consecutive lots; any deviation from prescribed sampling locations or testing methods must be documented at the preconstruction meeting otherwise the work may be deemed defective [2]. Longitudinal joint density (Section 405.3) fixes lot boundaries at 12 500 feet composed of five 2 500‑foot sublots, excludes joints within one foot of an obstruction, requires a single core per sublot plus five random 6‑inch cores for the first‑day quality‑control strip, uses a lower specification limit of 91 percent with no upper limit for percent‑within‑limits calculations, and failure to backfill and seal cores as prescribed can be a source of misuse; if mixture set time exceeds one hour a warning is issued and placement stops when set time reaches one hour and twenty minutes [2]. Polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing (Section 483.3) mandates that the mixture set within one hour, issues a warning if it exceeds that time and stops placement when set time reaches one hour and twenty minutes, allows water to be sprayed into the spreader box without harming the mix but requires avoidance of excess crowning of rutted areas, defines aggregate sampling at a boundary of 500 tons with quantities below this treated as a single lot divided into three equal sublots and larger quantities split into lots not exceeding 1 000 tons each also divided into three sublots, rejects any lot where percent‑within‑limits falls below 90, requires compaction before opening to traffic with a pneumatic‑tire roller at tire pressures of 40 psi to 60 psi, mandates a test strip length between 100 feet and 550 feet evaluated for ten minutes, and obliges Representative approval before any production work proceeds; non‑compliance constitutes misuse [2]. Asphalt‑treated rich base course (Section 314.3) prohibits placement on wet surfaces or when air or prepared‑surface temperature is 35°F or lower, limits compacted layer thickness to between 3 inches and 6 inches, declares the pavement defective if surface irregularities exceed ¼ inch, and requires corrective action for any depth deficiency of ½ inch or more that cannot be satisfactorily corrected [2]. Base course (Section 313.3) similarly forbids placement on wet prepared surfaces or when air or surface temperature is 35°F or lower, permits the Representative to allow limited quantities if work is halted because of weather, requires two‑or‑more layers of approximately equal compacted depth with no layer less than 3 inches nor more than 6 inches for Superpave 25.0 mm asphalt base courses whose indicated compacted depth exceeds 6 inches, deems the pavement defective if surface irregularities exceed ¼ inch and any random full‑depth core showing a deficiency of ½ inch or more that cannot be satisfactorily corrected as defective [2]. Pavement relief joints (Section 504.3) limit saw‑cut placement to within ±¼ inch of the asphalt interface, permit cutting only after the wearing course has cooled below 140°F and no later than seven days after joint placement, restrict sealing material temperature to between 40°F and 90°F unless Representative permission is obtained, require heating and pouring temperatures follow manufacturer ranges and batch temperature be maintained for no more than six hours, limit bond‑breaker tape extension to not more than ⅛ inch up the face, and mandate seal fill level ⅛ to ¼ inch below the pavement surface without spilling [2]. Slab‑jacking (Section 681.3) caps continuous grout pressures at 200 psi, short‑term pressures at 300 psi unless the pavement is bonded in which case brief spikes to 600 psi are allowed, confines acceptable elevation outcomes within ±0.02 foot of the string line grade for continuously reinforced concrete and within ±0.03 foot for jointed slabs, and requires removal and replacement when over‑jacking exceeds 0.10 foot [2]. Unbonded concrete overlay (Section 548.3) limits geotextile overlap to eight inches ± 2 inches, restricts the number of geotextile layers to three, mandates a free edge extending at least four inches beyond the pavement, caps fabric surface temperature at 120°F before overlay placement, requires installation of geotextile when joint faulting exceeds ¼‑inch and an asphalt scratch course or milling when faulting exceeds ½‑inch, and obliges patching of any surface defect larger than four square inches and one inch deep prior to interlayer placement; failure to meet these conditions can lead to misuse [2]. Concrete pavement cross‑stitching (Section 528.3) prohibits work if ambient air temperature or pavement surface temperature falls below 40°F, requires a full‑depth saw cut along the entire longitudinal joint before drilling, limits drill hole diameter to no more than 3/8 inch larger than the tie bar and forbids penetration through the slab bottom, and demands compressed‑air cleaning at a minimum of 100 psi free of oil, moisture and contaminants; non‑compliance constitutes misuse [2]. All of these provisions together define the uncertainties, simplifications, boundary conditions and potential sources of misuse that must be managed when employing Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M).

The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) states that additional engineering judgment, analysis, testing, or reference is required whenever a project’s specific conditions or problems are not fully addressed by the generalized guidance, because each project presents unique circumstances that must be thoroughly evaluated and to which engineering judgment must be applied [1]. In the context of concrete pavement patching, the manual requires a pavement design analysis when a full‑width reconstruction exceeds roughly five hundred feet of Portland cement concrete pavement in order to determine whether a thicker slab depth is needed [1]. If flexible base material is proposed as a substitute for jointed plain concrete in a full‑depth patch, a waiver with detailed justification must be submitted and the testing provisions of Publication 408 Section 316 must be satisfied, which goes beyond the standard RC‑26M guidance [1]. When accelerated concrete patching is used on high‑ADT highways, liquidated‑damage calculations referencing Publications 448, 10C and the RULD Calculator are required to establish user‑delay penalties [1]. For projects that propose continuously reinforced concrete pavement (CRCP), the Roadway Construction Standards no longer provide construction details; therefore additional engineering judgment, analysis, testing, or reference to other documents is required, the structural design must follow the 1993 AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures using DARWin, and CRCP can only be used with approval from the Director, BODD [1].

The Publication 408/2026 Specifications require additional engineering judgment whenever the acceptance criteria set out in Pub 72M cannot be satisfied. Under Section 413.3(j) for mat‑density acceptance, if the target density is not achieved a new optimum‑rolling pattern must be established and paving may be suspended; if cores are not taken within one day after placement or if the density for two consecutive lots or for a total of three lots fails to meet the density payment factor percentage of ≥ 100, paving operations must be stopped as directed [2]. The LTS will analyze bulk‑density results for extreme values according to PTM No. 4 at the 5 % significance level and may discard them, invoking further review if fewer than three test results remain; compromised sample containers require replacement cores, an investigation, and a written explanation before any expedited delivery can continue [2]. Section 413.3(h) for spreading and finishing mandates that when asphalt content or the percent passing the No. 200 sieve is not within the single‑sample or multiple‑sample tolerances in Table A for two consecutive lots or a total of three lots, production must be stopped, the cause determined, and a corrective solution proposed before the Representative authorizes resumption [2]. Likewise, if the measured difference in average macrotexture depth between segregated and non‑segregated areas exceeds 0.024 inch, work must be suspended, the underlying cause evaluated according to the QC plan, corrective actions submitted, and paving may only continue after Representative approval and successful test sections not exceeding 200 tons; if flushing is indicated by an average texture depth less than or equal to 0.006 inch, the same actions are required [2].

Section 210.3 requires that when GPS or laser grading equipment is employed a machine‑control grading QC plan be submitted at least one week before the preconstruction conference to verify that the control system meets required tolerances; additionally, if more than ten percent of all acceptance tests exceed a half‑inch surface irregularity in any project phase, the Representative may require extra stakes and stringlines as specified [2].

Section 522.3 for slab fracturing requires submission of a pavement‑fracturing plan and equipment list for approval before construction begins; if satisfactory fracture results are not being obtained, operations must cease and an alternate action plan be submitted for Representative review; a minimum one‑thousand‑foot test section must be established to determine the optimal fracturing pattern, and the section is deemed satisfactory only if the average elastic modulus of the fractured concrete layer falls within the range specified in Table A; if two consecutive lots fail to meet Table A criteria a new test section must be established to determine the optimum pattern, invoking further engineering assessment [2].

Section 501.3 states that adjustments to aggregate proportions that also require changes to cementitious material or water content trigger development of new concrete mix designs and trial batches; if the combined‑gradation percent retained falls outside the limits defined by PTM No. 529, the District Materials Manager may permit a deviation of less than 5 percent but any larger discrepancy must be evaluated and approved, indicating further judgment beyond the standard specifications; use of mixtures from volumetric mixing plants or mobile mixers, as well as non‑steel forms, requires written approval from the District Executive before proceeding [2].

Section 419.3 provides that when mixture‑acceptance, density, asphalt‑content or sieve‑passing test results fall outside the single‑sample or multiple‑sample tolerances and the deviation persists for two consecutive lots or a total of three lots, the Representative must stop paving, investigate the cause and propose a corrective solution before work may resume; if the difference between Independent Assurance and HOLA test averages exceeds the maximum IA tolerances in Table G, an investigation is required; the completed angle of the Safety Edge must be 26 to 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope extended, and failure to meet this range requires stopping paving and providing corrective action [2].

Section 529.3 mandates that concrete mix designs include trial batch test data demonstrating achievement of the minimum required compressive and flexural strengths; the Representative may request flexural strength test results at any time, and if those values are not met the Representative may require the contractor to direct the manufacturer to retrial the concrete mix design; fabricated slab dimensions falling outside the tolerances shown in Table A trigger rejection; recurring damage or distress observed during installation prompts a pause until the cause is determined; grout that has not reached the specified minimum compressive strength before opening to traffic requires further analysis, testing, or reference [2].

Section 526.3 requires additional engineering judgment, analysis, or testing whenever rubblizing does not achieve the specified results; in such cases work must be stopped and an alternate plan with a new equipment list submitted for Representative approval, and each time the equipment, rubblizing pattern, or breaking force is altered the initial two full‑depth sections (3 ft × 12 ft) within the first half‑mile must be re‑excavated to confirm that the required concrete piece dimensions are being maintained [2].

Section 350.3 for subbase specifies that when the subbase quantity exceeds greater than 1,500 tons a Subbase Delivery Plan may be prepared and submitted to the Representative if directed; when the measured subbase depth is deficient by one‑half inch or more the Representative may require additional test holes to delineate the defective area and subsequent recompaction; and when subbase remains in place over winter without a protective asphalt base course for flexible pavements or cement concrete for rigid pavements, proof rolling must be performed before placing the final pavement layer, with any instability prompting removal and repair of the subgrade and replacement of the subbase [2].

Section 509.3 for next‑generation concrete surface requires construction of a 300‑foot test section to demonstrate that equipment and procedures will provide an acceptable surface, and work may not proceed until the test section has been approved unless the District Executive waives the requirement in writing; localized depressions caused by subsidence, edge slump or similar conditions are excluded from texture percentage requirements only when approved [2].

Section 310.3 for crushed‑aggregate base course calls for selection of an alternative acceptable method when spreaders or rollers cannot be used, which requires engineering judgment beyond the standard procedure; if subgrade or subbase material becomes mixed with the base course, the mixture must be removed and the layers reshaped and re‑compacted; any surface irregularity exceeding one‑half inch or any test hole showing depth deficiency of one‑half inch or more triggers corrective action determined by the Inspector‑in‑Charge, who also decides the exact quantity of fines and water needed to produce a smooth, hard monolithic surface [2].

Section 530.3 for long‑life concrete pavement requires additional engineering judgment or testing whenever the aggregate gradation produced on a given day falls outside the cumulative percent retained limits defined in PTM No. 529, because such deviation triggers adjustment of proportions and possibly new mix designs with trial batches; if concrete temperature at placement exceeds 80°F a temperature‑control plan must be prepared; when the first truck of each lot does not meet the plastic air requirement of 7.0 percent plus or minus 1.5 percent, testing must continue until three consecutive passes are obtained; and for long‑term utility assemblies after any day’s placement greater than 700 square yards vertical and horizontal alignment must be verified by an acceptable nondestructive method at specified joint locations [2].

Section 405.3 for evaluation of asphalt pavement longitudinal joint density requires additional engineering judgment whenever the contractor, after obtaining five six‑inch diameter quality‑control cores on a longitudinal joint and testing them within 24 hours, determines that the in‑place density may not meet the lower specification limit of 91 percent, prompting submission of any proposed changes that affect the field quality control plan or job mix formula to the Department for review and a written request to the Representative if the contractor wishes to exclude a joint area [2].

Section 483.3 for polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt paving system calls for additional engineering judgment or extra testing whenever standard conditions are not met—for example, hand spreading may require adjustment of the additive to slow set time; any on‑site blending of aggregates must receive written approval from the District Materials Manager and be supported by a portable laboratory staffed by a PennDOT‑certified aggregate technician; before construction proceeds a test strip must be placed and approved by the Representative, who cannot waive this requirement; and if the mixture does not set within one hour or exceeds one hour and twenty minutes placement must be stopped and corrective action taken [2].

Section 507.3 for evaluation of concrete pavement ride quality requires additional engineering judgment, analysis, testing, or reference when a ride‑quality lot shows any defect that violates the limits—such as an MRI exceeding the maximum acceptable value, an individual bump greater than one‑quarter inch measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge, or a ridge or valley between adjacent lots larger than one‑eighth inch—because corrective action and retesting are mandated; likewise, if smoothness verification testing for federally funded projects reveals that the verification MRI exceeds the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches per mile on at least ninety percent of the five‑hundred‑twenty‑eight foot lots or that the average verification MRI exceeds the average acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches per mile, the Department will review testing methods, equipment and personnel and may suspend the contractor’s profiler certification until recertified [2].

Section 360.3 for asphalt‑treated permeable base course requires additional engineering judgment or testing when conditions fall outside normal construction limits—for example, ATPBC must not be placed on unstable, frozen surfaces or when the temperature is below 35°F, and only a Representative’s discretion can permit limited placement of material that is already en‑route; the standard depth verification calls for one six‑inch diameter test hole per three thousand square yards of completed base course, but the Representative may require extra test holes in any area where they suspect a deficiency in depth, triggering further analysis beyond the baseline specification [2].

Section 545.3 for bonded concrete overlay requires additional testing when bond‑strength lot tests prescribed do not meet thresholds; specifically, if the average of three ASTM C1583 results for a placement area is below 175 psi or any individual result is less than 160 psi, the area is deemed defective and further testing must be performed at one‑hundred‑foot intervals longitudinally from the defective locations to delineate the extent of the problem [2].

Section 412.3 for 6.3 mm thin asphalt overlay requires additional engineering judgment, analysis, testing, or reference whenever project conditions fall outside explicit limits; placement of mixtures outside the seasonal windows (October 1–March 31 in most districts or October 16–March 31 in certain districts) or when the surface is wet or the air/surface temperature is 50°F or lower requires written permission of the District Executive and a trial demonstration, with any failure of density, air‑voids, asphalt content, gradation or draindown tests to meet specification limits prompting another demonstration and further testing before work can continue; vibratory rollers may only be used after the Representative is satisfied that no aggregate breakage or binder flushing occurs, which must be demonstrated by monitoring pavement cores for each lot; if asphalt content or the percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve fails to meet the single‑sample or multiple‑sample tolerances in Table B for two consecutive lots or a total of three lots, production must be stopped, the cause analyzed and a corrective proposal submitted for Representative approval before resuming work, and the Representative may require construction of a new control strip during paving to verify the optimum rolling pattern, prompting additional density testing [2].

Finally, across these sections any lot that is deemed defective because the overall lot pay factor drops below 90.00, or either the asphalt‑content pay factor or the density pay factor falls below 80.00, or any individual percent‑within‑limits parameter is less than 50 triggers a defect determination and may require the District Executive to decide on removal, replacement, or an overall lot payment factor of 70, all of which invoke extra engineering evaluation and possible retesting with supporting data submitted within three weeks [2].
