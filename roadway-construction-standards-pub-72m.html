Roadway Construction Standards (Publication 72M) is the PennDOT manual that establishes design criteria, construction methods, quality‑control procedures, and inspection requirements for both rigid and flexible pavement projects—including subbase, shoulder, concrete patching, slab stabilization, and related work—serving as a governing specification in many sections while also providing procedural guidance.

Purpose and intended use

Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) serve as the reference that furnishes engineers with the planning, design, construction‑control, and acceptance criteria needed to decide whether a proposed or ongoing pavement activity complies with prescribed performance thresholds and procedural requirements. When engineers need to design or construct a dowel‑bar retrofit or cross‑stitching on Portland cement concrete pavements they consult Pub 72M which provides the RC‑26M standard drawing set that defines the required materials and installation procedures for these rehabilitation treatments. For new or reconstructed rigid‑pavement projects the standard requires provision of Portland Cement Concrete shoulders, while for flexible‑pavement projects the pavement shall be constructed two feet wider into the shoulder area before applying the designated shoulder type; full‑depth concrete shoulders shall be provided on Interstate and Other Limited Access Freeways, Type 2 concrete shoulders shall be provided on Arterials, Collectors, and Local Roads, and Type 1, 1‑F, 1‑S, or 1‑SP shoulders adjacent to flexible pavements are directed when drainage problems are anticipated, when truck traffic exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150 on two‑lane roads, or when specific intersection geometry calls for shoulder use. For reconstruction lanes longer than about 500 feet the standard specifies joint spacing of 30 feet for jointed reinforced concrete pavement per RC‑21M and 15 feet for jointed plain concrete pavement per RC‑27M, with designers required to select skewed or non‑skewed joints as shown on RC‑26M and to identify these design elements as separate pay items during the preliminary engineering phase. Slab‑stabilization repairs are required to be performed in accordance with RC‑26M, which defines the number and pattern of holes to drill at joints or cracks, and for estimating purposes the standard mandates that at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints be stabilized when no preliminary testing has been performed. For urban interstate or expressway projects the standard directs construction of full‑depth cement‑concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints and requires that shoulder materials match those of the mainline pavement from subgrade to surface. Acceptance of rigid concrete placement is guided by a requirement for a QC plan submitted before paving, daily monitoring of aggregate gradations, prohibition of placement when evaporation exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·h unless mitigated, and form deflection limits of 0.01 inch under a 1,700‑lb load; slip‑form placement must use a fully energized self‑propelled machine with side forms, longitudinal joints within 12 inches of final markings, edge slump not more than 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes and ramps or 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders, and surface tolerance not exceeding 3/16 inch measured with a straightedge parallel to the centerline. Concrete‑pavement patching acceptance is supported by requiring a QC plan, prohibiting saw cuts greater than ½ inch in width or less than full depth from being opened to traffic, and allowing opening only after cylinders show at least 1,000 psi compressive strength; any high point or depression of 3/16 inch or more must be ground. Subgrade construction is directed to formation to the established top‑of‑subgrade elevation, daily sealing with a smooth‑drum steel‑wheel roller in static mode, correction of surface irregularities exceeding ½ inch, and permits the Representative to require stakes and stringlines if more than 10 percent of acceptance tests exceed the ½‑inch limit. Slab fracturing and seat rehabilitation require a fracture test section of at least 1,000 feet, submission of a fracture plan and equipment list for approval, use of a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller with at least three passes for Crack‑and‑Seat or five passes for Break‑and‑Seat, and verification by falling‑weight deflectometer testing. Full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slabs must have mix designs submitted at least 28 days before production, shop and working drawings plus a QC plan delivered no later than 21 calendar days prior to the pre‑production meeting, and must meet performance criteria that include minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi, minimum flexural strength of 650 psi, slab thickness fabricated ¼ inch greater than nominal, and dimensional tolerances; compliance determines acceptance. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses are planned with equipment capable of metering liquids within ±2.0 percent by weight, placement using an asphalt paver, compaction to at least 96 percent of the PTM 402 target density, in‑place density verification per AASHTO T 310, a curing period of at least two weeks or until moisture is below 2 percent before subsequent courses, and milling depth measurements at intervals of no more than 3,000 square yards. Concrete pavement rubblizing proceeds only after a pre‑construction rubblizing plan and equipment list are approved, removal of any asphalt overlay, verification of piece size through two full‑depth sections 3 ft by 12 ft within the first half‑mile, seating with a 50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller limited to four passes and deemed satisfactory when vertical deflection under the roller is less than ¾ inch, and backfill placed in lifts not exceeding 6 inches compacted until non‑movement. Next‑generation concrete surfaces require a self‑propelled diamond‑grinding machine weighing at least 40,000 lb with a grinding head of minimum 4 ft width, a feather pass of at least 100 ft extending 2 ft onto shoulders, construction of a 300‑ft test section to demonstrate equipment and procedures, and a final smoothness limit not exceeding an IRI of 70 in/mi where posted speed exceeds 45 mph or 90 in/mi where posted speed is 45 mph or less. Crushed‑aggregate base courses type DG and general must be compacted to not less than 100 percent of maximum density, surface‑tolerance checks at intervals of no more than 25 ft with correction of irregularities exceeding ½ inch, a full‑depth test hole cut for each 3,000 square yards, and layer thickness limits of 4–8 in (or 5–10 in for vibratory methods) with shoulders built up to elevation before or simultaneously with base placement. Plant‑mixed asphalt mixtures are accepted when the Inspector‑in‑Charge approves density by non‑movement for scratch or leveling courses less than 1 inch deep or ≤110 lb/yd², pavement cores are required for RPS construction, density is determined with an approved nuclear gauge per PTM 402 or an approved electrical‑impedance gauge per PTM 403 in the presence of the Representative and a certified field technician, and the mixture is spread and struck off across the entire lane width; mixtures placed in quantities not exceeding 500 tons continuously may be accepted by lot. Paved shoulders type 4 are constructed by removing surplus material to the bottom of the proposed base course or a directed depth, scarifying existing shoulder areas to a maximum depth of 4 inches below grade, compacting graded material as specified, and the material is considered satisfactorily compacted when it does not move under compaction equipment. Continuously reinforced concrete pavement patching decisions require saw cuts no wider than ½ inch to be opened to traffic only after compressive strength of at least 1,000 psi is verified, and limit wheel penetration into the subbase to a maximum of ½ inch and jackhammer size to 30 pounds. Ride‑quality evaluation defines defect criteria that allow engineers to declare a lot defective if the MRI exceeds the maximum acceptable value, any bump exceeds ¼ inch with a 12‑foot straightedge, or verification MRI exceeds acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile on at least 90 percent of the 528‑ft lots. Concrete pavement cross‑stitching repairs require submission of a QC plan at least 14 days before work, prohibit work when ambient or surface temperature is below 40°F, and specify hole‑drilling tolerances relative to tie‑bar size; traffic may not be opened until anchoring material has fully cured per the manufacturer’s recommendations. Full‑depth longitudinal concrete joint repairs mandate a QC plan submitted at least two weeks prior, forbid saw cuts greater than ½ inch in width to be opened to traffic, prohibit leaving excavated repair areas unpatched for more than 48 hours, and require use of a diamond‑tipped blade equipped with guard, alignment guides, water cooling, and cut‑depth controls. Subbase construction planning requires a Subbase Delivery Plan for quantities greater than 1,500 tons, placement of No. 2A aggregate layers to a minimum compacted depth of 3 inches and No. OGS aggregate layers to a minimum of 4 inches, constructing layers not more than 8 inches deep (or 4 inches when granulated slag is used), and allows the Representative to require stakes and stringlines if needed. Long‑life concrete pavement acceptance defines a full lot as 5,600 sq yd divided into four sublots of 1,400 sq yd, requires sampling and testing of compressive strength and plastic air content before placement, verifies water‑to‑cementitious ratio between 0.37 and 0.42 with ±0.03 tolerance, compares core depth to design thickness minus 0.5 inch, ensures the PWL index is at least 55 percent, and classifies a lot as defective if any threshold is not met, which then must be removed and replaced. The Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) are directed primarily to pavement design and construction personnel—including the project review team, contractors, and the District’s design and construction staff—who are responsible for preparing specifications and drawings [1]. The standards are also addressed to the contractors or construction teams that perform the various pavement activities, with oversight provided by the District Representative, the Technical Representative, and Department inspectors‑in‑charge where noted [2]. For concrete‑pavement rehabilitation treatments such as dowel‑bar retrofit or cross‑stitching, the manual is consulted during the design and execution phases because it supplies the standard drawing set for those repairs [1]. When new‑construction or reconstruction projects involve rigid or flexible pavements, the standards are used by the District Executive and the design and construction team to specify shoulder types (Types 1‑F, 1‑S, 1‑SP, 6‑F, 6‑S, 6‑SP) during the design phase of any Change in Road Type (CIRT) or No Change in Road Type (NoCIRT) work [1]. Design engineers developing concrete‑pavement plans rely on the publication in the preliminary engineering/design stage to define patch boundaries, select appropriate joint types—skewed or non‑skewed—and determine quantities that must appear on contract drawings and pay items [1]. For urban Interstate or Expressway projects that require full‑depth cement concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints, the District’s design and construction staff refer to Pub 72M during the construction phase to detail and build those shoulders as shown in the standard [1]. During slip‑form concrete paving (Section 501.3(g)) the standards are aimed at the contractor and its field personnel under the Representative’s supervision; they become applicable during the concrete placement stage, dictating edge‑slump limits of 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes and lanes and ramps and 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders and ramps, requiring suspension of paving after 200 feet if those limits cannot be maintained, and mandating tie‑bar pull‑out testing after the concrete reaches 3,500 psi compressive strength [2]. For plain or reinforced cement concrete work (Section 501.3) the contractor must submit a comprehensive field‑operation QC plan at least two weeks before paving; the plan is reviewed before any placement and governs aggregate monitoring, weather restrictions, mix approvals, steel form placement, base preparation and actual concrete placement [2]. In concrete pavement patching (Section 516.3) the contractor or construction team must develop and submit a quality‑control plan, provide trained personnel and adequate hauling units, saw‑cut existing pavement, limit traffic on patched areas to a maximum of 72 hours before new concrete is placed, then cure and open to traffic [2]. Slab‑fracturing rehabilitation (Section 522.3) requires the construction contractor or responsible party to submit a pavement fracturing plan and equipment list for approval prior to work; a test section of at least 1,000 feet is established, fracturing is performed, broken pieces are seated with a qualified roller, falling‑weight deflectometer testing is conducted and the surface is swept before an asphalt overlay [2]. The standards for stone matrix asphalt courses (Section 419) direct the field technician who constructs the safety edge and require lot acceptance sampling for RPS construction before the project proceeds [2]. Cold‑recycled asphalt base course placement (Section 341.3) is directed to the contractor, who must use equipment that meters liquids within ± 2.0 percent by weight, place the mixture with an asphalt paver, compact to a minimum density of 96 percent of the PTM 402 target density and cure for at least two weeks or until moisture content falls below 2 percent before any wearing or binder course is placed [2]. Rubblizing of concrete pavements (Section 526.3) requires the contractor or engineering team to submit a pavement rubblizing plan and equipment list for Department approval; the standards are useful during pre‑construction planning and throughout the rubblizing and seating activities [2]. For cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303.3) the contractor places the material with an approved paver or spreader, compacts it with steel‑wheel power rollers of 8 to 10 tons, limits compaction time to no more than one hour after water addition (or up to 1½ hours with a retarding admixture), applies a membrane‑forming compound at 1 gallon per 100 sq ft for 24 hours (extended if temperature is below 40 °F) and prohibits construction traffic for at least three days [2]. Production and placement of plant‑mixed asphalt for scratch courses, leveling courses less than 2 inch deep and similar mixtures (Section 413.2(i)) are aimed at the Department’s inspector‑in‑charge as well as engineers or contractors who produce the mix; daily certification must be provided, quality‑control test results attached to Form CS‑4171B within one working day, quantities may not exceed 500 tons in a continuous operation unless directed by the Representative, and when printed ticket results replace laboratory tests at least 90 percent of each day’s tickets for asphalt content must be within 0.2 percentage points of the JMF [2]. Placement of an Asphalt Rich Base Course (Section 314.3) is meant for the contractor who places and finishes the ARBC; it governs construction when the material is placed in a single layer not less than 3 inches or more than 6 inches thick, prohibits placement on wet surfaces or when air or surface temperature is 35 °F or lower, allows the Representative to authorize limited placements if work is halted by weather, and requires the Inspector to measure core depths according to PTM No. 737 [2]. Paved‑shoulder work (Section 654.3) is directed to the contractor responsible for the shoulder; it guides removal of surplus material, scarifying to a maximum depth of 4 inches, shaping and compacting the base, backfilling, applying an asphalt prime coat and surface treatment during construction [2]. Concrete pavement cross‑stitching (Section 528.3) targets the contractor or construction team; a QC plan must be submitted at least fourteen days before work, the operation demonstrated to the Representative for final approval, work may not be performed if ambient or pavement temperature is below 40 °F and traffic is prohibited until the anchoring material has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations [2]. Full‑depth longitudinal concrete joint repair (Section 519.3) is directed to the contractor or repair crew and the Representative; a QC plan is required at least two weeks before repairs, after which saw cutting, removal of existing pavement and subbase, placement of new concrete, finishing and sealing are governed, traffic may be allowed on cut areas for a maximum of 72 hours and excavated areas must not remain un‑patched for more than 48 hours [2]. Subbase construction (Section 350.3) is aimed at the contractor who prepares a Subbase Delivery Plan and the Representative who reviews it; subbase work may not begin until the Representative reviews the plan, the plan is required when quantities exceed 1,500 tons, and the subbase must be placed before any paving activity [2]. Long‑life concrete pavement (Section 530.3(t)) standards are directed to the district’s Representative, who may act under direction from the District Executive; they are applied during the concrete placement and acceptance phase, defining lots of 5,600 square yards divided into four sub‑lots of 1,400 square yards each, accepting a lot when the average core depth exceeds design thickness minus 0.5 inch, verifying water‑cement ratio, and requiring a compressive‑strength quality index (PWL) of at least 55 percent, with any defective work removed or replaced as directed [2]. For asphalt paving projects (Section 413.3(a)) the standards are aimed at the paving contractor; a paving‑operation QC plan on Form CS‑413 must be prepared and submitted to the Technical Representative before the pre‑construction conference, placement is prohibited until the Representative reviews the plan, weather limits prohibit placement when air or surface temperature is 40 °F or lower, normal paving may occur from April 1 through October 15 (or through October 31 for other courses), and required plant modifications, hauling‑vehicle specifications and extended‑season procedures are prescribed, making the standards essential throughout the planning and execution phases of asphalt paving work [2].

Scope and applicability

Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) address both flexible asphalt‑concrete pavements and rigid Portland‑cement concrete (PCC) pavements, providing separate guidance for each system【1】. For flexible pavements the standard covers Hot Mix Asphalt (HMA), Warm Mix Asphalt (WMA) and Superpave wearing, binder, leveling or base courses, including Stone Matrix Asphalt as a specific mix type【2】. The flexible‑pavement guidance requires evaluation of drainage, base repair, leveling or milling, binder‑course thickness, surface friction and shoulder condition before resurfacing【1】. It also defines a Restricted Performance Specification that may be applied to HMA or WMA when Superpave wearing and binder courses meet minimum thickness requirements and the existing pavement is stable, leveled, sealed and patched prior to resurfacing【1】. Field densities for standard and RPS wearing‑course construction must fall within a 92.0 % to 98.5 % range, with contractors required to achieve values near the middle of that interval【1】. Recycling provisions require retaining at least 15 % of milled material and permit use of Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement in HMA, WMA or cold‑recycled base courses【1】. Cold‑recycled asphalt base (CRABC) may be placed only when the milling equipment can meter liquids to within ± 2.0 percent by weight, is prohibited from September 1 to April 30 in specified districts, and must be compacted to at least 96 % of PTM 402 target density with a moisture content below 2 % after curing for at least two weeks when emulsified asphalt is used【2】. Full‑depth reclamation (FDR) is described as a sustainable rehabilitation technique that may employ mechanical, chemical, asphalt or other stabilizers to produce a stabilized base whose structure‑layer coefficients range from subbase to cement‑type values【1】. For rigid pavements the standard covers plain or reinforced cement‑concrete slabs placed by slip‑form paving machines and specifies concrete, steel reinforcement, dowel bars, load‑transfer units, joint sealants and expansion‑joint filler【2】. Concrete pavement surface acceptance tolerances include edge slump not exceeding 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders, and a straightedge deviation not greater than 3/16 inch; for pavements on curves with radii less than 50 feet the final surface finish must achieve a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 inch【2】. Curing temperature changes may not exceed 40 °F within any one‑hour period and must be monitored at least hourly during curing【2】. Concrete‑pavement patching is divided into Type A, B and C patches, with excavated areas prohibited from remaining un‑patched for more than 48 hours and traffic on saw‑cut pavement limited to a maximum of 72 hours after cutting【1】. Dowel‑bar retrofit is required when transverse cracks develop, and dowel anchoring is acceptable only when the summed voids around any dowel perimeter are less than 0.50 inch【1】. Cross‑stitching of longitudinal cracks or joints in plain cement concrete pavements is achieved by placing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle across the joint or crack【1】. Slab stabilization addresses voids caused by pumping action, subgrade consolidation or settlement and prescribes drilling holes at joints or cracks and injecting either cement grout (≈ 1 cubic foot per hole), hot asphalt (30 to 50 gallons per hole) or high‑density polyurethane (about 0.4 lb per square foot)【1】. At least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized when no testing has been performed, and the procedures must follow RC‑26M together with Publication 408 Section 679 and traffic‑control guidance in Publication 213【1】. Full‑depth plain cement concrete shoulders are required on Interstates and other limited‑access freeways, while Type 2 concrete shoulders are specified for arterials, collectors and local roads; Type 1, 1‑F, 1‑S or 1‑SP shoulder treatments may be used adjacent to flexible pavements when serious drainage problems are anticipated, truck traffic exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150 on two‑lane roadways, or intersection geometry such as curves sharper than 12°‑30′ or tight ramp radii would cause vehicles to use the shoulder【1】. For collectors and local roads Type 3 shoulders may be provided using the same surface material as the pavement structure, and bicycle‑friendly rumble strips are addressed through referenced publications【1】. When a continuous section of about 500 feet or more is being reconstructed, design must address slab thickness, joint alignment and spacing, including an intermediate joint if existing spacing exceeds 20 feet; patches longer than 65 feet require longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts【1】. The standard restricts the use of high‑early‑strength concrete because of shrinkage cracking but recommends accelerated concrete for early traffic opening, which can achieve required strength in four to seven hours versus twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours for standard concrete【1】. Precast concrete paving systems are covered as an alternative to cast‑in‑place PCC; precast slabs may be no wider than a single lane and must be at least six feet wide, with lengths from fifteen feet up to twenty feet for prestressed units, allowing up to four inches of thickness reduction but never less than eight inches【1】. Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (LLCP) must be placed with a water‑to‑cementitious ratio between 0.37 and 0.42; each lot is sampled for core depth, compressive‑strength quality index (PWL) and plastic air content, and a lot is deemed defective when the average core depth is less than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch or when the 28‑day PWL is less than 55 percent【2】. Full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and flexural strength of 650 psi; transverse joint widths shall be between 0.25 inch and 0.5 inch, longitudinal joints may not exceed 0.75 inch, and vertical differentials across any joint may not exceed 0.25 inch (or 0.5 inch before grinding)【2】. Continuously reinforced concrete pavement patching requires concrete, reinforcement bars, a minimum 4.0‑mil polyethylene bond breaker, subbase material, wheel saws with carbide steel tips and jackhammers up to 30 lb, with full‑depth transverse cuts made from the edge toward the longitudinal cut and a second transverse cut of at least 1 inch depth at each end of the patch【2】. The next‑generation concrete surface (NGCS) requires grinding and grooving with a power‑driven self‑propelled machine equipped with diamond blades weighing at least 40,000 lb and a grinding head no less than four feet wide; differential between adjacent joint sides must not exceed one‑sixteenth inch and existing cross‑slope may deviate no more than one‑eighth inch in twelve feet【2】. NGCS also mandates a feather pass of at least two feet into the shoulder, gore area, auxiliary lane or ramp lane with a minimum length of 100 feet to provide drainage, and the finished surface must have at least 98 percent of the area textured, a maximum IRI of 70 in/mile for posted speeds greater than 45 mph or 90 in/mile for speeds less than or equal to 45 mph【2】. Safety edge construction is required on outer pavement and shoulder with a depth of at least 1.5 inches, shall not be placed where total depth exceeds 5 inches, and for Stone Matrix Asphalt the edge must be formed from the same course material set at an angle of 26 to 40 degrees measured from the extended cross‑slope【2】. Pattern segregation is unacceptable when the difference in average pavement texture depth between segregated and non‑segregated areas exceeds 0.024 inch, and a pavement is considered flushed and defective when the average texture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 inch【2】. Engineering problems evaluated for both rigid and flexible pavements include ride quality using a measured roughness index (MRI) with defect criteria that the MRI not exceed the maximum in Table A, any individual bump measured with a 12‑foot straightedge exceeding ¼ inch, or any ridge or valley exceeding ⅛ inch; additional acceptance criteria address pattern segregation, flushing, texture depth limits, fatigue cracking, transverse cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, loss of bond/delamination, and settlement‑related issues such as edge stresses and horizontal separation between pavement and shoulder【2】. The standards also prescribe that all materials of a full‑depth cement concrete shoulder must match those of the mainline pavement from subgrade to surface, and they address engineering problems such as edge stresses, differential heaving, settlement, and horizontal separation between pavement and shoulder【1】. Key content and structure

The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10) defines Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) as a comprehensive set of evaluation items and procedural criteria for both asphalt‑concrete and Portland cement concrete pavements, covering drainage improvement, base repair, surface friction upgrades, shoulder rehabilitation, dowel‑bar retrofit or cross‑stitching with epoxy‑coated tie bars【1】. It establishes performance thresholds such that diamond grinding is permitted only when wheel ruts are less than 0.5 inches deep and restricted‑performance specifications require surface‑course thicknesses to meet the minimums in Table 9.5, while a uniform nominal depth of not less than 5,600 square yards with continuous placement is required for projects meeting the specified criteria【1】. Quality measures include a Percent Within Limits field density target range of 92.0 %–98.5 % that ties directly to graduated pay factors【1】. Recycling practices mandate that the contractor retain at least 15 % of milled material, or provide all reclaimed material when the total milled quantity is under 200 tons【1】. Full‑depth reclamation guidance incorporates structure‑layer coefficients that vary with the type of stabilization employed【1】.

For shoulder design, any new construction or reconstruction project having a rigid pavement structure shall have Portland Cement Concrete shoulders provided, while flexible pavements shall be constructed two feet wider into the shoulder area (excluding inside shoulders on Interstate and Other Limited Access Freeways) and the remainder of the shoulder shall use the designated type【1】. Full‑depth concrete shoulders are required on Interstates and other limited‑access freeways; Type 2 concrete shoulders are specified for arterials, collectors and local roads; and Type 1, 1‑F, 1‑S or 1‑SP shoulders adjacent to flexible pavements are required for all such freeways and arterials, for roadways with serious drainage problems, for two‑lane roads where truck traffic exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150, and where traffic is anticipated to use the shoulder at intersections with ramps having radii sharper than 12°‑30′ or where left‑turn vehicles may force through traffic onto the right shoulder, in which case the higher‑type paving shall start 250 ft before the intersection and stop 150 ft downstream【1】. For collectors and local roads either a Type 3 shoulder of the same surface material as the pavement structure or one of the Type 1 families may be provided, and bicycle‑compatible rumble strips are referenced to Publication 13 with standard strip details found in RC‑22M【1】.

Concrete pavement patching requires load transfer at transverse joints to be achieved by dowels bonded along their entire embedded length, and designers must define patch boundaries so all deteriorated material is removed; joint details follow RC‑26M with separate items for skewed or non‑skewed baskets【1】. When reconstructing 500 feet or more of adjacent lanes with Portland cement concrete the work shall follow RC‑21M for jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP), which requires 30‑foot joint spacing, perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, or RC‑27M for jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP), which requires 15‑foot joint spacing with the same joint features【1】. Accelerated concrete pavement patching may be used where opening to traffic is controlled by the patch operation, providing early traffic opening in four to seven hours【1】. For intermittent repairs using precast slabs slab widths shall not exceed one lane width and slab lengths shall be at least six feet but no more than 15 feet ( 20 feet for prestressed slabs)【1】.

Slab stabilization is required when voids under concrete cause transverse joint faulting, fines near joints, small depressions, corner breaks or settlement; the work shall follow RC‑26M for the number and pattern of holes and may use cement grout estimated at one cubic foot per hole ( 0.25 bag of cement) with traffic reopening only after the grout cures (which can be twelve hours), hot asphalt typically 30 to 50 gallons per hole with traffic reopening after a minimum of thirty minutes once the asphalt cools, or high‑density polyurethane at 0.4 lb per square foot with traffic reopening after a minimum of thirty minutes once the foam expands and hardens【1】. For estimating purposes at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized if no preliminary testing has been performed; joint and crack sealing must accompany stabilization, subsurface drains must be kept in good condition, and drilling and stabilizing are confined to a single lane using the same traffic control devices as required by Publication 213【1】.

The Manual notes that Pub 72M contains no construction details or specifications for continuously reinforced concrete pavement (CRCP); any CRCP work must be governed by special provisions inserted into the contract, may proceed only after approval from the Director of BODD, and structural design should follow the 1993 AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures using DARWin software, with guidance on appropriateness obtained from HDTD【1】. An illustration RC‑25M showing full‑depth cement concrete shoulders constructed with dowelled transverse joints is required for all urban Interstate or Expressway projects that must use tied concrete shoulders, supplying visual and dimensional details for proper construction of tied concrete shoulder sections【1】.

The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2020 Specifications prescribe slip‑form concrete paving to be performed with a fully energized self‑propelled machine equipped with side forms, automatic alignment, grade controls and vibrating/tamping devices; edge slumping is monitored with a twelve‑foot straightedge before the concrete loses plasticity and shall not exceed one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes (or ramps) and one‑quarter inch between lanes and shoulders; if slumping cannot be controlled within these limits for two hundred feet, paving must be suspended and the section evaluated【2】. Joint work requires pre‑staking of load‑transfer units, accurate dowel placement, sawing transverse joints to a depth of one‑third slab thickness and a width of one‑eighth inch, immediate curing, sealing and testing, while tie‑bar pull‑out resistance must meet Table B criteria after testing at three‑thousand‑five‑hundred psi compressive strength【2】. Final surface preparation includes machine or manual strike‑off, floating, straightedge testing with a twelve‑foot tool to correct low or high spots until the slab meets a three‑sixteenth inch tolerance parallel and perpendicular to the centreline, followed by broom or tine texturing that produces grooves of one‑eighth inch (± one‑thirty‑second) width and depth between one‑eighth and three‑sixteenths inch, with macrotexture achieving a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 inches as measured per ASTM E965【2】.

Concrete pavements are built in lane widths shown on the Standard Drawings; for projects exceeding fifteen thousand square yards of mainline paving the contractor must monitor aggregate gradations each day by sieve analysis of plant‑stockpiled material, generate a cumulative percent retained chart and keep daily production within the limits of the Combined Gradation Percent Retained Chart (PTM No. 529), adjusting proportions as needed【2】. A comprehensive field‑operation quality‑control plan covering personnel responsibilities, sampling frequencies, methods for determining traffic‑opening strength and pavement depth, weather‑placement procedures, joint construction, curing and other controls must be submitted at least two weeks before paving and approved prior to start【2】.

Temperature and moisture controls are strictly enforced: concrete placement shall not occur when the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·hr unless mitigation measures such as windbreaks or fog misting are employed; cold‑weather work must stop when air temperature falls to forty degrees Fahrenheit (forty‑five degrees for accelerated‑strength concrete) and resume only above those limits, while hot‑weather operations must keep plastic concrete between fifty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit and cease if it exceeds ninety degrees【2】. Asphalt mixtures may not be placed on wet surfaces or when air or surface temperature is thirty‑five degrees Fahrenheit (or forty for some specifications), and pervious asphalt pavement systems may not be placed when the air or surface temperature is lower than 50°F【2】.

Forms shall be steel at least ten feet long with depth equal to pavement depth, may not deviate more than one‑eighth inch in ten feet, and must not deflect more than 0.01 inch under a 1,700‑lb centre load over a four‑foot span; wood forms are permitted on curves of radius less than one‑hundred fifty feet under specified bracing requirements【2】.

Concrete pavement patching (Section 516.3) requires a quality‑control plan that identifies action points for mixing, curing, joint sawing and testing before traffic opening; saw‑cutting shall be performed with a diamond‑tipped blade producing full‑depth cuts no wider than one‑half inch, excavated patches may not remain un‑patched for more than forty‑eight hours and traffic may be allowed on cut areas of existing pavement for a maximum of seventy‑two hours【2】. Corner repairs must use a jackhammer not exceeding thirty‑five pounds, dowel bars must be anchored with material that sets within five minutes and core testing shall verify voids less than 0.50 inch; expansion joint material shall be three‑quarters inch (½ inch for corners) with bond‑breaker lubricant applied at least one hour before concrete placement, longitudinal joints shall be cut one‑quarter inch wide and one inch deep, surface tolerance is limited to depressions or high points of three‑sixteenth inch, and traffic may be opened only after cylinders reach a compressive strength of one thousand psi【2】.

Subgrade construction (Section 210.3) requires the roadbed to be formed to the established top‑of‑subgrade elevation and compacted as specified in Section 206.3(b), then fill material is placed and compacted to that elevation, proof‑rolled, and all constructed or disturbed surfaces are sealed each workday with a smooth‑drum steel‑wheel roller operated in static mode to promote drainage; after the subgrade has remained in place for more than sixty days or at least one winter season, it is compacted and proof rolled again as specified in Section 206.3(a) before a Class 4, Type A geotextile is installed continuously over lane widths of eight feet or greater, placed longitudinally with transverse limits between six inches and twelve inches from the interior edge of the pavement base drain, and any damaged geotextile is repaired at no additional cost to the Department【2】. Grade control requires installing reference grade lines and checking grade and slope by stretching a taut string line across the subgrade at longitudinal intervals of not more than twenty‑five feet (or up to fifty feet when an automatic grading machine is used, or up to five hundred feet with GPS/laser‑controlled equipment); surface irregularities that exceed one‑half inch are corrected by loosening, adding or removing material and re‑compacting【2】.

Asphalt mat‑density acceptance (Section 413.3(j)) may be achieved by non‑movement, optimum‑rolling pattern or pavement‑core methods, each triggered by specific material thicknesses, weights, placement quantities or base conditions; the Inspector‑in‑Charge must approve the chosen method before compaction proceeds, and density is measured with an approved nuclear or electrical impedance gauge whose annual calibration certificate (ASTM D2950) and operator training records are submitted【2】. For non‑movement acceptance the mixture must not move under the rollers and applies to courses less than one inch deep or ≤110 lb/yd², as well as narrow patches; optimum‑rolling pattern requires establishing a rolling plan with the Representative present, documenting it on Form TR‑4276B/C, and re‑establishing if target density is missed【2】. Pavement cores are required for RPS work and for standard extended‑season paving unless waived, with cores taken within one day of placement, labeled per ECMS project data, and tested for bulk specific gravity (Gmb) and density; Gmm verification values must match the contractor’s daily Gmm values within ± 0.030, otherwise the laboratory value is used, and all calculated densities are rounded to the nearest tenth of a percent according to ASTM E‑29【2】.

Asphalt spreading and finishing (Section 413.3(h)) requires mixtures to be delivered, placed and compacted during daylight at the laying temperatures listed in Table A, with no lumps of cold material and using a Material Transfer Vehicle unless waived; spreading must cover the full lane width, screeds are adjusted for required cross‑section, and any course deeper than six inches must be built in two or more layers each between three and six inches thick【2】. The standard also requires construction of a Safety Edge on outer pavement edges with a minimum depth of one‑and‑a‑half inches (not exceeding five inches total) and an angle of twenty‑six to forty degrees measured from the cross‑slope, using an approved device that extrudes a wedge‑shaped profile【2】.

Lot acceptance is organized into two‑thousand‑five‑hundred‑ton lots divided into five five‑hundred‑ton sublots (unless operational conditions dictate otherwise), with one loose mixture sample taken per sublot for asphalt content and sieve analysis; samples are packaged, identified and secured with numbered tape for LTS testing, and for federally funded projects a second sample is retained to verify theoretical maximum specific gravity【2】. Payment may be 100 percent of the contract unit price when the asphalt‑content sample meets the n=1 criteria of Table I and the density sample meets the criteria in Table F; reduced payment applies if density falls within ± 2.0 percent of limits【2】. Pattern segregation and flushing are evaluated by measuring surface macrotexture, with segregation deemed unacceptable if texture difference exceeds 0.024 inches and flushing defective when average texture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 inches; such conditions trigger immediate suspension of paving, corrective actions, and test sections not exceeding two hundred tons before resuming work【2】.

Manual asphalt patching (Section 450.3) defines Type A treatment for potholes and abrupt depressions three inches or less, requiring cutting a patch of at least one square foot, excavating to sound material with vertical edges, cleaning and drying the surface, applying a tack coat to the bottom and vertical faces, and placing asphalt; for Type A only an asphalt wearing course is placed and compacted with a steel‑wheeled roller until it matches surrounding grade【2】. For Type B (depressions over three inches) a binder course is laid in lifts not exceeding three inches uncompacted depth, each lift compacted with an approved tamper, followed by a topmost wearing course of one‑and‑a‑half inches compacted with a steel‑wheeled roller; all excavated areas must be filled before the end of the workday and edges are sealed with emulsified asphalt, Class TACK or NTT, or the same PG binder used in the pavement【2】.

Slab fracturing (Section 522.3) requires a pavement‑fracturing plan that details equipment capable of variable‑force impact, pre‑fracturing removal of overlying asphalt and sawcutting slabs no longer than twenty feet; strict exclusion zones of ten feet horizontal buffer from culverts and three feet clearance from fixed objects are required. A minimum one‑thousand‑foot test section must be fractured, wetted and verified with falling‑weight deflectometer testing using a nine‑thousand‑pound drop weight at temperatures between forty‑five and ninety degrees Fahrenheit to ensure the average elastic modulus of the broken concrete lies within the range given in Table A before production fracturing proceeds【2】. After fracturing, a minimum fifty‑ton pneumatic tire roller with at least four rubber‑tired wheels inflated to at least one hundred psi must seat the pavement with three passes for Crack and Seat or five passes for Break and Seat; final surface preparation includes cleaning, tack coat application and placement of an asphalt leveling course within one week while prohibiting traffic until completion【2】.

Rubblizing (Section 526) requires a rubblizing plan and equipment list approved before start, excavation of two three‑by‑twelve foot full‑depth sections within the first half‑mile to verify dimensions, backfill placed in six‑inch lifts and compacted until non‑movement is achieved, and final seating satisfactory when vertical deflection under the roller is less than ¾ inch; if an asphalt leveling course is required a prime coat must be applied before placement with exposure to freezing conditions avoided【2】.

Pervious asphalt pavement (Section 420) mandates two test sections of at least 225 square feet each, average infiltration exceeding 200 inches per hour, compacted thickness not more than one‑quarter inch less than design thickness, placement in maximum four‑inch lifts using a track paver or mechanical spreader, compaction with smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode for one to four passes (pneumatic rollers prohibited), and mixture temperature must be allowed to cool to 100 °F before any subsequent layer【2】.

Next‑generation concrete surface (Section 509) requires a power‑driven self‑propelled diamond‑blade machine weighing at least forty‑thousand pounds with a grinding head no less than four feet wide, grinding in the longitudinal direction to eliminate faults so that adjacent sides of joints or cracks differ by no more than one‑sixteenth inch, maintaining cross‑slope and limiting depressions to one‑eighth inch over a twelve‑foot straightedge; the operation may be single‑pass with stacked blades or two‑pass with separate grooving, passes may not overlap more than one inch, and a feather pass of at least two feet into shoulders must extend for a minimum of one hundred feet【2】.

Crushed aggregate base course (Section 312) requires mass‑proportioning plants with separate bins for each aggregate size, calcium chloride added at seven pounds per ton for Type 1 or 5.7 pounds per ton for Type 2 when directed, placement in two or more layers of approximately equal depth if compacted depth exceeds six inches (no layer less than three inches nor more than six inches), each layer compacted to not less than one hundred percent of maximum density according to PTM No. 106 Method B, and surface tolerance controlled with a cross‑section template inspected at intervals not more than twenty‑five feet; any irregularity exceeding one‑half inch must be corrected by blading, reshaping or reconstructing the top layer【2】.

Cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303) allows half‑pavement or full‑pavement width construction, requires thorough sprinkling of rubblized pavement/subbase until moisture is readily absorbed before placement, use of an approved asphalt paver or mechanical spreader equipped with a screed, plate vibrator and automated sensors together with steel‑wheel power rollers weighing eight to ten tons that must make at least one pass within one hour after water is added (or up to one‑half hour if a retarding admixture is used), surface tolerance checked with a ten‑foot straightedge moving no more than five feet per stage and any irregularity exceeding one‑half inch corrected without grinding or milling, immediate curing by applying a white membrane forming compound at one gallon per 100 square feet (±10 percent) for twenty‑four hours (extended if temperature falls below forty degrees Fahrenheit), traffic prohibited on fresh material for at least three days and thereafter only equipment needed for the next layer may be allowed, with damaged or contaminated areas removed and replaced at no cost to the Department【2】.

Across all pavement types the standards require preparation and submission of detailed quality‑control plans—often on Form CS‑413 or Form CS‑413ES—for paving operations, subbase delivery, slab fracturing, rubblizing, reclamation, and other specialized work, with review periods ranging from fourteen days to two weeks before start of work【2】. Temperature limits are consistently enforced: asphalt mixtures may not be placed on wet surfaces or when air or surface temperature is thirty‑five degrees Fahrenheit (or forty for some specifications), hot‑weather concrete placement must keep plastic concrete between fifty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit, and evaporation rates above 0.20 lb/ft²·hr preclude concrete placement unless mitigation such as windbreaks or fog misting is employed【2】. Payment incentives tied to ride quality (Section 507) require MRI values not to exceed Table A limits, bumps larger than one‑quarter inch measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge are prohibited, and lots failing these criteria require removal of at least ten feet of pavement between transverse joints or replacement of the entire panel【2】. The Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) are organized hierarchically, using numbered sections and clearly labeled subsections that lead the user through each construction activity [1][2]. In Chapter 2 (section 2.1 GENERAL) of the manual the material is divided into subsections A‑F, each containing enumerated items a., b., c. that function as checklists for evaluating project needs such as drainage, base repair and surface friction, and the checklist items should be evaluated when designing a project [1]. The hierarchical structure in the Pennsylvania specifications similarly presents the content as a set of numbered sections and subsections, each accompanied by checklist‑style narratives that guide the user step by step [2]. The concrete pavement patching portion (section 4.2) is arranged into clearly labeled subsections—General Guidelines, Accelerated Concrete Pavement Patching, Precast Concrete Paving, Concrete Pavement Patching Policy and Exemption Criteria—each introduced by a heading such as “A. General Guidelines,” and within each subsection the guidance appears in step‑by‑step prose together with numbered lists that spell out required actions; the policy items are enumerated 1 through 8 [1]. The slab‑stabilization chapter (section 4.5) adds bulleted lists that identify conditions indicating a loss of slab support and provides three numbered descriptions of grout material options, each with specific application notes, while also pointing users to RC‑26M in Publication 72M for detailed hole‑pattern tables [1]. Section 501.3(g), which covers slip‑form paving, uses a checklist format that specifies the edge slump must not exceed 1/8 in between adjacent lanes or ramps and 1/4 in between lane and shoulder; verification is performed with a 12‑ft straightedge, and each selected tie bar must be tested for pull‑out resistance after the concrete has attained a compressive strength of 3,500 psi using a 12 000‑lb load that may allow 1/32 in slippage, with the average result compared to the values in Table B [2]. Section 501.3 overall requires submission of a field operation QC Plan at least two weeks before paving, limits the evaporation rate to 0.20 lb/ft²·h unless mitigation measures are used, calls for discontinuation of concreting when descending air temperature falls to 40°F (45°F for accelerated‑strength concrete), and mandates plastic‑concrete temperature monitoring with half‑hour readings when ambient temperature reaches 85°F [2]. Section 516.3 breaks construction into alphabetic subparts; subsection (a) General requires preparation of a QC Plan as specified in Section 106.03, while subsection (b) Saw Cutting specifies the use of a diamond‑tipped blade, blade guard, alignment guides, water cooling system and cut‑depth controls for perimeter cutting [2]. Section 210.3 directs the user to tabulate stake offsets and elevations on Form D‑413 and to test surface irregularities at longitudinal intervals not more than 25 ft, with any irregularity exceeding ½ in considered unacceptable [2]. Section 522.3 organizes slab fracturing around a required pavement fracturing plan and equipment list that must be approved before construction; the contractor must provide variable‑force impact equipment, establish at least one 1 000‑foot test section, and achieve an average elastic modulus within the range shown in Table A for acceptance [2]. Section 450.3 presents manual asphalt patching as a numbered checklist divided into Type A and Type B treatments; potholes or abrupt depressions three inches and less in depth are treated under Type A, lifts must not exceed three inches uncompacted depth for Type B, and the final wearing‑course thickness shall not exceed 1½ in [2]. Section 413.3(j) governs mat density acceptance; the Department will accept density according to one of the levels in Table F, the Inspector‑in‑Charge may accept density using an optimum‑rolling pattern for specified materials and conditions, and documentation of the pattern must be provided on Form TR‑4276B or Form TR‑4276C [2]. Section 413.3(h) details spreading and finishing; mixtures must be delivered and placed at laying temperatures specified in Table A, a normal lot size of 2 500 tons with five 500‑ton sublots is required for JMFs unless operational conditions dictate adjustment per Table D, density evaluation follows the criteria in Table E, and sample collection must follow prescribed packaging dimensions and be recorded on Form TR‑447 [2]. Section 419 includes a field‑technician checklist (1.c) and safety‑edge construction instructions (1.d), with the completed angle of the safety edge required to be 26 to 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross slope extended, and lot acceptance is required for RPS construction [2]. Section 529 organizes full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete slab work into step‑by‑step checklists covering fabrication, submittals, drawing preparation, project meetings, installation planning and field processes; shop drawings, working drawings and the manufacturer’s quality‑control plan must be provided to the Representative at least 21 calendar days before the pre‑production meeting, dimensions must fall within the tolerance shown in Table A, and minimum material strengths are specified as 4 925 psi compressive and 650 psi flexural [2]. Section 341 uses lettered clauses to list equipment requirements such as variation of metered liquids not more than ±2.0 percent by weight, compaction to a minimum density of 96 percent of the PTM 402 target density, and milling depth measurement at least once for each 3 000 sq yd of work [2]. Section 344 presents full‑depth reclamation as a numbered checklist: pulverization must precede stabilizing additives, mixing moisture may not exceed optimum by more than three percent, compaction must achieve at least 95 percent of the laboratory‑compacted maximum density, curing materials must be maintained for 24 hours and traffic is prohibited for at least three days [2]. Section 420 uses lettered clauses to require two test sections each at least 225 ft², prohibits placement when air or surface temperature is lower than 50°F, limits compaction with a 10‑ton steel‑wheeled static roller or vibratory roller operated in static mode to no more than four passes, and specifies equipment for grinding and grooving that must weigh a minimum of 40 000 lb and have a cutting width of at least four feet [2]. Section 509 organizes concrete surface construction into paragraphs (a) equipment, (b) grinding/grooving, (c) test section, (d) final surface finish and (e) smoothness requirements; the grinding machine must weigh at least 40 000 lb, cross‑slope deviation may not exceed 1/8 in in 12 ft, a test section of 300 ft single lane is required before full work proceeds, and smoothness limits are a maximum IRI of 70 in/mile for posted speeds greater than 45 mph and 90 in/mile for speeds less than or equal to 45 mph, with a full lot defined as 528 ft of a single lane [2]. Section 312 presents crushed‑aggregate base‑course construction as numbered sections followed by lettered subsections; if compacted depth exceeds six inches the base must be placed in two or more layers each between three and six inches, each layer must be compacted to not less than 100 percent of maximum density, equipment requirements are listed first, then mixture preparation, delivery, subgrade work, spreading and compaction, and placement must use an approved asphalt paver or mechanical spreader equipped with a screed, plate vibrator and fully automated sensors [2]. Section 303 organizes cement‑treated permeable base course construction into numbered sections divided by lettered subsections that address general scope, weather limits, mix requirements, conditioning, placement, compaction, surface tolerance, curing and protection; placement must use an approved asphalt paver or mechanical spreader with screed, plate vibrator and automated sensors, compaction must be performed with steel‑wheel power rollers of 8 to 10 tons, water may be added only if compaction can be completed within one hour, curing materials must be maintained for 24 hours and construction traffic is prohibited for at least three days [2]. Both documents rely heavily on cross‑referenced tables—such as Table 9.5 for minimum surface‑course thicknesses in the manual, Tables A through F for temperature limits, density criteria and acceptance ranges in the specifications—and on figures or standard drawings like RC‑26M, RC‑21M and RC‑27M that provide joint spacing diagrams, as well as on forms that capture measured data [1][2]. This layered arrangement of hierarchical sections, checklist narratives, step‑by‑step procedures, required QC plans, referenced tables, figures and standard drawings is intended to give users a clear sequential path and quantitative thresholds for compliance without relying on separate decision trees or figures [1][2].

Required inputs and assumptions

When applying Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M), the user must first provide a project‑specific assessment of drainage needs and any base failures that require removal and replacement to achieve adequate structural capacity and facilitate drainage [1]. The user also has to evaluate surface levelness and determine the required binder‑course thickness, which shall be derived from non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures [1]. Desired friction improvements must be identified, as well as the existing shoulder condition for asphalt pavements; for Portland‑cement concrete pavements the user must identify drainage issues, patching requirements, slab‑jacking or stabilization needs, and indicate whether dowel‑bar retrofit, diamond grinding, joint repair, crack sealing, shoulder upgrades or cross‑stitching are required [1]. If a Superpave Restricted Performance Specification is to be used, the user must confirm that the existing pavement is stable, properly leveled, sealed and patched, and provide clear plans showing the State Route limits of paving for both surface courses and structural overlays [1]. The project classification must be supplied, indicating whether it is new construction or reconstruction (with or without a change in road type) and whether the pavement structure is rigid or flexible, because rigid projects require Portland Cement Concrete shoulders while flexible projects require a two‑foot widening into the shoulder area before applying the designated shoulder type [1]. The effective shoulder width taken from the design‑criteria charts must be provided together with the roadway classification (Interstate, Other Limited Access Freeways, Arterials, Collectors or Local Roads) and any anticipated drainage problems; Type 1, 1‑F, 1‑S or 1‑SP shoulders are required on all Interstates and other limited‑access freeways and arterials, on roads where serious drainage problems are expected, on two‑lane roads where truck traffic exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150, and on two‑lane roads where traffic is expected to use the shoulder for left‑turn or right‑turn movements at intersections with tight ramp radii or curves sharper than 12°‑30’ [1]. The user must also supply the two‑way average daily traffic so that the condition of less than 10,000 can be evaluated for decisions on full‑depth cement concrete shoulders versus widened slabs with asphalt or RCC shoulders [1]. For patching operations the designer must define the patch boundaries and state whether the affected length exceeds about 500 feet, because lengths of that magnitude trigger full‑width reconstruction criteria; quantities for new pavement joints and an explicit indication of whether skewed or non‑skewed baskets are required per RC‑26M must also be supplied [1]. Joint spacing details must be given—30‑foot spacing for JRCP or 15‑foot spacing for JPCP, with an intermediate joint added when existing spacing exceeds 20 feet on a single‑lane rebuild—and the need for longitudinal tie bars for patches longer than 65 feet must be indicated [1]. The user must provide excavation and sawcutting limits (Class 1) and any “As Directed” undercut quantity for patches with poor subgrade [1]. When precast or prestressed concrete slabs are considered, the user must supply slab dimensions that do not exceed one lane width and have lengths of six to fifteen feet (up to twenty feet for prestressed slabs), any required thickness reduction, associated prestress forces, and the subgrade and subbase strength parameters that will be incorporated into design calculations [1]. For urban or rural Interstate/Expressway projects the user must indicate whether the shoulder is expected to support traffic during work‑zone control or incident‑management activities on a high‑volume corridor, because this determines whether full‑depth cement concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints (urban) or tied shoulders with undowelled joints (rural) are required [1]. Concrete paving requires precise layout data for all joints and load‑transfer units, including accurately marked dowel‑bar locations and pre‑staked LTU assemblies; reinforcement must be free of rust, dirt, oil or grease, and edge‑slump measurements may not exceed 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes and ramps and 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders [2]. Tie‑bar pull‑out test results are required after the concrete reaches a compressive strength of 3,500 psi using a load gauge up to 12,000 lb or 1/32 inch slippage, with calculations compared to Table B; macrotexture test data must show a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 inch for pavements with radii under 50 ft and additional testing or grooving is required if this limit is not met [2]. A comprehensive paving‑operation QC plan must be submitted at least two weeks before the start of concrete paving, detailing personnel responsibilities, sampling and testing frequencies, methods for determining opening‑to‑traffic strength and pavement depth, and all required weather, subgrade, joint, grade, curing and damage‑prevention procedures; if the project exceeds fifteen thousand square yards of mainline concrete paving the user must also provide daily aggregate sieve‑analysis results, generate a cumulative percent‑retained chart and verify that the values plot within the limits defined by the Combined Gradation Percent Retained Chart according to PTM No. 529 [2]. For asphalt work the user must establish a sub‑lot size for each Job Mix Formula based on the project’s plan quantity and keep it constant throughout construction, supply a certified asphalt field technician meeting Publication 351 qualifications, obtain Representative approval of any safety‑edge device before use, and provide full identification data for every mixture acceptance or Gmm verification sample (ECMS project number, lot and sublot numbers, station/offset location, placement date and mixture type) together with the nuclear gauge’s annual calibration certificate and proof of operator training; optimum‑rolling patterns must be recorded on Form TR‑4276B or TR‑4276C and any expedited‑delivery details included in the QC plan [2]. Manual asphalt patching requires assessment of each defect’s depth to classify it as Type A (≤ 3 inches) or Type B (> 3 inches), confirmation that the outlined patch area is at least 1 square foot, determination of whether the pothole reaches the shoulder and identification of the shoulder material, specification of the appropriate asphalt wearing‑course material, and for Type B patches specification of binder‑course thickness with lifts not to exceed 3 inches uncompacted as well as selection of a tack coat and edge sealant from the allowed options such as emulsified asphalt, Class TACK, NTT or the PG‑Binder used in the existing pavement [2]. Concrete pavement patching demands a quality‑control plan that details actions for concrete mixing, curing, joint sawing, sealing and sampling/testing; the user must provide the manufacturer’s recommendations for the epoxy bonding compound and rapid‑set patching material together with a material specification data sheet for the anchoring material used in dowel‑bar installation, supply compressive‑strength test results showing that the concrete attains a minimum strength of 1,000 psi before traffic is opened, and provide surface‑tolerance measurements confirming that any high points or depressions do not exceed 3/16 inch [2]. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete slabs the user must submit concrete mix designs at least twenty‑eight days before slab production with trial‑batch test data demonstrating a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and flexural strength of 650 psi; complete shop and working drawings plus the manufacturer’s quality‑control plan must be delivered no later than twenty‑one calendar days prior to the pre‑production meeting, and these documents must include core locations from existing pavement, a slab layout showing dimensions, joint spacing, grout‑reservoir placement, reinforcing size/position, dowel and tie‑bar details, thermal‑expansion information for ambient temperatures, and a detailed slab‑leveling system package (steel bearing plate size/thickness with design calculations, corrosion protection, bolt hardware and fabricator name); for prestressed slabs the user must also provide tendon type, layout, prestressing force and mid‑slab stress calculations, as well as survey data establishing theoretical surface elevations and joint locations, a grouting work plan, bedding‑layer specifications and grout strength criteria [2]. Pervious asphalt pavement systems require test‑section dimensions of at least 225 square feet each, verification that the average infiltration from ASTM C 1701 testing exceeds 200 inches per hour, confirmation that the compacted thickness will not be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness, documentation of subgrade condition as undisturbed soil with any eroded fine material removed and scarified to a minimum depth of 6 inches before placement, avoidance of placement when air or surface temperature is lower than 50°F, placement in maximum 4‑inch compacted lifts, cooling of the pavement to 100°F before subsequent layers, and limitation of roller compaction to no more than four passes with a 10‑ton steel‑wheeled static roller [2]. Crushed‑aggregate base course Type DG demands exact coarse‑ and fine‑aggregate quantities measured by volume or weight, water amount measured by weight, volume or a metering device, and any calcium chloride dosage (7 pounds per ton for Type 1 or 5.7 pounds per ton for Type 2) when directed; the subbase or subgrade must be documented as completed and maintained at least 1,500 feet ahead of the spreader, free of ruts, irregularities and loose material, and each base layer must be compacted to not less than 100 percent of maximum density as determined by PTM No. 106 Method B with field density measured per AASHTO T 191 [2]. For cement‑treated permeable base course the user must determine the exact surface area so that curing compound can be applied at a rate of one gallon per 100 square feet ±10 percent, assess moisture condition of any rubblized pavement or subbase and sprinkle enough water to achieve a moist state before placing the CTPBC, record the time when water is added to the aggregate‑cement mix and ensure compaction is completed within one hour (or up to 1½ hours if a retarding admixture is used), monitor the temperature of the fresh CTPBC during curing and extend the cure period by an additional 24 hours if it falls below 40°F, and remove and replace the material if the temperature drops to 32°F within 72 hours of placement [2]. Superpave mixture acceptance requires certification from the mixture producer and attachment of all quality‑control test results for asphalt content and gradation on Form CS‑4171B, provision of that form to the Inspector‑in‑Charge within one working day after testing, daily certification when QC data meet single‑sample and multiple‑sample JMF production tolerances in Table A, and if printed ticket results are used, at least 90 percent of those tickets must be within 0.2 percentage points of the JMF; all failed mixes must have their QC test results supplied to the Inspector‑in‑Charge [2]. Paved shoulders Type 4 require the user to provide design elevations, slopes and any directed depths for each shoulder operation, specify the aggregate base‑course material required by Section 350.3 and the exact backfill depth when excavation extends below the base‑course elevation, indicate surplus material removal to the bottom of the proposed base course or to a directed depth, and reference the appropriate sections for asphalt prime coat and surface treatment [2]. Concrete cross‑stitching demands submission of a QC plan prepared per Section 106.03 delivered at least fourteen days before work begins that details all construction procedures, equipment and materials; ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature must not be below 40°F before any activity, drill‑hole specifications shall be limited to no more than three‑eighths of an inch larger than the tie bar with offset distance and spacing shown on drawing RC‑26M adjustable ±1 inch, and crack‑routing dimensions shall be three‑quarters of an inch wide by one inch deep with sealant fill level one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the surface [2]. Full‑depth longitudinal concrete joint repair requires a QC plan at least two weeks before the start of repairs that follows Section 106.03(a)2.a, describing construction equipment, personnel and methods necessary to construct and test concrete, including testing frequencies and action points that trigger corrective measures [2]. When subbase placement exceeds 1,500 tons the user must prepare a Subbase Delivery Plan detailing construction equipment and methods that will place the material without harming the subgrade, assess the existing subgrade per Section 210 and correct any deficiencies before subbase placement, verify that a Class 4, Type A geotextile has been installed as required, specify aggregate layer thicknesses with each No. 2A layer to a minimum compacted depth of 3 inches and each No. OGS layer to a minimum compacted depth of 4 inches, ensure no subbase layer exceeds 8 inches (or 4 inches when granulated slag is used unless a 6‑inch maximum is permitted), and show that at least 1,500 linear feet of completed subbase will be maintained ahead of any succeeding pavement course [2]. Long‑life concrete pavement requires the user to provide the project‑specific design thickness (D) together with core depth measurements so that the average core depth can be checked against D minus 0.5 inch and each sublot’s individual depth compared to the same limit, supply compressive‑strength results required to calculate a performance weight factor that must not be less than 55 percent, provide plastic‑air‑content percentages from specimens taken in each sublot, ensure water‑to‑cementitious ratios derived from plant delivery tickets fall between 0.37 and 0.42 and are verified by AASHTO T 318 testing within ±0.03 of the ticket values, and report the area of pavement placed so that lots of 5,600 square yards and sublots of approximately 1,400 square yards can be defined for acceptance [2]. Preplacement requirements for asphalt demand a paving‑operation QC plan on Form CS‑413 specifying construction equipment, methods, mixture acceptance criteria, density acceptance limits and sample‑custody procedures before the pre‑construction conference, an asphalt preplacement meeting at least two weeks prior to any placement of asphalt mixtures, a written request for extended‑season paving at least fourteen calendar days in advance together with an Extended‑Season Paving Plan on Form CS‑413ES, and submission of Form CS‑413EQC within twenty‑four hours after the operation containing all measurements and documentation associated with that work; for recycled‑asphalt mixtures RAP/RAS mass measurements, drying temperatures and confirmation that aggregates are free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture must also be provided [2].

Section 2.1 GENERAL of the Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) assumes that a project’s scope shall be consistent with Federal pavement‑management policy and that each project presents unique conditions that must be evaluated using engineering judgment [1]. The Superpave Restricted Performance Specification is limited to overlay work only when the existing pavement is stable, properly leveled, sealed and patched, and when surface‑course thicknesses satisfy the minimum values in Table 9.5; plans must also identify State Route limits for the RPS portion [1]. Percent‑Within‑Limits density acceptance is required to consider base condition, with contractors expected to achieve densities near the middle of the target field range—for example 92.0 % to 98.5 % for Standard and RPS Wearing Courses [1]. Recycling provisions assume that at least fifteen percent of the estimated total milled material will be retained by the contractor, and if the total milled quantity is less than two hundred tons the entire amount is provided to the contractor [1].

Section 2.6 SHOULDERS assumes that every new construction or reconstruction project is classified by pavement type; rigid‑pavement projects shall receive Portland Cement Concrete shoulders while flexible‑pavement projects shall extend the pavement two feet into the shoulder area, except for inside shoulders on Interstate and Other Limited Access Freeways where this widening does not apply [1]. Shoulder material and design are further limited by road classification and anticipated conditions: full‑depth concrete shoulders are required on Interstates and other limited‑access freeways; Type 2 concrete shoulders are prescribed for arterials, collectors and local roads; and Type 1 (or its variants) is required when serious drainage problems exist, truck traffic exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150, or geometry anticipates shoulder use such as curves sharper than twelve degrees‑thirty minutes or specific intersection turning movements [1]. For collectors and local roads with flexible pavements either Type 3 shoulders of the same surface material or the Type 1 family may be provided [1].

Section 4.2 CONCRETE PAVEMENT PATCHING assumes that full‑depth patching is permissible only where the deteriorated slab can be completely removed; if a length of approximately five hundred feet requires intermittent patches leaving ten feet or less of original pavement between them, the entire section must be reconstructed [1]. Joint design is bounded by RC‑21M, which mandates thirty‑foot joint spacing with perpendicular joints and coated dowel bars, and by RC‑27M, which limits spacing to fifteen feet; longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only for concrete patches longer than sixty‑five feet [1]. The use of high early‑strength concrete should be avoided because of potential shrinkage cracking, and accelerated concrete may be used only when traffic opening is planned within four to seven hours and the mix meets the minimum compressive‑strength schedule in Publication 408 Section 501 [1]. Precast concrete paving shall not be selected where reasonable detour routes exist or where MPT is not a concern, and load‑transfer devices must be bonded along the full embedded length of dowels; any design for patches longer than five hundred feet on all lanes requires a pavement‑design analysis to verify slab thickness [1].

Section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION assumes that loss of support is caused by voids beneath the concrete, especially near transverse joints and corners, and limits treatment to restoring sub‑base support without correcting surface depressions, increasing structural capacity, or eliminating faulting; the procedure must be accompanied by joint and crack sealing and functional subsurface drains [1]. When no preliminary testing has been performed, at least twenty‑five percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints shall be stabilized, and traffic may reopen only after the specified cure times for each grout material have elapsed [1].

Section 8.13 CONTINUOUSLY REINFORCED CONCRETE PAVEMENT (CRCP) assumes that CRCP will be employed only in special cases; its structural design must follow the 1993 AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures using DARWin, and because Pub 72M no longer includes detailed construction specifications for CRCP, any CRCP project must contain special provisions in the contract documents and may proceed only after approval from the Director of BODD [1].

Section 8.15 SHOULDERS FOR JOINTED PLAIN CONCRETE PAVEMENT assumes that shoulder design reflects corridor classification and expected traffic conditions: urban Interstates/Expressways require full‑depth PCC shoulders with dowelled transverse joints, while rural routes may use tied concrete shoulders without dowels only when there is no need for work‑zone or incident‑management traffic support; if the two‑way ADT is less than ten thousand, widened slabs with asphalt or RCC shoulders become an allowable alternative [1]. When a full‑depth cement‑concrete shoulder is specified—either on urban interstates or rural high‑volume corridors anticipated to carry work‑zone traffic—the standard assumes that shoulder materials must match those of the mainline pavement from subgrade through surface, and it limits the use of dowelled joints to these defined situations [1].

The slip‑form concrete paving assumptions in Pub 408 require a fully energized, self‑propelled machine equipped with side forms; longitudinal joints shall be placed parallel to and within twelve inches of final pavement markings unless construction drawings specify otherwise, edge slump may not exceed one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and may not exceed one‑quarter inch between lanes and shoulders, and paving must be suspended after two hundred feet if these limits cannot be maintained [2]. During curing the concrete temperature change may not exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit within any one‑hour period [2]. Joint construction for plain or reinforced cement concrete assumes longitudinal joints are offset approximately six inches from the joint in the layer below, positioned near the pavement centreline or within twelve inches of lane lines on multi‑lane roads, and sealed with hot PG 64S‑22 binder kept between 265 °F and 320 °F; sealing shall not be applied when air temperature is below forty degrees Fahrenheit [2].

Plant‑mixed asphalt courses assume that non‑movement verification is permissible only for scratch or leveling courses less than one inch deep or weighing no more than 110 lb/yd², and only when paving strips are narrower than four feet or the total placement does not exceed five hundred tons; an optimum‑rolling pattern may be used under the same depth or weight limits on unstable or non‑uniform bases, or when the courses meet or exceed the minimum compacted depths listed in Table G [2]. Standard and RPS construction require pavement cores whenever material is placed at compacted depths greater than or equal to the Table G minima on stable, uniform bases; one core per sublot is required unless otherwise authorized, and the core must be drilled no later than the day following placement and tested according to PTM procedures [2]. Density measurements must be obtained with an approved nuclear or electrical‑impedance gauge operated by a licensed nuclear‑gauge operator, the gauge must be calibrated annually in accordance with ASTM D2950, and results are compared against daily theoretical maximum specific‑gravity values with acceptance of a plus‑minus 0.030 deviation [2].

Spreading and finishing assume that paving operations occur during daylight unless a waiver is granted, and mixtures must be placed within the temperature limits shown in Table A; when a course exceeds six inches in compacted depth it must be built in multiple lifts each between three and six inches, with no layer less than three inches or more than six inches [2]. The default lot size for asphalt work is two‑thousand‑five hundred tons divided into five five‑hundred‑ton sublots; short work stoppages of less than five calendar days do not trigger a new lot, whereas interruptions of five days or more require establishing a new lot [2]. Pattern segregation is deemed unacceptable when the macrotexture difference between non‑segregated and segregated areas exceeds 0.024 inch, and flushing is classified as defective when the macrotexture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 inch [2].

Safety edges for stone‑matrix asphalt (SMA) courses are required only when the SMA course is at least one‑and‑a‑half inches thick; they must be built with the same material as the roadway course and the finished edge slope shall lie between twenty‑six degrees and forty degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope, and may not be placed where curbs, sidewalks, guide rails or organic material directly overlie the edge [2]. For very small SMA placements (≤150 tons) mixture acceptance relies on certification rather than full sampling, and partially completed lots with two or fewer sublots are evaluated individually using single‑sample criteria for asphalt content, sieve passing and density, where density results may deviate no more than plus‑minus 2.0 % before payment is reduced [2]. Paving must be stopped if cores are not taken within one day, if density or asphalt‑content fails to meet tolerance for two consecutive lots (or three total), or if flushing, surface‑tolerance or depth defects occur, and only one loose mixture sample per sublot may be obtained unless expressly authorized [2].

Concrete‑pavement patching provisions assume that a quality‑control plan is prepared and approved before work begins and that appropriate equipment, trained personnel and hauling capacity are available to maintain continuous placement; excavated areas may not remain un‑patched for more than forty‑eight hours or over weekends or holidays, traffic on saw‑cut sections may be permitted for a maximum of seventy‑two hours, and saw cuts shall not exceed one‑half inch in width nor be less than full depth [2]. Surface tolerances are bounded by a limit of three‑sixteenths inch for high points or depressions, which must be ground out before opening to traffic, and the concrete must achieve at least one thousand psi compressive strength as verified by cylinder testing per PTM No. 604 [2].

Subgrade construction assumes that the top‑of‑subgrade elevation is already established and that fill materials will be placed and compacted in accordance with Section 206.3(b); when a completed subgrade has been in place for more than sixty days or at least one winter season it must be recompacted and proof rolled before a Class 4, Type A geotextile is installed over any lane width of eight feet or greater, and the geotextile shall extend only six to twelve inches from the interior edge of the pavement‑base drain [2]. Grade verification intervals are limited by equipment: manual string‑line checks may not exceed twenty‑five feet longitudinally, automatic grading machines may increase that interval to fifty feet, and GPS or laser‑controlled systems may extend it to five hundred feet provided the quality‑control plan in Section 210.3(d) is satisfied [2]. Surface irregularities greater than one‑half inch must be corrected, and acceptance testing tolerates no more than ten percent of random checks (every five hundred feet) exceeding that limit; if the tolerance is exceeded the Representative may require additional staking and string‑line verification [2].

Slab‑fracturing of existing concrete pavements assumes that sonic vibratory equipment will not cause subgrade liquefaction and requires replacement of any unsuitable subgrade at no cost; fracturing is limited to locations more than ten feet horizontally and vertically from culverts, cross pipes or utility sleeves, more than forty feet from bridge, tunnel or structure approaches, and more than three feet from fixed objects, with prohibited zones saw‑cut into full‑depth panels about five feet longitudinally by six feet transversely [2]. Equipment must be capable of variable impact force without extensive surface spalling, punching holes or shattering, and a minimum fifty‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller (four to seven wheels, inflation pressure ≥ 100 psi) must make at least three passes for Crack‑and‑Seat or five passes for Break‑and‑Seat; vibratory rollers are not allowed [2].

FWD testing shall not be performed when the pavement surface is frozen or when air temperature falls below forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or exceeds ninety degrees Fahrenheit, and a one‑thousand‑foot test section must achieve an average elastic modulus within the range specified in Table A; if it exceeds the upper limit the breaking pattern must be modified and retested [2].

Manual asphalt patching assumes that potholes or abrupt depressions three inches and less in depth are treated as Type A repairs, while deeper defects are classified as Type B; each repair must cover at least one square foot, preparation is limited to cutting vertically to sound, stable material, and after the surface is cleaned, dried and tack‑coated a Type A patch receives an asphalt wearing course compacted with a steel‑wheeled roller so that the finished surface matches the surrounding grade, while a Type B repair places a binder course in lifts not to exceed three inches uncompacted depth, each lift compacted with an approved mechanical tamper, followed by a topmost wearing course of one‑and‑a‑half inches that may be laid on the next day [2].

Concrete pavements are assumed to be placed in one‑ or two‑lane widths as shown on the Standard Drawings, and a one‑lane layout is permitted when two lanes would interfere with traffic [2]. Projects larger than fifteen thousand square yards of mainline paving are required to monitor aggregate gradation daily by sieve analysis, and the resulting plots must fall within the limits of the Combined Gradation Percent Retained Chart, allowing up to five percent deviation if authorized [2]. Weather‑related limitations include a prohibition on placing concrete when the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·hr unless mitigation measures such as windbreaks or fog misting are employed; cold‑weather placement must be discontinued when descending air temperature falls to forty degrees Fahrenheit (forty‑five degrees for accelerated‑strength concrete), and hot‑weather placement requires plastic‑concrete temperatures to remain between fifty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit, with operations halted if the temperature exceeds ninety degrees Fahrenheit [2]. Formwork is limited to steel forms at least ten feet long that may not deflect more than 0.01 inch under a 1,700‑lb center load within a four‑foot span and must not exceed an out‑of‑plane tolerance of one‑eighth inch in ten feet; wood forms are allowed only on curves with radius less than one hundred fifty feet [2]. The quality‑control plan must be submitted at least two weeks before paving begins and include detailed procedures for subgrade control, joint construction, curing and vibration; vibrators are required to operate at a frequency of not less than one hundred impulses per second, and manual operation is limited to five seconds in one spot [2].

Diamond‑grinding assumes that the entire lane width shall be ground unless a waiver is granted, and defective ride‑quality lots require removal of at least ten feet of pavement between transverse joints or an entire plain‑concrete panel before replacement; defective work is identified when the measured roughness index exceeds the maximum in Table A, when a bump larger than one‑quarter inch on a twelve‑foot straightedge exists, or when adjacent ridges or valleys exceed one‑eighth inch, and verification MRI may not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than six‑point‑one inches per mile on at least ninety percent of the 528‑foot lots for each lane, nor may the average exceed that same margin [2].

Cross‑stitching assumes work can only proceed when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at least forty degrees Fahrenheit, is limited to longitudinal joints or cracks after a full‑depth saw cut has been made along the joint, requires drill holes no more than three‑eighth inch larger than the tie bar, mandates that holes be cleaned and dry before epoxy injection and tie‑bar placement, allows sealing only after the joint is routed, flushed and completely dry under the same forty‑degree limit, and does not permit traffic to resume until the anchoring material has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations [2].

Longitudinal concrete joint repair assumes that a quality‑control plan will be submitted at least two weeks before repairs begin and that all required equipment, trained personnel and hauling units will be in place prior to concrete placement; traffic on saw‑cut repair areas is permitted for a maximum of seventy‑two hours, saw cuts greater than one‑half inch in width may not be opened to traffic, excavated areas may not remain un‑patched for more than forty‑eight hours or over weekends or holidays, any subbase disturbance deeper than one inch must be removed and replaced with concrete during paving, and all original joints shall be resealed as specified [2].

Subbase construction assumes the subgrade has been prepared and any deficiencies corrected as required in Section 210 before subbase work begins, limits placement to conditions where the ground is not soft, muddy or frozen, requires a Subbase Delivery Plan for quantities greater than one‑thousand‑five hundred tons, mandates that moisture be sufficient to prevent segregation throughout stockpiling, hauling and compaction, bounds layer thickness to a maximum compacted depth of eight inches but limits each layer to no more than four inches when granulated slag is used (with an optional increase to six inches if the full layer is compacted per Section 206.3(b)), requires each aggregate course to meet minimum compacted depths of three inches for No. 2A and four inches for No. OGS, declares a depth shortfall of one‑half inch or more defective, requiring scarification, addition of material, recompaction and verification [2].

Long‑life concrete pavement acceptance assumes pavement is placed in contiguous panels of constant thickness; a lot is defined as exactly five‑thousand‑six hundred square yards consisting of four sublots of one‑thousand‑four hundred square yards each, with partial areas combined with the preceding lot only when less than four‑thousand‑two hundred square yards remain, otherwise forming a separate lot, and no lot may contain mixed depths [2]. Depth compliance is limited to an average core depth greater than the design thickness minus half an inch while allowing at most one sublot core that falls below that same tolerance; compressive‑strength acceptance is bounded by a quality‑index lower limit of fifty‑five percent PWL calculated from the sublot test results, and the water‑to‑cement‑ratio incentive applies only to concrete delivered in dump or agitator trucks with a target ratio between 0.37 and 0.42; verification requires the ticket‑derived ratio to agree within plus‑minus 0.03 of an AASHTO T 318 test performed no later than forty‑five minutes after water contact, and any deviation disqualifies the lot from the incentive [2]. Edge slumping, surface defects, honeycombing or spalling that cannot be satisfactorily repaired are treated as defective work and require removal of a full panel rather than spot repairs [2].

Asphalt paving may only be placed within prescribed seasonal windows; for PG 64E‑22 wearing courses, >10 million ESALs wearing courses, 4.75 mm wearing courses or other wearing courses placed at compacted depths less than one‑and‑a‑half inches the allowable period is April 1 to October 15, while all other courses may be placed April 1‑October 31, and placement must not occur when surfaces are wet or when air or surface temperature is forty degrees Fahrenheit or lower [2]. Extended‑season work requires a written request submitted at least fourteen calendar days in advance and an Extended‑Season Paving Plan; the extended dates for those same wearing courses are April 1‑November 15 and for all other courses March 1‑December 15 [2]. Density acceptance for mixtures placed at the minimum compacted depths in Table G must be verified by pavement cores regardless of quantity, and mandatory pre‑placement quality‑control plans and meetings are required (QC plan on Form CS‑413 and a meeting at least two weeks before paving) [2]. When paving length exceeds one‑thousand‑five hundred feet a Material Transfer Vehicle must be used, plants must be fully automated as listed in Bulletin 41, haul trucks must be sealed with insulation or heating when air temperature is below fifty degrees Fahrenheit from October 1 to April 30, and temperature checks are performed via a three‑eighth inch hole in the truck body [2].

Next‑generation concrete surface grinding assumes a self‑propelled machine weighing at least forty‑thousand pounds equipped with a diamond blade head at least four feet wide; joint differentials are limited to a maximum of one‑sixteenth inch, slope depressions or misalignment greater than one‑eighth inch over twelve feet measured perpendicular to the centreline are prohibited, feather passes must extend at least two feet onto shoulders with a minimum length of one hundred feet, and overlap between successive passes may not exceed one inch [2]. Single‑pass texture grooves must be one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths inch deep, spaced about one‑half inch centre‑to‑centre, with groove blades one‑quarter to three‑eighth inch taller than the grinding blades; two‑pass texture follows the same depth and spacing limits [2]. The final surface shall have at least ninety‑eight percent of the pavement textured, ridges not exceeding one‑eighth inch, and ride quality is limited to a maximum IRI of seventy in/mile for posted speeds greater than forty‑five miles per hour or ninety in/mile for speeds less than or equal to forty‑five miles per hour; lot lengths are defined as five hundred twenty‑eight feet excluding bridges, ramps shorter than one‑thousand‑five hundred feet, tapered sections narrower than twelve feet, partial lots under one hundred feet, shoulders and medians, and a three‑hundred‑foot test section must be approved before full work proceeds [2].

General plant‑mixing assumptions include that a fully accepted processing and mixing plant is available, proportioning devices are calibrated, mass‑proportioning bins and automatic cutoff prevent loss of any component, aggregates are stockpiled without segregation and spread with adjustable self‑propelled spreaders; construction is limited to conditions where the subbase or subgrade has been prepared at least one‑thousand‑five hundred feet ahead of the leading spreader, base layers thicker than six inches must be built in two or more lifts each between three and six inches, placement is prohibited on frozen surfaces, when air temperature is below forty degrees Fahrenheit, or when freezing is forecast within the next twenty‑four hours [2]. Compaction must achieve not less than one hundred percent of maximum density as defined by PTM No. 106 Method B with field verification by AASHTO T 191, and surface tolerances are checked at intervals no more than twenty‑five feet, requiring correction of any irregularities exceeding one‑half inch [2].

Method and workflow

The first step in applying the Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) is to identify whether a project is new construction or reconstruction and then classify its pavement structure; rigid structures require Portland Cement Concrete shoulders while flexible structures are built two feet wider into the shoulder area [1]. For flexible pavements the appropriate shoulder type is selected based on functional classification, traffic volume, drainage concerns and anticipated use: full‑depth concrete shoulders are required for Interstates and other limited‑access freeways, Type 2 concrete shoulders for arterials, collectors and local roads, Type 1, 1‑F, 1‑S or 1‑SP shoulders adjacent to flexible pavements when the road is an Interstate or arterial, drainage problems are serious, truck Design Hourly Volume exceeds 150, or shoulder use is expected at tight‑radius intersections; Type 3 shoulders may be used on collectors and local roads when the same surface material as the main pavement is desired [1]. When the project is an urban Interstate or Expressway full‑depth plain cement concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints are constructed exactly as shown in Pub 72M RC‑25M, and all such projects must use tied concrete shoulders; for a rural Interstate or Expressway where shoulder use for work‑zone traffic control or incident management on a high‑volume corridor is not anticipated, tied shoulders with undowelled transverse joints using jointed plain concrete pavement are placed, while corridors with two‑way ADT less than 10 000 may consider widened slabs with asphalt or RCC shoulders [1]. If concrete pavement patching is required the designer first determines the patch boundaries so that all deteriorated pavement is removed; when the affected length approaches ±500 feet a full‑width reconstruction may be required instead of intermittent patches [1]. The patch is then designed in accordance with Pub 72M RC‑26M, selecting either skewed or non‑skewed new‑pavement joints and applying joint spacing dictated by the chosen pavement type—RC‑21M calls for 30‑foot spacing with perpendicular joints, while RC‑27M calls for 15‑foot spacing—and dowel bars are installed that are bonded along their entire embedded length with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders; any patch longer than 65 feet must also include longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts, and high early‑strength concrete should be avoided unless accelerated concrete meeting the minimum compressive strength in four to seven hours per Publication 408 Section 501 is required for an early traffic opening [1]. Slab stabilization begins with identifying loss‑of‑support signs such as transverse joint faulting or corner breaks, then selecting a grout material—cement, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane—based on site conditions; holes are drilled according to the number and pattern specified in RC‑26M using an estimate of one cubic foot of cement grout per hole (0.25 bag), 30–50 gallons of hot asphalt per hole, or 0.4 pounds of polyurethane per square foot of slab, after which the chosen material is pumped, traffic and crew are protected during pumping, and the slab remains closed to traffic until the material cures—12 hours for cement grout, a minimum of 30 minutes for hot‑asphalt cooling, or a minimum of 30 minutes for polyurethane expansion [1]. After stabilization joints are sealed and subsurface drains maintained, at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints are stabilized when no preliminary testing has been performed [1]. Subgrade work proceeds by forming the roadbed to the established top‑of‑subgrade elevation and compacting as specified in Section 206.3(b); fill material is then placed and compacted to that same elevation while proof‑rolling existing or cut‑area subgrades as required, sealing all constructed or disturbed surfaces each day with a static‑mode smooth‑drum roller, and after the subgrade has remained in place for more than sixty days or at least one winter season it is re‑compacted and proof‑rolled before installing the Class 4, Type A geotextile separator as specified [2]. Grade and slope of the finished surface are checked with a taut string line at longitudinal intervals of not more than twenty‑five feet (or up to fifty feet or five hundred feet when automatic GPS/laser grading is used) and any irregularity exceeding one‑half inch is corrected by loosening the surface and removing or adding material as required; when GPS or laser equipment is employed a machine‑control grading QC plan must be submitted at least one week before the preconstruction conference, construction stakes are set at five hundred‑foot intervals (with a minimum of two on each ramp), offsets are tabulated on Form D‑413 and provided to the Representative, the control system is calibrated daily, and the finished surface is tested at all hinge points and random locations every five hundred feet, with any area exceeding one‑half inch corrected and if more than ten percent of acceptance tests exceed that limit the Representative may require additional stakes and string‑line checks before subgrade acceptance [2]. Before any rigid pavement work begins a comprehensive paving operation QC plan must be submitted at least two weeks before paving starts; the plan shall detail personnel responsibilities, sampling frequencies, methods for determining traffic strength and pavement depth, weather‑related procedures, subgrade control, joint construction, curing, and testing [2]. To apply Pub 72M using the slip‑form paving method the contractor first mobilizes a fully energized self‑propelled slip‑form machine equipped with side forms, automatic alignment and grade controls, and vibrating/tamping capability; the pavement line and grade are then set out so that longitudinal joints are parallel to and within twelve inches of final markings, concrete is placed continuously while coordinating mixing, delivery and spreading, and if the paver stops the vibration and tamping elements are immediately disengaged [2]. Before the concrete hardens edge slumping is tested with a twelve‑foot straightedge for no more than one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes or one‑quarter inch between lane and shoulder over the six‑inch edge zone, and work is suspended after two hundred feet if limits cannot be controlled; load‑transfer units are staked, dowel locations marked, transverse and longitudinal joints are sawed and cured as specified, and final strike‑off, consolidation, floating, straightedge correction, texturing and curing are performed according to subsections (k) through (l), ensuring surface tolerances of three‑sixteenths inch and macrotexture MTD ≥ 0.040 inch [2]. Traffic may be opened only after the concrete attains the minimum required compressive strength of one thousand pounds per square inch as measured by compressive strength testing of cylinders according to PTM 604, or after cement grout cures for twelve hours when slab stabilization has been performed [1][2]. Throughout the process the contractor must monitor aggregate gradations for projects exceeding fifteen thousand square yards of mainline concrete by performing daily sieve analysis and verifying that the results plot within the limits of the Combined Gradation Percent Retained Chart per PTM 529, and all QC plans referenced in Section 106.03 must be submitted at least fourteen days before any field activity begins [2].

Criteria, outputs, and interpretation

In the general provisions (section 2.1) the Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) reference the standard drawing set RC‑26M as defining the detailed geometry and installation of dowel bar retrofits and cross‑stitching for concrete pavements, indicating how load‑transfer devices are placed across transverse cracks or joints [1]. The shoulder provisions (section 2.6) classify shoulder treatments by pavement type and roadway function: any new construction or reconstruction project having a rigid pavement structure shall have Portland Cement Concrete shoulders provided, while on any new construction or reconstruction project having a flexible pavement the pavement shall be constructed two feet wider into the shoulder area before applying the designated shoulder type [1]. Concrete full‑depth shoulders are mandated on Interstate and other limited‑access freeways; Type 2 concrete shoulders are required on arterials, collectors and local roads; and Type 1 (including 1‑F, 1‑S, 1‑SP) shoulders are prescribed adjacent to flexible pavements on all Interstates, arterials, on roadways with serious drainage problems, on two‑lane roads where the Design Hourly Volume of truck traffic exceeds 150, or where anticipated vehicle movements require shoulder use such as tight‑radius ramp intersections, curves sharper than 12°‑30′, and left‑turn situations that start 250 ft before an intersection and stop 150 ft downstream [1]. For collectors and local roads the standard also permits Type 3 shoulders of the same surface material as the pavement or the use of the aforementioned Type 1 variants, and bicycle‑friendly shoulder types (Types 1‑F, 1‑S, 1‑SP, 6‑F, 6‑S, 6‑SP) may be provided at the District Executive’s discretion [1]. In the concrete pavement patching provisions (section 4.2) full‑depth concrete patches shall follow RC‑26M and designers shall select either skewed or non‑skewed new pavement joint items according to the basket type; if a roadway segment of approximately ±500 feet requires intermittent patches leaving ten feet or less of original pavement between them, the pavement section should be removed and reconstructed for its entire length [1]. When reconstructing all adjacent traffic lanes for 500 feet or more with Portland cement concrete the pavement must be reconstructed according to Publication 72M RC‑21M for jointed reinforced concrete pavement (which requires thirty‑foot joint spacing, perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals) or RC‑27M for jointed plain concrete pavement (which requires fifteen‑foot joint spacing, perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals) [1]. When only one lane is rebuilt the new joints must align with existing joints and an intermediate joint is required if existing spacing exceeds twenty feet [1]. Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are only required in concrete patches more than 65 feet long [1]. Accelerated concrete pavement patching provides a concrete patch that can be opened to traffic as early as four to seven hours after placement, whereas standard concrete patches achieve opening‑to‑traffic strength in twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours [1]. Precast slabs must not exceed one lane width and shall be six feet minimum up to fifteen feet maximum (twenty feet for prestressed); transverse joints shall be aligned to adjacent lanes and the delivered elevation shall be at least one‑quarter inch higher; prestressed units may reduce slab thickness by up to four inches provided the final thickness does not fall below eight inches [1]. In the slab stabilization provisions (section 4.5) when no preliminary testing has been performed at least twenty‑five percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints shall be stabilized; material quantities are estimated as one cubic foot of cement grout per hole (0.25 bag of cement), typically between thirty and fifty gallons of hot asphalt per hole, and an estimate of 0.4 pounds of high‑density polyurethane per square foot of slab [1]. Stabilization does not correct surface depressions or increase the pavement’s design structural capacity [1]. Continuously reinforced concrete pavement (CRCP) may be used only in special cases with approval of the Director, BODD; its structural design shall follow the 1993 AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures using DARWin, and because construction details have been deleted from Pub 72M and Publication 408, contracts must contain special provisions [1]. For shoulders of jointed plain concrete pavement (section 8.15) full‑depth PCC shoulders with dowelled transverse joints are required for urban Interstates/Expressways and for rural routes where the shoulder must support traffic during incident management; rural routes without such need may use tied shoulders with undowelled joints, and when the two‑way ADT is less than 10 000 widened slabs with asphalt or RCC shoulders may be considered [1]. When full‑depth cement concrete shoulders are used, all materials of the mainline pavement structure from subgrade to surface must match, and the appropriate specifications from Publication 408 (Sections 501/506 for dowelled shoulders, Section 658 for non‑dowelled/RCC shoulders) shall be applied [1]. Slip‑form placement of plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 501.3(g)) requires a fully energized self‑propelled paving machine equipped with side forms, automatic alignment and grade controls, and vibrating or tamping across the full width; edge slumping must be measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge before the concrete loses plasticity and may not exceed one eighth inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and one quarter inch between lanes and shoulders, and if this limit cannot be kept within any two hundred foot segment paving must be stopped for evaluation [2]. Transverse joint dowel placement is limited to a maximum vertical tilt or horizontal skew of 0.25 inch at each end, longitudinal translation no more than one inch, and horizontal translation no more than one inch; tie‑bars selected by the Representative must pass a pull‑out test after the concrete reaches 3,500 psi using a hydraulic jack rated to 12,000 pounds or a 1/32‑inch slip and must meet the resistance values in Table B [2]. Final surface tolerances are limited to three sixteenth inch deviation when measured with a straightedge parallel or perpendicular to the centreline; depressions greater than three sixteenth inch between lanes are defective, macrotexture must achieve a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 inch (ASTM E965) and any shortfall requires grooving to specified dimensions [2]. Curing must begin within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and temperature changes exceeding forty degrees Fahrenheit in one hour render the work defective [2]. Concrete may be placed in one or two traffic lane widths as shown on the Standard Drawings, using one‑lane construction when two lanes would interfere with traffic, and for projects exceeding fifteen thousand square yards of mainline paving a daily sieve analysis must verify that the combined gradation percent retained stays within the limits of PTM No. 529 with deviations of less than five percent allowed if authorized by the DME/DMM [2]. Concrete may not be placed when the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 pounds per square foot per hour unless windbreaks or fog misting are used; cold‑weather work must stop when air temperature away from artificial heat falls to forty degrees Fahrenheit (forty‑five for accelerated strength) and resume only above those values, while hot‑weather placement must be halted if plastic concrete temperature exceeds ninety degrees Fahrenheit after mixing and the mix water or aggregates must be cooled to keep the temperature between fifty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit [2]. Steel forms must be at least ten feet long and may not deflect more than one hundredth inch under a 1,700 pound centre load over a four‑foot span; wood forms for curves under one hundred fifty foot radius may be used only with one‑inch seasoned planks braced by steel pins spaced no greater than twenty‑four inches, shims may not exceed one inch in height, and vibrators must deliver at least one hundred impulses per second with manual vibrators not applied to one spot for more than five seconds [2]. For concrete pavement patching (Section 516.3) a quality‑control plan must be prepared describing action points for all construction phases; excavated patch areas may not remain un‑patched for more than forty‑eight hours and, if they stay open longer adjacent to traffic, suitable material must be placed to grade and compact to non‑movement; saw cuts opened to traffic are limited to a maximum width of one half inch and may be exposed for no more than seventy‑two hours; dowel bar anchoring is acceptable only when the summed voids around the perimeter of each inspected core are less than 0.5 inch, surface tolerances require any high point or depression of three sixteenth inch or greater to be ground out at no additional cost, and pavement may be opened to traffic only after concrete reaches a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi as measured by cylinder testing [2]. Subgrade work (Section 210.3) must form the subgrade to the established top‑of‑subgrade elevation and compact it as specified; when a subgrade has remained in place for more than sixty days or at least one winter season it must be re‑compacted and proof‑rolled before installing a Class 4, Type A geotextile over lane widths of eight feet or greater, the geotextile extending transversely between six inches and twelve inches from the interior edge of the pavement base drain [2]. Surface grade and slope are to be checked at transverse intervals not exceeding twenty‑five feet using a taut string line and longitudinally at intervals not more than twenty‑five feet, although automatic grading machines may increase that interval to fifty feet and GPS or laser‑controlled equipment may increase it to five hundred feet provided the QC plan requirements are met; any irregularities greater than one half inch must be corrected by loosening, adding or removing material and re‑compacting [2]. Construction stakes for machine control shall be placed at five hundred foot intervals along tangents and radial offsets on curves with a minimum of two stakes on each ramp, intersecting roadway, and on projects shorter than one thousand feet, and the Representative will perform acceptance checks at random points every five hundred feet, requiring correction of any exceedance over one half inch and may require additional staking if more than ten percent of those tests exceed that limit [2]. For plant‑mixed asphalt courses (Section 413.3) the Department will accept mat density according to one of the levels in Table F and may use either non‑movement or optimum‑rolling pattern criteria; non‑movement acceptance is permitted for scratch or leveling courses less than one inch deep or ≤110 pounds per square yard, for paving or patching areas less than four feet wide, and for small placements not exceeding five hundred tons, as well as for mixtures on unstable bases deemed non‑critical [2]. Optimum‑rolling pattern acceptance may be used for the same small‑quantity limits, for courses greater than or equal to one inch deep or ≥110 pounds per square yard, for mixtures placed at depths below the minimum in Table G, and requires density measurement with an approved nuclear or electrical impedance gauge operated by a licensed operator and documented on Form TR‑4276B/C [2]. Pavement cores are required for RPS construction and for standard extended‑season paving unless waived; cores must be taken within one day from placement at compacted depths greater than or equal to the minimum in Table G, tested for bulk specific gravity and density, and if cores are not taken within that time or two consecutive lots fail to meet a density payment factor of 100 percent paving must be stopped [2]. Asphalt mixtures must be placed in daylight at the laying temperatures listed in Table A and may not contain cold lumps, segregation, or an asphalt content outside the specified limits; any course deeper than six inches compacted must be built in two or more layers each between three and six inches, with isolated deep areas treated by a scratch or leveling layer before full‑depth paving [2]. The Safety Edge depth shall be at least 1.5 inches but not exceed five inches and after compaction its slope must fall between twenty‑six degrees and forty degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope; daily angle checks are required and paving must stop if the range is not met (Section 419) [2]. Lot acceptance follows a classification based on tonnage: for jobs of two thousand five hundred tons or more a normal lot is two thousand five hundred tons divided into five five hundred‑ton sublots unless operational conditions or project size dictate otherwise, while partially completed lots consisting of two sublots or fewer are evaluated individually against the single‑sample tolerances in Table A for asphalt content and percent passing the No. 200 sieve and against Table E density limits, receiving one hundred percent payment if both criteria are met, ninety percent if density is within plus or minus two point zero percent of limits, or being deemed defective otherwise (Section 419) [2]. Pattern segregation is considered unacceptable when the macro‑texture depth difference exceeds 0.024 inch, and flushing is declared defective when average texture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 inch; both conditions trigger immediate suspension of paving, corrective action plans, and test sections not exceeding two hundred tons before work may resume [2]. Manual asphalt patching distinguishes two types based on pothole depth: Type A applies to depressions three inches or less and requires each patch be at least one square foot, vertical edge cuts, removal of loose material, drying, a tack coat on the bottom and vertical surfaces before placing the wearing‑course asphalt that is compacted with a steel‑wheeled roller until the surface matches the surrounding grade and all excavated areas are filled before the end of the workday; Type B covers potholes deeper than three inches and after the same preparation an asphalt binder course is placed in lifts not to exceed three inches uncompacted depth, each lift leveled and compacted with an approved mechanical tamper, followed by a topmost wearing‑course layer of one and a half inches finished with a steel‑wheeled roller to achieve grade conformity (Section 450) [2]. Slab fracturing of existing concrete pavements (Section 522.3) requires submission of a pavement fracturing plan that must include equipment capable of variable force and a minimum one thousand foot test section to establish the optimal fracture pattern; pre‑fracturing operations mandate removal of overlying asphalt, sawcutting slabs no longer than twenty feet with depth plus or minus one inch of two thirds slab thickness, and prohibit fracturing within ten feet horizontally and ten feet from the bottom of culverts or utility sleeves, within forty feet of bridge approaches, and within three feet of fixed objects [2]. Seating must be performed with a pneumatic tire roller of at least fifty tons, having four to seven wheels, tires inflated to at least one hundred psi, providing at least three passes for Crack‑and‑Seat or five passes for Break‑and‑Seat methods while avoiding vibratory rollers [2]. The standard also governs cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303.3) where water must be sprinkled thoroughly on rubblized pavement or subbase when needed to obtain a moist condition before placing the CTPBC; compaction shall be performed with steel‑wheel power rollers certified at eight tons to ten tons and a vibratory paver screed alone is not allowed, and at least one roller pass must be completed within one hour of adding water to the aggregate and cement (or 1½ hours when a retarding admixture is used) [2]. Irregularities greater than one half inch are corrected by loosening the surface mixture and adding or removing CTPBC; curing begins immediately after compaction using a white membrane forming compound applied at a rate of one gallon per 100 sq ft ±10 percent or by covering with white polyethylene, which must be maintained for twenty‑four hours and extended another twenty‑four hours if temperature falls below forty degrees Fahrenheit, and replacement of the curing cover is required at no cost if temperature drops to thirty‑two degrees Fahrenheit within seventy‑two hours of placement [2]. Construction traffic is not allowed on the CTPBC for at least three days after placement, after which only trucks and equipment needed for the next layer may be permitted and any damaged areas must be removed and replaced at no cost (Section 303) [2]. For unbonded concrete overlay (Section 548) surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch shall be filled with approved patch material, geotextile interlayers must overlap edges by eight inches ± two inches, may not exceed three layers in any location, and must extend at least four inches beyond the pavement edge; joint faulting greater than one quarter inch triggers placement of geotextile, while faulting exceeding one half inch requires a scratch course or milling, and dowel basket movement is limited to one quarter inch before anchoring is approved [2]. Panel dimensions are governed by an aspect‑ratio limit of 1.5 and a maximum size not to exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness; saw‑cut depths shall be at least D/3 within twelve inches of edges and at least D/4 elsewhere, and curing compound must be applied at a minimum rate of one gallon ±0.1 gallon per 150 sq ft for pavements over six inches (or per 125 sq ft for ≤6 in), with temperature changes during insulation removal not exceeding forty degrees Fahrenheit in any one‑hour period [2]. Paved shoulders (Section 654) require removal of surplus material down to the bottom of the proposed base course or a directed depth, scarifying may be limited to a maximum depth of four inches below existing grade or to the directed depth, and shaping and compacting bring all areas to the bottom elevation of the proposed base course using aggregate base material when needed; the material is considered satisfactorily compacted when it does not move under the compaction equipment, after which asphalt prime coat and surface treatment are applied [2]. Continuous reinforced concrete pavement patching (Section 518) mandates that saw‑cuts shall not be located within twenty‑four inches of an existing crack and that a secondary transverse cut at each end of the patch area must be at least one inch deep; wheel‑saw penetration into the subbase is limited to a maximum of one half inch, jackhammer size is limited to thirty pounds maximum, and any break back exceeding two inches requires a new full‑depth transverse cut [2]. Evaluation of concrete pavement ride quality (Section 507) classifies a lot as defective if its measured roughness index exceeds the maximum acceptable MRI listed in Table A, if any individual bump measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge is greater than one quarter inch, or if the adjacent surface shows a ridge or valley larger than one eighth inch; such defects require removal of at least ten feet of pavement between transverse joints and replacement before retesting [2]. Incentive payment is allowed only when verification MRI does not exceed acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile for at least ninety percent of the 528‑foot lots in each lane, when the average verification MRI does not exceed the average acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches/mile for all lots tested in a lane, and when the total length difference among all tested lots in a lane is less than 0.2 percent [2]. Concrete pavement cross‑stitching (Section 528) requires a QC Plan submitted at least fourteen days before work begins; stitching may only proceed when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are forty degrees Fahrenheit or higher, is prohibited on transverse joints or cracks, and the practice of moving drifted slabs back against adjacent slabs is forbidden. Before stitching longitudinal joints a full‑depth saw cut must be made along the entire slab length, drill holes must not exceed three eighths inch larger than the tie bar and may not extend through the slab bottom, compressed‑air cleaning must use a stream of at least one hundred psi free of contaminants, tie bars are rotated three to five times during insertion and must receive one inch of epoxy coverage at the surface, and sealing requires routing the joint to three quarters inch wide by one inch deep, flushing with water, cleaning until dry, and placing sealant only when temperatures are forty degrees Fahrenheit or above, filling to a level one eighth‑to‑quarter inch below the surface without runoff. Traffic may not be allowed until the anchoring material has fully cured per manufacturer recommendations, and any slab with holes that penetrated the bottom must be patched at no extra cost (Section 528) [2]. Full‑depth longitudinal concrete joint repair (Section 519) requires a QC Plan submitted at least two weeks before repairs begin; after saw cutting the existing pavement traffic may be allowed on repaired areas for a maximum of seventy‑two hours and saw cuts wider than one half inch must not be opened to traffic; excavated repair areas must be patched within forty‑eight hours and cannot remain un‑patched over weekends or holidays. Full‑depth transverse saw cuts at the repair limits may extend into adjacent pavement up to full depth + 2 inches provided dowel bars in the adjacent lane are not damaged, and any subbase surface irregularities exceeding one inch in depth must be corrected by loosening, adding or removing material and compacting to proper grade [2]. Subbase (Section 350) quantities exceeding one thousand five hundred tons must be documented in a Subbase Delivery Plan for Representative review before work begins; the subgrade shall be prepared as specified with no placement on soft, muddy or frozen areas. Aggregate layers must meet minimum compacted depths of three inches for No. 2A material and four inches for No. OGS material, while individual subbase lifts may not exceed eight inches in compacted depth (limited to four inches when granulated slag is used, or up to six inches only if the full layer depth is compacted as specified). The subbase is required to be completed and maintained at least one thousand five hundred linear feet in advance of the succeeding pavement course; compaction must achieve a non‑movement condition with overlapping passes, and depth testing declares the subbase defective when the measured depth is short by one half inch or more, triggering scarification, blending, recompaction and verification [2]. Long‑life concrete pavement (Section 530) classifies a lot as defective when its average core depth falls below the design thickness minus 0.5 inch or any individual sublot core depth does so, and also when the percent passing the lower quality index for compressive strength is less than fifty‑five percent; acceptance requires that the average core depth be more than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch with at most one sublot below that limit, that the 28‑day compressive strength PWL be at least fifty‑five percent, and that concrete be placed with a water‑to‑cementitious ratio between 0.37 and 0.42 without added water or finishing aids [2]. Verification of the water‑to‑cement ratio requires the ticket‑derived ratio to be within ±0.03 of the AASHTO T 318 test result; otherwise the lot is not considered verified and no incentive is paid [2].

Authority and technical basis

In section 2.6 Shoulders the publication is referenced to define shoulder types, construction details and standard rumble‑strip designs, indicating that in that context it functions as a reference manual providing agency guidance rather than a governing specification or test method. In sections 4.2 Concrete Pavement Patching and 4.5 Slab Stabilization the work must be performed “in accordance with Publication 72M…”, thereby treating the source as a governing specification for design and construction procedures. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications describe Pub 72M throughout its many sections as imposing mandatory construction limits and quality‑control procedures for both rigid and flexible pavements, which makes it operate as a governing specification. At the same time the same document contains procedural guidance that the agency uses as an internal manual—for example directing preparation of QC plans on Form CS‑413, requiring certification from mixture producers, and outlining internal shop‑drawing review processes—showing that portions serve as an agency manual describing departmental procedures. Full‑depth longitudinal concrete joint repair also requires a QC Plan submitted at least two weeks before work; saw cutting must use a diamond‑tipped blade with water cooling, cuts opened to traffic may not exceed one‑half inch in width, and traffic may be permitted on repaired sections for a maximum of seventy‑two hours (source: Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2020 Specifications) [2]. Excavated repair areas may not remain un‑patched for more than forty‑eight hours or over weekends or holidays, drop or hydro‑hammers are prohibited, and subbase recompaction is required if disturbance exceeds one inch (source: Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2020 Specifications) [2]. Subbase construction for quantities greater than 1,500 tons requires a Subbase Delivery Plan; each layer of No. 2A aggregate must be placed to a minimum compacted depth of three inches and each layer of No. OGS aggregate to a minimum of four inches, layers may not exceed eight inches in compacted depth (or four inches when granulated slag is used), compaction proceeds from the sides toward the center with uniformly overlapping passes, and after each density test a core hole must be dug to the full depth of the completed subbase; if the measured depth is deficient by one‑half inch or more the subbase is considered defective and must be scarified, blended with additional material and recompacted (source: Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2020 Specifications) [2]. Long‑Life Concrete Pavement acceptance criteria require each lot to be evaluated for core depth, compressive strength, plastic air content and water‑cement ratio; a lot is defective if the average core depth is less than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch or if the percent quality index (PWL) for compressive strength is less than fifty‑five percent (source: Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2020 Specifications) [2]. Compressive strength specimens are prepared per PTM No. 604, cured according to Subsection 12.1 of PTM No. 611, and plastic air content is verified by sampling locations selected under PTM No. 1; the water‑to‑cement ratio incentive applies only to concrete mixed in dump or agitator trucks, requires a measured W/C ratio between 0.37 and 0.42, and must be verified by AASHTO T318 within ± 0.03 of the ratio computed from plant delivery tickets using an approved electronic meter for water content and the cementitious weights printed on the delivery slip (source: Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2020 Specifications) [2]. Asphalt paving operations must be governed by a paving operation QC Plan prepared on Form CS‑413 and reviewed before any paving, accompanied by a preplacement meeting to confirm specifications, sequence, mixture acceptance and density criteria; seasonal limits allow paving from April 1 to October 15 (or to November 15 when an approved Extended‑Season Paving Plan is in place), and placement may not occur when air or surface temperature is 40 °F or lower (source: Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2020 Specifications) [2]. Density acceptance will be by pavement cores for mixes placed at the minimum compacted depths in Table G, otherwise by the optimum‑rolling pattern; plant procedures must measure RAP/RAS mass, dry virgin aggregate and keep mixture temperatures within Table A, hauling shall use tightly sealed insulated trucks with a three‑eighths inch temperature check hole, and a Material Transfer Vehicle shall be utilized when paving length exceeds 1,500 linear feet (source: Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2020 Specifications) [2].

Practical application and case studies

In concrete pavement rehabilitation projects the need for a dowel‑bar retrofit is identified when transverse cracks are active, and the design and installation follow the standard drawings in Publication 72M RC‑26M to restore load transfer across the slab[1]. When longitudinal cracks or joints require stabilization, cross‑stitching is performed by placing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle according to Publication 72M RC‑26M, a procedure also referenced in Publication 408 Section 528[1][2]. For any new construction or reconstruction project that uses a rigid pavement the standards shall provide full‑depth Portland cement concrete shoulders, while for flexible pavements the roadway is widened two feet into the shoulder and the remaining width is built with the designated shoulder type; thus Interstates and other limited‑access freeways receive full‑depth concrete shoulders and arterials, collectors and local roads receive Type 2 concrete shoulders[1]. When a flexible pavement is used the publication directs that Type 1 (or its variants 1‑F, 1‑S, 1‑SP) shoulders shall be provided on all freeways and arterials, on roadways where serious drainage problems are anticipated, or on two‑lane roads where truck traffic exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150, thereby ensuring smooth surfaces for bicyclists and accommodating expected shoulder use[1]. Additional criteria trigger the use of these higher‑type shoulders near intersections: if curves are sharper than 12°‑30′, or if left‑turning vehicles are anticipated to force through traffic onto the right shoulder, the pavement structure may be extended into the shoulder starting 250 ft before the intersection and ending 150 ft downstream[1]. When a deteriorated concrete slab exceeds roughly ±500 feet in length designers follow Pub 72M RC‑21M or RC‑27M to reconstruct the entire width, selecting jointed reinforced concrete pavement with 30‑foot perpendicular joints or jointed plain concrete pavement with 15‑foot joints; the choice is documented in the contract and influences pay items[1]. For full‑depth patches shorter than 500 feet the patch must be designed per Pub 72M RC‑26M, using dowelled load‑transfer at all transverse joints and specifying high‑early‑strength concrete only when accelerated opening of 4–7 hours is required, otherwise standard concrete achieves traffic strength in 24–48 hours[1]. In situations where rapid lane reopening is critical accelerated concrete patching may trigger liquidated damages based on user delay calculations[1]. The slab‑stabilization procedure described in Chapter 4 §4.5 applies Pub 72M RC‑26M to diagnose loss of support indicated by transverse joint faulting, fines near joints, small depressions, corner breaks or settlement; when voids are confirmed the crew drills holes at the pattern prescribed by Pub 72M and selects a grout‑cement mix (≈1 cubic foot per hole), hot asphalt (30–50 gallons per hole) or high‑density polyurethane (0.4 lb per square foot) based on site conditions, then pumps the material under the slab[1]. Traffic may not be reopened until the cement cures (up to 12 hours), the asphalt cools (minimum 30 minutes) or the polyurethane expands and hardens (minimum 30 minutes)[1]. For estimating purposes at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints are stabilized if no preliminary testing has been performed, restoring structural integrity by filling voids, reducing slab deflections and thereby lowering the potential for future pumping, faulting and cracking[1]. The Roadway Construction Standards are also applied in shoulder design for jointed plain concrete pavement: urban Interstates/Expressways are required to use tied concrete shoulders and, per Pub 72M RC‑25M, full‑depth PCC shoulders with dowelled transverse joints; rural Interstates/Expressways without anticipated work‑zone or incident‑management use tied shoulders with undowelled JPCP joints, while those that do anticipate such uses must employ full‑depth dowelled shoulders; when the two‑way ADT is less than 10 000 designers may consider widened slabs with asphalt or RCC shoulders[1]. In full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement projects the contractor must provide shop drawings, working drawings and the manufacturer’s quality‑control plan at least 21 calendar days before the pre‑production meeting; load‑transfer dowels are positioned within ± 1⁄8 inch of the drawing locations, transverse joint widths shall not exceed one‑half inch and shall be a minimum of one‑quarter inch along their entire length, subbase grading tolerances are plus or minus one‑half inch, and grout must reach a compressive strength of at least 2 500 psi before traffic is allowed[2]. A concrete pavement cross‑stitching job requires a quality‑control plan prepared in accordance with Section 106.03 to be submitted for review at least fourteen days before work begins; ambient temperature must be at least 40 °F, the hydraulic drill holes shall be no more than three‑eighths inch larger than the tie bar, and traffic cannot be restored until the anchoring material cures per the manufacturer’s recommendations[2].

Limitations and cautions

When Roadway Construction Standards (Pub 72M) are applied under the General provisions (section 2.1), engineers must recognize that the guidance is deliberately generalized, so project‑specific conditions such as existing pavement stability, drainage adequacy, and traffic‑control options introduce uncertainty and require professional judgment; simplifications arise from using uniform thickness assumptions and standard performance thresholds like the minimum surface‑course thicknesses prescribed for Restricted Performance Specification projects, which are only valid if the overlay base is stable, properly leveled, sealed and patched [1]. The boundary conditions include coordinating detours with the District Traffic Unit when feasible, retaining at least 15 % of the estimated total milled material (or providing all material when the estimate is less than 200 tons) for recycling, and satisfying a target field‑density range of roughly 92.0 % to 98.5 % for Percent Within Limits contracts, with density acceptance methods adjusted based on base condition [1]. Potential misuse may occur if RPS criteria are applied without confirming pavement stability or uniform layer thickness, if PWL density targets are pursued without accounting for base variability, or if recycled‑material percentages are misapplied beyond the stipulated minimums [1].

For shoulder design (section 2.6), the standard sets boundary conditions that Type 1‑type shoulders on two‑lane roads are required only when truck traffic exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150, the 2‑foot pavement widening does not apply to inside shoulders on Interstate and other limited‑access freeways, and special shoulder extensions are permitted only where curve radii are sharper than 12°‑30′ or within distances starting 250 ft before an intersection and stopping 150 ft downstream; the decision to provide smooth‑surface bicycle shoulders outside these situations is left to District Executive discretion, so misuse can arise if the discretionary nature or anticipated drainage problem criteria are ignored [1].

In concrete pavement patching (section 4.2), uncertainties stem from length and joint‑spacing limits: full‑depth patches longer than about ±500 ft trigger a design analysis for slab thickness, and when intermittent patches leave 10 ft or less of original pavement between them the entire section should be removed and reconstructed; simplifications include assuming JRCP joints may be spaced at 30 ft and JPCP joints at 15 ft, while tie bars are required only for patches more than 65 ft long [1]. Potential misuse includes relying on reinforcement in JRCP despite guidance that it does not add strength, using high‑early‑strength concrete despite its potential for excessive shrinkage cracking, or substituting flexible base material for full‑depth concrete patches, which would compromise ride quality and pavement life [1].

Slab stabilization (section 4.5) is bounded by the fact that it only restores support by filling voids and does not correct surface depressions, increase structural capacity, or eliminate faulting; because void locations are uncertain, the guidance simplifies field implementation by recommending that at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints be stabilized when no preliminary testing has been performed [1]. Boundary conditions require joint and crack sealing, functional subsurface drains, and compliance with traffic‑control requirements before reopening the pavement, with traffic not to be opened until cement grout cures (up to 12 hours), asphalt cools (minimum 30 minutes) or polyurethane expands and hardens (minimum 30 minutes) [1]. Misuse may result if stabilization is treated as a cure for faulting or if traffic is reopened prematurely [1].

Continuously reinforced concrete pavement (section 8.13) carries uncertainty because the standard no longer contains construction details or specifications, limiting designers to follow the 1993 AASHTO Guide using DARWin and requiring special contract provisions; the boundary condition is that CRCP may be used only in special cases with approval of the Director, BODD [1]. Misuse occurs if CRCP is employed without those approvals or contractual provisions [1].

Jointed plain concrete pavement shoulder design (section 8.15) imposes boundary conditions that urban Interstates/Expressways must use full‑depth PCC shoulders with dowelled transverse joints, while rural Interstates/Expressways may use tied shoulders with undowelled joints unless a high probability of incident‑management or work‑zone traffic exists; additionally, when the two‑way ADT is less than 10 000 widened slabs with asphalt or RCC shoulders may be considered, and full‑depth concrete shoulders require that all materials from subgrade to surface match those of the mainline pavement [1]. Potential misuse includes applying the wrong shoulder type for the corridor classification, ignoring the ADT threshold, failing to match material properties, or not following Publication 408 specifications for dowelled or non‑dowelled shoulders [1].

Slip‑form paving (section 501.3(g)) assumes continuous forward movement of a fully energized machine; if the paver’s motion stops, vibrating and tamping elements must be immediately disengaged, and edge slump must be kept within 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and within 1/4 inch between lane and shoulder before the concrete becomes non‑plastic, otherwise paving must be suspended after 200 feet [2]. Joint and dowel placement tolerances are limited to a vertical tilt or horizontal skew of no more than 0.25 inch per end of the dowel relative to its midpoint and longitudinal or horizontal translation of no more than 1 inch; tie‑bar pull‑out testing must use a gauge whose calibration date does not exceed six months, so using an outdated gauge can lead to load‑transfer failures [2].

Mat‑density acceptance (section 413.3(j)) allows density to be accepted without movement only for scratch or leveling courses that are less than 1 inch deep or have a unit weight of ≤110 lb/yd² and for placements not exceeding 500 tons in a continuous run; any larger depth, weight, or quantity requires an optimum‑rolling pattern or pavement cores, and treating those cases as non‑critical would be misuse [2]. Acceptance relies on nuclear or electrical impedance gauges operated by a licensed operator with an annual ASTM D2950 calibration, so using an uncalibrated gauge or an unlicensed operator adds potential error; core sampling must occur within one day of placement and is limited to one core per sublot unless otherwise authorised, with failure to meet the ≤1‑day timing or exceeding the core limit triggering a stop‑paving action [2].

Spreading and finishing (section 413.3(h)) imposes temperature limits listed in Table A, prohibiting placement of any mixture outside those limits; work stoppages of less than five calendar days do not require a new lot, whereas a stoppage of five days or more forces a redefinition of lot size and sublot count as specified in Table D [2]. Only one loose acceptance sample may be obtained per sublot, and the certified field technician required by Publication 351 must be present throughout placement; pattern segregation is judged unacceptable when the measured macro‑texture difference exceeds 0.024 inch, while flushing is deemed defective when the average texture depth is ≤0.006 inch, both conditions triggering mandatory suspension of paving and corrective actions [2]. Misuse can arise from adding material that is segregated, below minimum temperature, or has an incorrect asphalt content, as well as from using prohibited hand tools such as rakes or exceeding the Safety Edge depth limits (minimum 1.5 inches, not greater than 5 inches) [2].

Concrete pavement patching (section 516.3) specifies that excavated areas may not remain un‑patched for more than 48 hours (or over weekends/holidays), traffic on saw‑cut sections is limited to a maximum of 72 hours, and any saw cut opened to traffic must not exceed ½ inch in width or be less than full depth [2].

Subgrade work (section 210.3) requires submission of a machine‑control grading QC plan at least one week before the preconstruction conference and demonstration that GPS or laser control equipment meets acceptable tolerances; inspection intervals may not exceed 25 feet for manual methods, may be increased to not more than 50 feet when an automatic grader follows reference lines, and may be increased to not more than 500 feet provided the requirements of Section 210.3(d) are met; any surface irregularity exceeding ½ inch must be corrected, and acceptance testing tolerates no more than 10 percent of all tests exceeding that limit, otherwise additional stake and stringline checks may be required [2].

Slab‑fracturing (section 522.3) relies on a 1,000‑ft test section to establish an optimal breaking pattern and on falling‑weight deflectometer results that must fall within the modulus range in Table A; deviation introduces uncertainty about pavement performance and may require re‑fracturing, while the specifications simplify field conditions by assuming a stable subgrade, and sonic vibratory equipment is permitted only when it will not cause soil liquefaction [2]. Fracturing must be kept at least 10 feet horizontally and vertically from culverts, 40 feet from bridge approaches, and 3 feet from fixed objects, and FWD testing should not be performed when the pavement surface is frozen or when air temperature falls below 45°F or exceeds 90°F; potential misuse arises if equipment that produces surface spalling, holes or shattering is used, if rollers exceed weight or tire‑pressure limits, or if work proceeds without required approvals for alternate plans, bridge crossings, or utility clearances [2].

Concrete placement (section 501) imposes strict weather and temperature boundaries: concrete may not be placed when the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·h unless mitigation such as windbreaks or fog misting is used; cold‑weather operations must stop when ambient air temperature falls to 40°F (45°F for accelerated strength concrete) and only resume above those values; hot‑weather placement requires monitoring plastic concrete temperatures, discontinuing work if they rise above 90°F and keeping them between 50°F and 90°F by cooling water or aggregates [2]. Mix designs from volumetric plants or mobile mixers are prohibited unless written approval is obtained, and forms must meet deflection limits of no more than 0.01 in under a 1,700‑lb center load over a 4‑ft span with tolerances not exceeding 1/8 inch in 10 feet; shims are limited to 1 inch height, and a comprehensive field‑operation QC plan must be submitted at least two weeks before paving and approved prior to start [2].

Safety Edge requirements (section 419.3) state that the edge is not required where curb or sidewalk are encountered or where the face of a guiderail is directly over the pavement edge, and it may not be placed on organic material; the device used to form the edge must be approved before construction [2].

Full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs (section 529.3) require that the manufacturer be listed in Bulletin 15 and mix designs submitted at least 28 days before production achieve a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a flexural strength of 650 psi; any deviation from these strengths or failure to provide accepted mix data introduces uncertainty. The slab is cast with a sacrificial concrete layer that is ¼ inch thicker but will not be included in design calculations, and grout for dowel slots and bedding must reach a compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is allowed; dowel placement must be within ±1/8 inch, and failure to meet these limits or opening to traffic prematurely represents potential misuse [2].

Cold‑recycled asphalt base (section 341.3) imposes seasonal limits prohibiting placement from September 1 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0, 2‑0, 3‑0, 4‑0, 9‑0, and 10‑0, and from October 1 to April 30 elsewhere; ambient air must be at least 45°F and rising with no freezing for 24 hours before paving. The mix must be cured for at least two weeks or until the average moisture content drops below 2 percent, each compacted layer must achieve a minimum density of 96 percent of the PTM 402 target, and density checks are required at each 3,000‑square‑yard segment to avoid under‑compaction or improper curing [2].

Rubblizing operations (section 526.3) demand an approved detailed equipment plan, and work must be halted if expected results are not obtained; only overlaying asphalt may be removed while full‑depth patches remain in place, backfill is placed in lifts no greater than six inches and compacted until non‑movement is achieved, and final seating of the broken concrete is satisfactory when a 50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller contacts every point within four passes and achieves a vertical deflection of less than three‑quarters inch. Traffic is prohibited after rubblizing and seating, any overlay must be placed within sixty calendar days, and the surface must not be exposed to freezing conditions [2].

Pervious asphalt systems (section 420.3) treat infiltration as a boundary condition: two test sections each at least 225 sq ft must average more than 200 in/hr, and placement is prohibited when air or surface temperature is lower than 50°F; the mix must cool to 100°F before subsequent layers are laid. Only smooth steel‑wheeled rollers may be used, compaction is limited to no more than four passes, tack coat between lifts is prohibited, and storage of equipment or materials on pervious beds is forbidden because these actions can reduce porosity or drainage performance [2].

Grinding operations (section 509.3) assume grinding equipment meets a minimum weight of 40,000 lb and a blade width of at least 4 ft; cross‑slope deviations must not exceed 1/8 inch in 12 feet, pass overlap may not be greater than 1 inch, and a feather pass of at least 2 ft extending 100 ft must be provided to maintain drainage. A 300‑foot test section must be approved unless waived in writing, and the finished surface must meet ride‑quality limits of IRI ≤ 70 in/mile for speeds greater than 45 mph or ≤ 90 in/mile for speeds less than or equal to 45 mph; exceeding these thresholds will prevent payment and indicate misuse [2].

Base‑course construction (sections 312.3, 313, 314) assumes accurate proportioning and moisture control, yet uncertainty can arise because acceptance of the processing plant, verification of measuring devices, and frequent moisture tests are required; if aggregates are too coarse the prescribed density test cannot be used and compaction must instead rely on a non‑movement criterion, which introduces variability. The procedure also imposes clear boundary conditions: mixture may not be placed on a frozen surface, when air temperature is below 40°F, or when freezing temperatures are forecast within the next 24 hours; each layer must be between 3 inches and 6 inches thick (or 4‑8 inches for Superpave 37.5 mm bases) and compacted to at least 100 percent of maximum density, and the pavement is defective if irregularities exceed ¼ inch or a core measurement shows a deficiency of ½ inch or more; potential sources of misuse include using unclean hauling vehicles, failing to cover the mix during transport, neglecting automatic cutoff devices that stop feed if a component stops, or allowing moisture loss that would prevent proper bonding between lanes [2].

Continuously reinforced concrete pavement patching (section 518) mandates that saw cuts be located at least 24 inches from any existing crack, limits wheel penetration into the subbase to one‑half inch, restricts jackhammer size to thirty pounds maximum, requires secondary transverse cuts to be at least one inch deep, and specifies that break back greater than two inches or damage to reinforcement bars triggers a new full‑depth transverse saw cut beyond the area of break back [2].

Cross‑stitching (section 528) may be performed only when both ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at least forty degrees Fahrenheit, is limited to longitudinal joints and cracks, requires drilled holes no more than three‑eighths inch larger in diameter than the tie bar and not extending through the slab bottom, and demands oil‑free compressed air of at least one hundred psi; deviation such as attempting cross‑stitching on transverse joints, exceeding hole size limits or opening to traffic before epoxy cure can result in defective work that must be replaced [2].

Full‑depth longitudinal concrete joint repair (section 519) requires a quality‑control plan submitted at least two weeks before work begins; traffic may be permitted on saw‑cut areas for no more than seventy‑two hours, any saw cut wider than one‑half inch must not be opened to traffic, excavated repair zones cannot remain unpatched for more than forty‑eight hours or over weekends or holidays, cooling water, slurry and dust from sawing may not enter open lanes, prohibited equipment such as drop hammers or hydro‑hammers shall not be used, and extensions of saw cuts into adjacent pavement are limited to full depth plus two inches without damaging dowel bars [2].

Subbase construction (section 350) requires a Subbase Delivery Plan when quantities exceed 1,500 tons if directed; placement is prohibited on soft, muddy or frozen areas, moisture content must be sufficient to prevent segregation and the material must remain well‑moistened until compaction, layers may not exceed eight inches in compacted depth (four inches when granulated slag is used unless a single six‑inch layer is fully compacted), and any subbase depth deficient by one‑half inch or more is deemed defective, requiring scarification, blending, recompaction and retesting [2].

Long‑life concrete pavement specifications (section 530) simplify depth control by using the lot‑average core depth, which must be less than the design thickness minus 0.5 inch; a single sublot may fall below that limit. Compressive strength is accepted only if the lower quality index (PWL) reaches at least fifty‑five percent for the lot based on a limited number of test cylinders per sublot and testing before placement. A full lot is defined as exactly 5,600 square yards of pavement of constant depth, and partial lots under 4,200 square yards are merged with the preceding lot; this definition may mask localized defects because sampling is limited to the first sublot and then every tenth thereafter. The water‑to‑cement ratio incentive requires plant delivery ticket values to match laboratory AASHTO T 318 results within plus or minus 0.03, yet verification relies on calibrated meters, handwritten temperature‑water entries and a forty‑five minute testing window, all of which are potential sources of error or misuse [2].

Preplacement requirements (section 413.3(a)) expressly prohibit placement of any asphalt paving mixtures outside the prescribed seasonal windows unless a written extension is granted by the District Executive; for PG 64E‑22 wearing courses, >10 million ESALs wearing courses, 4.75 mm wearing courses, or other wearing courses placed at compacted depths less than 1.5 inches, paving may occur April 1 to October 15, while for all other courses paving may occur April 1 to October 31. Placement must not be undertaken when surfaces are wet or when the air or surface temperature is 40°F or lower, and the required pre‑placement procedures include submission of a paving operation QC Plan on Form CS‑413, holding a pre‑placement meeting at least two weeks before work, and employing a Material Transfer Vehicle whenever the paving length will exceed 1,500 ft unless the Representative determines the MTV to be infeasible; failure to follow any of these steps may result in the work being stopped or rejected as misuse [2].

The Roadway Construction Standards provide general guidance, but additional engineering judgment, analysis, testing, or reference is required whenever a project’s specific conditions or problems are not fully addressed by that generalized guidance. Likewise, the specifications require extra judgment whenever field observations fall outside the tolerances or criteria set in any particular section of Pub 72M. For concrete pavement patching, if a full‑width reconstruction exceeds roughly five hundred feet of Portland cement concrete pavement, a pavement design analysis must be performed to determine whether a thicker slab depth is required. If flexible base material is proposed as a substitute for jointed plain concrete in a full‑depth patch, the Pavement Engineer must submit a waiver with detailed justification and satisfy the testing provisions of Publication 408 Section 316, which goes beyond the standard RC‑26M guidance. When accelerated concrete patching is used on high‑ADT highways, liquidated‑damage calculations referencing Publications 448, 10C and the RULD Calculator are required to establish user‑delay penalties. For projects that propose continuously reinforced concrete pavement, the Roadway Construction Standards no longer provide construction details; therefore additional engineering judgment, analysis, testing, or reference to other documents is required, the structural design must follow the 1993 AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures using DARWin, special provisions describing CRCP construction have to be inserted into the contract because the specifications are deleted from Pub 72M and Publication 408, and use of CRCP also requires approval from the Director, BODD. Under Section 413.3(j) of the Superpave mixture‑design specifications, if mat‑density acceptance cannot be demonstrated with the standard non‑movement or optimum‑rolling pattern, the Representative may require verification of the target density and, when the target is not achieved, a new optimum‑rolling pattern must be established as directed; paving also must be stopped when cores are not taken within one day after placement or when the density for two consecutive lots or for a total of three lots fails to meet the density payment factor percentage of ≥100 pending review and approval of a corrective solution. When the LTS bulk‑specific‑gravity (Gmm) value differs from the Contractor’s daily Gmm value by more than ± 0.030, the LTS result becomes the acceptance Gmm value, requiring extra testing to verify density calculations. If asphalt content or percent passing the 75 mm sieve is not within the single‑sample or multiple‑sample limits for two consecutive lots or a total of three lots, production must be stopped, the cause investigated and a corrective proposal reviewed and authorized before work can resume. Pattern segregation becomes unacceptable when the average texture depth difference between segregated and non‑segregated areas exceeds 0.024 inch, triggering suspension, investigation, test sections and approval prior to continuation. Flushing is deemed defective when macrotexture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 inch, also requiring an immediate stop, corrective action development and Representative sign‑off before paving can proceed. Under Section 210.3, additional judgment is required whenever the Representative suspects a subgrade area is deficient or irregular; this triggers checking the finished surface with a template and straightedge, and it is also triggered if more than 10 % of all acceptance tests exceed the half‑inch tolerance in any phase of the project, at which point the Representative may require stakes and stringline be established as specified in Section 210.3(c). If GPS or laser grading equipment is employed, a machine‑control grading QC Plan must be submitted at least one week before the preconstruction conference to demonstrate that the equipment meets performance tolerances. Under Section 522.3, additional judgment is required whenever the prescribed fracturing process does not meet the performance criteria; for example, if satisfactory fracture results are not being obtained operations must be stopped and an alternate action plan and/or different equipment submitted to the Representative for approval, and if Falling Weight Deflectometer testing shows the average elastic modulus of the fractured concrete layer falls outside the range specified in Table A, the breaking pattern must be adjusted, retested, and a new 1,000‑ft test section may be required, especially after two consecutive lots fail the test criteria. Adjustments to aggregate gradation during concrete production that also necessitate changes to cementitious material or water content require a new mix design and trial concrete batches; likewise, if the combined gradation percent retained is within less than 5 % of the limits set by PTM No. 529, the District Maintenance Engineer/District Materials Manager may allow it, but that discretionary allowance requires further engineering judgment. Additional judgment is required whenever routine lot‑acceptance results fall outside the tolerances defined in the standards; for instance, if an individual density increment lies outside the mat‑density limits in Table I, the LTS or HOLA Representative must analyze the bulk‑density data for extreme values using PTM No. 4 at the 5 % significance level, and when IA sample results differ from HOLA sample results by more than the maximum tolerances in Table G, calibration factors are determined and extra IA samples are collected. Additional judgment, analysis, or testing is required whenever the Representative requests data beyond the baseline standards; for example flexural strength test results may be demanded at any time and, if those results do not meet the minimum values, the Representative can require a retrial of the concrete mix design, design documentation with calculations and test data must also be supplied to verify that steel bearing plates can support loads without excessive settlement or deflection, and grout materials cannot be placed in traffic until testing confirms they have reached at least 2,500 psi compressive strength. For cold‑recycled asphalt base courses, additional actions are required when the minimum density of 96 % of the PTM 402 target is not met; a new target density may be set per PTM 402 and further compaction, scarifying or moisture adjustment must be performed, and if those steps still fail construction is halted until the cause is corrected. Placement outside the normal seasonal window is permitted only with written approval of the Representative after confirming no freezing for 24 hours and an ambient temperature of 45°F and rising. If the Representative directs surface‑tolerance testing, the base course must be checked for smoothness and any area where the average gap exceeds ½ inch must be corrected, adding extra testing not otherwise mandated. Under Section 344.3(e) for full‑depth reclamation, additional judgment is required when the compaction control strip fails to achieve a minimum density of at least 95 % of the laboratory‑compacted maximum density; if further compaction does not bring the strip to that density, a new control strip must be constructed and the mix design re‑approved. Strength tests on cured Proctor specimens that do not reach a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi for asphalt‑stabilized FDR or an unconfined compressive strength outside the range of 300 to 500 psi for chemically stabilized FDR also require rework and a revised mix design, and field adjustments are required when moisture content deviates more than 3 % above optimum. Section 526.3 requires work to be stopped and an alternate plan with a new equipment list prepared whenever the rubblizing operation does not produce the specified results; each time the equipment, rubblizing pattern or breaking force is changed, the initial two‑section full‑depth excavation verification must be repeated. Under Section 509.3, a 300‑foot test section in a single lane must be constructed to demonstrate that the equipment and procedures will provide an acceptable surface, and work may not continue until the test section has been approved, unless a written waiver is issued by the District Executive. Section 548.3 calls for additional judgment or reference when field conditions depart from prescriptive limits; if dowel baskets move during paving, paving must be suspended until a plan for securely anchoring the baskets is approved and implemented, with anchor type, location, number and length selected based on observed conditions; if joint faulting exceeds ½ inch, a scratch course or milling must be performed to reduce faulting, and determining an appropriate sawing window may require FHWA’s HIPERPAV or similar software approved by the Department. Section 507.3 triggers additional testing when smoothness verification results fail tolerances; specifically, if the verification mean roughness index exceeds the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches per mile for at least 90 % of the 528‑foot lots, or if the average verification MRI exceeds the average acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches per mile, the Department will review testing procedures, equipment and personnel and may suspend the contractor’s profiler certification until recertified. Section 519.3 requires a Quality Control Plan describing construction equipment, personnel, methods, testing frequencies and corrective‑action points to be prepared and submitted at least two weeks before repairs begin. Under Section 350.3, when the subbase quantity exceeds 1,500 tons, a Subbase Delivery Plan must be prepared and submitted for review before work can begin; additional judgment is also required whenever the Representative directs correction of unsatisfactory subbase conditions, which may involve removal, subgrade repair and reinstallment, and if density testing shows the finished subbase depth is deficient by ½ inch or more, the Representative may require extra test holes to delineate the defective area and subsequent remedial compaction. Section 530.3 invokes additional judgment, analysis, testing or other references whenever standard acceptance criteria are not met or a District Executive issues a written direction; examples include a lot whose average core depth is less than design thickness minus 0.5 inch, a compressive‑strength PWL below 55 %, surface defects, edge slumping or honeycombing that cannot be satisfactorily repaired as determined by the Representative, and a computed water‑to‑cement ratio that differs from the AASHTO T 318 verification result by more than ± 0.03, each of which triggers further testing and may affect incentive payment.
