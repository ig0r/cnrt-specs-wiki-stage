Bond Breaker

Part of: Concrete Overlay is-subtopic-of
Bond Breaker is a topic under Concrete Overlay.
bond-breaker
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Bond breakers are employed to create an intentional unbonded interface between existing pavement or structural components and a new overlay or adjacent elements, preventing unintended adhesion that would otherwise transmit distress or compromise load transfer[1][2]. They achieve this separation through material layers—such as a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt coat, a non‑woven geotextile interlayer, dual 4 mil polyethylene sheets, or lubricating compounds applied to steel dowel bars—that provide a chemically inert, durable barrier capable of withstanding traffic and environmental exposure[1][2]. By isolating the old substrate, the bond breaker enables the overlay or new structural element to act as an independent load‑bearing layer, preserving ride quality, serviceability, and required pull‑out performance throughout its design life[1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Bond breakers are employed to create an intentional unbonded interface between existing pavement or structural components and new overlay or adjacent elements, preventing unintended adhesion that would otherwise transmit distress or compromise load transfer[1][2]. In the case of concrete pavements, a thin asphalt separation layer at least 1 inch thick or a non‑woven geotextile is placed over the deteriorated slab so that the overlay does not bond to the weakened base, allowing the overlay to act as a new structural layer on a stable platform and restoring capacity without relying on the condition of the old concrete[1]. For dowel‑bar installations, a shop‑applied or field‑applied lubricant must be applied to at least one‑half of the bar length at least one hour before concrete placement, and the exposed ends are provided with a minimum 1‑inch clearance packet; this ensures that the dowel does not bond to the surrounding concrete, satisfying the pull‑out resistance required by AASHTO M 254 and preserving intended shear transfer across joints while permitting independent movement and future replacement[2]. When new concrete is placed over an existing pavement, two layers of polyethylene sheeting with a minimum thickness of 4.0 mil are installed beneath the fresh concrete; this non‑bonding surface prevents adhesion to the underlying base, facilitates removal of failed sections without damaging reinforcement or subbase, and supports proper recompaction[2]. Similar separation is required at geomembrane–geotextile interfaces where adhesive overspray could unintentionally glue the membranes together; an additional piece of geomembrane extending at least six inches beyond the overspray acts as a deliberate separator, and low‑density polyethylene foam may be used between joint filler material and joint sealer to keep those layers from adhering, thereby preserving the designed movement of each component[2]. Across bridge approach slabs, the same 4.0 mil polyethylene sheeting provides a smooth, non‑bonding surface under the slab, preventing unintended adhesion to the subbase and ensuring that the intended structural performance is achieved[2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 182–184), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–447), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–691)

Bond breaker must function to prevent any adhesive connection between the existing concrete slab and a new overlay, thereby allowing the overlay to act as a separate structural element, and more generally it must provide a reliable non‑adhesive barrier that prevents unintended bonding among pavement layers, concrete and steel dowel bars, bridge approach slabs, and geotextile/geomembrane systems. This separation enables the overlay to perform as an independent structural layer, preserving ride quality and serviceability throughout the design life. The material may be a fine‑graded asphalt separation layer of at least one inch thickness or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer placed between the old concrete and the overlay. For bridge approach slabs and slab‑on‑grade applications the barrier consists of two separate polyethylene sheets each with a minimum thickness of 4.0 mil, laid smooth with longitudinal seams overlapped by at least twelve inches to provide continuous separation across the entire slab area. When used with dowel bars that require a Type B coating, the lubricant must be applied to at least one‑half of each dowel’s length, using a uniform film applied by spray, daubing, mopping or a gloved hand, and the application must occur no later than one hour before concrete placement. The tape used as a bond breaker in joint reservoir installations shall be positioned on the bottom of the reservoir after the faces have been cleaned and dried, and may not project upward from the face by more than one‑eighth inch; the sealant is then filled to a level between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface. The selected barrier materials must retain their separating action for the intended design life without degrading, and any bond‑breaking lubricant must have a flash point of at least 149 °F, contain no graphite, and be stored protected from environmental exposure; if left unprotected for more than six months the coating must be retested to AASHTO M 254 or refreshed with new lubricant. The non‑adhesive barrier must remain stable under traffic loading and environmental conditions, ensuring that the overlay continues to act as a separate pavement without loss of function. Safety considerations require that the lubricant’s flash point meet the minimum temperature to reduce fire risk, that graphite‑based products be avoided, and that sealant placement avoid spilling onto the riding surface so as not to create slip hazards. By preventing unintended bonding, the bond breaker preserves smooth riding surfaces, maintains proper dowel load transfer, and protects joint geometry, thereby supporting long‑term serviceability of the pavement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 182–184), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 359–433), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

Scope and applicability

Bond‑breaker layers are employed whenever a non‑bonding interface must be assured in rigid cement‑concrete pavement systems, encompassing new construction, precast‑slab placement, patching, rehabilitation, Long Life Concrete pavements, and the placement of unbonded concrete overlays over existing slabs [2]. For an existing concrete pavement that is moderately to significantly deteriorated yet capable of serving as a stable platform, the overlay is separated from the old slab by either a fine‑graded minimum one‑inch asphalt separation coat or a non‑woven geotextile interlayer, thereby preventing adhesion and allowing the new layer to act as an independent structural element [1]. When load‑transfer units contain steel dowel bars whose coating is classified as Type B, a bond‑breaker lubricant must be applied to at least one‑half of the dowel length at least one hour before fresh concrete placement; Type A coated dowels provide sufficiently low bond strength and do not require lubricant, while Type C dowels are excluded from certain polystyrene board solutions [2]. All lubricants used as bond‑breakers must be commercially available, compatible with steel, have a flash point of no less than 149 °F, must not be graphite‑based, and shall be stored protected from the elements in accordance with ASTM A775; coatings exposed for more than six months require retesting or recoating per AASHTO M 254 [2]. For rigid‑pavement joints and cracks that incorporate dowel bars—construction or expansion joints, transverse joints where an asphalt relief joint meets the slab, spall‑repair joints, and joints created during patch work—the bond‑breaker may be provided as a lubricant on the dowels, a full‑depth 1/4‑inch polystyrene board placed in longitudinal joints of Type A or B patches (but not Type C), or a tape/backer‑rod assembly positioned on the bottom of the sealant reservoir before sealant placement; the tape must be a self‑adhesive material whose width matches the reservoir and may extend no more than 1/8 inch up the face, with duct tape of equivalent width acceptable as an inexpensive alternative [2]. In situations where adhesive overspray occurs between a geomembrane and a geotextile, an additional piece of geomembrane extending at least six inches beyond the overspray zone serves as a bond‑breaker to maintain separation [2]. For bridge‑approach slabs constructed on rigid pavement, two layers of polyethylene sheeting not thinner than 4 mil are laid smooth beneath the sleeper slab, with seams running longitudinally and overlapping a minimum of 12 inches; manufacturer certification of the material is required [2]. When an existing pavement was built over a prior bond‑breaker film, that film must be removed and replaced by two layers of polyethylene sheeting each at least 4.0 mil thick before new concrete placement [2]. Verification of proper application in load‑transfer units is achieved through LTU samples—at least six dowels with pre‑applied lubricant for standard bars or three for high‑performance bars listed in Bulletin 15—submitted for testing a minimum of 45 days prior to paving [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 182–184), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 359–458), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

When the existing pavement exhibits significant structural deterioration, unstable support, stripping of the asphalt surface, or drainage problems that have not been remedied, relying on adhesion between old and new layers is inadvisable, prompting designers to select alternatives such as unbonded concrete overlays, cracking and seating, or rubblizing which introduce a separation layer or fully break the bond [1]. Similarly, in concrete pavement repairs where the dowel‑bar coating is classified as Type A, its inherently low bond strength satisfies pull‑out requirements without any supplemental material, making an approach that omits a shop‑applied bond breaker the preferred choice [2]. For repair types designated as Type C patches, specifications explicitly prohibit the use of a bond breaker in the longitudinal joint and instead require tie bars to provide the necessary load transfer, so the tie‑bar arrangement supersedes any bond‑breaker application [2]. The polystyrene board bond breaker, typically a 1/4‑inch full‑depth sheet, is therefore limited to situations where the coating falls under Type A or Type B patches or corner repairs that permit its use; outside those categories other design measures are mandated [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–412), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

Design inputs and requirements

Bond breaker use for unbonded concrete overlays is governed by the requirement to provide a minimum one‑inch thick fine‑graded asphalt separation layer between the existing pavement and the new overlay, or alternatively a non‑woven geotextile interlayer as specified in Publication 408, Specifications Section 548. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Design methods and criteria

The bond‑breaker concept is implemented through prescribed material layers and coating procedures rather than analytical formulas or software tools [1][2]. For unbonded concrete overlays the standard practice is to place a fine‑graded asphalt separation layer at least one inch thick on the existing pavement, or alternatively to install a non‑woven geotextile interlayer as allowed by Publication 408 Section 548 [1]. In dowel bar applications AASHTO M 254 classifies coating materials into Type A, which inherently exhibit low bond strength and therefore require no shop‑applied breaker, and Type B, which develop measurable bond strength and must be supplemented with a shop‑applied bond‑breaker that satisfies the specification’s pull‑out load criteria [2]. For Type B dowels the procedure mandates coating at least one‑half of the bar length with a lubricating compound that is thoroughly stirred and applied by spraying, daubing, mopping or with a gloved hand, while brushes are expressly prohibited [2]. The coated portion must be placed on the free end of the dowel and the application completed no later than one hour before concrete placement to allow the breaker to take effect; if a shop‑applied lubricant has already been used, field coating is unnecessary but the same timing and handling rules apply should additional coating be required [2]. Physical separation layers are also prescribed for various joint configurations, including a full‑depth ¼‑inch polystyrene board that provides continuous separation in longitudinal joints of both Type A and Type B patches as well as corner repairs [2]. Under bridge approach slabs, under‑slab separations, and pavement repairs where the original breaker has been removed, two layers of polyethylene sheeting with a minimum thickness of 4 mil are installed beneath the new concrete, the sheets must be smooth, free of deep folds, and seams oriented longitudinally with at least a 12‑inch overlap, and manufacturers must supply certification confirming compliance [2]. Existing bond‑breaker material must be removed from the base before installing the new polyethylene layers [2]. Quality assurance requires preparation of an LTU sample containing at least six dowels pre‑coated with the lubricant, submission of full PTM No. 642 test reports a minimum of 45 days prior to paving, and concrete placement may commence only after a representative verifies that the acceptance criteria have been satisfied; additionally, dowel bars must be stored no longer than 30 days unless protected by an opaque covering [2]. When adhesive overspray threatens bonding between a geomembrane and geotextile, the remedial measure is to insert an additional piece of geomembrane extending at least six inches beyond the overspray zone to act as a supplemental bond‑breaker layer [2]. For joint reservoirs that receive sealant, a bond‑breaker tape is placed on the bottom of each reservoir immediately before sealing, the tape may not project more than one‑eighth inch up the face, the reservoir is cut to a depth of one inch and a width of one‑half inch, faces must be cleaned and dried, ambient temperature during placement must lie within the sealant’s approved range (typically 40 °F to 90 °F), and sealant is poured to just below the pavement surface so that the tape remains fully embedded beneath the surface [2]. Across all of these practices no analytical equations, empirical relationships, or design software are prescribed; the guidance consists solely of classification criteria, material specifications, and step‑by‑step application instructions [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 182–184), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–448), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

In unbonded concrete overlays the bond breaker is placed between the existing pavement and the new concrete and must consist of at least one inch of fine‑graded asphalt or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer, which serves solely to separate the two layers while the underlying pavement provides a stable platform; the design assumes that no structural bond is required between overlay and existing pavement. Across all applications the selected bond breaker is assumed to remain chemically inert relative to adjacent pavement materials and to retain its physical integrity under anticipated traffic loads and environmental conditions, with manufacturer certification acting as the primary quality‑assurance safety factor and pull‑out load limits providing a performance‑based margin. For dowel‑bar installations the type of coating determines whether a breaker is needed: Type A coatings develop sufficiently low bond strength so that no additional material is required, whereas Type B coatings develop measurable bond strength and therefore require a separate breaker that satisfies the pull‑out load requirements of AASHTO M 254; the lubricant used as a breaker must be a commercially available product approved for steel contact, possess a flash point of at least 149 °F, exclude graphite‑based formulations, be stored in accordance with ASTM A775, and if left exposed to the environment for more than six months must be retested or reapplied. Application of the lubricant to Type B dowels must achieve thorough coverage of at least fifty percent of each bar’s length by spraying, daubing, mopping, or hand‑application with a gloved hand (brushes are prohibited), and must be completed no later than one hour before concrete placement around the dowel assembly; a protective tube is required over the lubricated end, leaving a minimum clearance of one inch. For longitudinal joints in pavement patches and corner repairs involving Type A or B dowels, a full‑depth polystyrene board 0.25 in thick serves as a solid bond breaker, while Type C joints receive none. In rigid‑pavement patch work the prescribed barrier consists of two overlapping layers of polyethylene sheeting each at least 4.0 mil thick, installed over a prepared subbase, trimmed to the exact dimensions of the patch, placed on a smooth surface free of excessive depth variations, with seams running longitudinally and overlapped by a minimum of twelve inches; manufacturer certification confirming thickness, orientation and overlap is required, and the same dual‑layer polyethylene requirement applies under bridge approach slabs. General joint applications may employ an expanded low‑density polyethylene (LDPE) foam barrier that must be produced by a manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15, conform to ASTM D5249, and have each shipment certified before placement. For sealant reservoirs a self‑adhesive tape whose width matches the reservoir is used as a bond breaker; it is installed on the bottom of the reservoir immediately after spall repair, cleaning and flushing, prior to sealing material placement, and its upward projection along the reservoir faces may not exceed one‑eighth inch, with dimensional compliance being the sole requirement. Together these criteria—minimum thicknesses, flash‑point thresholds, coverage extents, timing constraints, durability provisions such as storage conditions and retesting after six months, and mandatory certification—constitute the reliability framework that a bond breaker must satisfy to function effectively without compromising pavement integrity. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 182–184), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 359–433), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–691)

Structural section and dimensions

The bond‑breaker specifications impose only a few explicit size limits on the materials that are used in conjunction with pavement structures. For reinforced dowel bars of Type B, the lubricant designated as a bond‑breaker must be applied to at least half of each bar’s length before paving, whether the coating is performed onsite or supplied from the shop. When a full‑depth polystyrene board serves as a bond‑breaker in longitudinal joints of Type A and B patches or corner repairs, the board must be one‑quarter inch thick; the accompanying saw cut that creates the joint is required to be one‑quarter inch wide and one inch deep. In applications where polyethylene sheeting functions as the bond‑breaker, two sheets each having a minimum thickness of 4.0 mil are placed and trimmed so that their edges coincide with the surface dimensions of the repaired patch. A tape‑type bond‑breaker positioned at the bottom of a joint reservoir is limited to a projection no greater than one‑eighth inch above the reservoir face. Apart from these items, the specification does not prescribe any dimensional requirements for the pavement layer itself, the base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab, shoulder, edge‑support, or other reinforcement elements. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–415)

Materials and mixture design

For unbonded concrete overlays the bond‑breaker may be provided either as a fine‑graded asphalt separation layer having a minimum thickness of one inch placed over the existing pavement or as a non‑woven geotextile interlayer used in lieu of the asphalt layer. When dowel bars are coated with a Type A coating, the inherent low bond strength eliminates any requirement for an additional shop‑applied or field‑applied lubricant. For dowels bearing a Type B coating, a bond‑breaking agent must be supplied either as a shop‑applied coating that satisfies the pull‑out load criteria of AASHTO M 254 or as a field‑applied lubricant meeting specific performance limits. The field lubricant must be a commercially available product approved for direct contact with steel, possess a flash point not less than 149 °F, and expressly exclude graphite‑based formulations; at least one‑half of each dowel’s length shall be coated by spraying or applying with a gloved hand, and the coating must be placed no later than one hour before concrete placement unless a shop coating has already been applied. For longitudinal joints in concrete patches classified as Type A or Type B, a full‑depth polystyrene board 1/4 inch thick is required as the physical barrier bond breaker, whereas no such board is used for Type C patches. When a membrane barrier is specified, two layers of polyethylene sheeting each having a minimum thickness of 4.0 mil are installed after removal of any existing bond‑breaker material, cut to match the exact surface dimensions, and in bridge‑approach slab applications the seams must run longitudinally, overlap by at least twelve inches, and present a smooth surface without excessive depth; both layers must be supplied with manufacturer certification confirming compliance. An alternative barrier may consist of expanded low‑density polyethylene foam produced by a manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15, conforming to ASTM D5249, with each shipment accompanied by the required certification as stipulated in Section 106.03(b)3. For sealant‑reservoir applications a self‑adhesive tape sized to the width of the reservoir serves as the bond breaker and no further material property or mixture ratio is prescribed. Where a geomembrane contacts a geotextile, an additional piece of geomembrane extending at least six inches beyond any adhesive overspray functions as the bond breaker, without a mandated composition. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 182–184), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 359–466), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–691)

The selection of a bond‑breaker material begins with identifying a product that can reliably isolate the existing pavement or structure from the new overlay while meeting structural separation, durability, constructability and performance criteria [1][2]. For unbonded concrete overlays the guides accept either a fine‑graded asphalt separation layer placed directly on the old concrete with a minimum thickness of one inch, or a non‑woven geotextile interlayer used in place of the asphalt film, each providing the required continuous barrier and compatible surface for the overlay mix [1]. When the application involves bridge approach slabs, the recommended system consists of two sheets of polyethylene sheeting, each at least 4.0 mil thick, stacked to create a smooth, shallow surface that does not add excessive depth beneath the slab [2]. The polyethylene sheets must be installed with all joints oriented in the longitudinal direction of the bridge and overlapped by no less than twelve inches to maintain continuity of the separation layer [2]. Regardless of material type, manufacturers are required to supply certification confirming that the product meets the prescribed thickness, strength, and durability specifications, thereby assuring that the bond‑breaker will perform as intended throughout service life [2]. The chosen material must also be compatible with the overlying pavement mixture; the fine‑graded asphalt layer is designed to work with concrete overlay mixes, while the polyethylene sheeting provides a smooth substrate that does not interfere with slab placement or bonding of subsequent layers [1][2]. By adhering to these thickness minima, continuity requirements, and certification provisions, the bond‑breaker satisfies structural separation, resists premature bonding under traffic loads, facilitates straightforward installation, and contributes to the long‑term performance of the pavement system [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 387)

Structural details and interfaces

When an unbonded concrete overlay is placed, the existing slab is separated from the new layer by either a fine‑graded asphalt separation sheet at least one inch thick or a non‑woven geotextile interlayer [1]. The joints in the overlay are laid out exactly as they would be for a brand‑new concrete pavement, using standard joint spacing based on the overlay’s thickness and type, and they do not need to line up with any transverse joints in the underlying slab [1]. No special reinforcement or load‑transfer devices beyond those required for normal new‑concrete joints are called for in this situation [1]. When dowel bars are incorporated, the need for a bond‑breaker depends on the coating type; a Type A coating provides sufficiently low bond strength so that no shop‑applied breaker or lubricant is required, whereas a Type B coating requires a bond‑breaker that satisfies the pull‑out load criteria of AASHTO M 254 and may be applied either in the fabrication shop or on site [2]. In all cases the dowel locations are marked so that transverse joints can be sawed directly over the bar centre, and every load‑transfer unit is placed and firmly anchored before paving with alignment tolerances as specified by the guides [2]. For longitudinal joints in Type A and Type B patch or corner repairs a full‑depth quarter‑inch polystyrene board serves as the bond‑breaker, while Type C joints receive none [2]. When a dowel bar crosses such a joint, at least half of its length must be treated with a commercially available steel‑compatible lubricant whose flash point is not less than 149 °F and that is not graphite based, the lubricated portion being protected by a tube that provides a minimum one‑inch clearance over the treated end and applied at least one hour before concrete placement unless pre‑applied in the shop [2]. If an existing bond‑breaker must be removed during patch work, it is stripped and replaced with two layers of polyethylene sheeting each a minimum of four mil thick, cut to the repair dimensions, and no additional reinforcement, tie bars or transition details are required [2]. When a joint is prepared for a dowel bar, a slot is cut to mid‑depth and a quarter‑inch‑thick preformed cellular polystyrene insert matching the slot cross‑section is placed, providing an opening for the bar; a non‑metallic expansion cap one and a half inches high is positioned with a quarter‑inch clearance from the dowel end, and plastic or other non‑metal chairs maintain at least a half‑inch gap between the cap and all slot surfaces, after which the temporary joint material is sealed on the sides and bottom before being filled with concrete patching material [2]. Load‑transfer unit samples must be submitted at least forty‑five days prior to paving; each sample must contain a minimum of six dowel bars (or three for bars listed in Bulletin 15), all with bond‑breaker lubricant pre‑applied, and no further reinforcement, tie bars, transition or connection details are prescribed beyond these provisions [2]. For interfaces between geomembrane and geotextile, a strip of geomembrane extending at least six inches beyond any area where adhesive overspray might occur acts as the bond‑breaker, with no joints, reinforcement or load‑transfer devices required [2]. In rigid pavement joint rehabilitation the approved bond‑breaker is a tape—either duct tape or a self‑adhesive tape—cut to the width of the sealant reservoir and placed on the bottom of the freshly cut reservoir after cleaning, with its upper edge not projecting more than one‑eighth inch above the reservoir face; this requirement applies to both Type 1 and Type 2 joint rehabilitations, the latter also requiring removal of any protruding steel plate before forming the reservoir [2]. Transverse relief joints between asphalt and underlying concrete use the same tape bond‑breaker placed in a shallow one‑inch‑deep by one‑half‑inch‑wide reservoir with the same projection limit, and when a joint backing material is specified an expanded low‑density polyethylene foam meeting ASTM D5249 is installed between the joint filler and sealant, each shipment accompanied by certification, without any additional reinforcement or load‑transfer components [2]. Bridge approach slabs employ two layers of polyethylene sheeting each at least four mil thick laid on a smooth surface, with longitudinal seams overlapping a minimum of twelve inches and certified by the supplier; across all these applications the bond‑breaker itself constitutes the sole structural detail required to prevent unintended bonding, and no extra reinforcement, load‑transfer systems, transitions or connections are mandated beyond the material placements and installation steps described [2].

When a pavement design calls for an unbonded concrete overlay, the interface between the existing slab and the new concrete is deliberately separated by placing a fine‑graded asphalt layer at least one inch thick or, alternatively, a non‑woven geotextile interlayer over the old surface before the fresh concrete is placed; the joints of the overlay are then laid out as for any new concrete pavement without attempting to align them with the underlying joint pattern. For dowel‑bar connections, the guides first classify the bar coating: a Type A coating develops sufficiently low bond strength with concrete so that no shop‑applied bond breaker or lubricant is required, whereas a Type B coating creates appreciable bond and therefore mandates a bond‑breaker material that satisfies the pull‑out load requirements of AASHTO M 254. When a bond breaker is needed for Type B bars, it may be supplied as a pre‑applied lubricant on the dowel or applied in the field, and at least half of the length of each bar must receive the lubricant unless the shop has already treated it; the lubricant must be commercially available, approved for steel contact, have a flash point not less than 149 °F, contain no graphite, and be mixed, stirred and applied by spray, daubing, mopping or with a gloved hand, with brushes prohibited, and it must be placed no later than one hour before concrete placement to prevent unintended adhesion while allowing proper positioning. If dowel bars coated with a shop‑applied bond breaker are stored outdoors for more than six months, they must be retested in accordance with AASHTO M 254 or receive an additional coat of lubricant. For longitudinal joints in Type A and Type B patches and corner repairs, a full‑depth quarter‑inch polystyrene board is installed as the bond breaker, while Type C joints receive none. When existing pavement was constructed over a previous bond‑breaker material, that material is removed and two layers of polyethylene sheeting at least four mil thick are laid directly on the prepared subbase, cut to match the patch dimensions, thereby providing a new non‑bonding interface before fresh concrete placement. At sealant reservoirs, an acceptable self‑adhesive tape whose width exactly matches the reservoir is placed on the bottom of the reservoir immediately before sealing; the tape is trimmed so that it does not project more than one‑eighth inch above the reservoir faces, and duct tape may be used provided it spans the full width. When a joint filler material must be separated from a joint sealer, expanded low‑density polyethylene foam conforming to ASTM D5249 may be installed as the intervening layer. For bridge approach slabs, two layers of polyethylene sheeting at least four mil thick are placed beneath the pavement; seams run longitudinally along the bridge and overlap a minimum of twelve inches, creating a smooth surface without excessive depth, and manufacturer certification confirming compliance with thickness, seam and overlap requirements must accompany the material. In geotextile‑to‑geomembrane interfaces adhesive bonding is prohibited; if overspray cannot be avoided, an additional piece of geomembrane extending at least six inches beyond the overspray zone is inserted to act as a bond breaker between the two membranes. Prior to installing any bond‑breaker—whether tape, foam, polystyrene board or polyethylene sheeting—all joint surfaces are cleaned and media‑blasted to remove oil, moisture and other contaminants, ensuring that adhesion occurs only where intended. The required documentation for each interface includes test reports, shop drawings, and, when applicable, a Long‑Life Transfer Unit sample containing at least six pre‑treated dowel bars submitted well in advance of paving for representative approval; only one dowel‑bar type may be used on a long‑life concrete project unless special approval is obtained. Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Bond‑breaker materials must be protected from moisture and climatic exposure throughout storage and installation. They are to be stored indoors or otherwise shielded in accordance with ASTM A775, and any coating left exposed for more than six months must be either retested according to AASHTO M 254 or receive an additional coat of lubricant before use. During joint preparation the reservoir floor is flushed immediately after sawing—whether a dry or wet cut—to remove standing water, and the tape‑type bond breaker is placed directly on the bottom of the reservoir just prior to sealing. All vertical faces are media‑blasted and then dried with high‑pressure compressed air at approximately 100 psi to ensure surfaces are free of oil, moisture, and other contaminants. The tape is limited to a maximum protrusion of one‑eighth inch above the joint face, which prevents water from accumulating against the barrier and thereby controls drainage within the joint. Sealing material may only be applied when ambient temperature is between 40 °F and 90 °F unless an exception is granted, ensuring that temperature extremes do not compromise the bond‑breaker performance. The bond‑breaker required for pavement repairs must meet a series of geometric, safety and user‑operation criteria. For rigid‑pavement patches classified as Type A or B, and for corner repairs, a full‑depth polystyrene board one‑quarter inch thick is placed in the longitudinal joint; Type C joints receive no board. All longitudinal joints are saw‑cut to a width of one‑quarter inch and a depth of one inch with the cut centered over the joint. Where a polymeric sheet is specified, two layers of polyethylene sheeting each at least four mil thick are laid over the prepared base, trimmed precisely to the dimensions of the patch or bridge approach slab so that the surface remains smooth without excessive buildup. In bridge applications the seams run longitudinally and must overlap by no less than twelve inches; the material must be supplied with a manufacturer’s certification confirming compliance. When a tape bond‑breaker is used, it is a self‑adhesive strip whose width exactly matches the sealant reservoir and it is positioned on the bottom of the reservoir immediately before sealing, extending no more than one‑eighth inch up the faces. After the joint is sawed, the cavity is flushed with water, all vertical faces are media‑blasted, and a 100 psi stream of compressed air is applied to remove oil, moisture and other contaminants before the tape is placed. The bond‑breaker material must be stored indoors or otherwise protected in accordance with ASTM A775. If exposure exceeds six months after the coating has been applied, the contractor must retest the material per AASHTO M‑254. The lubricant used with dowel bars must be a commercially available product approved for steel contact, possessing a flash point of at least 149 °F; graphite‑based formulations are prohibited. The lubricant is to be stirred before use and applied by daubing, mopping or with a gloved hand—brushes are not permitted—and it must coat at least one‑half the length of Type B coated dowel bars unless the coating was already applied in the shop. Application shall occur at least one hour prior to concrete placement. These requirements collectively ensure that the bond‑breaker provides the necessary geometric fit, maintains safety during handling and storage, and can be applied by field personnel using prescribed procedures without additional tooling. Verification and expected performance

The completed bond breaker is verified through a series of documented checks that address specification compliance, constructability, performance, and alignment with project requirements. First, for dowel‑bar applications a commercially available lubricant whose flash point is not less than 149 °F and which is approved for steel contact must be applied; graphite‑based products are excluded. The contractor demonstrates constructability by storing shop‑applied bond breakers indoors or in protected conditions as required by ASTM A775, and any units that remain exposed beyond six months are either retested according to AASHTO M‑254 or receive an additional coating of the approved lubricant. Compliance is further confirmed through Long‑Term Use (LTU) testing: a sample containing at least three (or six) dowel bars pre‑coated with the lubricant is submitted together with complete test reports, shop drawings and manufacturer certification at least forty‑five days before paving, and no concrete placement proceeds until the representative reviews and approves the documentation and verifies that the test results meet the required criteria. Only a single type of dowel bar may be used unless a specific authorization is obtained, ensuring consistency across the pavement structure. For bridge approach slabs the bond breaker consists of two polyethylene sheets each meeting a minimum thickness of 4.0 mil; the installed surface must be smooth and free of excessive material depth, seams are required to run longitudinally along the bridge and overlap by at least twelve inches, and a manufacturer’s certification confirming these material properties must be provided. In joint‑sealant applications a tape bond breaker is placed on the bottom of the sealant reservoir immediately before the sealing material is applied; its protrusion above the reservoir face is limited to one‑eighth inch and the adjoining joint surfaces are thoroughly cleaned and flushed as shown in the standard drawings. These placement tolerances, together with the documented cleaning steps, verify that the bond breaker will isolate the joint surfaces effectively, thereby satisfying performance expectations. Across all applications the combination of material specifications, storage and handling procedures, testing documentation, and dimensional checks ensures that the installed bond breaker complies with the design intent, can be constructed as planned, performs its intended function throughout service life, and remains consistent with the overall project requirements.
