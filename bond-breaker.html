Bond Breaker

Part of: Concrete Overlay is-subtopic-of
Bond Breaker is a topic under Concrete Overlay.
bond-breaker
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Bond breaker is a material system used in rigid pavement and related structural repairs to create an intentional unbonded interface between existing concrete or asphalt substrates and newly placed overlays or adjacent components, thereby preventing unintended adhesion that would transmit distress or restrict designed movement[1][2]. It provides a low‑shear barrier—commonly a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt separation layer, a non‑woven geotextile interlayer, two 4 mil polyethylene sheets overlapped at least twelve inches longitudinally, a quarter‑inch polystyrene board in joint cuts, or a lubricating coating applied to at least half the length of Type B dowel bars—to isolate the new layer from the old while maintaining durability against moisture and temperature extremes[1][2]. This controlled separation allows the overlay or repair to act as an independent structural element, preserving ride quality, preventing reflective cracking, and extending service life by eliminating paths for distress transmission[1][2]

Purpose and design objectives

Bond breakers are employed to create an intentional unbonded interface between existing pavement or structural elements and new overlay or adjacent components, preventing unintended adhesion that would otherwise transmit distress or restrict designed movement. By separating a deteriorated concrete slab from a fresh asphalt or concrete overlay with a minimum 1‑inch fine‑graded asphalt separation layer or with two 4 mil polyethylene sheets overlapped at least twelve inches longitudinally, the overlay can act as a new structural layer on a stable platform without relying on the condition of the old base. The unbonded condition also eliminates the path for reflective cracking and differential movement to be transferred from the underlying slab into the overlay, thereby preserving capacity and extending service life. In jointed concrete pavements, dowel bars that are coated with a Type B coating develop bond strength that would lock the steel to the surrounding cement; applying a bond‑breaker lubricant to at least one‑half of the dowel length, placed at least one hour before concrete placement and protected by a 1‑inch clearance tube, prevents this locking and allows joint slip as intended. The same principle applies to precast slabs where coating the embedded portion of dowels prevents grout from bonding, ensuring independent slab movement. For bridge‑approach slabs or new concrete overlays, inserting a thin polyethylene separation layer—typically two 4 mil sheets with seams overlapped a minimum twelve inches—breaks bond between the fresh slab and the underlying base, facilitating differential movement and future removal without damaging the subbase. In geotechnical installations, an additional geomembrane extending at least six inches beyond any adhesive overspray acts as a barrier to keep overlying geotextile from adhering, preserving independent behavior of each layer. Expanded low‑density polyethylene foam conforming to ASTM D5249 may be used between joint filler material and joint sealer to prevent bonding that would reduce joint flexibility and cause seal failure. Thus the engineering problem addressed by bond breakers is unintended adhesion that can transmit distress, restrict movement, lock dowels, or impede future removal, and the design provides a controlled separation to meet these performance needs. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 178–179), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–435), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–678)

The primary functional objective of a bond breaker is to provide a reliable low‑shear barrier that prevents any unintended adhesive connection between the existing pavement substrate and the newly placed overlay or repair material, thereby allowing the new layer to act as an independent structural element and preserving ride quality throughout service life. [1][2] To satisfy this function the bond breaker must be capable of maintaining its non‑bonding performance over the intended design period without degrading, while also permitting the designed slip and load transfer between layers where required. [2] The guides prescribe a fine‑graded asphalt separation layer of at least one inch thickness as an acceptable material for unbonded concrete overlays, or alternatively a non‑woven geotextile interlayer that can serve the same purpose. [1] For full‑depth patch repairs classified as Type A or B, the required separator is a polystyrene board cut to a width of one quarter inch and a depth of one inch, providing a continuous barrier across the longitudinal joint. [2] When an existing pavement has been constructed over a prior bond breaker, the old material must be removed and replaced with two layers of polyethylene sheeting each having a minimum thickness of 4.0 mil, cut to the patch dimensions, with seams oriented longitudinally and overlapped at least twelve inches, accompanied by manufacturer certification confirming compliance. [2] The structural objective also includes dimensional limits for auxiliary bond‑breaking elements such as tape placed in sealant joint reservoirs, which may not project more than one eighth inch above the reservoir face so that sealant can fill the joint unobstructed. [2] In membrane applications the bond breaker must extend at least six inches beyond any overspray zone to keep the geomembrane from bonding to the underlying geotextile. [2] Durability criteria require the selected material to resist moisture intrusion, remain effective within the temperature range specified for sealant placement (generally 40 °F to 90 °F unless otherwise approved), and retain its separating action throughout the pavement’s service life. [1][2] Safety considerations dictate that any bond‑breaking lubricant used with dowel‑bar coatings must have a flash point not less than 149 °F, be approved for direct steel contact, and graphite‑based formulations are prohibited; the lubricant must be applied by daubing, mopping, or with a gloved hand rather than with brushes. [2] The installation procedure also requires that the lubricant be stirred and applied at least one hour before concrete placement to ensure uniform film formation and avoid premature bonding. [2] Serviceability objectives encompass ensuring a smooth finished surface without excessive thickness of sheeting, proper overlap of polyethylene seams, and documented material certification, all of which contribute to predictable performance and ease of future maintenance. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 178–179), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 352–423), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–673)

Scope and applicability

Bond breaker is used in rigid concrete pavement systems that require an intentional non‑bonded condition at steel interfaces such as load‑transfer units or dowel bars. When dowel bars are coated with a Type B material, which develops significant bond strength, a shop‑applied bond‑breaker meeting the pull‑out load requirements of AASHTO M 254 must be provided; Type A coatings, which develop sufficiently low bond strength, do not require a bond breaker. In those cases the lubricant or coating is applied to at least one‑half of the dowel length in the field unless it was already lubricated in the shop, and the material must be allowed to set for a minimum of one hour before concrete placement, a requirement that also applies to high‑performance dowel bar installations and Long Life Concrete projects where laboratory LTU samples containing pre‑lubricated dowels are submitted for verification. For patching operations on rigid pavements a 1/4‑inch full‑depth polystyrene board is placed in the longitudinal joint of Type A and Type B patches or corner repairs performed one lane at a time, while no board is provided for Type C patches; the same Type B dowel lubricant requirement governs these repair patches. In precast concrete slab systems the portion of each dowel that will be embedded in grout within the slab is coated with bond‑breaker prior to slab placement to prevent unintended bonding. If an existing pavement was originally constructed over a legacy bond‑breaker material, that material must be removed and replaced by two layers of polyethylene sheeting at least 4.0 mil thick, with longitudinal seams overlapping a minimum of twelve inches and accompanied by manufacturer certification, a configuration also specified beneath bridge approach slabs built on rigid pavement. The bond‑breaking lubricant itself must be a commercially available product approved for steel contact, have a flash point not less than 149 °F, and graphite‑based lubricants are prohibited. For unbonded concrete overlays (JPCP) placed over an existing concrete pavement that is moderately to significantly deteriorated but can serve as a stable platform, a fine‑graded minimum one‑inch asphalt separation coat or a non‑woven geotextile interlayer serves as the bond‑breaker layer, preventing adhesion between the old slab and the new overlay while allowing the overlay to act as an independent structural layer. For sealant reservoirs that require an unbonded joint, a self‑adhesive tape the width of the reservoir may be applied to the bottom of the cut reservoir immediately before sealing; this practice is required for both Type 1 and Type 2 joint rehabilitations as well as for transverse joints connecting an asphalt relief joint to underlying concrete, with sealant placement limited to ambient temperatures between 40 °F and 90 °F unless a waiver is granted. An expanded low‑density polyethylene foam listed in Bulletin 15 and conforming to ASTM D5249 may also be used as a bond breaker between joint filler material and joint sealer regardless of pavement type. When geomembrane and geotextile are installed together and adhesive overspray threatens the geomembrane, an additional piece of geomembrane extending at least six inches beyond the overspray zone is placed to act as a barrier, thereby providing a bond‑breaking function. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 178–179), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 352–436), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–678)

When the existing pavement shows significant structural deterioration, unstable support, stripping of asphalt, or unresolved drainage problems that have not been corrected, a bonded overlay that relies on adhesion is not advisable and designers should turn to alternatives such as unbonded concrete overlays, cracking/breaking and seating, or rubblizing, which either insert a separation layer or completely break the bond to provide a stable rehabilitation platform. Likewise, when a dowel‑bar coating is classified as Type A, its inherently low bond strength with concrete satisfies AASHTO M 254 pull‑out load requirements, allowing designers to omit any shop‑applied bond breaker or lubricant and rely solely on the coating. In contrast, for pavement patches identified as Type C, specifications expressly prohibit placing a 1/4‑inch full‑depth polystyrene board bond breaker in longitudinal joints; instead tie bars must be installed, making an alternative design approach mandatory and precluding use of conventional bond breakers. Consequently, across the guides the presence of major structural deficiencies, unresolved drainage issues, low‑bond Type A coatings, or Type C patch conditions constitute limitations that make unbonded overlays, crack‑and‑seat methods, rubblizing, omission of bond breakers, or tie‑bar installations preferable to conventional bond‑breaker applications. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–402), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–673)

Design inputs and requirements

The use of a bond‑breaker to prevent adhesion between an existing concrete pavement and a new unbonded concrete overlay is required to be a fine‑graded asphalt separation layer at least one inch thick, or alternatively a non‑woven geotextile interlayer as prescribed in the relevant specifications. [1] For rigid pavements that incorporate load‑transfer units, the governing national specification AASHTO M 254 defines two coating types; Type A coatings develop sufficiently low bond strength so that no additional breaker is needed, while Type B coatings create enough adhesion to require a shop‑applied or field‑applied bond‑breaker lubricant meeting the pull‑out load provisions of AASHTO M 254. [2] Contract sections further mandate that for any Type B‑coated dowel at least one‑half of its length must receive a field‑applied bond‑breaker lubricant unless the lubricant was already applied in the shop, and the application must occur no later than one hour before concrete is placed around the assembly. [2] The same half‑length requirement applies to dowels located in transverse joints, while longitudinal joint repairs that are classified as Type A or Type B patches and corner repairs require a full‑depth 1/4‑inch polystyrene board as the bond‑breaker, with its use expressly prohibited in Type C patches. [2] When dowels are cast into precast slabs, the coating requirement is extended to any portion of the dowel that becomes embedded, as stipulated in the contract specifications. [2] The lubricant itself must be a commercially available product with a flash point not less than 149 °F, approved for steel contact by the manufacturer, and graphite‑based lubricants are excluded; it may be applied by spray or with a gloved hand but brushes are prohibited. [2] Tape‑type bond‑breakers are defined as self‑adhesive duct tape cut to match the width of the sealant reservoir, placed on the bottom of each joint reservoir immediately before sealing, limited to extending no more than one‑eighth inch up the face of the reservoir, and may only be installed when ambient temperature is between 40 °F and 90 °F unless a Representative grants an exception. [2] When a foam bond‑breaker is specified, it must be expanded low‑density polyethylene foam that conforms to ASTM D5249, supplied by a manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15, with each shipment certified according to the applicable section of the contract. [2] For bridge approach slabs and for situations where an existing bond‑breaker is removed from a pavement base, the specifications require two layers of polyethylene sheeting at least 4.0 mil thick, cut to match the patch dimensions, with seams oriented longitudinally to the structure and overlapped a minimum of twelve inches; these new sheets become part of the construction scope without additional cost. [2] Documentation for load‑transfer units must include a sample LTU containing at least six dowels pre‑treated with bond‑breaker lubricant, accompanied by PTM No. 642 test reports or approved shop drawings submitted at least forty‑five days before paving, and only one dowel type may be used on a Long Life Concrete project unless an exception is granted; paving cannot commence until the Representative reviews and accepts the LTU package. [2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 178–179), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 352–455), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–678)

Design methods and criteria

The bond‑breaker concept is implemented through prescribed material layers and application steps rather than analytical calculations or design software [1][2]. To provide an unbonded concrete overlay, a fine‑graded asphalt separation layer at least one inch thick is placed between the existing pavement and the new overlay, with a non‑woven geotextile interlayer permitted as an alternative per Section 548 [1]. For dowel‑bar assemblies the guides classify coating types according to AASHTO M 254; Type A coatings exhibit sufficiently low bond strength with concrete and require no additional lubricant, whereas Type B coatings develop enough adhesion to mandate a shop‑applied or field‑applied bond‑breaker lubricant, and compliance is demonstrated by performing the prescribed pull‑out test [2]. The only analytical requirement for Type B assemblies is meeting the pull‑out load criteria of AASHTO M 254, with no design equations, empirical relationships, or specialized software tools required [2]. When a lubricant is needed it must be mixed thoroughly and applied to at least one‑half of the dowel length by spraying, daubing, mopping, or using a gloved hand—brushes are expressly prohibited—and the application must occur no less than one hour before concrete placement; if the lubricant was already applied in the fabrication shop the field step may be omitted [2]. Prior to paving the contractor submits a load‑transfer unit (LTU) sample containing three standard dowels (or six for high‑performance units) together with PTM 642 test reports, shop drawings and manufacturer certification at least forty‑five days before concrete placement, and concrete placement is withheld until a Representative verifies that the LTU passes the pull‑out test [2]. Dowel bars may not remain on site for more than thirty days without special approval [2]. When a longitudinal joint occurs in single‑lane or one‑at‑a‑time patching of either Type A or Type B patches, a full‑depth ¼‑inch polystyrene board is installed as a physical bond breaker within the joint [2]. If repair work is performed over existing pavement that was originally constructed on a bond‑breaker, the legacy material must be removed and replaced with two layers of polyethylene sheeting each having a minimum thickness of 4.0 mil, cut to match the patch dimensions; seams are laid longitudinally with at least twelve inches of overlap and the surface must be smooth without excessive depth [2]. The same dual‑layer configuration—minimum 4.0 mil per layer, longitudinal seam overlap of at least twelve inches, and manufacturer certification confirming compliance—is required beneath bridge approach slabs [2]. In situations where adhesive overspray contacts a geomembrane, an additional piece of geomembrane extending at least six inches beyond the overspray zone is installed to act as a bond breaker between the geomembrane and adjacent geotextile [2]. For joint reservoirs a tape‑type bond breaker is placed directly on the cleaned and dried bottom immediately before sealing; the tape must not project more than one‑eighth inch up the side of the reservoir, sealant may be applied only when ambient temperature lies between 40 °F and 90 °F unless a waiver is granted, and it is filled to leave a clearance of one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface [2]. Across all applications the specifications rely solely on these procedural steps, thickness thresholds, overlap requirements, timing constraints, and the pull‑out test; no further analytical models or design tools are prescribed [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 178–179), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–436), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–673)

In unbonded concrete overlays the bond‑breaker is required to be placed between the existing pavement and the new concrete and must have a minimum thickness of one inch when a fine‑graded asphalt layer is used, or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer may be substituted; the design assumes that no structural bond is needed between the overlay and the underlying slab [1]. For dowel bar installations the type of coating on the bars governs whether a shop‑applied lubricant is required: a Type A coating provides sufficiently low bond strength with concrete so that no additional bond‑breaker is needed, whereas a Type B coating develops appreciable bond strength and therefore mandates a lubricant that satisfies the pull‑out load requirements of AASHTO M 254 [2]. The lubricant must be a commercially available product approved for steel contact, have a flash point not less than 149 °F, and graphite‑based formulations are prohibited; it must be applied by spraying or with a gloved hand to at least one‑half of each bar’s length no later than one hour before concrete placement around the dowel assembly [2]. A protective tube providing a minimum clearance of one inch is required over the lubricated end of every coated dowel bar, and dowel bars bearing lubricant may be stored on site for no more than thirty days unless special approval is obtained [2]. Solid bond‑breakers are prescribed for certain joint configurations: a full‑depth polystyrene board exactly one‑quarter inch thick is placed in the longitudinal joint of all patches, with a saw cut centered over the joint that is one‑quarter inch wide and one inch deep [2]; when repairs involve pavement originally constructed on a bond‑breaker, the existing material must be removed and replaced by two layers of polyethylene sheeting each at least 4.0 mil thick, with seams running longitudinally, a minimum twelve‑inch overlap, and a smooth surface free of excessive depth, accompanied by manufacturer certification [2]. The same dual‑layer polyethylene configuration is required under bridge‑approach slab joints, and any expanded low‑density polyethylene foam used as a bond‑breaker must conform to ASTM D5249, be supplied by a Bulletin 15 listed manufacturer, and each shipment must be certified according to the specified section before placement [2]. When a self‑adhesive tape serves as a bond‑breaker it shall match the width of the sealant reservoir, be placed at the base of the reservoir immediately after cleaning, and may not project more than one‑eighth inch above the reservoir face so that sealant thickness is not compromised [2]. Reliability of the bond‑breaker system is ensured through documented verification: a load‑transfer unit sample containing pre‑applied lubricant (six dowel bars for non‑Bulletin 15 dowels or three for Bulletin 15 dowels) must be submitted with test reports, shop drawings and other required paperwork at least forty‑five days before paving, and concrete placement may not commence until the Representative approves these documents and verifies passing LTU test results; in addition, if geotextile seaming adhesive oversprays onto an underlying geomembrane, an extra piece of geomembrane extending at least six inches beyond the overspray area must be installed to act as a bond‑breaker [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 178–179), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 352–455), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–678)

Structural section and dimensions

The bond‑breaker specification does not prescribe any size for the pavement, base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab, shoulder, edge‑support or most reinforcement elements except where explicitly called out. For reinforced concrete dowel bars of Type B, a bond‑breaker lubricant must be applied to at least one‑half of each bar’s length before placement (or when field‑coated as directed). In longitudinal joints of Type A and Type B patches and corner repairs a full‑depth polystyrene board 1/4 inch thick is required; no bond breaker is used in the longitudinal joint of Type C patches. A new bond‑breaker consisting of two polyethylene sheets, each at least 4 mil thick, must be cut to the exact surface dimensions of the concrete patch and placed in duplicate; when installed under bridge approach slabs the sheets are also overlapped longitudinally by a minimum of 12 inches. For sealant reservoirs a duct‑tape bond‑breaker is cut to match the reservoir width. Apart from these items, the specification provides no dimensional requirements for pavement, base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab, shoulder, edge‑support, or other reinforcement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–406)

The bond breaker for bridge approach slabs is specified as two layers of polyethylene sheeting, each at least 4.0 mil thick, laid on a smooth surface that does not create excessive depth. Seams must be placed in the longitudinal direction of the bridge and overlapped by no less than 12 inches, with manufacturer certification required for the material. The source does not provide guidance on joint spacing, cross‑slopes, transitions, or tie‑ins. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 378)

Materials and mixture design

The bond‑breaker must be identified by the type of material selected, which may include a fine‑graded asphalt separation layer, a non‑woven geotextile interlayer, a polystyrene board, polyethylene sheeting, a cement‑concrete interface lubricant, a self‑adhesive tape, an auxiliary geomembrane, or expanded low‑density polyethylene foam. When the asphalt option is used it shall be placed over the existing pavement and must have a minimum thickness of one inch; no additional mixture proportion limits are imposed beyond the requirement that the asphalt be fine‑graded. The non‑woven geotextile may be employed in lieu of the asphalt layer and is specified simply by its classification as a non‑woven fabric without further proportional constraints. For patch and corner repairs the specification calls for a full‑depth polystyrene board with a minimum thickness of one‑quarter inch placed in the longitudinal joint, regardless of whether the coating material is classified as Type A or Type B under AASHTO M 254. An alternative physical barrier consists of two layers of polyethylene sheeting, each layer required to be at least 4.0 mil thick; the sheets are trimmed to the patch dimensions, laid over the pavement base, and must present a smooth surface with all seams oriented longitudinally and overlapped by no less than twelve inches. When a lubricant‑based bond breaker is specified, the product must be a commercially available cement‑concrete interface lubricant that has a flash point of not less than 149 °F, is approved for contact with steel, and expressly excludes graphite‑based formulations; for Type B coated dowel bars the lubricant shall cover at least one‑half of each bar’s length and the lubricated end must be protected by a tube providing a minimum one‑inch clearance unless the lubrication was performed in the shop. A tape‑type bond breaker may be used provided it is a self‑adhesive tape cut to the width of the sealant reservoir, such as duct tape, and it must not project more than one‑eighth inch above the reservoir face when placed on the bottom of the joint. In geomembrane applications an additional piece of geomembrane is required between the primary geomembrane and the geotextile, extending at least six inches beyond any adhesive overspray, and the geotextile must remain unbonded to the geomembrane during adhesive application. If expanded low‑density polyethylene foam is selected as the bond breaker, it must be supplied by a manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15, conform to ASTM D5249, and each shipment must be certified according to the procedure outlined in Section 106.03(b)3. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 178–179), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 352–455), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–678)

For an unbonded concrete overlay the bond‑breaker is supplied by a fine‑graded asphalt separation layer that must be at least one inch thick, or alternatively by a non‑woven geotextile interlayer, both of which create the required structural separation while offering durability under traffic and ease of placement compatible with the overlay mix. [1] In situations where specifications call for a different type of bond breaker, two sheets of polyethylene sheeting each having a minimum thickness of 4 mil are prescribed, providing a double‑layer configuration that delivers the necessary separation between pavement layers or between pavement and adjacent structures. [2] The combined thickness of the polyethylene layers is required not to be less than 4 mil, ensuring sufficient structural integrity and resistance to traffic stresses over the service life. [2] Before installing any bond‑breaker material the existing barrier must be stripped from the base so that the new layer rests directly on a prepared, smooth surface, a step that eliminates stress concentrations and supports long‑term durability. [2][1] The bond‑breaker is cut to the exact dimensions of the treated area and placed tightly against the substrate, which simplifies construction operations and guarantees a continuous separation throughout the pavement system. [2][1] For polyethylene sheets the seams are laid in the longitudinal direction of the element and overlapped by at least 12 inches, preventing leakage of bonding agents and contributing to overall performance reliability. [2] Manufacturers must supply certification confirming that the polyethylene meets the prescribed thickness, smoothness, and overlap requirements, thereby verifying compliance with structural and durability criteria. [2] Across the guides, both asphalt or geotextile layers and polyethylene sheets are selected for their compatibility with typical overlay mixes, ensuring that the bond‑breaker does not adversely affect the mechanical behavior of the pavement while meeting performance objectives. [1][2]

Structural details and interfaces

When a bond‑breaker is employed to create an unbonded concrete overlay, the existing slab is separated from the new layer by either a fine‑graded asphalt sheet that is at least one inch thick or a non‑woven geotextile interlayer, and the overlay joints are laid out according to the standard joint spacing rules for new concrete pavements without any requirement to line up with transverse joints in the underlying slab; no special reinforcement or load‑transfer devices beyond those normally required for new‑concrete joints are called for [1]. Bond‑breaker is also mandated wherever load‑transfer units such as dowel‑bar assemblies are used, in the longitudinal joints of Type A and B patches and corner repairs, when an existing expansion joint is replaced, at the edges of precast slabs, within joint sealant reservoirs, at transverse relief joints where asphalt relief joints meet underlying concrete, and beneath bridge approach slabs [2]. For the longitudinal joints of Type A and B patches a full‑depth 1/4‑inch polystyrene board serves as the bond‑breaker, whereas Type C patches rely on tie bars instead of a board; when an existing expansion joint is replaced a 3/4‑inch expansion‑joint filler is placed in the joint [2]. Under bridge approach slabs the required bond‑breaker consists of two layers of polyethylene sheeting each at least 4.0 mil thick, laid on a smooth surface with longitudinal seams overlapping a minimum of twelve inches; no reinforcement or load‑transfer system is prescribed for this application [2]. In sealant reservoirs the bond‑breaker is provided as a self‑adhesive tape cut to the full width of the reservoir and placed on the bottom of the groove immediately before sealant placement, with the tape limited to projecting no more than one‑eighth inch up the faces and any steel plate intrusion trimmed to leave no more than a quarter‑inch remaining; this system does not call for additional reinforcement or load‑transfer devices [2]. When dowel bars are used as load‑transfer units, at least half of each dowel’s length must be coated with a bond‑breaker lubricant unless the coating was applied in the shop, and the dowels are positioned within temporary slots cut from 1/4‑inch cellular polystyrene that match the slot dimensions; non‑metallic chairs provide a minimum clearance of 0.5 inch around the dowel cap and 1½‑inch expansion caps leave a 0.25‑inch gap between the cap end and the bottom of the cap, while alignment tolerances limit vertical tilt or horizontal skew to no more than 0.25 inch at each end and overall translational displacement to one inch, and the assemblies must be staked, anchored, and remain secured during paving [2]. Sample panels for load‑transfer units must include pre‑coated dowels, with high‑performance dowel panels requiring a minimum of six dowels and bar systems requiring at least three, and all slot surfaces must be cleaned within 30 minutes before concrete placement [2]. For precast slab edges the portion of each dowel that will be cast into the slab and the vertical slab edges are coated with bond‑breaker prior to grout placement; if an older bond‑breaker layer is encountered it must be removed and replaced by two sheets of polyethylene sheeting cut to match the patch dimensions [2]. If adhesive overspray threatens bonding a geomembrane to a geotextile, an additional piece of geomembrane extending at least six inches beyond the overspray zone is inserted as a supplemental bond‑breaker layer [2].

When an unbonded concrete overlay is required, the existing pavement and the new concrete slab are deliberately separated by placing a fine‑graded asphalt separation layer at least one inch thick over the old surface, or alternatively installing a non‑woven geotextile interlayer before the fresh concrete is placed.[1] The joints of the overlay are then laid out in the same manner as for any new concrete slab and are not required to align with the transverse joints of the underlying pavement.[1] Bond‑breaker design for dowel bars begins with classifying the coating type; a Type A coating provides sufficiently low bond strength that no additional shop‑applied breaker or field lubricant is needed, whereas a Type B coating must be paired with a bond‑breaking material that satisfies the pull‑out load criteria of AASHTO M 254.[2] For all Type B dowels at least half of the bar length receives a spray‑applied or gloved‑hand lubricant no less than one hour before concrete placement, and brushes are expressly prohibited; the same lubrication requirement applies to dowels cast into precast slabs, covering both the embedded portion and the vertical slab edges as dictated by the approved installation plan.[2] In longitudinal joints of Type A and Type B patches or corner repairs a full‑depth quarter‑inch polystyrene board is installed as a bond breaker, while Type C joints receive no such material.[2] When repairing a rigid pavement that originally contained a bond breaker, the existing material must be removed and replaced by two layers of polyethylene sheeting each at least four mil thick, trimmed to the patch dimensions, in order to re‑establish the non‑bonded interface.[2] Across other interfaces the same isolation principle is applied; where a geotextile meets a geomembrane adhesive is applied only to the overlapping geotextile and never allowed to contact the membrane, and any overspray is mitigated by inserting an additional piece of geomembrane that extends at least six inches beyond the affected area to act as a barrier.[2] Steel components are treated with a commercially available bond‑breaker lubricant having a flash point of no less than 149 °F and approved for steel contact, while graphite‑based lubricants are prohibited.[2] Joint reservoirs receive a self‑adhesive tape cut to match the reservoir width and placed on the bottom of the reservoir immediately before sealant placement, with the tape limited to rising no more than one‑eighth inch above the reservoir face.[2] If a steel plate intrudes into a new joint, any portion within the reservoir is removed so that no more than a quarter‑inch protrudes into the sealed area.[2] Bridge approach slabs are protected by two layers of four‑mil polyethylene sheeting whose seams run longitudinally and overlap by at least twelve inches.[2] Asphalt relief joints are prepared by applying a thin coat of PG 64S‑22 to joint faces, saw‑cutting a reservoir one inch deep by half an inch wide, and then installing the tape bond breaker as described for other reservoirs.[2] Throughout all pavement‑to‑pavement, pavement‑to‑structure, and adjacent‑feature interfaces the bond‑breaker serves to prevent unintended adhesion while adhering to the specified material thresholds and dimensional tolerances outlined in the guides.[1][2]

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

The bond‑breaker system is protected from moisture and adverse climate by a series of installation controls. All surfaces that will receive the bond‑breaker must be kept dry; standing water, snow, ice or debris are not allowed on previously placed units before the next unit is positioned. Blocks are always set with their crown upward so that water cannot pond on them, and joints are tightened to leave only minimal gaps, limiting infiltration. When adhesive overspray contacts a geomembrane, an additional piece of geomembrane extending at least six inches beyond the overspray is installed to serve as a dedicated bond‑breaker between the geomembrane and any overlying geotextile, thereby preventing unintended bonding under wet conditions. De‑icing salts or other chemicals that could degrade the materials are prohibited. In joint construction, a strip of bond‑breaker tape is placed on the bottom of each reservoir immediately before sealant application. After a wet saw cut, the 1‑inch‑deep by ½‑inch‑wide reservoir must be flushed to remove any water and prevent standing moisture. All reservoir faces are required to be clean and dry prior to sealing; any compressed air used for cleaning must be free of oil or moisture. Sealant placement is permitted only when ambient temperature lies between 40 °F and 90 °F, ensuring that the bond‑breaker and sealant are not exposed to extreme temperatures that could impair performance. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 178–179), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 376–377)

The bond‑breaker must meet a coordinated set of geometric, safety, accessibility and user requirements that are dictated by the type of joint, the pavement repair method, and any associated structural elements. For longitudinal joints in Type A and Type B patches as well as corner repairs, a full‑depth polystyrene board one‑quarter inch thick is required; the joint must be saw‑cut to a width of one‑quarter inch and a depth of one inch with the cut centered over the joint. No bond‑breaker is permitted in Type C joints. Where a flexible barrier is specified, two layers of polyethylene sheeting each at least four mil thick are placed over the pavement base, trimmed to match the exact dimensions of the patch, and any existing bond‑breaker remaining on top of the base must be removed. The sheets must provide a smooth surface without adding excessive depth, their seams oriented in the longitudinal direction of the structure and overlapped by a minimum of twelve inches; certification from the manufacturer confirming compliance with these material specifications is required. In bridge approach slabs the same two‑layer polyethylene configuration is used, and the tape‑type bond‑breaker employed in sealant reservoirs must be self‑adhesive duct‑tape cut to the exact width of the reservoir, placed on the bottom of each joint immediately before sealing, and limited to an extension of no more than one‑eighth inch above the reservoir face. If adhesive overspray occurs, an additional piece of geomembrane extending at least six inches beyond the overspray limits must be installed to act as a bond‑breaker between the geomembrane and any geotextile, and the two layers must not be bonded during adhesive application.

Safety considerations govern the selection and handling of lubricants used with dowel‑bar load‑transfer units. The lubricant must be a commercially available product approved for steel contact, possess a flash point of at least 149 °F, and graphite‑based formulations are expressly prohibited. Application is performed by hand—using daubing, mopping or a gloved hand—and brushes are not allowed; the coating must be free of oil, moisture and other contaminants, and it must be applied at least one hour before concrete placement. For high‑performance dowel bars listed in Bulletin 15, an LTU sample accommodating a minimum of three dowel bars with pre‑applied lubricant must be submitted for verification at least forty‑five days prior to paving; for other dowels the sample must accommodate six bars. All joint faces must be thoroughly cleaned, flushed and blasted so that they are dry and free of contaminants before any sealing material is placed, and any compressed air used for cleaning must be oil‑free and moisture‑free. Sealing work may proceed only when ambient temperature is between 40 °F and 90 °F unless an exception is granted, and the sealing material itself must remain within the manufacturer’s approved temperature range and not be held at pouring temperature for longer than six hours without following supplier reheating instructions. The installation steps are required to preserve access to the joint for subsequent work, ensuring that the bond‑breaker does not impede future maintenance or inspection activities. [2]

Verification and expected performance

The completed bond‑breaker system is first checked against the project‑specified coating type; when a Type B coating is required, a shop‑applied bond breaker or compatible lubricant must be used and its pull‑out load verified in accordance with AASHTO M 254. A visual inspection using a 67.5‑V holiday detector confirms that holidays do not exceed two per linear foot on average and that any damaged areas have been repaired within the permitted percentage of surface area, demonstrating compliance with material specifications and constructability. Concurrently, the contractor must submit an LTU sample containing at least six dowel bars already coated with the bond‑breaker lubricant together with full test reports prepared to PTM No. 642, the shop drawings, and any other required documentation. These items are delivered to the project Representative no later than 45 days before paving; the Representative reviews the LTU results, confirms that pull‑out load and other performance criteria meet the specifications, and only after formal approval may concrete placement commence, ensuring performance and alignment with project requirements. Finally, the installed bond‑breaker layers are inspected to verify that two polyethylene sheets each at least 4 mil thick have been placed, that the exposed surface is smooth without excessive depth, and that all seams run longitudinally along the bridge with a minimum overlap of 12 inches. A manufacturer’s certification confirming thickness, smoothness, and seam compliance provides the final record of conformity, tying together constructability, performance, and overall project consistency.
