Base Course

Part of: Pavement Structure is-subtopic-of

Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-28

The base course is the structural layer placed beneath the surface treatment of both flexible and rigid pavements that supplies a uniform load‑bearing platform and rapidly drains surface and subsurface water, thereby preventing excessive deflection, rutting, bottom‑up cracking and loss of subgrade support [1][2]. It is constructed from highly permeable granular aggregates or treated mixes such as asphalt‑treated permeable base, cement‑treated permeable base, or an Asphalt Rich Base Course when thickness exceeds six inches, and must be placed at the optimum moisture content and compacted to at least 95 % of laboratory maximum dry density (or ≥96 % of the PTM‑402 target) within a limited time after water addition [1][2]. The resulting layer must achieve prescribed early‑age strengths—typically 300–500 psi unconfined compressive strength for chemically stabilized bases, at least 50 psi indirect tensile strength for asphalt‑stabilized mixes, and a minimum of 2,750 psi compressive strength before traffic is allowed—ensuring long‑term durability and a service life of about twenty years [1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

The base course is employed to remedy insufficient structural capacity of the pavement system and to manage moisture that would otherwise cause excessive deflection, rutting, bottom‑up cracking, or loss of subgrade support. Its primary performance need is to provide a uniform load‑bearing foundation that distributes vehicle stresses while allowing rapid gravity drainage of surface and subsurface water, thereby preventing “bathtub” conditions and preserving the bearing value of the underlying soil. To satisfy this need designers specify highly permeable granular or treated materials such as asphalt‑treated permeable base, cement‑treated permeable base, or Asphalt Rich Base Course when total thickness exceeds six inches, the latter delivering the highest tensile strength at the bottom of the pavement to improve resistance to cracking and moisture damage. The material must be placed at the proper moisture content, spread, bonded and compacted to achieve prescribed density targets—generally not less than 95 % of laboratory maximum dry density, at least 96 % of the PTM‑402 target, and in some cases 100 % of the maximum dry density—to develop the required strength without crushing aggregate or impairing drainage. Compaction must be completed within one hour after water is added to the aggregate and cement (extendable to 1½ hours with a retarding admixture) and must reach at least the specified densities, ensuring uniform thickness within a half‑inch tolerance and eliminating surface irregularities greater than that amount. Chemically stabilized bases are required to attain a seven‑day unconfined compressive strength of 300–500 psi or an indirect tensile strength of at least 50 psi, while asphalt‑stabilized bases must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 2,750 psi before traffic is permitted. In rehabilitation projects where cold recycling is appropriate, existing asphalt is milled to a prescribed depth, mixed with emulsified asphalt and compacted to a uniform thickness between three and five inches, producing a higher‑strength base suitable for roads carrying fewer than about 200 daily equivalent single axle loads. When the subgrade is frost‑susceptible or otherwise weak, a stronger granular base compensates for reduced bearing support, limiting differential heave during freeze‑thaw cycles and contributing to overall stability. For rigid‑pavement designs on interstates and expressways, the combined base and subbase must provide an effective modulus of subgrade reaction—15 000 psi for spring‑thaw, 30 000 psi for normal summer/fall, and 50 000 psi when frozen—using a weighted‑average loss‑of‑support factor to adjust the effective k‑value for potential erosion or pumping. During construction the base must be protected from marring, distortion and premature traffic; only essential local traffic is allowed and a minimum curing period (e.g., three days for cement‑treated bases or until moisture content falls below two percent) is required before opening to traffic. The base course is required to act as a stable load‑bearing platform that spreads vehicular stresses to the underlying subgrade while simultaneously allowing rapid removal of surface and subsurface water, thereby protecting the pavement from moisture‑related damage [1][2]. To achieve this function each lift is placed in compacted layers whose individual thicknesses are limited to between two and six inches for most mixes, with no layer thinner than three inches nor exceeding six inches for a 25 mm Superpave mix and four to eight inches for a 37.5 mm mix; when the total design depth exceeds these limits the course is built in two or more lifts of approximately equal thickness [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete bases the design thickness may range from four to eighteen inches, providing compressive strengths on the order of 4 000–10 000 psi, while asphalt‑rich base courses require a total thickness greater than six inches with the bottom three to six inches constructed from low‑traffic (<0.3 million ESAL) mixes containing 2.5 % air voids and a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25 mm to obtain the highest tensile strength at the pavement base [1]. All lifts must be compacted to achieve full maximum dry density—100 % of laboratory maximum for conventional bases, at least 96 % of the PTM‑402 target density for stabilized mixes, and not less than 95 % of the laboratory‑determined maximum for chemically‑stabilized material—with moisture content held within three percent of optimum and never more than three percent above it [2]. Chemically stabilized bases are required to reach an unconfined compressive strength of 300 to 500 psi after seven days, and asphalt‑stabilized mixes must develop a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi; the base may not be opened to traffic until a compressive strength of at least 2 750 psi is verified by cylinder testing or maturity methods [2]. Roller‑compacted concrete mixes need a minimum curing period of one week under low humidity and high temperature before the wearing course is placed, whereas emulsified‑asphalt bases must cure for at least two weeks or until average moisture content falls below two percent [1][2]. Durability is promoted by using highly permeable aggregates with large voids that prevent capillary rise, allowing gravity‑drainable water to be removed rapidly and reducing frost‑heave, rutting, surface roughness and bottom‑up cracking, which together can extend the pavement’s service life to about twenty years when supported by a properly prepared subgrade or subbase capable of sustaining compaction [1]. Placement on frozen, wet, or otherwise unstable surfaces is prohibited, as is placement when air or surface temperature is 35 °F (or 40 °F in certain specifications), and hot‑mix temperatures must be kept below 320 °F to ensure adequate coating of aggregate without segregation [2]. During construction the base course must be protected from all but essential local traffic; construction traffic is prohibited for at least three days after placement, and any damage caused by vehicles or equipment must be repaired before the wearing course is installed [2][1]. Safety considerations include providing a stable work platform for equipment, maintaining prescribed smoothness and grade tolerances (surface irregularities greater than one‑quarter inch are defective, and deviations exceeding one‑half inch over a 3 000 sq yd area must be corrected), and ensuring adequate surface texture—exposed roller‑compacted concrete on roads with speed limits of 35 mph or higher must be diamond‑ground to meet skid‑resistance requirements [1][2]. The finished base must provide a minimum cross‑slope of one‑quarter inch per foot to promote safe vehicle operation and drainage, and the final compacted depth must be within a half‑inch tolerance; cores that are deficient by one‑half inch or more are deemed defective and must be corrected immediately [2]. Serviceability is addressed by preserving ride quality through control of roughness (avoiding excessive International Roughness Index values), maintaining free‑draining conditions, and conducting random full‑depth core testing in each 3 000 sq yd to verify density, strength, smoothness and grade throughout the pavement’s service life [1][2].

Scope and applicability

Base course is considered appropriate for a broad spectrum of pavement types and project conditions, including both flexible and rigid structures [1][2]. In flexible pavements it functions as the layer directly beneath the surface course and is required whenever the approved structural design calls for an aggregate‑cement or aggregate‑lime/pozzolan base, with the two alternatives offered in the bid and thickness selected within allowable lift limits while balancing unit costs, minimum and maximum depths, and structural coefficients to meet the target structural number [1][2]. For asphalt‑concrete pavements the base course is used on all new or reconstructed sections, and when the total base thickness exceeds six inches an Asphalt Rich Base Course is placed in the bottom three to six inches of the first lift using mix designs limited to less than 0.3 million ESALs and a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25 mm to provide high tensile strength and resistance to bottom‑up cracking [1]. When the twenty‑year design traffic falls between 0.3 and less than 3.0 million ESALs the base course does not require a binder change, although polymer‑modified binders may be specified for severe rutting and fiber‑modified mixes may be used in the bottom lift with PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22 or PG 64E‑22 binders [1]. Superpave asphalt base courses of 25‑mm and 37.5‑mm gradations are acceptable provided the prepared surface is dry and the air or surface temperature exceeds 35 °F, and when a 25‑mm course requires more than six inches of compacted depth it must be placed in two or more lifts each between three and six inches, while a 37.5‑mm course deeper than eight inches must be laid in lifts each between four and eight inches [2]. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses stabilized with emulsified or foamed asphalt are suitable for rehabilitating existing asphalt pavements when at least two inches of sound in‑place asphalt is present, the underlying base and subgrade are structurally sound and well drained, traffic can be limited or detoured during construction and curing, and the mix is placed in compacted lifts of two to five inches achieving a minimum density of 96 percent of the PTM 402 target [1][2]. Placement of cold‑recycled base courses is restricted to periods when freezing has not occurred for at least twenty‑four hours and ambient temperature is forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or higher and rising, and seasonal restrictions prohibit placement from September 1 to April 30 in certain districts [2]. Projects with daily truck traffic exceeding two hundred ESALs, average daily traffic greater than roughly three thousand two‑lane vehicles, or poor drainage conditions should not employ cold recycling, and where such higher traffic is encountered polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended to improve early strength [1]. In rigid pavement systems the base layer provides structural support beneath concrete surface courses such as Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement or Roller Compacted Concrete and is normally constructed of Asphalt Treated Permeable Base or Cement Treated Permeable Base placed over a 2A subbase, with open‑graded subbase prohibited for concrete pavements [1]. All Interstate and Expressway rigid pavement designs require such an ATPB or CTPB layer on a 2A subbase, and the configuration may also be used in rehabilitation projects where the original pavement was built on OGS, ATPB or CTPB subject to coordination with the appropriate design team [1]. Roller‑compacted concrete can serve as a concrete base course when placed beneath an asphalt wearing course or other surface treatment on low‑volume roads having average daily traffic of two thousand vehicles or less and speed limits of fifty‑five miles per hour or lower, and it is also permitted for temporary pavements such as median crossovers or widening areas [1]. When used as a base, RCC is placed using a low‑slump stiff concrete mix with paving equipment and compacted to achieve the required thickness and density, resting on a prepared subgrade and subbase capable of supporting compaction during construction [1][2]. Across all applications the subbase must be fully prepared, inspected, corrected if necessary, protected from winter exposure before placement, and construction should be completed before the end of the paving season [2].

Design inputs and requirements

The design of a base‑course begins with a comprehensive site assessment that records the present condition of the pavement, its construction and maintenance history, and the quality of drainage. A distress survey is performed to document existing cracking, rutting or other failures and to identify the underlying causes so that any wet subgrade zones can be corrected before work proceeds. Traffic information must include both the current average daily traffic and an estimate of future loading; for a roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) base the roadway should not exceed 2 000 vehicles per day and the posted speed limit must be 55 mph or less, while projects that experience 200 or more equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) per day are generally excluded from cold‑recycling approaches. The anticipated traffic levels are used to size the structural layers and to evaluate life‑cycle performance.

Material data are obtained by sampling and testing the existing asphalt surface, base, subbase and subgrade. These tests verify the thickness of reclaimed aggregate, confirm that virgin aggregates meet gradation requirements, and define the composition of any reclaimed layer that will be incorporated into the new base. When an RCC mix is specified, the design calls for cement, a dense fine‑aggregate gradation with a maximum particle size under one inch, and a low water‑cement ratio in the range of 0.30 to 0.40; air entrainment may be used but admixtures are limited.

Geotechnical input consists of in‑situ testing such as falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD), dynamic cone penetrometer (DCP) or light weight deflectometer (LWD) to quantify subgrade stiffness and strength, and a verification that the prepared subgrade and any underlying subbase are capable of supporting the compaction effort required for the RCC layer. The presence of untreated reclaimed material, soils, or oversized stones must be ruled out.

Environmental considerations include climate effects on pavement life expectancy and the need for at least one week of curing under favorable weather—low humidity and high temperature—before the wearing course is placed. If the RCC base will remain exposed to traffic traveling 35 mph or faster, surface‑texturing provisions are required. Proper drainage must be ensured throughout the design period to prevent moisture‑related deterioration.

Together these site, traffic, material, geotechnical, environmental and existing‑condition inputs provide the basis for determining appropriate base‑course thicknesses and ensuring long‑term structural performance. [1]

The design and construction of a base course are governed by an integrated set of PennDOT specifications, Publication 408, AASHTO pavement‑design procedures, and project‑specific contractual manuals that together define structural capacity, drainage performance, and durability requirements [1][2]. Material eligibility is established in Publication 408 where recycled concrete aggregate may be used for subbase (Section 703.2(a)7) and reclaimed asphalt pavement is authorized for hot‑mix, warm‑mix, and cold‑recycled bases, while the Superpave Restricted Performance Specification (Section 413) prescribes the volumetric mix system, binder grade selection from Table 5.5, and an ESAL range for a 20‑year design life [1][2]. When the calculated ESAL falls between 0.3 and less than 3.0 million, the base course mix may remain unchanged; however, if the total base thickness exceeds six inches an Asphalt‑Rich Base Course (ARBC) must be placed in the lower three to six inches with a maximum aggregate size of 25 mm, air voids not exceeding 2.5 %, and an ESAL design below 0.3 million, and mix‑design diversity is limited to three designs per ordinary project or five for major projects, any deviation requiring PDAU approval [1][2]. Cold‑recycled base courses are defined in Publication 408 Sections 341 and 342 and follow the mix‑design procedures of Bulletin 27; they must be compacted to a thickness between three and five inches, cured for at least one week under low humidity and elevated temperature, and are limited to routes carrying fewer than two hundred daily ESALs or where at least two inches of existing asphalt is available for recycling, with heavier traffic corridors requiring polymer‑modified emulsions [1][2]. Full‑Depth Reclamation is addressed in Publication 408 Section 344 and Bulletin 27 Chapter 5, providing additional guidance for extensive rehabilitation projects [1]. Roller‑compacted concrete may be employed as a base course under the authority of Publication 408 Sections 502 and 323; design thickness ranges from four to eighteen inches and placement is restricted to roadways with an average daily traffic of 2,000 vehicles or less and a speed limit not exceeding 55 mph, while control‑joint spacing follows Publication 72M RC‑27M, being fifteen to twenty feet for sections thinner than eight inches and equal to the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet for thicker sections [1]. Drainage provisions are dictated by Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards for edge and outlet protection and by Publication 13M Design Manual Part 2 Chapter 10 Section 10.3.D for sizing base drains and determining outlet spacing; continuous base drains must be installed along pavement edges and the low side of superelevated sections, with all outlets protected from crushing or plugging to maintain system functionality [1]. For rigid‑pavement projects the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures executed through the AASHTOWare DARWin program are required, mandating input of traffic loading, serviceability limits, material moduli, reliability level, and a uniform drainage coefficient (mi = 1.0); the base layer must consist of approved permeable materials—Asphalt‑Treated Permeable Base or Cement‑Treated Permeable Base—bid as alternatives, rest on a 2A subbase for Interstate and Expressway alignments, prohibit open‑graded subbase, and allow drainage layers only where a positive outlet can be guaranteed, otherwise requiring a dense base with longitudinal edge drains [1]. Seasonal elastic‑modulus values are prescribed at 15 000 psi during spring thaw, 30 000 psi under normal conditions, and 50 000 psi when frozen, while the resilient‑modulus ratio of subbase to native soil may not exceed four; thickness limits are taken from the minimum and maximum lift depths in Tables 9.4 and 9.5 and structural coefficients are obtained from Table 9.3 [1]. Construction practices require removal of unsuitable subgrade or subbase and backfilling as specified in Section 203.1, coating all exposed vertical faces with a uniform application of PG 64S‑22, and ensuring that the mixing plant obtains acceptance approval; hot‑mix base material must be produced at temperatures below 320 °F, placed within eight hours of manufacture, and never placed on wet, frozen, or air‑temperature‑35 °F-or‑colder surfaces [2]. Lift thicknesses are limited to four to eight inches when compacted with steel‑wheel power rollers (8–10 tons) and five to ten inches with vibratory equipment; if a base exceeds six inches the material must be placed in two or more layers of approximately equal depth, each layer not less than three inches and not greater than six inches for Superpave mixes (or four to eight inches for thicker mixes), and no layer may be thinner than three inches nor thicker than six inches where PTM No. 106 Method B governs density [2]. Compaction requirements demand at least 100 % of the laboratory maximum dry density per PTM No. 106 Method B and not less than 96 % of the target density defined in PTM No. 402, with reclamation mixes required to achieve a minimum of 95 % of the laboratory maximum at optimum moisture; verification is performed by AASHTO T‑310 nuclear testing or AASHTO T‑191 field‑density tests for each 3,000 sq yd area and random full‑depth core drilling according to PTM No. 737 [2]. Surface quality is controlled using cross‑section templates, straightedges, and frequent checks; any longitudinal irregularity greater than one‑quarter inch or surface deviation exceeding one‑half inch must be corrected immediately by adjusting the base rather than grinding, and shoulders must be built up and compacted to at least the elevation of each base layer before placement [2]. Rolling consists of four passes per lift with steel‑wheel rollers, applied in a manner that does not crush aggregate or impede drainage, while curing continues until moisture content falls below two percent; emulsified binders require a minimum cure period of two weeks before the wearing course is placed and white membrane forming compound may be applied at a rate of one gallon per 100 sq ft (±10 %) or the surface may be covered with polyethylene for twenty‑four hours, with extensions when ambient temperature drops below 40 °F [2]. Strength criteria stipulate a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi and an unconfined compressive strength between 300 and 500 psi after seven days for reclaimed or stabilized bases, while new asphalt‑rich bases may not be opened to traffic until a compressive strength of at least 2,750 psi is demonstrated by cylinder testing per PTM No. 604 or the maturity method per PTM No. 640; unlimited traffic is prohibited until these strengths are verified and only light local traffic may be allowed after a stable condition is observed [2]. Project‑specific evaluation requires identification of the cause of any existing base failure, with repairs made using material similar in type and thickness to preserve drainage and avoid bathtub conditions; subgrade CBR values greater than ten must receive approval from the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, no CBR may exceed twenty in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation, and frost‑susceptible subgrades demand a stronger base course as prescribed by AASHTO guidance [1]. Measurement and payment for the base course are based on quantities expressed in square yards or tons as modified in Section 413.4(a) [2].

Design methods and criteria

The base course may be designed either as a roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) layer using the PennDOT concrete pavement design procedures in Chapter 8 Section 8.15 to determine required thickness, with control‑joint spacing set equal to 2.5 times the RCC thickness in feet for lifts eight inches or thicker [1]. When the base is asphaltic, the Superpave volumetric mixture system is applied, selecting a performance‑grade binder and calculating 20‑year design ESALs so that the project falls within one of five ESAL ranges that dictate mix proportions [1][2]. For total base thicknesses greater than six inches an Asphalt Rich Base Course (ARBC) is required in the lower three to six inches, using a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25 mm, 2.5 % air voids and limiting the mix to less than 0.3 million ESALs (approximately 50 gyrations) [1]. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses are produced by milling existing pavement to the specified depth, mixing polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt when daily AADT exceeds about 3 000 vehicles or truck percentages are high, placing to a target grade and compacting to achieve density, with mix design governed by Publication 27 which prescribes test methods, allowable emulsions and a minimum one‑week curing period under warm, low‑humidity conditions; the compacted thickness must lie between three and five inches and projects with daily ESALs of 200 or more are excluded [1]. In rigid pavement analysis the base course is combined with the sub‑base to obtain an effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k) by weighting the loss‑of‑support factors of each layer according to their depths using LS₁·D₁/(D₁+D₂)+LS₂·D₂/(D₁+D₂), after which k is adjusted with seasonal elastic moduli of 15 000 psi for spring thaw, 30 000 psi for normal summer/fall and 50 000 psi for frozen periods while ensuring the sub‑base modulus does not exceed four times the resilient modulus of the underlying soil [1]. For full‑depth flexible pavements the required structural number is computed as SN = Σ a_i d_i m_i, where the structural coefficients a_i are taken from Table 9.3 and all drainage coefficients m_i are set to 1.0, and the resulting SN is converted to individual layer thicknesses by either the Specified Thickness Design or the Optimized Thickness Design procedures available in DARWin software; the Layered Analysis method is not permitted and material cost per inch together with minimum and maximum depth limits guide selection of an economically optimal base‑course thickness [1]. The Superpave mixture design also generates aggregate‑asphalt proportions and imposes performance limits such as a minimum compressive strength of 2 750 psi, which must be demonstrated by laboratory cylinder tests in accordance with PTM No. 604 or by the maturity method described in PTM No. 640; quantities placed are recorded in square yards or tons [2]. Prior to placement the work area is marked, saw‑cut or milled along the perimeter, all existing material within that boundary is excavated to the plan depth, the underlying subgrade or subbase is compacted (and re‑compacted if disturbed), any loss of grade is corrected, unsuitable subgrade is removed, vertical faces are cleaned and a uniform coating of PG 64S‑22 is applied before the base material is laid [2]. The mix design requires precise proportioning of coarse and fine aggregates, water and optional liquid additives, mixing at temperatures below 320 °F to maintain suitable viscosity, and metering liquids within ±2 % by weight; materials are delivered in clean vehicles to prevent segregation and placed with adjustable self‑propelled spreaders or slip‑form pavers equipped with screeds, while elevation and thickness are controlled using templates, string lines, GPS or laser devices [2]. When the required compacted depth exceeds a single lift, the material is placed in two or more lifts of approximately equal depth—each lift between three and six inches for total depths greater than six inches and between four and eight inches for total depths greater than eight inches—with no lift thinner than the minimum or thicker than the maximum specified [2]. Compaction is achieved with steel‑wheel rollers weighing eight to ten tons, applying four passes for asphalt‑treated base courses (ATPBC) and at least one pass for cement‑treated pavement base courses (CTPBC) within one hour of water addition (or up to 1½ hours when a retarding admixture is used), targeting densities of at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density or, where required, 100 % of the PTM 106 maximum density [2]. In‑place density for each 3 000‑square‑yard segment is verified using AASHTO T 191, AASHTO T 310 or nuclear density gauges per PTM 402, and any segment that fails is scarified, re‑moistened and recompacted; depth control is confirmed by drilling full‑depth cores at one random location per 3 000 sq yd as required by PTM 1 and measuring core depths according to PTM 737, with a deficiency of half an inch or more that cannot be corrected rendering the pavement defective [2]. Surface irregularities are inspected at intervals not exceeding 25 ft and must not exceed one‑quarter inch (or one‑half inch for certain specifications), placement is prohibited on wet subgrades or when air or surface temperature is 35 °F or lower, and the mix must be placed within eight hours of mixing; stabilized mixes are cured until moisture drops below 2 % or strength criteria are met—an indirect tensile strength of 50 psi for asphalt‑stabilized mixes or a seven‑day unconfined compressive strength of 300–500 psi for chemically stabilized mixes [2]. RCC bases use a low‑slump concrete mix spread and compacted with rollers of various sizes until the prescribed thickness and density are attained, longitudinal ties are formed as shown on standard drawings, full‑width construction does not require sawing while transverse joints are cut to full depth after interruptions longer than thirty minutes, the finished surface may receive a rake or broom finish, and curing compounds are limited to black liquid‑membrane forms with white compounds prohibited [2]. Base‑course reclamation follows similar procedures in which existing pavement is pulverized, mixed with aggregates, reclaimed asphalt pavement, stabilizing additives and water to the design moisture content, compacted to at least 95 % of laboratory maximum density per PTM 106, verified on a 300‑ft control strip using nuclear gauges per PTM 402, and cured until required strength is achieved; throughout all phases the process relies on standards and tools such as PTM 106, PTM 402, PTM 604, PTM 640, AASHTO T 191, T 310, ASTM D2216, D44643, D1633, adjustable spreaders, rollers, GPS/laser alignment devices and nuclear density gauges [2].

The design of the base course is governed by explicit structural criteria that require the calculated structural number derived from the chosen layer thicknesses to exceed the required design structural number and to remain within the minimum and maximum lift depths prescribed in standard tables [1][2]. Structural coefficients for each layer are obtained from the designated tables, while all layer drainage coefficients are set to unity, and a loss‑of‑support factor is applied to each layer to account for potential erosion or pumping, with a weighted average of base and subbase depths used to obtain an overall subgrade reaction modulus [1]. For flexible pavements the thickness of each lift must also satisfy equipment‑specific limits; static rollers require lifts between three and six inches, roller‑only operations allow four to eight inches, and vibratory equipment permits five to ten inches, with a universal lower bound of two inches imposed by material‑metering requirements, and any lift exceeding its upper limit must be divided into approximately equal layers that each meet the applicable interval [2]. When the total compacted base thickness exceeds six inches, the bottom three to six inches shall consist of an Asphalt Rich Base Course (ARBC) mix designed for less than 0.3 million equivalent single axle loads, limited to about 2.5 % air voids and a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25 mm to provide high tensile strength and resistance to bottom‑up cracking [1]. If the design predicts a 20‑year ESAL forecast between 0.3 and less than 3.0 million, no further alteration of the base composition is required beyond the ARBC requirement, whereas for designs with higher traffic levels cold‑recycled bases are permitted only when heavy truck traffic is absent; projects generating 200 or more daily ESALs are excluded unless a detour can be provided during construction and curing [1]. Cold‑recycled mixes must be compacted to a thickness between three and five inches, cured for at least one week under warm, low‑humidity conditions before the wearing surface is placed, and if the reclaimed asphalt pavement is fine‑graded, virgin coarse aggregate (No. 8, 57 or 67) must be added to satisfy specifications; polymer‑modified emulsions are required when two‑lane AADT exceeds three thousand, truck percentages are high, or early/late season recycling could impair curing [1]. Roller‑compacted concrete may serve as a base only on roads with an average daily traffic of 2 000 vehicles or less and a posted speed limit of 55 mph or lower; its thickness may range from four to eighteen inches and the concrete must achieve a compressive strength between roughly 4 000 and 10 000 psi [1]. All lifts are required to attain field densities that meet or exceed the most stringent of the applicable standards—100 % of the maximum dry density as defined by PTM No. 106 Method B, at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density, and not less than 95 % of the laboratory‑determined maximum dry density for reclaimed material—and compaction shall be verified with AASHTO T 191 or T 310 testing using steel‑wheel power rollers weighing eight to ten tons and applying four roller passes per lift [2]. Surface quality is tightly controlled; any longitudinal or cross‑section irregularity greater than one‑half inch must be corrected on site before the next rolling pass, and a pavement is deemed defective if deviations exceed a quarter‑inch after final finishing, with depth deficiencies of half an inch or more requiring removal, recompaction, and sealing [2]. Placement conditions are limited to dry, unfrozen surfaces with ambient air temperatures generally above 35 °F (some provisions require at least 40 °F or 45 °F), the prepared surface must be free of moisture, and forecast freezing within the next twenty‑four hours precludes work; mix temperatures must not exceed 320 °F and the material must be placed within eight hours of production for asphalt‑stabilized mixes [2]. Moisture content during compaction shall be at the optimum value determined by the mix design and may not exceed that optimum by more than three percent, while emulsified asphalt bases must be cured until average moisture falls below two percent and a minimum two‑week cure is required before overlay placement [2]. Curing commences immediately after compaction and must be sustained for twenty‑four continuous hours; if the temperature of the base course falls below forty degrees Fahrenheit during this period, the cure is extended an additional twenty‑four hours, and no construction traffic is permitted on the freshly placed base for a minimum of three days, with any damage or contamination repaired at no cost to the Department [2]. Before traffic may be opened, the base course must satisfy strength criteria that ensure structural reliability: a compressive strength of at least 2 750 psi as verified by cylinder testing or maturity methods, an indirect tensile strength of not less than 50 psi for asphalt‑stabilized material, and a seven‑day unconfined compressive strength between 300 and 500 psi (with upper limits around 900 psi to avoid shrinkage cracking) [2]. Drainage design requirements mandate that both base and subbase materials be highly permeable so that gravity‑drainable water can be removed rapidly, the slope of the subbase should be maximized, base drains and outlet spacing are sized in accordance with the PennDOT design manual, lateral underdrains are strongly encouraged at wet subgrade locations or cut‑to‑fill transitions, and all outlets must be protected from crushing or plugging [1]. When a permeable asphalt‑treated or cement‑treated base is used over a 2A subbase in rigid pavement, its contribution to the effective modulus of subgrade reaction is evaluated with season‑dependent elastic moduli—15 kpsi for spring thaw, 30 kpsi under normal conditions and 50 kpsi when frozen—and these values are adjusted as necessary so that the ratio of subbase resilient modulus to native soil does not exceed four [1]. The overall design incorporates a loss‑of‑support factor applied to each layer, drainage coefficients set to unity, and adherence to the specified thickness, density, surface‑regularity, temperature, moisture, curing, testing, and strength requirements, thereby providing the necessary safety factors and reliability margins for the base course [1][2].

Structural section and dimensions

The base course may be placed in a range of overall compacted thicknesses that varies with material and design intent, reflecting the differing practices described across the guides [1][2]. When reinforced cement concrete (RCC) is employed as the base beneath an asphalt wearing surface, the structural thickness can be any value between four and eighteen inches; sections thinner than eight inches require control joints spaced fifteen to twenty feet, whereas thicker sections use joint spacing equal to 2.5 times the pavement thickness in feet [1]. For conventional new or reconstructed pavements the minimum total base‑course thickness is six inches, with an Asphalt Rich Base Course (ARBC) placed in the lower portion of that layer and occupying between three and six inches of the first lift; the nominal maximum aggregate size for ARBC is 25 mm [1]. In a cold‑recycled base course the compacted depth must be at least three inches and may not exceed five inches, a condition achieved by ensuring an existing asphalt layer of at least two inches and adding one inch of reclaimed or virgin aggregate to meet the minimum thickness [1]. The guides also state that base‑course thickness is generally limited to between two and five inches, although material‑specific allowances permit up to six inches for a 25 mm Superpave mix and up to eight inches for a 37.5 mm Superpave mix [2]. When the required compacted depth exceeds these limits, the course must be built in two or more lifts of roughly equal thickness; each lift may not be less than three inches nor greater than six inches for the 25 mm mix, and may not be less than four inches nor greater than eight inches for the 37.5 mm mix, with any base thicker than eight inches (or ten inches when vibratory compaction is used) also requiring each lift to fall within a four‑to‑eight inch (or five‑to‑ten inch) range, while the ATPBC specification further restricts lifts to a maximum of four inches [2]. Depth control is verified by drilling a six‑inch diameter test hole for every 3,000 square yards, and any area found short by half an inch or more must be corrected [2]. The guides do not prescribe separate dimensional requirements for the subbase, treated‑soil, slab, or reinforcement layers [1][2]. Shoulders are built up and compacted across the full width to at least the elevation of each base‑course layer [2]. Edge support is provided by longitudinal joints that overlap the adjacent lane edge by one to two inches, while transverse joints extend through the full depth of the lift [2].

Materials and mixture design

The Base Course specification must first identify the material class that will constitute the layer, which may be a granular aggregate base, roller‑compacted concrete (RCC), Asphalt Rich Base Course (ARBC), cold‑recycled asphalt base, an Asphalt‑treated or Cement‑treated Permeable Base, or a cement‑based mixture such as aggregate‑cement or aggregate‑lime‑pozzolan [1][2]. For each class the required material properties and mix proportions are prescribed. Granular bases are required to be highly permeable aggregates that convey water by gravity; this condition is demonstrated through permeability performance and verified primarily by particle‑size distribution (gradation) testing, which must produce a void structure that allows rapid flow [1]. When the base is provided as RCC, the mix consists of cement, aggregate and water in the same basic proportions as conventional concrete but with a lower water‑to‑cement ratio, typically 0.40 and permitted down to 0.30; a dense aggregate gradation with a maximum size not exceeding one inch is required, air entrainment may be used, admixtures are limited, and the resulting mixture should achieve a compressive strength in the range of approximately four thousand to ten thousand pounds per square inch [1][2]. If the total base thickness exceeds six inches, an Asphalt Rich Base Course must be placed in the bottom three to six inches of the first lift; ARBC mixes are limited to low‑traffic designs handling fewer than 0.3 million equivalent single axle loads, use a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25 mm (1 inch), and are compacted to design air voids of about 2.5 percent to provide high tensile strength at the pavement bottom [1][2]. Cold‑recycled asphalt bases consist of reclaimed asphalt pavement milled to a specified depth and blended with an emulsified asphalt binder; when the reclaimed material is a wearing course or fine‑graded mix, virgin coarse aggregate of No. 8, 57 or 67 size must be added to satisfy the mix design, polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended for high‑traffic conditions, the compacted thickness shall lie between three and five inches, and a minimum curing period of one week under favorable weather (or at least two weeks until average moisture is below two percent) is required before the wearing course can be placed [1][2]. Permeable bases used in rigid pavements may be Asphalt‑treated Permeable Base or Cement‑treated Permeable Base placed over a 2A subbase; the design assigns an elastic modulus of approximately 15 kpsi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30 kpsi for normal summer/fall conditions, and 50 kpsi for frozen winter conditions, with adjustments so that the ratio of subbase to soil resilient modulus does not exceed four [1]. When a cement‑stabilized or lime‑pozzolan base is specified, the mix must include clean, dry aggregate measured separately as coarse and fine fractions by volume or weight, the appropriate cementitious additive, water added after uniform blending of aggregates to achieve the design moisture level (not exceeding the optimum moisture content by more than three percent), and, where required, calcium chloride added at 7 lb per ton for Type 1 mixes or 5.7 lb per ton for Type 2 mixes before water is introduced [2]. A binding agent is mandatory: heated asphalt binder for hot‑mix bases, or an emulsified or foamed asphalt stabilizing agent for cold‑recycled mixes; the production temperature for hot mixes may not exceed 320 °F to maintain suitable viscosity and prevent segregation, and the asphalt film coating on aggregate must be of adequate thickness to provide the required binding strength [2][1]. All base layers must be compacted to achieve at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum dry density at optimum moisture (or at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density for cold‑recycled mixes), with indirect tensile strength not less than 50 psi for asphalt‑stabilized bases, unconfined compressive strength between 300 and 500 psi after seven days for cement‑stabilized bases, and an overall compressive strength of at least 2,750 psi before the base may be opened to traffic; additionally, all layer drainage coefficients are set to 1.0 and thicknesses must fall within the minimum and maximum lift depths prescribed in the design tables [2][1]. The appropriate structural coefficient for each selected mix is obtained from the relevant tables, and repairs to an existing base shall use material similar in type and thickness to preserve drainage characteristics and avoid bathtub failure [1].

The base course must be installed so that it creates a seamless and durable interface with both the subbase beneath it and the surface treatment above, thereby ensuring effective load distribution.[1][2] High‑permeability materials are selected for the base and subbase by appropriate gradation to allow gravity‑drainable water to pass rapidly, and the subbase is sloped as steeply as practical to promote drainage toward a network of closely spaced drain pipes and continuous edge drains located on the low side of any superelevation and in median sections where water problems have been identified.[1] Lateral underdrains are installed at cut‑to‑fill transitions and other wet subgrade areas, and all base‑drain outlets are protected by headwalls to prevent crushing or plugging, guaranteeing reliable operation of the drainage system.[1] Before placing the base course, a thin uniform bedding of fine material is spread over the prepared subgrade, subbase, or existing pavement—approximately two inches on subgrade or one inch on subbase or an existing surface—to provide a stable bed for the coarse base material.[2] When a roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) base is used, it is placed on a prepared, adequately compacted subgrade and subbase capable of supporting the dry‑mix concrete during placement; the RCC layer may be positioned directly beneath an asphalt wearing course or other surface treatment without diamond grinding unless it will remain exposed as the final pavement surface for speeds of 35 mph or higher, and control joints in the RCC are aligned with the spacing and alignment of any adjacent concrete mainline to maintain continuity across the joint interface.[1] For cold‑recycled base courses, existing asphalt pavement is milled, mixed with emulsified binder, placed to the required grade and compacted, and then bonded to a Superpave asphalt overlay, although a cold‑mix asphalt overlay or surface treatment may be used when traffic volumes are low.[1] The interface between successive base‑course lifts is treated as a bonded joint; fresh mix is placed immediately after the preceding layer and the underlying surface is kept moist until it is covered, creating a positive bond and uniform compaction across lane edges.[2] If new material must be joined to work placed on a prior day, the existing edge is scarified and moistened before blending with the fresh mixture so that no construction joint remains.[2] Base‑course lifts are laid in uniformly thick layers whose compacted thickness is not less than four inches and not more than eight inches (or five to ten inches when vibratory equipment is used), and each lift is spread, compacted, and inspected for surface irregularities before the next lift is placed.[2] Any deviation greater than one‑half inch is corrected by loosening the surface and adding or removing material, followed by re‑compaction to achieve the required density.[2] Longitudinal joints are formed by overlapping the asphalt‑treated base course (ATPBC) over the edge of the previously placed lane by one to two inches, producing a smooth joint after compaction; when work stops for more than thirty minutes, a temporary vertical bulkhead is installed to create a straight transverse construction joint that extends the full depth and width of the ATPBC.[2] Transverse joints follow the provisions of Section 501.3(i)1, while longitudinal joints placed for construction convenience are tied joints as shown on standard cement‑concrete pavement drawings; full‑width construction does not require sawing.[2] Forms are omitted when widening the base course up to six feet, and the finished surface may receive a rake or broom texture; a black liquid‑membrane forming curing compound may be used only if the cement concrete base will not be exposed to traffic before the surface course is placed, and it must be discontinued if it becomes unsatisfactory.[2] The structural interaction between the base and subbase is treated as a combined system; their thicknesses are added and a weighted loss‑of‑support factor is applied to compute an effective modulus of subgrade reaction, with design specifications requiring an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) over a 2A subbase and any additional subbase material incorporated before evaluating combined stiffness, thereby ensuring appropriate load distribution while respecting limits on the ratio between subbase modulus and roadbed resilient modulus.[1] Prior to base‑course placement, the interface with the underlying subbase is protected and verified; for flexible pavements a prepared subbase receives an asphalt base course, whereas rigid pavements require a reinforced or plain cement concrete layer before season’s end, and if the subbase has been exposed to traffic or weather for more than sixty days, surface irregularities are retested, deficiencies corrected, and a proof roll performed before installing pavement.[2] In replacement areas, the perimeter is marked, existing material removed to the required depth, vertical faces are cleaned and uniformly coated with PG 64S‑22 to ensure a sound bond, the existing subgrade or subbase is fully compacted, and the asphalt base course is placed without segregation to the specified lift thickness and compacted to the density required by the approved equipment.[2]

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

The base course is designed as a well‑drained system that rapidly removes water from the surface and from within the pavement layers [1]. To achieve this, highly permeable granular aggregates are specified for the base and subbase so that gravity‑drainable water can flow freely through the material [1]. The slope of the subbase is maximized and a positive longitudinal slope is maintained throughout the pavement to increase the rate of drainage and keep the layer dry [1]. Lateral underdrains are installed in wet subgrade zones and at every cut‑to‑fill transition, while continuous edge drains run along the outer low side of superelevated sections and wherever median drainage is required, with outlets protected from crushing or plugging [1]. Where frost‑susceptible subgrade exists, a stronger base course is provided together with pavement base drains (or comparable drainage provisions) to mitigate moisture infiltration, freeze‑thaw effects, and other environmental stresses; the design may be divided into sections so that each thickness can accommodate appropriate drainage [1]. For total base thicknesses greater than six inches, an Asphalt Rich Base Course (ARBC) is placed as a three‑to‑six‑inch bottom lift, limited to designs of less than 0.3 million ESAL and compacted to about 2.5 % air voids to maximize tensile strength at the base and improve resistance to moisture‑induced cracking; binder grades and mixture properties are selected with the Superpave system to match local climatic extremes [1]. In cold‑recycled pavement projects, only structurally sound, well‑drained bases and subgrades are accepted, and a minimum one‑week curing period under low humidity and high temperature is required before the wearing course is applied to ensure adequate strength despite moisture and climate effects [1]. For rigid Interstate and Expressway pavements, an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) over a 2A subbase is required where a positive outlet can be provided; otherwise a dense base with longitudinal edge drains is specified, and the elastic modulus of the base is adjusted for seasonal climate conditions—approximately 15 000 psi during spring thaw, 30 000 psi in normal summer/fall, and 50 000 psi when frozen—to capture moisture and temperature effects, with loss‑of‑support factors applied for potential erosion or pumping [1]. All pavement layers are assigned a uniform drainage coefficient of 1.0, indicating that no additional environmental adjustments to the coefficient are prescribed beyond the physical drainage features described above [1]. Placement of the base course is prohibited during cold periods; reclaimed‑asphalt base (CRABC) may not be placed from September 1 through April 30 in districts 1‑0, 2‑0, 3‑0, 4‑0, 9‑0 and 10‑0, and from October 1 through April 30 elsewhere, and no placement may occur unless the surface has been free of freezing for at least twenty‑four hours and the ambient air temperature is a minimum of 45 °F and trending upward [2]. For all other base types, work must be suspended when the prepared surface is wet or when either the air or surface temperature falls to 35 °F or lower; placement may resume only after conditions improve, although limited quantities already en route can be placed with representative approval [2]. The moisture content of the mix is tightly regulated; sufficient liquid must be retained during mixing and water added with a calibrated meter so that the mixture reaches the optimum moisture specified by the mix design but does not exceed it by more than three percent, and if target density cannot be achieved the layer must be scarified, moisture adjusted, and re‑compacted [2]. Prior to placement of cement‑treated or rubberized bases, the underlying rubblized pavement or subbase is thoroughly sprinkled with water until it readily absorbs, establishing a moist condition well in advance of laying [2]. Compaction may be performed with an 8‑10 ton steel‑wheel roller for no more than four passes, and must not be so severe that the layer loses its free‑draining character or that aggregate is crushed, thereby preserving permeability throughout the base course [2]. Emulsified‑asphalt stabilized CRABC must be cured until the average moisture content falls below two percent, which in practice means a minimum curing period of two weeks before any wearing or binder course is placed; cement‑treated bases are protected from low temperatures by extending the cure period an additional twenty‑four hours if surface temperature drops below 40 °F during the first twenty‑four hours, and must be removed and replaced at no cost if temperature reaches 32 °F within seventy‑two hours [2]. Chemically stabilized reclaimed layers are kept damp daily either by water application or by a light asphalt seal (emulsified prime followed by a fog seal applied at a residual rate of 0.02–0.10 gal/yd²) or tack coat, and curing proceeds more rapidly under dry, sunny, low‑humidity, high‑temperature conditions [2]. Collectively, these provisions ensure that the base course is placed only when climatic conditions permit adequate compaction, that moisture levels are controlled to achieve design density while retaining permeability, and that post‑placement curing accommodates temperature fluctuations, thereby safeguarding drainage and long‑term performance [2].

Verification and expected performance

The completed base course is accepted only after a series of field inspections that verify compliance with the contract, constructability limits, performance criteria, and overall consistency with project requirements. Prior to placement the mixing plant and proportioning equipment must be approved, and work may commence only when the underlying surface is dry and the ambient or surface temperature exceeds 35 °F. Layer thicknesses are controlled by the mix design: a Superpave 25‑mm base cannot exceed six inches in a single lift and each lift must be at least three inches thick, while a Superpave 37.5‑mm base is limited to eight inches per lift with a minimum of four inches; any excess depth is placed in two or more lifts of approximately equal compacted depth. Each lift is spread uniformly and compacted to the density required by the applicable specifications – typically not less than 95 % of the laboratory maximum, at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target, or 100 % of the maximum density defined by PTM 106 Method B. Nuclear density readings are taken after each roller pass and a minimum of ten random tests are performed in every 3,000‑square‑yard area to establish an average in‑place density that meets the prescribed minimum.

After compaction the surface is surveyed for smoothness and grade. Cross‑sectional checks are made at intervals not greater than 25 feet using templates or GPS/laser equipment, and a straightedge – twelve feet long for asphalt bases or ten feet for cement‑treated bases – is moved in stages no farther than five feet apart to detect longitudinal waviness. Any irregularity exceeding one‑half inch is corrected by loosening the surface, adding or removing material, and re‑rolling; if after correction an irregularity still exceeds one‑quarter inch the pavement is deemed defective. Depth verification is performed by drilling a six‑inch diameter test hole or extracting a full‑depth core for each 3,000 sq yd (or as directed) and measuring the compacted thickness against the design grade; sections short by half an inch or more are removed and replaced, and any area that cannot be remedied within this tolerance is classified as defective. Joint continuity is inspected, scarification and moisture adjustment are applied where necessary, and a prime coat not exceeding the specified rate is placed before the surface course.

For cement‑treated bases, compaction must be achieved with steel‑wheel rollers weighing eight to ten tons within one hour of water addition (or within one and a half hours when a retarding admixture is used). The surface is then cured for at least twenty‑four hours and traffic is prohibited for three days. Finally, before any traffic is allowed onto the base course, its compressive strength must reach at least 2,750 psi; this is verified either by laboratory compression testing of cylinder specimens in accordance with PTM 604 or by applying the maturity method outlined in PTM 640. Through these coordinated inspections and corrective actions the completed base course demonstrates full compliance with contractual, constructability, performance, and project‑consistency requirements.
