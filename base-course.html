Base Course

Part of: Pavement Structure is-subtopic-of

Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-29

The base course is the structural layer placed directly beneath the wearing or binder surface in both flexible and rigid pavement systems, providing a stable platform that spreads vehicular stresses to the subgrade while preventing “bathtub” moisture conditions [1][2]. It achieves this by using highly permeable granular, asphalt‑treated, cement‑treated, or roller‑compacted concrete materials placed in controlled lifts—typically three to six inches per lift—and compacted to at least 95 % of laboratory maximum density (or the PTM‑402 target), thereby creating a uniformly dense, load‑bearing foundation that rapidly drains water by gravity [1][2]. Design specifications also require minimum strength properties—such as an indirect tensile strength of 50 psi for asphalt‑stabilized mixes, a seven‑day unconfined compressive strength of 300–500 psi for chemically stabilized bases, and a final compressive strength of about 2,750 psi before traffic opening—to ensure long‑term durability across the expected traffic and environmental conditions [1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

The base course is employed to remedy insufficient structural capacity of existing pavement structures and to manage moisture that would otherwise cause excessive deflection, rutting, bottom‑up cracking, or loss of subgrade support. [1] Its primary performance need is to provide a uniformly compacted, load‑bearing foundation that distributes vehicle stresses while rapidly draining surface and subsurface water by gravity, thereby preventing “bathtub” conditions and preserving the bearing value of the underlying soil. [1][2] To satisfy this need designers specify highly permeable granular or chemically treated materials such as asphalt‑treated permeable base (ATPB), cement‑treated permeable base (CTPB) or roller‑compacted concrete, placed over an appropriate subbase layer. [1][2] The thickness of the base must be controlled—typically no single lift less than three inches and no more than six to eight inches depending on material—with greater total depths constructed in two or more equal lifts; for new or reconstructed sections with total base thickness greater than six inches an Asphalt Rich Base Course (ARBC) is required to deliver the highest tensile strength at the bottom of the pavement. [2][1] Minimum density targets of at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum density and strength criteria such as a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi for asphalt‑stabilized material, an unconfined compressive strength of 300–500 psi within seven days for chemically stabilized mixes, and a compressive strength of about 2,750 psi before traffic opening are imposed to ensure the base can safely bear anticipated loads. [2] When subgrades are frost‑susceptible or otherwise weak, a stronger granular base is provided to compensate for reduced bearing support and limit differential heave during freeze‑thaw cycles. [1] In rehabilitation projects where cold recycling is appropriate, existing asphalt is milled, mixed with emulsified asphalt, and compacted to a uniform thickness of three to five inches, producing a higher‑strength base suitable for roads carrying fewer than roughly 200 daily equivalent single axle loads. [1] For rigid‑pavement designs on interstates and expressways the combined base and subbase must achieve effective moduli of subgrade reaction ranging from 15 000 psi in spring‑thaw conditions to 50 000 psi when frozen, using weighted‑average loss‑of‑support factors to account for potential erosion or pumping. [1] By meeting these density, strength, moisture‑temperature, and drainage requirements the base course addresses the fundamental engineering problem of insufficient structural capacity and inadequate moisture management, ensuring load distribution, stability, and durability across a range of traffic loads and environmental conditions. [1][2]

The base course functions as the primary load‑distribution layer beneath the surface course, providing a stable platform that spreads vehicular stresses to the subgrade while allowing rapid removal of surface and subsurface water to protect the pavement from moisture‑related damage [1][2]. To achieve this function the material is placed in compacted lifts whose individual thicknesses are limited by mix design—typically no less than three inches and no more than six inches for a 25 mm asphalt blend, with alternative limits of two to five inches in some specifications and a maximum four‑inch lift for certain treated bases—and when greater total depth is required the course is built up in two or more approximately equal layers [2][1]. Compaction must reach the density targets prescribed by the applicable specifications, such as 100 % of the maximum dry density defined by PTM 106 Method B, at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target, or a minimum of 95 % of laboratory‑maximum dry density at optimum moisture, and the moisture content during compaction may not exceed three percent above the optimum value [2][1]. Structural strength is provided by achieving specified compressive and tensile capacities; roller‑compacted concrete bases are designed with thicknesses from four to eighteen inches to obtain compressive strengths of 4 000–10 000 psi, while asphalt‑rich base courses require a total thickness greater than six inches and must develop an indirect tensile strength of at least 50 psi, an unconfined compressive strength between 300 and 500 psi after seven days, and a minimum overall compressive strength of about 2 750 psi before traffic is permitted [1][2]. For low‑traffic mixes the bottom three to six inches are placed with air voids of approximately 2.5 % and a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25 mm to maximize tensile strength at the pavement base, and cold‑recycled mixes are limited to compacted depths of three to five inches and require the addition of virgin coarse aggregate when fine reclaimed material dominates [1][2]. Durability is enhanced by maintaining a dry subgrade through the use of highly permeable aggregates that provide large voids for rapid gravity drainage, thereby reducing capillary rise, frost‑heave, rutting, surface roughness and bottom‑up cracking, and by observing strict temperature and moisture controls such as prohibiting placement on frozen or wet surfaces, requiring ambient temperatures above 35 °F (or 40 °F where specified), limiting hot‑mix temperatures to 320 °F, and keeping the surface temperature of permeable bases below 115 °F [1][2]. Additional durability measures include sprinkling water before paving to keep the base moist, applying a curing compound at roughly one gallon per hundred square feet for at least twenty‑four hours (extended when temperatures fall below forty degrees), and allowing a minimum curing period of one week under low humidity and high temperature for roller‑compacted concrete or two weeks for emulsified‑asphalt stabilized mixes before the wearing course is placed [1][2]. Safety objectives are met by providing a stable work platform, restricting construction traffic until required strength and cure periods have elapsed—no construction traffic for at least three days after placement of the base course—and protecting form lines; when the base course forms an exposed surface on roads with speed limits of 35 mph or higher it must be diamond‑ground to satisfy skid‑resistance requirements [1][2]. Serviceability is ensured by limiting surface irregularities to no more than one‑half inch (with a stricter quarter‑inch tolerance in some specifications), maintaining cross‑slope of at least ¼ inch per foot, preserving ride quality as measured by the International Roughness Index, and guaranteeing that the finished base remains free‑draining throughout its design life [1][2]. When any deviation exceeds the allowable limits—such as depth deficiencies of half an inch or more—the affected area must be corrected by loosening, adding, or removing material before the surface course is installed [2][1].

Scope and applicability

The base course functions as the structural layer directly beneath the surface (wearing or binder) course in a broad spectrum of pavement systems, including flexible asphalt pavements and rigid concrete pavements. In flexible pavement designs it is required when the approved structure calls for either an aggregate‑cement or an aggregate‑lime/pozzolan base, with both material options offered as alternatives in the bid and thicknesses selected within allowable lift limits while balancing unit costs, minimum and maximum depths, and structural coefficients to achieve the target structural number. For asphalt‑concrete pavements a base course is used for all new or reconstructed sections, particularly when the total base thickness exceeds six inches; in those cases an Asphalt Rich Base Course (ARBC) is placed in the bottom three to six inches of the first lift using mix designs limited to less than 0.3 million ESALs and a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25 mm to provide high tensile strength and resistance to bottom‑up cracking. When the 20‑year design traffic falls between 0.3 and less than 3.0 million ESALs, the base course does not require a binder change, although polymer‑modified binders may be specified for severe rutting and fiber‑modified mixes may be used in the bottom lift with PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22 or PG 64E‑22 binders. Superpave asphalt bases are subject to additional thickness layering rules: a 25 mm nominal aggregate mix must be placed in two or more layers when the compacted depth exceeds six inches, each layer ranging from three to six inches; a 37.5 mm mix requires layering above eight inches, with individual layers between four and eight inches. If the compacted depth of any base course exceeds eight inches (or ten inches when vibratory compaction is used), placement must be divided into two or more approximately equal lifts, each lift not less than four inches nor more than eight inches (or five to ten inches for the ten‑inch limit). The material may be placed in brand‑new structures, as an overlay or build‑up on existing pavements where the old surface has been removed to neat lines, and also in designated replacement zones, provided that a prepared subgrade, subbase, or existing pavement that is dry, unfrozen and suitable underlies it. A fine‑material bedding of two inches is required when the base is placed on subgrade and one inch when placed on subbase or existing pavement. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses are appropriate for rehabilitating existing asphalt pavements by processing in‑place material without heating, provided that at least two inches of existing asphalt remain, the underlying base and subgrade are structurally sound and well‑drained, and traffic can be limited or detoured during construction and curing. Cold‑recycled bases should not be used where daily truck traffic exceeds 200 ESALs, where average daily traffic is roughly greater than 3,000 two‑lane vehicles, or where drainage conditions are poor; in such situations polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended to improve early strength. Placement of cold‑recycled base courses is prohibited when the ambient temperature is below 45 °F, when freezing has occurred within the preceding 24 hours, and during seasonal windows that run from September 1 to April 30 in certain districts (or October 1 to April 30 elsewhere). All placements are also prohibited if the surface or air temperature is at or below 35 °F. Compaction requirements call for achieving a minimum density of 96 % of the target density specified in PTM 402 for Superpave bases, and at least 95 % laboratory compacted maximum density for reclaimed‑material bases, with cold‑recycled mixes typically compacted to no less than 96 % before curing. Reclaimed‑material bases must attain a seven‑day cure to develop compressive strengths of 280–500 psi (for thin overlays) or tensile strengths of at least 50 psi, and they must meet a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi when tested according to Bulletin 27. In rigid pavement systems the base layer provides structural support and load distribution beneath concrete surface courses such as Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement or Roller Compacted Concrete, and it is normally constructed of Asphalt Treated Permeable Base (ATPB) or Cement Treated Permeable Base (CTPB) placed over a 2A subbase; the use of open‑graded subbase is prohibited for concrete pavements. All Interstate and Expressway rigid pavement designs require an ATPB or CTPB layer on a 2A subbase, and similar configurations may be employed in rehabilitation projects where the original pavement was built on OGS, ATPB, or CTPB, subject to coordination with the highway design team. Roller‑Compacted Concrete (RCC) can serve as a concrete base course when placed beneath an asphalt wearing course or surface treatment on low‑volume roads having average daily traffic of 2,000 vehicles or less and speed limits of 55 mph or lower, and it is also permitted for temporary pavements such as median crossovers or widening areas. In all of these applications the RCC or other base material rests on a prepared subgrade and subbase capable of supporting compaction during construction. [1][2]

When traffic volumes exceed roughly two thousand average daily trips, speed limits are higher than fifty‑five miles per hour, or the contract requires International Roughness Index measurements for ride‑quality incentives, a roller‑compacted concrete base is no longer appropriate and designers must turn to conventional pavement designs. Similarly, if an RCC surface will be exposed to speeds of thirty‑five miles per hour or more, diamond grinding becomes mandatory, which often leads designers to select alternative base treatments. For projects that call for a total base thickness greater than six inches, the guidelines prescribe the use of an Asphalt Rich Base Course because its low‑ESAL mix design provides the highest tensile strength at the bottom of the pavement and improves resistance to bottom‑up cracking and moisture damage. In low‑traffic situations where annual ESALs range between 0.3 and less than three million and rutting is not a concern, districts may retain the existing base and concentrate any material changes on the wearing or binder courses instead of replacing the base. Cold‑recycled bases become unsuitable when daily equivalent single axle loads reach two hundred or more, when the existing pavement lacks at least two inches of asphalt to achieve a three‑inch total thickness, when poor drainage or overly coarse underlying soils are present, or when traffic cannot be controlled or detoured during construction and curing; under those conditions designers typically turn to conventional hot‑mix placement or full‑depth reclamation. If the subgrade exhibits low or highly variable California Bearing Ratio values—whether isolated weak zones, widespread low CBR across the site, or large stretches with markedly different soils—and especially when CBR approaches five, a uniform base‑course thickness is insufficient and the preferred response is to improve the problematic subgrade areas, subdivide the project into sections that reflect the differing soil conditions, or apply subgrade strengthening methods such as lime, cement stabilization, or rock capping. The base‑course method is also constrained by seasonal and temperature limits; it may not be placed during the fall‑winter window specified for certain districts, and even outside that period it can proceed only when freezing temperatures have not occurred for a full day and the ambient air temperature is at least forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit and rising, unless written approval is obtained from the representative. When those seasonal or temperature conditions are not met, designers must consider an alternative pavement design or construction approach. [1][2]

Design inputs and requirements

The design of a base‑course begins with a comprehensive site assessment that records the present condition of the pavement, its construction and maintenance history, and the quality of drainage. A distress survey is performed to document existing cracking, rutting or other failures and to identify the underlying causes so that any wet subgrade zones can be corrected before work proceeds. Traffic information must include both the current average daily traffic and an estimate of future loading; for a roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) base the roadway should not exceed 2 000 vehicles per day and the posted speed limit must be 55 mph or less, while projects that experience 200 or more equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) per day are generally excluded from cold‑recycling approaches. The anticipated traffic levels are used to size the structural layers and to evaluate life‑cycle performance.

Material data are obtained by sampling and testing the existing asphalt surface, base, subbase and subgrade. These tests verify the thickness of reclaimed aggregate, confirm that virgin aggregates meet gradation requirements, and define the composition of any reclaimed layer that will be incorporated into the new base. When an RCC mix is specified, the design calls for cement, a dense fine‑aggregate gradation with a maximum particle size under one inch, and a low water‑cement ratio in the range of 0.30 to 0.40; air entrainment may be used but admixtures are limited.

Geotechnical input consists of in‑situ testing such as falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD), dynamic cone penetrometer (DCP) or light weight deflectometer (LWD) to quantify subgrade stiffness and strength, and a verification that the prepared subgrade and any underlying subbase are capable of supporting the compaction effort required for the RCC layer. The presence of untreated reclaimed material, soils, or oversized stones must be ruled out.

Environmental considerations include climate effects on pavement life expectancy and the need for at least one week of curing under favorable weather—low humidity and high temperature—before the wearing course is placed. If the RCC base will remain exposed to traffic traveling 35 mph or faster, surface‑texturing provisions are required. Proper drainage must be ensured throughout the design period to prevent moisture‑related deterioration.

Together these site, traffic, material, geotechnical, environmental and existing‑condition inputs provide the basis for determining appropriate base‑course thicknesses and ensuring long‑term structural performance. [1]

The design and construction of a base course are governed by an integrated set of PennDOT specifications, the Publication 408 series, and the AASHTO pavement‑design procedures, which together define material eligibility, mix‑design methods, performance criteria, and project‑specific constraints [1]. Material options include recycled concrete aggregate for subbase or cold‑recycled base layers as outlined in Section 703.2(a)7, and reclaimed asphalt pavement may be used in hot‑mix, warm‑mix, or cold‑recycled bases to conserve binder and aggregates; the Superpave Restricted Performance Specification is applied through Section 413 with a performance‑grade binder selected from Table 5.5 based on the projected ESAL range for a 20‑year design life [1]. When the calculated ESAL falls between 0.3 and less than 3.0 million, the base mix may remain unchanged, but if the total base thickness exceeds six inches an Asphalt Rich Base Course must occupy the lower three to six inches, limited to a maximum aggregate size of 25 mm, air voids not exceeding 2.5 %, and an ESAL design below 0.3 million; mix‑design diversity is capped at three designs per project and five for major projects, with any deviation from the prescribed ESAL range requiring PDAU approval [1]. Cold‑recycled base courses are governed by Publication 408 Sections 341 and 342 together with Bulletin 27 procedures, must be compacted to a thickness of three to five inches, cured for at least one week under low humidity and elevated temperature, and are limited to routes carrying roughly fewer than two hundred daily ESALs or where at least two inches of existing asphalt is available for recycling; heavier‑traffic corridors should employ polymer‑modified emulsions, while Full‑Depth Reclamation follows Publication 408 Section 344 and Bulletin 27 Chapter 5 [1]. Roller‑Compacted concrete may be used as a base under Publication 408 Sections 502 and 323 with design thicknesses ranging from four to eighteen inches, but placement is restricted to roadways with an average daily traffic of 2,000 vehicles or less and a speed limit not exceeding 55 mph; control‑joint spacing follows Publication 72M RC‑27M, being fifteen to twenty feet for sections thinner than eight inches and equal to the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet for thicker sections [1]. Drainage provisions are dictated by Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards for edge and outlet protection and by Publication 13M Design Manual Part 2 Chapter 10 Section 10.3.D for sizing base drains and determining outlet spacing, requiring continuous base drains along pavement edges and the low side of superelevated sections with outlets protected from crushing or plugging to maintain system functionality [1]. For rigid‑pavement projects the base design must also comply with the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures executed through the AASHTOWare DARWin program, requiring input of traffic loading, serviceability limits, material moduli, a chosen reliability level, and uniform drainage coefficients (mi = 1.0); only approved permeable materials—Asphalt‑Treated Permeable Base or Cement‑Treated Permeable Base—may be used, must be bid as separate alternatives, rest on a 2A subbase on Interstate and Expressway alignments, and open‑graded subbase is prohibited; drainage layers may be specified only where a positive outlet can be guaranteed, otherwise a dense base with longitudinal edge drains shall be employed, with seasonal elastic modulus values of 15 000 psi in spring thaw, 30 000 psi under normal conditions, and 50 000 psi when frozen, and the resilient‑modulus ratio of subbase to native soil may not exceed four; thickness limits are taken from Tables 9.4 and 9.5 and structural coefficients obtained from Table 9.3 [1]. Subgrade strength expressed as CBR values imposes additional constraints: values greater than ten require approval by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, and no CBR exceeding twenty may be used in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation; frost‑susceptible subgrades demand a stronger base course in accordance with AASHTO guidance [1]. Equipment requirements specify that only adjustable, self‑propelled mechanical spreaders capable of placing and screeding material without segregation may be used, and any liquid‑metering devices must operate within a ± 2 percent weight tolerance; compaction equipment is limited to the roller types listed in Section 108.05(c)3, with steel‑wheel power rollers certified at eight to ten tons required for many mixes, and mix temperature must not exceed 320 °F [2]. Placement of cold‑recycled asphalt base course may occur only when ambient temperatures are above 45 °F and rising, and seasonal windows prohibit placement from September 1 to April 30 in certain districts and from October 1 to April 30 in others; a fine‑material bedding layer must be spread uniformly—two inches over subgrade or one inch over subbase or existing pavement—before the coarse aggregate is placed [2]. Each lift of compacted material must fall within the thickness limits for the specific mix, generally no less than three inches and no more than six inches per lift; when total compacted depth exceeds eight inches the course shall be built in two or more layers each between four and eight inches thick, with Superpave 25‑mm bases exceeding six inches placed in equal lifts of three to six inches, and Asphalt Pavement Concrete (ATPBC) is limited to a maximum lift thickness of four inches; placement may not occur on frozen surfaces, when air or surface temperatures are below 35 °F (or 40 °F for certain specifications), when the surface is wet, or when freezing conditions are forecast within the next twenty‑four hours [2]. Compaction density targets are expressed as percentages of laboratory maximum densities: the material must achieve at least the full maximum density defined by PTM No. 106 Method B (100 % of maximum density) and, for certain mixes, a minimum of 96 % of the PTM 402 target density; other provisions require a minimum of 95 % of the laboratory compacted maximum density at optimum moisture, with in‑place density verified for each 3,000 sq yd area using AASHTO T 310 and core‑drilling depth checks performed per PTM No. 1 and measured per PTM No. 737, any shortfall of a half inch or more being corrected [2]. Strength requirements differ by stabilization type: asphalt‑stabilized mixes must attain a minimum indirect tensile strength of fifty pounds per square inch, while chemically stabilized mixes must develop an unconfined compressive strength between three hundred and five hundred psi after seven days; the base may not be opened to traffic until it reaches a compressive strength of two thousand seven hundred fifty psi, demonstrated either by cylinder testing in accordance with PTM No. 604 or by the maturity method (AASHTO T 413), with entry authorized by the Representative [2]. Surface tolerances are strictly controlled: irregularities shall not exceed one‑half inch, tightening to one‑quarter inch for Superpave bases, and deviations over a ten‑foot template must be limited to half an inch while maintaining at least a quarter‑inch per foot cross‑slope; any larger surface defect must be remedied by loosening, adding, or removing material [2]. Moisture management requires the base to be kept moist by sprinkling water as needed, and before any wearing or binder course is placed the moisture content must be reduced to less than two percent and not exceed three percent above the optimum mix‑design moisture [2]. Project‑specific constraints include the omission of forms when widening does not exceed six feet, the requirement that longitudinal joints, where used, be tied as shown on standard concrete pavement drawings, and the prohibition of white curing compounds (black may be used only if the cement concrete base is not accessible to traffic before the surface course is placed); construction traffic must be barred until the surface course is placed and prohibited for at least three days after placement of a chemically treated or cement‑stabilized base, with any interruption longer than thirty minutes requiring appropriate bulkhead or saw‑cut provisions [2].

Design methods and criteria

The base course thickness for a roller‑compacted concrete layer is obtained by applying the PennDOT concrete pavement design procedures found in Chapter 8, Section 8.15 to compute the required structural thickness, and an empirical joint‑spacing relationship then sets control‑joint spacing equal to 2.5 times the RCC thickness in feet for lifts eight inches or thicker[1]. When the base is asphaltic, designers employ the Superpave volumetric mixture system, selecting a performance‑grade binder and calculating twenty‑year design ESALs so that the project falls within one of five traffic ranges that dictate mix proportions; the guides also limit traffic to less than 0.3 million equivalent single axle loads unless an exception is granted, with PG 64S‑22 commonly specified[1][2]. For total base thicknesses greater than six inches, an Asphalt Rich Base Course is required in the lower three to six inches, using a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25 mm, 2.5 % air voids and a mix limited to less than 0.3 million ESALs (approximately 50 gyrations)[1]. Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses are treated through field procedures that mill the existing pavement to the specified depth, blend polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt when daily AADT exceeds about 3,000 vehicles or truck percentages are high, and then place and compact the mixture to a target grade; the mix design follows Publication 27, which prescribes test methods, allowable emulsions and a minimum curing period of one week under warm, low‑humidity conditions, with a compacted thickness limited to three to five inches and projects carrying daily ESALs of 200 or more excluded from cold‑recycling selection[1]. In rigid pavement analysis the base course is combined with the sub‑base to obtain an effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k) by weighting the loss‑of‑support factors of each layer according to their depths using the expression LS₁·D₁/(D₁+D₂)+LS₂·D₂/(D₁+D₂), and the resulting k is adjusted with seasonal elastic‑modulus values—15 000 psi for spring thaw, 30 000 psi for normal summer/fall conditions, and 50 000 psi for frozen periods—while ensuring that the sub‑base modulus does not exceed four times the resilient modulus of the underlying soil[1]. For full‑depth flexible pavements the required structural number is calculated with SN = Σaᵢdᵢmᵢ, where the structural coefficients aᵢ are taken from Table 9.3 and all drainage coefficients mᵢ are set to 1.0; the computed SN is then converted to individual layer thicknesses using either the Specified Thickness Design or the Optimized Thickness Design procedures available in the DARWin software, with material cost per inch together with minimum and maximum depth limits guiding the selection of an economically optimal base‑course thickness[1]. The Superpave mix‑design methodology also establishes the optimum moisture content and target density, the latter being calculated in accordance with PTM 402 and required to be at least 96 % of that value for virgin material or 95 % for reclaimed material as defined by PTM 106; reclaimed material must meet at least 95 % of its laboratory‑maximum density[2]. Strength development is monitored either through standard cylinder compression tests performed per PTM 604 or by applying the maturity method described in AASHTO T413, with a minimum compressive strength of 2,750 psi required before traffic may be permitted[2]. Placement begins after the project perimeter has been marked and any existing pavement removed to the design depth; the subgrade or subbase is compacted, vertical faces are cleaned and coated with the specified binder, and base material is spread using adjustable self‑propelled mechanical spreaders that place and screed without segregation[2]. When the required compacted depth exceeds eight inches (or ten inches for Method 2) the course must be built in two or more lifts of roughly equal thickness, each lift no thinner than four inches and no thicker than eight inches; for a Superpave 25‑mm asphalt base that exceeds six inches, lifts are limited to three‑to‑six inches, while other mixes follow similar limits and may not exceed four‑inch compacted lifts[2]. Placement is prohibited on wet surfaces or when air or surface temperature is at or below 35 °F, the mix temperature must remain below 320 °F, and for permeable bases the base is thoroughly sprinkled with water before paving to maintain moisture and keep the surface temperature under 115 °F[2]. Compaction is achieved with steel‑wheel power rollers weighing eight to ten tons, vibratory equipment may be required for Method 2, and the target density of at least 96 % of the PTM 402 value (or 95 % for reclaimed material) must be attained; the moisture content cannot exceed the optimum by more than three percent[2]. In‑place density is verified on each 3,000‑square‑yard segment using AASHTO T310 and a 300‑foot control strip with nuclear‑gauge readings confirms that no further increase occurs; surface tolerances are checked with a ten‑foot straightedge and any irregularity greater than one‑quarter inch or depth deficiency of half an inch or more is deemed defective and must be corrected before final compaction[2]. When non‑destructive depth testing is employed, targets are placed at Representative‑determined locations in accordance with PTM 605, the measuring device and targets are verified daily in the Representative’s presence, and any misplaced targets or damaged base must be repaired prior to paving[2]. For cement‑treated pavement bases compaction must occur within one hour of water addition to the aggregate‑cement mix (extendable to 1½ hours with a retarding admixture), after which a white membrane curing compound is applied at approximately one gallon per hundred square feet for 24 hours, and the base must be protected from construction traffic for at least three days[2]. Roller‑compacted concrete bases use a low‑slump, stiff concrete mix placed with conventional or high‑density paving equipment and compacted to the required thickness and density using rollers of various sizes; forms are omitted for widenings six feet or less, tied longitudinal joints are provided as shown on standard drawings, a rake or broom finish is applied to the surface, and an optional black liquid‑membrane curing compound may be used provided the cement concrete base is not exposed to traffic before the surface course is placed[2].

For permeable asphalt‑treated or cement‑treated bases placed over a 2A subbase in rigid pavements, season‑dependent elastic moduli of approximately 15 kpsi during spring thaw, 30 kpsi under normal conditions and 50 kpsi when frozen are used, and the ratio of subbase resilient modulus to native soil modulus is limited to four. A loss‑of‑support factor is applied to each layer to account for potential erosion or pumping, and a weighted average of base and subbase depths is employed to obtain an overall k value. Both the base and subbase materials are required to be highly permeable so that gravity‑drainable water can be removed rapidly; consequently the subbase slope should be maximized, lateral underdrains are strongly encouraged at wet locations or cut‑to‑fill transitions, and base drains and outlet spacing are sized in accordance with the PennDOT design manual while all outlets must be protected from crushing or plugging. Roller‑compacted concrete may be used as a base only on roads with an average daily traffic of 2 000 vehicles or less and a posted speed limit of 55 mph or lower, with permissible thicknesses ranging from four to eighteen inches and a required compressive strength between roughly 4 000 and 10 000 psi. When the total base thickness exceeds six inches, the bottom three to six inches must consist of an Asphalt Rich Base Course mix designed for less than 0.3 million equivalent single axle loads, limited to about 2.5 % air voids and a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25 mm to provide high tensile strength and resistance to bottom‑up cracking; designs forecasting 20‑year ESAL values between 0.3 and less than 3.0 million do not require alteration beyond this provision. Cold‑recycled base courses are permitted only where heavy truck traffic is absent, projects generating 200 or more daily ESALs being excluded unless a detour can be provided; the recycled mix must be compacted to a thickness of three to five inches and allowed at least one week of curing in warm, low‑humidity conditions before the wearing surface is placed, with virgin coarse aggregate added when the reclaimed asphalt pavement is fine‑graded and polymer‑modified emulsions required for AADT exceeding three thousand vehicles or high truck percentages. For any aggregate‑cement or aggregate‑lime/pozzolan base, both material options must be listed as alternatives in the project bid to provide redundancy and ensure reliability. Placement of the base course is permitted only on a stable, unfrozen surface whose temperature exceeds thirty‑five degrees Fahrenheit and that is free of standing water; hot mix temperatures may not exceed three hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit, and for treated permeable bases the surface temperature must be kept below one hundred fifteen degrees Fahrenheit, with paving halted if this cannot be maintained. Lift thicknesses are governed by equipment capability: when only rolling is used each lift shall be between four and eight inches, while vibratory compaction permits lifts from five to ten inches; for Superpave 25‑mm bases deeper than six inches the total depth is divided into equal lifts of three to six inches, and for Superpave 37.5‑mm bases exceeding eight inches lifts must be four to eight inches, with any overall compacted depth greater than six, eight or ten inches requiring subdivision into two or more layers within the corresponding limits. Compaction is performed with steel‑wheel rollers weighing eight to ten tons and limited to no more than four passes per lift, and a layer is considered satisfactorily compacted when it does not move under the rollers. Density requirements vary by material but generally demand at least ninety‑five percent of laboratory maximum dry density at optimum moisture for reclaimed or stabilized bases, a minimum of ninety‑six percent of the PTM‑402 target density for conventional mixes, and in some specifications full (one hundred percent) of the maximum density as defined by PTM 106 Method B. The mixture moisture content must be at the optimum value determined from the mix design and may not exceed it by more than three percent; before applying a wearing or binder course the moisture content must be reduced to below two percent, and emulsified‑asphalt bases require a minimum two‑week cure period. Stabilized mixes must achieve an indirect tensile strength of at least fifty pounds per square inch and an unconfined compressive strength between three hundred and five hundred psi after seven days, while the overall base course must develop a minimum cylinder compressive strength of two thousand seven hundred fifty psi, verified by PTM 604 testing or the AASHTO T‑413 maturity method, before traffic may be authorized. The Superpave asphalt specifications further require a PG 64S‑22 binder and limit design traffic to less than three‑tenths million equivalent single axle loads unless otherwise directed. Surface tolerances are tight; any longitudinal or cross‑section irregularity greater than one‑half inch must be corrected immediately, and depth deficiencies of half an inch or more mandate removal and replacement of the affected area. After final testing the base course must remain undisturbed, form lines may not be altered, and material adjacent to forms must be removed to full form depth; construction traffic is prohibited for at least three days after placement, with only equipment needed for the subsequent layer permitted thereafter. Structural section and dimensions

Across the guides, the base course may be constructed with a structural thickness that varies according to material type, ranging from four inches up to eighteen inches when reinforced cement concrete is employed beneath an asphalt wearing surface [1]. For RCC sections thinner than eight inches, control joints are required at spacings of fifteen to twenty feet, while for thicker RCC sections the joint spacing equals 2.5 times the pavement thickness expressed in feet [1]. Conventional new or reconstructed pavements are required to have a minimum total base‑course thickness of six inches, with an Asphalt Rich Base Course placed in the lower portion of that layer and occupying between three and six inches of the first lift; the aggregate size for this material is limited to a nominal maximum of twenty‑five millimetres [1]. When a cold‑recycled base course is specified, the compacted depth of the recycled mixture must be at least three inches but may not exceed five inches, which is achieved by retaining an existing asphalt layer of at least two inches and adding one inch of reclaimed or virgin aggregate to meet the minimum [1]. The design of the base course also requires that any total compacted thickness greater than six inches be placed in two or more lifts of approximately equal depth, with individual lift thicknesses constrained by a lower limit of three inches and an upper limit of six inches for depths up to eight inches, and by broader limits of four to eight inches for depths exceeding eight inches and five to ten inches for depths over ten inches [2]. An additional overall restriction caps any single compacted lift at four inches, ensuring that no layer exceeds this thickness regardless of the total design depth [2]. Specific asphalt mixes impose comparable lift limits; a Superpave base with a nominal maximum aggregate size of twenty‑five millimetres deeper than six inches must be placed in lifts of three to six inches, while a Superpave base using thirty‑seven point five millimetre aggregate and exceeding eight inches depth requires lifts of four to eight inches [2]. Prior to spreading the coarse base material, a fine‑material bedding is laid on the prepared subgrade or existing subbase, with a thickness of two inches when placed directly on subgrade and one inch when applied over an existing subbase or pavement [2]. The guides do not prescribe explicit dimensional requirements for the subbase layer itself, treated‑soil, slab, reinforcement, or any additional pavement layers beyond those described for the base course [1][2]. Shoulders are required to be built up and compacted to at least the elevation of each successive base‑course layer, although no specific shoulder thickness is stipulated [2]. Edge support is addressed by extending longitudinal joints one to two inches beyond the adjacent lane edge to provide a smooth transition between lanes [2].

The Base Course specification must first identify the material class to be used, which may include granular aggregate base, roller‑compacted concrete, asphalt‑rich base, cold‑recycled asphalt base, asphalt‑treated or cement‑treated permeable base, or a cement‑based mixture such as aggregate‑cement or aggregate‑lime‑pozzolan. [1][2] For granular bases the guides require highly permeable aggregates whose gradation is used as the primary indicator test to ensure a void structure that permits rapid gravity drainage. [1] The mix design for an asphalt‑stabilized base must contain a well‑graded coarse aggregate limited to a maximum nominal size of 1¼ in when reclaimed pavement is incorporated, complemented by fine aggregate and any supplemental aggregate required by the design, together with reclaimed asphalt pavement meeting the same size limit. [2] When the selected base type is roller‑compacted concrete, the mixture consists of cement, aggregate and water in proportions similar to conventional concrete but with a lower water‑to‑cement ratio typically 0.40 and permitted down to 0.30, using a dense aggregate gradation whose maximum size does not exceed one inch; air entrainment may be used and admixtures are limited. [1][2] The resulting RCC mix is expected to achieve a compressive strength in the range of approximately four thousand to ten thousand pounds per square inch, providing high stiffness and low slump for dimensional stability under traffic loads. [1][2] If the total base thickness exceeds six inches, an asphalt‑rich base course must be placed in the bottom three to six inches of the first lift; this mix is limited to low‑traffic designs handling fewer than 0.3 million equivalent single axle loads, uses a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25 mm (1 in), and is compacted to design air voids of about 2.5 percent to develop high tensile strength at the pavement bottom. [1] Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses are produced by milling existing asphalt pavement to a specified depth, blending the reclaimed material with an emulsified (or foamed) asphalt binder, and when the reclaimed mix is a wearing course or fine‑graded material, adding virgin coarse aggregate of No. 8, 57 or 67 size to satisfy the design; polymer‑modified emulsions are recommended for high‑traffic conditions. The selection and proportioning of base‑course materials begins with an assessment of the structural demand imposed by projected traffic volumes, environmental conditions, and subgrade characteristics. For conventional granular bases the primary criterion is high permeability, achieved through a particle‑size gradation that also retains sufficient coarse content to provide a stable platform for construction equipment, recognizing that a modest reduction in permeability may be sacrificed to improve workability during placement. When a rigid concrete base is specified, the mixture consists of cement, densely graded aggregate with a maximum size under one inch, and water at a low water‑to‑cement ratio—typically around 0.40 and as low as 0.30—to obtain compressive strengths between 4,000 psi and 10,000 psi, with design thickness ranging from four to eighteen inches according to anticipated traffic loads and subgrade preparation. For base courses exceeding six inches in total thickness an Asphalt‑Rich Base Course is placed in the lower three to six inches; the mix is limited to less than 0.3 million ESALs, contains air voids near 2.5 %, and uses a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25 mm to deliver high tensile strength at the pavement bottom and resistance to moisture‑induced cracking while remaining constructible. In cold‑recycling applications the compacted thickness is limited to three–five inches and followed by at least one week of curing under warm, low‑humidity conditions; if the reclaimed asphalt pavement is fine‑graded, virgin coarse aggregate (No. 8, 57 or 67) is added, and polymer‑modified emulsions are used when daily traffic exceeds about three thousand AADT, truck percentages are high, or early strength is required, with projects carrying more than two hundred daily ESALs or exhibiting poor drainage excluded to safeguard durability and performance. Reclaimed‑pavement bases may be stabilized mechanically by adding aggregate or RAP to achieve a suitable gradation, or chemically using cement, lime, fly ash or similar agents, with type and dosage determined through laboratory testing to meet strength targets without inducing brittleness; excessive stabilizer content is avoided to preserve durability. The mix design for base courses specifies the proportions of coarse and fine aggregates, reclaimed material, any additional RAP or aggregate, stabilizing additives, binder type and water; coarse and fine aggregates are measured separately by volume or weight and fed to a mass‑proportioning plant equipped with separate bins for each size to ensure correct dry‑mix ratios. Liquid components—water, calcium chloride, emulsified asphalt or other stabilizers—are metered automatically within ± 2 % by weight; when calcium chloride is required it is added before water at 7 lb per ton for Type 1 mixes or 5.7 lb per ton for Type 2 mixes. For asphalt base courses the Superpave mixture design is applied, selecting a PG 64S‑22 binder and limiting expected traffic to less than 0.3 million ESALs unless otherwise directed; alternatively an aggregate base as referenced in Section 350.2 or a roller‑compacted concrete base using a stiffer low‑slump mix may be chosen. Placement depth governs layering: any course thicker than six inches in compacted depth must be constructed in two or more lifts, each lift between three and six inches, and each lift is compacted to meet or exceed the target density—at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum dry density at optimum moisture content for reclaimed mixes and a minimum of 96 % of the PTM 402 target density for stabilized mixes, with some specifications requiring 100 % of the maximum density. Moisture during compaction must be within three percent of the optimum value and may not exceed it by more than three percent; field verification is performed in increments such as 3,000‑square‑yard sections, and any low‑density areas are scarified, re‑moistened and recompacted. Structural details and interfaces

The base course, whether placed as roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) or as an asphalt‑treated base course (ATPBC), does not call for forms, steel reinforcement, dowel bars, or other load‑transfer devices. When RCC is used, control joints are optional and, if provided, are spaced at 15 to 20 ft for thicknesses under eight inches or at a distance equal to 2.5 times the pavement thickness in feet for sections eight inches thick or greater. For ATPBC, longitudinal construction joints are employed primarily for continuity and construction convenience, and they are formed as tied joints that overlap the previously placed lane by one to two inches and are compacted to create a smooth, bonded connection; full‑width joints do not require sawing. Transverse construction joints are required at the end of each workday or whenever paving is interrupted for more than thirty minutes, and they may be created either by installing a temporary vertical bulkhead that spans the full depth and width of the base course or by sawing a joint in accordance with Section 501.3(i)1. When new material meets material placed on the previous day, the existing edge must be scarified, moistened, blended with fresh mix, and compacted so that a continuous, joint‑free section is achieved, ensuring positive bonding and uniform compaction between adjacent lanes. For base‑course widening of six feet or less, formwork is not required provided an acceptable containment method is used. [1][2]

The base course is engineered to create a seamless and durable interface with the overlying surface layer, the underlying subgrade or subbase, adjacent structural elements, and surrounding roadway features [1][2]. High‑permeability aggregate for the base and subbase is selected by gradation so that water can flow freely, and the subbase is sloped as steeply as practical to promote gravity drainage [1]. Continuous edge drains are placed along the outer margin of the pavement on the low side of any superelevation and in median sections where water problems have been identified, with lateral underdrains installed at cut‑to‑fill transitions and other wet subgrade areas; all drain outlets are protected by headwalls to prevent crushing or plugging [1]. Before placing the coarse base material a uniform fine‑material bed is spread—two inches thick on subgrade or one inch on subbase or existing pavement—and must be dry and stable [2]. If the underlying layer has become contaminated with base‑course aggregate, the affected portion is stripped, reshaped, recompacted and rebuilt to restore a sound foundation, and all vertical faces of the patched area are coated with a uniform film of PG 64S‑22 to ensure bonding [2]. Successive base‑course lifts are placed in layers no thinner than three inches and no thicker than six inches; when more than six inches of compacted depth is required, the material is built up in two or more approximately equal lifts [1][2]. Each lift is scarified, moistened and blended with any adjacent material from a previous day’s work, then compacted to at least 100 % of its maximum dry density before the next lift is applied, thereby eliminating construction joints [2]. When the base course is installed over an existing pavement it is built in compacted layers, each layer being rolled, water‑sprinkled and re‑rolled to create a monolithic surface ready for the surface course [2]. Longitudinal joints are formed for construction convenience by overlapping the new material one to two inches onto the previously placed lane edge and, where required, tying the joint according to standard cement‑concrete pavement drawings; full‑width work omits sawing [2][1]. Transverse construction joints are created at the end of each workday or after any interruption longer than thirty minutes by installing a temporary vertical bulkhead that spans the full depth and width of the base course, or alternatively by sawing [2]. Any surface irregularities exceeding one‑half inch are corrected before final compaction, and any depth deficiency of half an inch or more is repaired by removal and replacement of the deficient portion [2]. At the form line the base must be smooth and correctly graded; low spots are raised with shims not greater than one inch in height, installed so that forms deflect no more than 0.01 inch under a 1,700‑pound load, while high areas are cut and recompacted to the design grade [2]. The surface is kept moist by thorough sprinkling of water, and for treated permeable bases the temperature is maintained below 115 °F before placement [2][1]. Expansion joint filler is placed around adjoining structures—at least one‑half inch thick around utilities and three‑quarters inch where the pavement meets bridges or culverts—to seal the interface [2]. Shoulder areas are cleared, scarified as needed, shaped and compacted to the exact bottom elevation of the new base, then backfilled with aggregate base‑course material and compacted until it will not move under equipment [2]. When a roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) base is used, it is placed on a prepared, adequately compacted subgrade and subbase surface capable of supporting the dry‑mix concrete during placement; the RCC layer can be positioned directly beneath an asphalt wearing course or other surface treatment without diamond grinding unless it will remain exposed as the final pavement surface for speeds of 35 mph or higher [1]. Control joints in the RCC are aligned with the spacing and alignment of any adjacent concrete mainline to maintain continuity across the joint interface [1]. For cold‑recycled base courses, existing asphalt is milled, mixed with emulsified binder, placed to the required grade and compacted; the restored base is then bonded to a Superpave asphalt overlay, although a cold‑mix asphalt overlay or surface treatment may be used when traffic volumes are low [1]. The structural interaction between the base and subbase is treated as a combined system: their thicknesses are added and a weighted loss‑of‑support factor is applied to compute an effective modulus of subgrade reaction, with design specifications requiring an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base over a 2A subbase and limits on the ratio between subbase modulus and roadbed resilient modulus [1]. Once a uniformly compacted base is achieved, an asphalt prime coat and surface treatment are applied as specified, the finished surface receives a rake or broom texture, only black liquid‑membrane curing compound may be used, and traffic is prohibited until the overlying surface course is placed [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Design of the base course must provide a well‑drained system that rapidly removes water from both the surface and interior layers; this is achieved by selecting highly permeable granular aggregates, maximizing subbase slope, maintaining positive longitudinal slope, installing lateral underdrains in wet subgrade zones and at all cut‑to‑fill transitions, and providing continuous edge drains along the low side of superelevations with protected outlets [1]. During mixing and placement the moisture content is tightly controlled by measuring water, adding calcium chloride before water when required, covering the mix during haulage, excluding frost‑containing material, drying aggregates as needed, limiting mixture temperature to below 320 °F, and placing the product within eight hours of production; placement on frozen or unstable surfaces, on wet prepared subgrade, or when air temperature is at or below critical limits (35 °F for general base work and 40 °F for mixes containing calcium chloride) is prohibited, and a forecast of freezing within the next 24 hours also precludes placement [2]. In districts that restrict work from early September through April, placement may proceed only with a written waiver and when ambient temperature has been at least 45 °F and rising for 24 hours without freezing [2]. Construction crews keep each layer moist but free of standing water by sprinkling sufficient water to achieve full saturation without creating puddles, cooling the surface of treated permeable bases below 115 °F when required, and limiting compaction so that the material remains free‑draining and aggregate particles are not crushed; for fine‑material layers the surface is sprinkled and rolled, then allowed to dry before the next course, and the interval between adding water to an aggregate‑cement mix and completing compaction must not exceed one hour (or up to 1½ hours when a retarding admixture is used) [2]. Where frost‑susceptible subgrade exists, the design calls for a stronger base course capable of withstanding reduced bearing support, supplemented by pavement base drains or comparable drainage provisions to mitigate moisture infiltration and freeze‑thaw effects; if soil conditions vary across the project the design may be divided into sections so that each thickness can accommodate appropriate drainage [1]. For projects whose total base thickness exceeds six inches an Asphalt Rich Base Course (ARBC) is placed as a three‑to‑six‑inch bottom lift, limited to designs with less than 0.3 million ESAL and compacted to about 2.5 % air voids to maximize tensile strength and resistance to moisture‑induced cracking, while binder grades and mixture properties are selected using the Superpave system to match local climatic extremes [1]. Cold‑recycled pavements are accepted only when the existing bases and subgrades are structurally sound and well‑drained; deteriorated sections caused by poor drainage are excluded, and after placement a minimum one‑week curing period under low humidity and high temperature is required before applying the wearing course to ensure adequate strength despite moisture and climate effects [1]. In structural analysis the base and subbase are treated as a single composite for the effective k‑value, and the elastic modulus of the base is adjusted for seasonal conditions—approximately 15 000 psi during spring thaw, 30 000 psi in normal summer/fall, and 50 000 psi when frozen—to capture moisture and temperature influences, with loss‑of‑support factors applied to account for potential erosion or pumping [1]. All Interstate and Expressway rigid pavement designs require an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) over a 2A subbase, but such drainage layers may be specified only where a positive outlet can be provided; otherwise a dense base with longitudinal edge drains is used, and the drainage coefficient for all pavement layers is uniformly set to 1.0 [1]. After placement the base course is cured either by applying a membrane‑forming compound at roughly one gallon per 100 ft² or by covering with polyethylene for a minimum of 24 hours; if temperature drops below 40 °F during curing the period is extended an additional 24 hours, and any drop to 32 °F within 72 hours requires removal and replacement of the material [2]. The cured base must reach an average moisture content below two percent before any wearing or binder course is installed, construction traffic is prohibited for at least three days, paving operations are halted if evaporation exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·hr unless mitigation such as windbreaks or fog misting is employed, and concrete placement is stopped when ambient temperature falls below 40 °F (45 °F for accelerated‑strength mixes) [2].

The base course shall be placed only after the subgrade has been prepared and is dry, with ambient or surface temperature above 35 °F, and all work must occur during daylight hours. When a roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) material is used as the base, it may be employed only on roadways whose average daily traffic does not exceed 2,000 vehicles and where the posted speed limit is 55 mph or lower. If the RCC surface will remain exposed and the design speed is 35 mph or greater, the pavement must be textured by diamond grinding before any wearing course is applied. Control joints in an RCC base are spaced between fifteen and twenty feet when the slab thickness is less than eight inches, and for slabs eight inches thick or more the spacing equals the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet. The material for a conventional asphalt base must be placed in lifts whose compacted thickness conforms to the mix design; for a 25‑mm Superpave base no lift may be less than three inches or greater than six inches, and for a 37.5‑mm base the limits are four to eight inches when the total depth exceeds those thresholds. Each lifted layer shall be compacted to at least ninety‑six percent of the target density specified by the PTM method. Geometric tolerances for the finished surface are tightly controlled using installed string lines, cross‑section templates and a twelve‑foot straightedge advanced in five‑foot increments; transverse irregularities must be checked at intervals not exceeding twenty‑five feet and longitudinal smoothness verified over each three‑thousand‑square‑yard segment. No average deviation may exceed one‑half inch when measured with a ten‑ or twelve‑foot straightedge, and any surface irregularity greater than one‑quarter inch is considered defective and must be corrected immediately by loosening the mix or adding material. Depth accuracy is verified with test holes, and sections that are more than half an inch short of the design elevation are deemed deficient and require prompt repair. The final cross‑slope of the base course shall be at least one‑quarter inch per foot. Shoulder construction follows the same geometric control, with shoulders built up and compacted to the elevation of each base layer, scarified no deeper than four inches below existing grade, shaped and compacted to the bottom elevation shown on the plans without disturbing existing guide rails, and any damaged rail must be satisfactorily repaired. Safety requirements limit traffic on the freshly placed base to only necessary local vehicles and essential construction equipment; a pilot car shall be used for speed control when raveling or other damage is possible, and the surface must be protected until an overlying pavement is applied. Construction traffic is prohibited for at least three days after placement, and the base may not be opened to unrestricted traffic until it attains the required compressive strength of 2 750 psi (or the specified seven‑day strength); light vehicles may be permitted once the material has achieved a stable condition, with full opening determined by the representative. Curing begins immediately after compaction, with a curing compound applied at the prescribed rate, and the cure period is extended if temperatures fall below forty degrees Fahrenheit. The interval from water addition to completion of compaction shall not exceed one hour, or one‑and‑a‑half hours when a retarding admixture is used. [1][2]

Verification and expected performance

The completed base course is subjected to a systematic series of inspections that verify compliance with the design, confirm constructability, demonstrate performance capability, and ensure alignment with all contract specifications. Compliance begins with density verification; each layer must achieve at least the target density prescribed in the applicable pavement test method—no less than 95 % of the laboratory maximum for reclaimed or asphalt‑treated mixes under PTM 106 and a minimum of 96 % of the PTM 402 target for new bases. In‑place density is measured on every 3,000‑square‑yard segment using AASHTO T310 nuclear methods, with a 300‑foot control strip established during reclamation and at least ten random readings taken after each roller pass. Moisture content is checked against the mix design and must not exceed optimum moisture by more than three percent; the surface is kept moist but free of standing water, and for permeable bases it is cooled below 115 °F before any concrete placement. Temperature limits are also enforced—placement is prohibited when air or surface temperature falls to 35 °F or lower.

Dimensional control is achieved by drilling a six‑inch diameter test hole in each designated area and measuring the compacted depth against the design elevation; any location short of half an inch must be removed and rebuilt. Where the specified compacted thickness exceeds six inches, the material is placed in two or more lifts, each between three and six inches, to avoid excessive layer depth. Surface smoothness and grade are evaluated with a straightedge or template at intervals not exceeding 25 feet (or every five feet when using a ten‑foot straightedge), advancing the tool parallel to the centreline across transverse joints and paving notches. The average deviation across three measured points must be less than one‑half inch; deviations greater than this trigger corrective action by loosening the surface mix and adding or removing cement‑treated base material until the tolerance is met. Irregularities exceeding a quarter inch are deemed defective, as are depth deficiencies of half an inch that cannot be satisfactorily corrected.

Constructability checks confirm that all preparatory work—scarifying, back‑filling, guide‑rail repair, and removal of unsuitable material—has been completed and that form lines remain undisturbed after final testing. Non‑destructive depth‑testing targets are placed as required and inspected daily; any misplaced or damaged target is repaired before paving. Compaction equipment must meet the specifications in Section 108.05, employing steel‑wheel rollers with certified weights of eight to ten tons (or the rollers specified for the particular base type) within one hour of water addition, or within ninety minutes when a retarding admixture is used. The material is considered satisfactorily compacted when it does not move under the rollers.

Performance verification includes strength and curing criteria. The Representative will not permit traffic until the base has attained a minimum compressive strength of 2,750 psi, demonstrated either by cylinder‑compressive testing in accordance with PTM 604 or by the maturity method prescribed in AASHTO T413. Immediate curing is provided by applying the specified membrane compound for at least twenty‑four hours, extending the period if ambient temperature drops below forty degrees Fahrenheit. After a seven‑day cure, core specimens are tested for indirect tensile or unconfined compressive strength, and moisture content must fall below two percent. Construction traffic is prohibited for three days after placement; thereafter only equipment required for the next pavement layer may enter the area.

Through this integrated sequence of density, moisture, temperature, thickness, grade, smoothness, constructability, and performance inspections—each anchored to explicit numerical thresholds and documented test frequencies—the completed base course is demonstrated to meet all contractual requirements, be constructible in the field, perform as intended under anticipated loads, and remain fully consistent with the project specifications.
