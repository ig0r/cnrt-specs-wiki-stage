Compaction

Part of: Pavement Structure is-subtopic-of
Compaction is a topic under Pavement Structure.
compaction
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-29

Compaction is the densification technique applied to virtually every pavement layer to eliminate voids and raise each material to its prescribed density, thereby preventing settlement, rutting, surface irregularities and loss of strength that would otherwise cause premature failure [2]. It works by controlling moisture within a narrow band around optimum, selecting rollers (steel‑wheel, pad‑foot, pneumatic‑tire or vibratory) with loads and speeds limited to about 1.5 ft s⁻¹, and making overlapping passes until no appreciable increase in density is observed [2]. The process is defined by strict density thresholds—e.g., at least 97 % of maximum dry density for deep embankments, 100 % within three feet of the finished grade, and greater than 96 % after final rolling—and verified through field tests that confirm a non‑movement condition and compliance with these targets [2].

PennDOT publications covering “Compaction” also include the following subtopics: Maximum Dry Density.

The Maximum Dry Density establishes the laboratory-derived reference unit weight against which field densities are measured and verified using calibrated nuclear or electrical-impedance gauges. [1][2] The target value dictates that in-place compaction must reach at least 97 percent of the maximum dry density for deep embankments, while the upper three feet require full compliance with this standard to ensure structural integrity. [2]

Maximum Dry Density

Purpose and applicability

Compaction addresses the fundamental problem of insufficient in‑place density that would otherwise create unstable, movable or defective pavement layers. When soil fills, granular bases, reclaimed‑asphalt mixes, cement‑treated layers or hot‑mix asphalt are placed, a lack of densification leaves voids and permits settlement, permanent deformation such as rutting greater than one‑half inch, surface irregularities exceeding three‑eighths of an inch, loss of strength, binder flushing, roller marks and other performance failures. By raising each layer to the prescribed density—97 % of maximum dry density for embankment material below three feet, 100 % for the top three feet and structural subgrade, at least 95 % of laboratory‑maximum density for reclaimed‑asphalt, SMA and similar mixes, 85–90 % of maximum wet density directly behind the paver (depending on thickness) and greater than 96 % after final rolling, a minimum of 96 % of the PTM 402 target for base courses, and an overall average not less than 98 % with no individual test below 96 % of maximum wet density—the process eliminates those defects. Moisture is controlled to within a few percent of optimum, roller speed is limited to no more than 1.5 ft s⁻¹, and appropriate rollers—steel‑wheel, padfoot, pneumatic‑tire or vibratory—are selected with loads ranging from eight to ten tons for asphalt concrete, ≥25 tons for reclaimed mixes, and applied in overlapping passes until no appreciable increase in density is observed. When the required density is achieved, field testing (AASHTO T 191, T 310, PTM 402, 715/716) shows that the material does not move under the equipment, settlement and rutting are limited to less than one‑half inch, surface undulations are below three‑eighths of an inch, permeability of pervious layers is retained, and the pavement meets its strength and durability criteria. If any layer fails to meet its density threshold, the material must be reshaped, re‑moistened, loosened or replaced and compacted again, thereby preventing premature failure once traffic service begins. [2]

Compaction is regarded as the preferred densification technique for virtually every pavement layer when the supporting conditions and material specifications are satisfied [2]. In the particular case of using reclaimed in‑place material, compaction—often termed pulverization stabilization—is appropriate only when that material can attain the required strength simply by re‑compacting it with moisture and the project objective is to improve support layers rather than to remedy surface distress [1][2]. The subgrade must be sound, free of soft, muddy or frozen zones, and the placed material’s moisture content must lie within the range needed for optimum density—typically from three percent below optimum up to optimum for cohesive soils and within plus‑or‑minus two percent of optimum for Type 1 granular fills [2]. Layers are placed in lifts that respect prescribed thickness limits; where standard full‑scale rollers can reach the surface, normal design thicknesses may be used, but in confined spaces such as pipe trenches, adjacent to structures, or when aggregate particles exceed two inches, lift thickness is reduced to four inches and smaller rollers are employed [2]. If the foundation is saturated, swampy, unstable, or frozen, compaction is abandoned and rock fill is placed instead [2]. When the reclaimed material’s inherent strength potential is insufficient, when additional aggregate or volume is required for structural reasons, or when the primary problems are surface‑related distresses, a different method—mechanical stabilization that adds aggregate or chemical stabilization incorporating cement, lime or fly ash—is recommended [1]. For cohesive soils and granular fills the target density is at least ninety‑seven percent of maximum dry density, increasing to one hundred percent in the top three feet of embankments or subgrades [2]. Granular Type 1 material is densified with a smooth‑drum vibratory roller while maintaining moisture within ±2 % of optimum; rock is considered compacted when it shows no movement under the same equipment [2]. Base‑course layers must reach at least one hundred percent of the maximum density determined by PTM No. 106 (Method B) or AASHTO T 191, and where the aggregate is too coarse for laboratory methods a field non‑movement test governs acceptance [2]. If additional passes fail to meet the target, the layer must be thoroughly loosened, re‑moistened, reshaped and compacted again; persistent failure mandates removal and replacement with a mix capable of attaining the required density [2]. When an asphalt lift is placed, compaction begins immediately behind the paver; lifts thinner than six inches must first achieve at least eighty‑five percent of the maximum wet density and lifts six inches or thicker at least ninety percent [2]. If the paver does not reach a field density of ninety‑five percent, a self‑propelled smooth dual steel‑drum roller weighing a minimum of eight tons is used for primary compaction before any final rolling, and after the ninety‑five percent threshold is met lighter equipment may be employed to finish the surface, with the final density required to exceed ninety‑six percent of the maximum wet density [2]. Fine‑graded aggregate may only be compacted when placed in uncompacted lifts not exceeding twenty‑four inches; for lifts between twelve and twenty‑four inches a tracked excavator or dozer applying ground pressures of six hundred twenty‑five to one thousand twenty‑five pounds per square foot is used, while lifts twelve inches or less are compacted with a plate compactor weighing one hundred ten to two hundred twenty pounds, and rollers are expressly prohibited on the FGA layer [2]. If lift thickness exceeds these limits, if crushing or gradation changes would occur, or if water or ice greater than six inches is present, compaction is avoided and a capping material is placed over the geotextile before rolling after the FGA installation [2]. Cement‑treated porous base course must be compacted within one hour of adding water to the aggregate and cement (extendable to one and a half hours with a retarding admixture) using an eight‑to‑ten‑ton steel‑wheel power roller for at least one pass; over‑compaction that eliminates drainage or crushes aggregate is prohibited, and if time, moisture or equipment constraints prevent compliance an alternative construction approach must be selected [2]. Stone‑matrix asphalt mixtures are rolled immediately after placement to meet density acceptance criteria, eliminate roller marks and avoid binder flushing; pneumatic‑tire rollers are required for scratch courses, while mechanical vibrating hand tampers replace rolling in tight curb areas where rollers cannot reach [2]. Pervious concrete that will carry trucks or buses requires compaction with a paver equipped with tampers and smooth steel‑wheeled rollers operated in static mode for one to four passes, whereas pneumatic‑tire rollers are prohibited and, where vehicular traffic is not anticipated, finishing may be accomplished with roller screeds, vibrating screeds, form‑riding paving machines or asphalt pavers instead of compaction rollers [2]. Special rolling is applied after the initial compaction of existing subgrades, raised embankments or spread‑footing foundations when permanent deflection exceeds allowable limits—greater than one‑half inch for subgrade and greater than one inch for embankments—and is not used on newly constructed subgrades; if three consecutive passes produce less than a one percent increase in measured density or moisture conditions are excessive, the material must be dried, re‑compacted or replaced before special rolling proceeds [2]. In all situations where any of the prescribed conditions—adequate subgrade strength, proper moisture, suitable temperature, appropriate layer thickness or accessible equipment—cannot be satisfied, compaction should be abandoned in favor of scarifying and repairing the material, adjusting moisture, postponing work, using hand‑tamping, employing core testing, or applying another acceptable method as dictated by specification [2].

Required inputs

Compaction in pavement construction requires a broad spectrum of materials that are each matched to appropriate handling and densification methods [1][2]. The guides accept cohesive soils, Type 1 and Type 2 granular fills, rock, fine‑graded aggregate, reclaimed‑base mixes produced through full‑depth reclamation, roller‑compacted concrete (RCC), cement‑treated pavement base course (CTPBC), stone‑matrix asphalt (SMA), hot‑mix asphalt (HMA) layers, sub‑base aggregates such as No. 2A, open‑graded slag (OGS) or granulated slag, cold‑recycled aggregate bases, and pervious asphalt mixes, among others [2]. Each material must be placed in uniform horizontal lifts of prescribed thickness; cohesive soils and Type 1 granular fill are typically laid in six‑inch lifts while fine‑graded aggregate may be placed in lifts up to twenty‑four inches when tracked equipment is used [2]. The densification targets are expressed as percentages of laboratory‑determined maximum dry or wet density, with soils and granular fills deeper than three feet required to reach at least 97 percent of maximum dry density and material within three feet of the finished grade demanded 100 percent [2]. RCC lifts less than six inches must achieve a minimum of 85 percent of the maximum wet density and lifts six inches or greater at least 90 percent, with a final steel‑wheel roller pass bringing the mix to more than 96 percent of design density, while full‑depth reclamation mixes are required to attain no less than 95 percent of their laboratory‑compacted maximum density at optimum moisture content [2]. The RCC mixture itself is a very dry concrete in which the water‑to‑cement ratio may be as low as 0.30 and is typically around 0.40, and it employs a dense aggregate gradation whose maximum particle size does not exceed one inch; the mix exhibits virtually no slump, contains limited air‑entraining agents or other admixtures, and is placed by a conventional or high‑density asphalt paver before roller densification [1]. In full‑depth reclamation the existing in‑place pavement is pulverized, water is added to bring the material to its optimum moisture content, and gradation may be improved by adding aggregate or reclaimed asphalt pavement; when higher strength is needed chemical stabilizers such as Portland cement, lime or fly ash are incorporated, but the mix design must balance density and strength to avoid excessive brittleness [1]. For native subgrade soils the achievable density depends on gradation quality (well‑graded versus poorly graded), the percent of material passing a #200 sieve (fines content), and the Atterberg limits that define the AASHTO group index; laboratory compaction testing determines the maximum dry density and its associated optimum moisture content for each soil type [1]. Moisture control is critical across all layers: cohesive soils may not be compacted at a moisture level more than three percent below optimum, Type 1 granular material must stay within ±2 percent of its optimum moisture, reclaimed‑base mixes may not exceed the optimum by more than three percent, and fine‑graded aggregate must be placed on a surface that is moist but not wet; RCC temperature must be maintained between 50 °F and 90 °F and all other mixes should remain above 40 °F, with HMA produced below 320 °F and placed while still hot enough to flow, typically within eight hours of production and on a surface at least 35 °F (50 °F for pervious mixes) [2]. Fine‑graded aggregate compaction requires the application of a ground pressure between 625 and 1,025 pounds per square foot, while steel‑wheel power rollers weighing eight to ten tons are specified for CTPBC and RCC densification, and pneumatic‑tire rollers operating at 40–60 psi are used for SMA [2]. Successful compaction also depends on proper site preparation; the subgrade, sub‑base and any embankment foundations must be clean, free of foreign material, standing water deeper than six inches, ice or frost, and must not be frozen at the time of placement, while RCC requires the underlying layers to be uniformly moistened but not ponded and fine‑graded aggregate typically requires a Class 4 Type A geotextile beneath it [2]. When expanded geocell cells are employed, the subgrade must first be uniformly compacted and the cells anchored at least twelve inches below their base with overfill ensuring the finished surface rises at least one inch above the cell tops [2]. Ambient temperature restrictions apply to all layers: placement is prohibited when air temperatures fall below 40 °F (35 °F for cement slurries with accelerators), pervious mixes may not be placed unless the surface temperature reaches 50 °F, and work must be halted if freezing is forecast within the next twenty‑four hours; rain forecasts that would wet hot‑mix asphalt layers also preclude compaction until conditions improve [2][1]. Effective drainage is required to identify and eliminate wet subgrade zones so that excess moisture does not compromise material integrity during densification [1]. Thus, across the guides, compaction demands careful selection of appropriate materials, strict control of mixture properties such as gradation, water‑cement ratio, optimum moisture content and temperature, and diligent preparation of site conditions including subgrade cleanliness, moisture management, temperature limits, and drainage to achieve the prescribed density targets [1][2].

Pre‑construction compaction control begins with a documented quality‑control plan that must be submitted at the pre‑construction conference or no later than fourteen calendar days before any material placement, and the plan must list make, model, weight, tire pressure and ground‑pressure data for each roller together with serial numbers for approval. Laboratory moisture‑density testing performed in accordance with PTM No. 106 establishes the maximum dry density and optimum moisture content that the field‑compacted subgrade, base or aggregate layers must achieve; these two parameters serve as the primary acceptance criteria for densifying soils, aggregates or base courses. In addition, a California Bearing Ratio test on soaked samples conducted per AASHTO T 193 is required, and any CBR result exceeding ten must be reviewed and approved by the district and chief geotechnical engineers before it can be used in pavement design. A proof‑rolling test of the entire embankment or fill foundation is also mandated, using a loaded 72 000‑lb triaxle dump truck inflated uniformly to between one hundred and one‑hundred ten pounds per square inch and operated at no more than one‑and‑a‑half feet per second; any permanent rut deeper than one‑half inch indicates instability that must be remedied before further work. Furthermore, a control strip of at least three hundred feet is constructed with the prescribed roller pattern, and nuclear‑gauge density readings after each pass must meet a minimum of ninety‑five percent of the laboratory‑maximum dry density at optimum moisture content, which may not exceed three percent above optimum; ten random measurements are taken on the strip and gauge calibration is required annually with verification tolerances of plus or minus two point‑cubic‑feet wet density and plus or minus zero‑point‑eight percent moisture.

Density requirements are expressed as percentages of maximum dry density. Material placed more than three feet below the proposed subgrade must be compacted to at least ninety‑seven percent, while material within three feet of the final grade, structure foundations and subgrades must achieve one hundred percent of the laboratory‑maximum dry density. Reclaimed base and other granular layers require a minimum of ninety‑five percent, interim densities behind the paver must be at least eighty‑five percent for lifts under six inches and ninety percent for lifts six inches or greater, and after final rolling all thicknesses must exceed ninety‑six percent of the maximum wet density. If a lift fails to meet its target it is loosened, re‑moistened, reshaped and re‑compacted; persistent failure results in removal and replacement of the material.

Lift thickness limits are governed by material type. Soil and Type 1 granular base lifts may not exceed six inches, Type 2 granular lifts eight inches, and fine‑graded aggregate placed with tracked equipment may be placed up to twenty‑four inches provided a ground pressure of six hundred twenty‑five to one thousand twenty‑five pounds per square foot is applied and four full passes are made; where tracked access is unavailable the lift is limited to twelve inches and a plate compactor weighing between one‑hundred ten and two‑hundred twenty pounds must also make four passes. Base‑course lifts must be no thinner than three inches and no thicker than six inches, and any required compacted depth greater than six inches shall be achieved in two or more layers of approximately equal thickness within that range.

Moisture content for all materials must lie between minus three percent of the optimum moisture content and the optimum value, with Type 1 granular material permitted a tolerance of plus‑or‑minus two percent; moisture is continuously monitored with frequent field tests. Rolling speed is limited to two miles per hour (approximately one foot per second), successive passes are acceptable only when the increase in in‑place moisture‑density is less than one percent and permanent deflection does not exceed one‑half inch for subgrade or one inch for embankments.

Compaction equipment must satisfy explicit weight, width and pressure criteria. Pneumatic‑tire rollers are limited to ten feet overall width, may carry twenty‑six to twenty‑eight tons, develop a ground pressure of at least three hundred pounds per inch of tire width, have wheel‑path overlap of at least half a foot and maintain tire inflation uniform within five pounds per square inch. Sheepsfoot or pad rollers must exert a minimum contact pressure of one hundred sixty psi; smooth steel‑drum vibratory rollers are required to deliver a dynamic force of fifty thousand pounds; three‑wheel power rollers must provide an unballasted load of at least three hundred pounds per linear inch of rear‑wheel tread and weigh no less than ten tons. Field density verification is performed using ASTM C1040 direct‑transmission tests (or AASHTO T191/T310 where specified) no later than thirty minutes after rolling, with a minimum of five measurements taken behind the paver and five after final compaction for each five hundred cubic yards placed; nuclear‑gauge readings are also taken after each roller pass on the control strip.

Special compaction operations follow additional criteria. Cold‑mix cementitious base courses require steel‑wheel rollers weighing eight to ten tons to make at least one full‑direction pass within one hour of water addition (or one and a half hours when a retarding admixture is used), and surface tolerances are checked with a ten‑foot straightedge moved no more than five feet at a time, any deviation greater than one‑half inch being corrected before final acceptance. Stone‑matrix asphalt must meet the percent‑compaction tolerances defined in the project tables; roller marks must be eliminated without causing binder flushing, steel‑wheel rollers are kept moist and clean, and speed, pattern, size and, where approved, vibratory amplitude or frequency are adjusted to avoid displacement, shoving, cracking or aggregate breakage. Reclaimed‑asphalt material requires a pneumatic‑tire roller of at least twenty‑five tons for breakdown and intermediate passes on lifts under eight inches, a twenty‑five‑ton vibratory pad‑foot roller for initial breakdown on lifts eight inches or deeper, and a twelve‑ to fourteen‑ton steel‑drum static roller for finish rolling; no layer thicker than six inches may be placed in a single lift, and such layers must be divided into two or more lifts each ranging between three and six inches. Base courses are compacted in layers two to five inches thick, subbase lifts not exceeding eight inches (four inches when granulated slag is used), and pervious asphalt lifts are limited to four inches with no more than four passes to preserve porosity. Surface irregularities exceeding one‑half inch must be corrected by loosening, adding or removing material.

All pre‑construction testing, including the thirty‑hundred‑foot test strip for reclaimed‑asphalt mixes and a minimum of five randomly placed six‑inch cores per quality‑control strip with a lower acceptance limit of ninety‑one percent, is performed before full production; cores are extracted within twenty‑four hours of placement, labeled and backfilled after testing. Compaction must be completed before traffic is permitted, using a pneumatic‑tire roller with tires inflated to forty to sixty pounds per square inch, the operator rolling the entire surface of each placed mixture, and when a double application is specified each layer must be fully rolled before the next layer is placed. Equipment and personnel

Compaction work begins with a thorough site preparation that removes any standing water deeper than six inches and all ice, then establishes a stable foundation by proof‑rolling the embankment or fill using a loaded triaxle dump truck of 72 000 lb gross vehicle weight whose tires are inflated uniformly between 100 and 110 psi. In reclamation projects a pulverizer may be employed to break the existing roadway, while a self‑propelled rotary reclaimer can cut up to sixteen inches when existing pavement must be removed. For cold‑recycled bases a self‑propelled milling machine equipped with a down‑cutting drum, screening and crushing plant feeds a pugmill mixer that meters liquids within ±2 % by weight; unbound subgrade is scarified to at least six inches with a spring‑tooth rake pulled by a light tractor. Once the surface is prepared, the mix is produced in a processing and mixing plant that proportionates coarse and fine aggregates and water according to the approved design, and continuous delivery is maintained with covered haul trucks or a material transfer vehicle (MTV). Where roller‑compacted concrete is placed, a weighted paver spreads and preliminarily compacts each lift; where a paver cannot be used, adjustable self‑propelled mechanical spreaders place and screed the material without segregation, and ready‑mix concrete trucks equipped with recirculating pumps keep slurries from settling. Fill‑grade aggregate is placed in lifts not exceeding twenty‑four inches using a tracked excavator or dozer that applies 625–1 025 psf ground pressure over four passes, while plate compactors weighing 110–220 lb are employed in confined zones.

Densification employs equipment selected for the material type and layer thickness. Soil layers are compacted with a pad‑foot roller meeting Section 108.05(c)3.g., whereas granular Type 1 and Type 2 materials are densified with smooth‑drum vibratory rollers as specified in Section 108.05(c)3.i. Asphalt, roller‑compacted concrete and pervious concrete lifts are consolidated with steel‑wheel power rollers having a certified metal weight of eight to ten tons; if the paver does not achieve at least 95 % field density, a self‑propelled dual steel‑drum roller of minimum eight tons operating in static or vibratory mode is used. For thin emulsion or foam mixes less than eight inches thick a 25‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller provides breakdown and intermediate rolling, while mixes eight inches or deeper receive an initial pass with a 25‑ton vibratory pad‑foot roller followed by finish rolling with a single or tandem steel‑drum static roller of twelve to fourteen tons. Double‑drum asphalt rollers not less than eight tons make one to four passes with water or soap systems to prevent adhesion, and pneumatic‑tire rollers are used for scratch courses. Tracked excavators or dozers continue to apply the required ground pressure during compaction, plate compactors (110–220 lb) address confined zones, hand‑operated compactors and walk‑behind vibratory units serve tight spaces, and mechanical vibrating hand tampers are deployed where rollers cannot reach.

Finishing of each layer is achieved by rolling the completed surface daily with a smooth‑drum roller to promote drainage, using the paver’s safety‑edge device and hand tools such as squeegees and lutes to eliminate ragged edges, and verifying tolerances with a ten‑foot straightedge, templates and string lines. Any irregularities exceeding one‑half inch are corrected before work proceeds; pervious concrete surfaces receive a maximum two‑ton roller or steel finishing tools to preserve voids, and fog seals may be sprayed for dust control after surface corrections.

Curing is maintained by keeping the compacted layer moist with water trucks equipped with fog nozzles or spray devices, applying emulsified asphalt primer, fog seal or tack coat at a residual rate of 0.02–0.10 gal per square yard, or spreading a white membrane‑forming curing compound at one gallon per 100 ft² (±10 %). Alternatively, the surface may be covered with white polyethylene sheeting for the prescribed period. Hot‑mix asphalt lifts are allowed to cool to approximately 100 °F before subsequent lifts, while emulsified or foamed asphalt layers require a minimum two‑week cure until moisture falls below 2 % and ambient temperatures remain above 40 °F for at least seven days. Pervious concrete curing begins within twenty minutes of discharge by completely covering the surface with polyethylene sheeting.

Testing to confirm that compaction objectives have been met utilizes calibrated nuclear density/moisture gauges operated in direct‑transmission mode by certified operators, with daily standardization and annual calibration. Nuclear readings are taken after each pass of the compaction equipment per PTM No. 402, and field‑density measurements follow ASTM C1040 (direct transmission) within thirty minutes of compaction, as well as PTM No. 106 Method B and AASHTO T310 for each 3 000‑square‑yard segment. Laboratory acceptance includes core sampling tested for density, asphalt content and gradation according to PTM 715/716, PTM 757/702 and AASHTO T209, with specimens prepared in a Superpave Gyratory Compactor or as standard Proctor samples per ASTM D1633. Infiltration performance of pervious concrete is verified by ASTM C1701 on test sections of at least 225 ft² requiring an average rate exceeding 200 in/hr. Core drilling follows AASHTO T24, with depth measured using a PTM No. 614 device, and truck‑mounted unit‑weight testing conforms to ASTM C1688. [2]

Work sequence

Before any compaction can be initiated, the entire supporting structure has to be prepared, placed, and formally accepted by the project Representative. First, the existing ground is excavated to undisturbed soil and all fines, standing water deeper than six inches, and any ice are removed; the surface is then scarified a minimum of six inches with a spring‑tooth rake, and any damaged or overly compacted zones are repaired before the Representative inspects and formally accepts the work. Simultaneously, the subgrade and sub‑base courses that were sized and verified during the design phase are placed, and they must be capable of bearing the loads imposed by subsequent rolling operations; these layers are required to remain smooth and free of ruts, irregularities, or loose material at least 1,500 feet ahead of the leading spreader. Shoulders are built up and compacted to the elevation of the first base‑course layer, or they may be rolled together with each course, and berms are constructed to confine fill material while a Class 4 Type A geotextile is installed as shown on the drawings and verified before any base placement. For embankments or fill areas, approved Type 1 or Type 2 granular material must be backfilled and then proof‑rolled using a 72,000‑lb triaxle dump truck inflated to 100–110 psi per tire traveling no faster than 1.5 ft/s; any permanent rutting greater than one‑half inch requires excavation of at least one foot, drying, and re‑compaction or replacement. If cement‑treated porous base courses are used, they are thoroughly sprinkled with water to achieve a moist condition prior to placement, and when reclaimed concrete or asphalt is incorporated the existing pavement is pulverized to the size and depth specified in the mix design, mixed with aggregates, RAP, stabilizing additives and water at the recommended moisture content, with the original moisture verified and adjusted so that the final mixture does not deviate more than two percentage points from optimum. For hot‑mix asphalt layers, the mixture must be fully placed, lot‑acceptance sampling performed on the uncompacted mat, and the mat allowed to cool to approximately 100 °F before rollers engage; if a thin‑wearing course is used it must retain at least 185 °F when rolling begins. All compaction equipment—including rollers, tampers, and other devices—must be submitted for review ten working days in advance with make, model, ground‑pressure data, manufacturer’s technical data, measured data and serial numbers; the Representative verifies that each roller meets minimum specifications, and any equipment failing to meet those requirements is rejected. The equipment must be on site, fully operational, calibrated, and equipped with functional water systems and scrapers before compaction can start. A compaction‑control quality‑control plan (Form CS‑206) is required to be submitted at the pre‑construction conference or at least fourteen calendar days before placement, and for full‑depth reclamation a similar plan must be provided a minimum of two weeks in advance together with a 300‑foot test strip that demonstrates shaping, density achievement and curing; written approval of this test strip by the Representative is required prior to production compaction. For ultra‑thin wearing courses, all crack sealing must be performed at least twenty‑four hours before paving, all thermoplastic or preformed markings are removed, epoxy and polyester markings are abraded to strip glass beads and roughen the surface, and the pavement is thoroughly cleaned before the UTWC emulsion and layer are placed with a self‑priming paver; rolling may only commence after these surface preparations are complete. For roller‑compacted concrete, the dry mix is laid with a conventional or high‑density asphalt paver onto the prepared subgrade and sub‑base, and compaction can begin only after the mixture has been correctly placed and the underlying structure has been verified as capable of supporting the rolling operation. The Department must also approve the density‑acceptance level for each mat; in the presence of the Representative and a certified field technician, in‑place density is measured with an approved gauge, the optimum rolling pattern is established according to PTM 402 or 403, documented on the appropriate form and submitted for approval before any compaction activity proceeds. The compaction process begins only after the subgrade or embankment foundation has been prepared and weather limits have been verified, requiring an air temperature above forty degrees Fahrenheit and no rain expected within two hours of placement [2]. The perimeter of the area to be replaced is marked, existing material is removed to the required depth, and the exposed subgrade or subbase is compacted to a uniform surface at the indicated lines and grade; any disturbance caused by removal is re‑compacted with vibratory equipment and low spots are corrected by adding graded fill and compacting until the material does not move under the equipment [2]. When geocell systems are employed, a Class 4 Type A geotextile is inspected and laid with at least a twelve‑inch overlap between sheets extending six inches beyond the cell perimeter, anchors are driven a minimum of twelve inches below the cell bottom, the cells are overfilled so that the compacted subbase rises at least one inch above the geocell, and the fill is then compacted in lifts [2]. Borrow material from a single source is placed in prescribed lift thicknesses—six inches for soil and Type 1 granular, eight inches for Type 2, twenty‑four inches for fine‑grained aggregate when compacted with tracked equipment (or twelve inches where only a plate compactor can be used)—while maintaining moisture within tolerance (soil –3 % of optimum, ±2 % for Type 1, surface damp for Type 2) and ground pressure for tracked equipment between six hundred twenty‑five and one thousand twenty‑five pounds per square foot [2]. The material is back‑dumped onto the prepared surface, spread ahead of the compaction equipment in uniform layers not exceeding eight inches in depth (four inches when granulated slag is used), and each layer is compacted from the sides toward the centre with overlapping passes until a non‑movement condition specified in Section 206.3(b) is achieved; rolling commences at the low side of each layer, initially leaving a three‑ to six‑inch strip along unsupported edges unrolled to avoid distortion [2]. The type of roller selected depends on the material being compacted—sheepsfoot, pad‑foot or pneumatic rollers operated in static mode for fine‑grained soils (maximum speed two miles per hour), smooth‑drum vibratory rollers for granular layers, steel‑wheel power rollers eight to ten tons for cement‑treated or stone‑matrix asphalt mixes, plate compactors of one hundred ten to two hundred twenty pounds where access is limited, and pneumatic‑tire rollers for scratch courses—and the equipment is operated at a speed not exceeding 1.5 ft/s (or two miles per hour for static soil rollers) [2]. The required number of passes is then executed, typically four full passes with tracked equipment, at least one full pass with steel‑wheel rollers for cement‑treated or stone‑matrix asphalt, no more than four passes for asphalt layers unless additional passes are needed to eliminate surface irregularities, and specific breakdown/intermediate rolling for reclaimed asphalt pavement using a twenty‑five‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller when the mix is less than eight inches deep or a twenty‑five‑ton vibratory pad‑foot roller when it is eight inches or deeper, followed by finish rolling with a twelve‑ to fourteen‑ton steel‑drum static roller [2]. After each pass a field‑density test is performed using a calibrated nuclear gauge or moisture‑density meter in accordance with AASHTO T 191/T 310 or PTM 402 and recorded within the prescribed time limits, and the measured density is compared to target values such as not less than ninety‑seven percent of maximum dry density for embankments, one hundred percent for foundations or material within three feet of the proposed grade, at least ninety‑five percent of laboratory‑compacted maximum density for reclaimed material, ninety‑six percent of the PTM 402 target density for base courses, and a minimum of ninety‑eight percent of the control‑strip density if the first strip fails [2]. If a lift does not meet its target, the area is reshaped, re‑moistened, loosened and recompacted; when additional passes are ineffective the material may be excavated and replaced [2]. Base courses thicker than six inches are constructed in two or more lifts each three to six inches thick, with each layer compacted to at least one hundred percent of maximum density as defined by PTM 106 Method B and verified by field‑density testing per AASHTO T 191 [2]. All compaction must be completed within prescribed time limits—four hours after water or additive mixing for chemically stabilized reclaimed material, no more than one hour (or one and a half hours with a retarding admixture) after water is added to cement‑treated mixes, and never when air temperature falls below forty degrees Fahrenheit or freezing is forecast within twenty‑four hours [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete the process starts after the dry mix is laid by a conventional or high‑density asphalt paver; the freshly placed material is then passed over with rollers to densify it, and any control joints are set before rolling, with placement possible in single or multiple lifts until the required pavement thickness is reached [1]. When an asphalt mixture has been placed, compaction is delayed only until the surface has cooled sufficiently to support a ten‑ton steel‑wheel static roller (or equivalent vibratory roller in static mode); rollers are kept moist but not wet and cleaned regularly while speed, amplitude, frequency, pattern and size are adjusted to prevent displacement, shoving, cracking or aggregate breakage [2]. After the coarse material is fully compacted, a uniform fine‑material overlay is spread to fill voids, swept, and rolled in sections ranging from one hundred fifty to one thousand feet; the surface may be sprinkled with water and re‑rolled using diagonal and cross‑rolling as required, repeating sprinkling and rolling on subsequent days until a smooth, hard monolithic base with no irregularities greater than one‑half inch is achieved [2]. Traffic is minimized or prohibited until the mix has set sufficiently and the Representative approves compliance, completing the compaction sequence [2].

After temperature and set criteria are satisfied, a steel double‑drum roller weighing at least eight tons makes two passes, and in‑place field density measurements must be performed no later than thirty minutes after rolling ends. In multi‑lift operations, each subsequent lift or adjacent lane must be placed within sixty minutes of the completion of the preceding lift to avoid cold joints. Base‑course material may not be spread on a frozen surface, when air temperature is below 40 °F, or when freezing temperatures are forecast within the next twenty‑four hours; successive layers should therefore be placed as soon as practical and kept moist until covered by the next layer. Staging and logistics

Compaction work zones are first defined by installing string lines along each side of the intended layer and positioning red flags at the front edge of the compacted material; these markers are advanced as each new section is finished, providing a visual control of elevation and depth. For large shipments exceeding 1,500 tons, a Subbase Delivery Plan must be submitted for approval before delivery, and berms are constructed to confine the stockpiled aggregate while a Class 4, Type A geotextile is placed beneath it. Aggregates are stockpiled in separate bins or on bermed pads so that segregation and degradation are avoided, and moisture content is kept sufficient to prevent drying during storage, hauling and placement. Material is transported in clean, covered haulers free of foreign substances; when spreaders cannot reach a location the trucks may back‑dump directly onto the prepared surface.

Access routes are prepared at least 1,500 feet ahead of the leading spreader, cleared of ruts, loose material and irregularities, and no construction traffic is permitted on subbases constructed of No. OGS aggregate. Haul trucks and other heavy equipment are kept out of the active installation area, and pervious pavement beds are never used for storage, thereby protecting both the work‑zone access and the underlying surface.

Equipment to be used for compaction must be submitted with make, model and ground‑pressure data; only units whose pressure falls between 625 psf and 1,025 psf receive approval after a ten‑working‑day review. Rollers are prohibited on fine‑grained aggregate (FGA) layers; instead tracked compactors or plate compactors—static weight 110–220 lb—are employed. Tracked equipment may place lifts up to 24 in thick, while plate compactors are limited to 12 in; each lift is compacted with four full passes, the pattern beginning on the shoulders or low side and progressing toward the centre, with each pass uniformly overlapping the previous one until a non‑movement condition is achieved. For pervious asphalt, compaction proceeds only after the surface has cooled enough to support a 10‑ton steel‑wheeled static roller or a vibratory roller in static mode, again limited to four passes to preserve porosity.

Traffic on freshly placed layers is restricted to essential construction equipment and local vehicles; no traffic may travel over a newly laid No. 2A haul‑road layer for more than sixty days without retesting and correction of surface irregularities. On fine‑grained aggregate zones, movement is minimized until a geotextile and at least six inches of compacted capping material are installed, and any use of the area as a haul road requires verification that the capping thickness is adequate. Should rutting exceed four inches, excavation to five inches below the rut bottom is required before re‑placement and compaction.

By coordinating delivery, moisture‑controlled stockpiling, pre‑prepared access routes, controlled traffic, and approved equipment operating in prescribed lift sizes and pass patterns, the compaction process achieves uniform density while protecting work‑zone integrity. [2]

Compaction operations require a minimum clear working lane width of four feet, because narrower sections cause equipment bridging that prevents the rollers from achieving the prescribed rolling pattern [2]. In areas where the maximum aggregate size is limited to two inches, the material must be placed in uniform horizontal layers whose compacted thickness does not exceed four inches and a hand‑operated plate compactor or comparable small equipment is used for densification [2]. Where space permits, rollers up to ten feet wide may be employed, but full‑width tracked rollers must maintain at least a six‑inch wheel‑path overlap and remain no closer than ten feet from bridge abutments, approach slabs, or lane edges (one foot when compacting roller‑compacted concrete lanes) [2]. A small clear zone of three to six inches of unsupported edge is deliberately left unrolled on the first pass to avoid distortion of the newly placed material [2]. Continuous sections prepared for spreading and rolling should be at least about one hundred fifty feet long but not exceed roughly one thousand feet, while sub‑base work demands a minimum of one thousand five hundred feet of advance preparation so that equipment can overlap uniformly from side to centre [2]. Access routes must be capable of supporting the selected roller type—whether pneumatic‑tire, sheepsfoot, pad‑foot, smooth steel‑drum vibratory, or a loaded triaxle dump truck—and pad‑foot rollers are expressly prohibited during capping placement to protect underlying geotextile [2]. When the available space is too tight for full‑width equipment, the maximum uncompacted lift is reduced to twelve inches and compaction must be performed with a plate compactor or mechanical vibrating hand tamper [2]. Construction traffic is barred from freshly placed cement‑treated permeable base course for at least three days, and haul trucks are not permitted on unfilled subgrade that will become pervious concrete; only light scarifying tools may be used in those zones [2]. The subbase must remain free of ruts, loose material, ice, or standing water deeper than six inches, and any damage to geotextile or depth deviation greater than a half‑inch requires repair before compaction can resume [2]. Compaction may not commence when the ambient air temperature falls below forty degrees Fahrenheit, nor when a freeze is forecast within the next twenty‑four hours; frozen soils or material must be removed prior to placement [2]. One guide further restricts compaction to temperatures no lower than fifty degrees Fahrenheit and requires adherence to seasonal limitations prescribed in the Standard Specifications [1][2]. Roller‑compacted concrete work must be halted if the surrounding air temperature drops to forty degrees, and the RCC mixture itself must be maintained between fifty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit, with cooling of mixing water or aggregates required if the mixture exceeds ninety degrees [2]. Asphalt mixtures require a surface temperature of at least fifty degrees and an air temperature of forty degrees before rolling can proceed rapidly, while pervious asphalt may not be placed when either the air or surface temperature is below fifty degrees [2]. Air‑temperature limits for specialized mixes range from thirty‑five degrees Fahrenheit for ATPBC to forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit for CRABC, with the additional condition that no freezing occur for twenty‑four hours before paving [2]. Reclaimed mixes must be compacted within four hours of water or additive addition, and cement‑treated bases allow only a one‑hour window (extendable to one and a half hours when a retarder is used) between water addition and completion of compaction [2]. Rainfall is prohibited during placement of these sensitive mixes, and a forecast of rain within two hours of completing reclamation also precludes rolling; only light rain may be tolerated if the surface remains undamaged [2]. An evaporation rate greater than 0.20 lb/ft²·hr triggers the use of windbreaks or fog‑mist mitigation before rolling can continue [2]. Moisture content must fall within three percent above or below the optimum value determined by the mix design, and material outside this band is dried by disking, scarifying, or similar means [1][2]. Excess water in existing layers—particularly the subgrade or base—impairs material integrity; wet zones must be identified and adequately drained before any pulverization and recompaction steps are undertaken [1]. Foundations that are in water, swampy, or saturated must be constructed with rock rather than standard fill, and standing water deeper than six inches as well as ice must be removed prior to placement of fine‑graded aggregate [2]. Storm‑water accumulation is prohibited until a capping layer thick enough to exceed the anticipated flood elevation has been placed, and material stockpiles are required to be sited away from flood‑prone areas [2]. Dust and wind control measures are mandatory when dry additives are applied so that wind does not carry material onto traffic or adjacent properties; dry additive application is avoided when wind conditions would make blowing additives objectionable [2]. Over‑compaction of pervious asphalt is limited to four roller passes in order to preserve surface porosity [2]. Because the pulverization‑stabilization method relies only on added moisture to achieve densification, strict control of moisture content is an essential environmental prerequisite for successful compaction [1].

Quality control and acceptance

Compaction inspection begins with confirming that the subgrade can attain its design density; this requires laboratory determination of both the maximum dry density and the corresponding optimum moisture content from representative soil samples using a moisture‑density test (PTM No. 106) and then comparing those values to the project specifications [1]. Moisture content in the field must be measured with calibrated nuclear or direct‑transmission gauges in accordance with ASTM C1040, AASHTO T191/T310, and shown to be at the mix‑design optimum; for most soils the moisture may not exceed three percent above or fall more than three percent below that optimum, while granular Type 1 material is limited to a two‑percent deviation [2]. Density is evaluated as both dry and wet percentages of the theoretical maximum: deep embankment foundations must achieve at least 97 % of the maximum dry density, structural foundations, subgrades and any layer within three feet of the proposed subgrade elevation must reach 100 %; reclaimed material (FDR) requires a minimum of 95 % of laboratory‑maximum density, and base‑course layers are required to meet 100 % of the maximum density per PTM No. 106 Method B [2]. For individual lifts thinner than six inches the wet‑density target is a minimum of 85 % of the maximum wet density, increasing to 90 % for lifts six inches or greater, and after final rolling every layer must exceed 96 % of the maximum wet density [2]. Field density testing is performed after each roller pass at random locations using AASHTO T310 or PTM 402; a minimum of ten control‑strip samples (or five per 500 cubic yards) are taken, and an independent gauge must agree within ±2.0 lb/ft³ for wet density and ±0.8 % moisture [2]. Surface quality is inspected visually and with a ten‑foot straightedge, and any permanent deformation greater than one‑half inch, roller marks, shoving, cracking or aggregate breakage must be corrected by adjusting roller speed, pattern, amplitude or frequency, or by reshaping, re‑moistening and recompacting the area [2]. Depth control is verified by digging test holes at least once per 3 000 sq yd (or at suspect locations) and confirming that the compacted depth lies within half an inch of the design elevation; deficiencies of a quarter‑inch or more trigger corrective action [2]. Drainage characteristics are checked to ensure the material remains free‑draining, with infiltration testing (ASTM C1701) required to average at least two hundred inches per hour [2]. Volumetric properties of the compacted mix are examined by laboratory analysis of cores taken from longitudinal joints; bulk specific gravity, air voids and Voids in Mineral Aggregate must meet the tolerances set out in the specifications, concrete base layers must exhibit a void content of approximately twenty percent ± five percent, an average hardened density within five pounds per cubic foot of the target (not exceeding 130 pcf), and aggregate coating must be at least ninety‑five percent with no more than four percent passing a No. 200 sieve [2]. Compaction equipment itself is inspected: tracked rollers must operate between 625 and 1 025 psf ground pressure, plate compactors between 110 and 220 lb static weight, power rollers must provide an unballasted load of at least 300 lb per linear inch of tread with a certified metal weight of ten tons or more, pad‑foot rollers must deliver at least 160 psi over a minimum 15 in² contact area with a three‑inch projection, vibratory rollers must achieve a roller factor of at least 2.0, and pneumatic‑tire rollers must maintain tire pressure uniform within five psi; the make, model and pressure capability of each piece are documented for verification [2]. Whenever any moisture, density, deformation, surface, depth, drainage, volumetric or equipment criterion is not satisfied, the material is reshaped, re‑moistened if necessary, recompacted and retested until full compliance is achieved [2].

The contract defines acceptance of compaction work by a combination of density, moisture, dimensional and material‑property limits that vary with the type of layer and the equipment used. For native soils and Type 1 granular base placed under Section 206.3(b)1.a‑b, the in‑place dry density must fall within the moisture envelope prescribed in PTM 402; the soil may not be more than three percent below its optimum moisture content while the granules must stay within plus or minus two percent of optimum. Measured wet density and moisture obtained with a calibrated nuclear gauge are required to agree with an independent reference gauge to within ±2.0 pcf and ±0.8 percent respectively. When compacting lifts directly behind the paver, at least 85 % of the maximum wet density is required for lifts thinner than six inches and 90 % for thicker lifts, and after all rolling passes the overall in‑place density must exceed 96 %. If an interim field density does not reach 95 %, a self‑propelled smooth dual‑drum roller weighing eight tons or more must be employed before final compaction.

Reclaimed material control strips are required to achieve a minimum of 95 % of the laboratory‑maximum dry density at optimum moisture, and the moisture content may not exceed optimum by more than three percent. Should a strip fall short, additional roller passes are applied; if the target still is not met, a new control strip is constructed with a density goal of at least 98 % of the original strip’s value. Base‑course layers must reach the full (100 %) maximum dry density defined by PTM 106 and verified by AASHTO T191; any lower reading triggers further compaction, followed if necessary by loosening, re‑moistening, reshaping and recompacting the deficient area, and ultimately removal and replacement with a mix capable of meeting the target. Embankment foundations are required to be compacted to at least 97 % of maximum dry density, whereas structure foundations, subgrades and material within three feet of the proposed subgrade elevation must attain 100 %. In‑place moisture for these layers must lie between minus three percent of optimum and the optimum value.

Compaction equipment must be pre‑approved; make, model and ground‑pressure data are submitted for verification. Completion is demonstrated by four full passes covering the entire lift with approved tracked or plate rollers operating at a ground pressure of 625–1,025 psf, while tire‑pressure variation may not exceed five pounds per square inch. Rutting deeper than four inches requires excavation to five inches below the rut, refilling with new material and recompaction; any damaged geotextile must be replaced or lap‑stitched.

For cement‑treated base courses the mix must remain free‑draining, aggregate crushing is prohibited, and surface irregularities are limited to one‑half inch. Steel‑wheel rollers weighing eight to ten tons must make at least one pass within one hour of water addition (or 1½ hours when a retarding admixture is used). Any deviation beyond the half‑inch tolerance demands loosening the surface mix, adjusting the cement‑treated aggregate and recompacting; areas still defective after final compaction are removed and replaced at no cost to the owner.

Asphalt mixes and other granular mats are evaluated by core samples. Percent‑compaction values must fall within the mat density tolerances set out in the contract and may not deviate more than ±2 % from the specified minimum or maximum densities; exceeding this band classifies the pavement as defective. Base‑course layers must achieve at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density for each 3,000‑square‑yard test area, while longitudinal joint lots have a lower specification limit of 91 %. Subbase depth must be within one‑half inch of design elevation and surface irregularities are limited to one‑half inch (three‑eighths of an inch for pervious asphalt). Pervious layers also must meet a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 in/hr. When density falls below the required minimum, the contractor first applies additional rolling; if that fails, the area is scarified to approximately three inches, moisture content is corrected and the material is recompacted. Should the revised compaction still not meet the threshold, a new target density is calculated per PTM 402 and the process repeated; persistent failure results in removal and replacement of the affected material.

Dimensional acceptance criteria require surface irregularities no greater than one‑half inch and compacted depth not short by more than one‑half inch. Test‑section thickness may be no more than a quarter inch below design, individual core lengths must stay within three‑eighths to three‑quarters of an inch with no single core deviating more than a half inch. Void structure for mixes shall be 20 % ± 5 %, average hardened density from test sections must lie within ±5 lb/ft³ and not exceed 130 lb/ft³, and infiltration rates must exceed 200 in/hr. Moisture content of the compacted material is required to remain within two percentage points of optimum, with at least ninety percent of daily test samples falling inside the composition limits.

For roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) the average density for each lot must be at least ninety‑eight percent of the laboratory maximum dry density and no individual core may fall below ninety‑six percent. The material must also achieve a 28‑day compressive strength of 3,500 psi; failure to meet either requirement classifies the lot as defective and mandates removal or replacement unless a written waiver is issued.

When any of these acceptance criteria are not met, corrective actions follow a prescribed sequence. Initial remedial work consists of additional roller passes; if density or thickness remains out of tolerance, the area is loosened, reshaped, re‑moistened and recompacted. Control strips that fail to meet their targets are rebuilt with higher density goals. Persistent non‑conformance after repeated compaction attempts leads to removal of the deficient pavement, excavation to the required depth, replacement with fresh material meeting mix design specifications, and recompaction using the approved equipment and rolling pattern. Surface irregularities exceeding allowable limits are corrected by loosening the affected zone and adding or removing aggregate base course material; depth deficiencies of a half inch or more require removal of the undersized pavement and reconstruction to bring the profile within tolerance. When volumetric or composition test results fall outside specified tables, the producer must discard the non‑conforming material, adjust the job‑mix formula, re‑sample within the prescribed tonnage limits and submit new test results; repeated failure may trigger suspension of production until a written cause analysis and approved remedial plan are provided. If density failures occur for two consecutive lots or three total lots, paving operations must be stopped until the contractor determines the cause, obtains written approval of a corrective proposal from the Representative, and demonstrates compliance before work can resume. Equipment that does not meet the minimum specifications is rejected, its use discontinued, and any work produced with it is removed and replaced with material compacted to the required quality. [2]

Safety and environmental controls

Compaction activities create a range of hazards that must be controlled for both workers and the public. The use of very heavy rollers—pneumatic‑tire units weighing up to 28 tons, sheepsfoot or pad‑foot rollers that generate high contact pressures, smooth steel‑drum vibratory rollers capable of delivering at least 50,000 lb of dynamic force, and loaded triaxle dump trucks with a gross vehicle weight of 72,000 lb—means that any person in the travel path is exposed to crushing, pinching or impact injury. To limit these risks the equipment must be operated at low speeds; compaction speed shall not exceed roughly one mile per hour (1.5 ft/s) and certainly not more than two miles per hour, and operators are required to keep a minimum clearance of ten feet from bridge abutments, approach slabs and other structures. When special rolling is performed, it must be supervised by an authorized representative and underground utilities must be protected throughout the operation.

Radiation hazards arise when nuclear density gauges are used for acceptance or verification testing. Only technicians holding a valid gauge‑operator certification may handle the equipment, and a separation of at least thirty feet from any other radioactive source must be maintained during use.

The freshly compacted base does not achieve full strength for several days. Consequently, heavy traffic—including construction vehicles—must be prohibited on the newly placed subbase for a seven‑day cure period. Appropriate traffic signs are required and traffic must be relocated using approved control devices until the curing is complete. In addition, no traffic of any kind may travel over a No. OGS aggregate subbase because even light loads could destabilize the material and endanger nearby workers or the public.

Dust and additive exposure present further occupational and community hazards. Wind‑driven fugitive dust can affect adjacent properties, so compaction equipment and techniques must be selected to minimize airborne particles. Dry additives are not to be applied when wind conditions would cause them to blow toward traffic or neighboring owners, and manual or gravity spreading of these materials is prohibited because it increases direct contact and inhalation risks for workers.

Finally, operational practices such as abrupt turning with tracked rollers can pull, fold or tension underlying geotextiles, potentially causing loss of machine control and creating additional safety concerns. Maintaining a short unrolled margin of three to six inches from any unsupported edge helps prevent distortion of the compacted surface and reduces the likelihood of equipment‑related injury. [2]

Compaction risk controls are built into the construction specifications by requiring equipment and techniques that limit fugitive dust, prohibiting dry‑additive application when wind could blow material onto traffic or neighboring properties, and imposing strict temperature limits—no placement if air temperature is expected to fall below 40 °F during the seven‑day cure period (or below 35 °F for cement slurry with accelerators) and no work in rain or when rain is forecast within two hours. Curing, protection, and handoff

After each lift is placed and compacted, a final smooth‑drum pass creates a continuous positive‑drainage skin that limits water infiltration and eliminates low spots. From that moment the newly densified material is protected or cured according to its type. For roller‑compacted concrete the surface is kept continuously moist for at least seventy‑two hours by means such as misting spray nozzles, soaking hoses, sprinklers or a similar system, and the moisture regime continues until the slab reaches a compressive strength of not less than three thousand pounds per square inch. The same result may be obtained by applying a white polyethylene sheet or a membrane‑forming curing compound at roughly one gallon per one hundred square feet. Field cure can be terminated when three‑day test cylinders show a strength of three thousand psi, otherwise it is continued to the seven‑day mark with the same benchmark and finally to twenty‑eight days where a minimum of three‑thousand‑five hundred psi is required. Traffic is prohibited until the mix has set, which must occur within one hour of placement and never exceed one hour and twenty minutes; before traffic is allowed a pneumatic‑tire roller operating at forty to sixty pounds per square inch compacts the surface and a Representative’s authorization is obtained. Any core holes created for verification are backfilled with RCC material or rapid‑set concrete patching to preserve surface integrity.

For reclaimed dense‑rock (FDR) layers the material is maintained in a damp condition by daily water application or by applying an emulsified asphalt primer followed by a fog seal at a residual rate of 0.02 to 0.10 gallons per square yard, while ensuring that moisture does not exceed three percent above optimum. Heavy traffic is prohibited for the seven‑day cure period, and placement is avoided when ambient temperature is expected to fall below forty degrees Fahrenheit during that period or when rain is forecast within two hours of completion. Cement slurries containing accelerating admixtures may be used only with written approval and are not permitted when temperatures are projected to drop below thirty‑five degrees Fahrenheit in the first twenty‑four hours.

Fine‑grained aggregate surfaces receive special attention: traffic on exposed, compacted material is minimized; any rut deeper than four inches is excavated to five inches below the rut, a Class 4 Type A geotextile is placed, and at least six inches of capping material are laid within two weeks, all compacted without pad‑foot rollers. Base courses and cold‑in‑place treated bases are kept moist until the next layer is placed to secure bonding, shielded from traffic except for essential local vehicles or construction equipment, and special rolling equipment is required to stay at least ten feet away from bridge abutments, approach slabs and similar structures while existing underground utilities are protected during undercut operations. Cold‑in‑place treated base is cured immediately with a membrane‑forming compound or polyethylene sheeting for twenty‑four hours, extended an additional day when temperatures fall below forty degrees Fahrenheit; construction traffic is barred for a minimum of three days, after which only equipment needed for the subsequent layer may enter.

Asphalt‑treated base courses are allowed to cool to roughly one hundred degrees Fahrenheit before any subsequent layer is installed; rolling commences only after this cooling period and is limited to four passes unless additional passes are required solely to eliminate surface irregularities. Steel‑wheel rollers are kept moist and clean without excess water to prevent binder flushing, while zones inaccessible to rollers are compacted with vibrating hand tampers. The mix is deliberately restrained so that it remains free‑draining and the aggregate is not crushed, thereby safeguarding the material during its curing phase. Emulsified asphalt‑stabilized or foamed‑asphalt‑stabilized subbases require at least two weeks of curing or until average moisture falls below two percent before a wearing or binder course is placed. Any cores taken for density verification are backfilled within twenty‑four hours with the same hot‑mix material, compacted and sealed to restore mat integrity.

Subbases constructed of No. OGS aggregate are protected from all traffic, including construction traffic, until they are covered. Before winter shutdowns they must be protected by placing an asphalt base for flexible pavements or reinforced/plain cement concrete for rigid pavements; if such a protective cover is absent, a proof roll is performed and any unstable zones are repaired prior to paving.

Longitudinal joints of surface courses receive a continuous band of hot PG 64S‑22 binder sealant applied at temperatures between two hundred sixty‑five and three hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit on clean, dry joint faces; this preserves density and prevents moisture or traffic damage while the pavement cures. Concrete slabs begin curing within twenty minutes of discharge and are then tightly covered with polyethylene sheeting that remains in place for a minimum of seven days before any traffic loading.

When subbase material is placed in expanded geocells, each cell is overfilled so that the compacted fill extends at least one inch above the cell top, protecting the cells from construction traffic and heat; equipment may not operate on unfilled sections and the drop height of the subbase is limited to three feet to prevent damage or displacement of the cell walls. In every case traffic should be minimized until protective measures are in place, any damage observed during proof‑rolling must be repaired, and traffic control measures such as pilot cars may be required when raveling or other damage is likely [2].

Effective compaction of pavement materials is sustained only when a disciplined program of preventive maintenance, continuous monitoring and prompt repair is applied. All rollers, dozers and excavators must be kept in reliable operating condition before and during use; serial numbers and manufacturer data are supplied to the Representative, tire pressures are checked so that variations never exceed five pounds per square inch, and ground‑pressure values for tracked equipment are maintained between 625 and 1,025 lb/ft². Rolling drums and cutting drums are kept clean and moist without excess water, and pneumatic‑tire rollers are similarly cleaned to prevent material adhesion. Equipment is calibrated in accordance with the schedule in Table B – nuclear density gauges are calibrated annually and inspected daily, and any gauge that fails the independent verification limits of ±2.0 pcf wet density and ±0.8 w moisture is repaired or replaced before work proceeds.

Monitoring begins as soon as material is placed. Proof‑rolling is used to locate permanent deformation greater than one‑half inch; in‑place density tests are performed no later than thirty minutes after each roller pass using calibrated nuclear or electrical‑impedance gauges and following ASTM C1040, AASHTO T310 or the applicable PTM method. The required compaction varies with lift thickness: lifts under six inches must reach at least 85 % of maximum wet density, lifts six inches or greater must achieve 90 %, and after final rolling every layer must be no less than 96 % (with a final average of at least 98 %). Embankment foundations are required to meet not less than 97 % of maximum dry density, while structure foundations, subgrades and any material within three feet of the proposed subgrade elevation must reach 100 %. Reclaimed‑material layers must attain at least 95 % of laboratory maximum dry density at optimum moisture; a control strip that falls short is re‑compacted or a new strip is constructed to achieve at least 98 % of the original strip density. Moisture content is kept within ±2 % of the optimum for Type 1 granular material and never more than three percent above optimum; any material found above optimum moisture is dried by disking, scarifying or other means before further compaction.

Surface tolerances are checked with straightedges and templates at intervals not exceeding 25 feet (or every five feet when using a twelve‑foot straightedge). Any deviation greater than one‑half inch triggers immediate correction: high spots are bladed off, or the surface is loosened, material added or removed, and the area re‑rolled to meet density criteria. For pervious pavements, irregularities exceeding three‑eighths of an inch are corrected in the same manner, and infiltration performance is verified at three locations per 10 000 sq ft with a minimum average rate of 200 in/hr.

When monitoring reveals deficiencies, corrective actions are taken without delay. Rutting greater than one‑half inch is excavated, dried, reshaped and re‑compacted; if the rut exceeds four inches, material is removed to at least five inches below the rut bottom, new fill is placed and compacted with four plate‑compactor passes. If a density test falls short after additional roller passes, the layer is loosened, re‑moistened, reshaped and recompacted; when density cannot be achieved by further compaction, the entire layer is removed and replaced. Surface irregularities exceeding one‑half inch are addressed by loosening the top layer, adjusting material and re‑compacting to the required density. Subgrade deflection greater than one‑half inch (or more than one inch in an embankment) is treated by excavating a one‑foot to three‑foot undercut, installing Class 4 geotextile, backfilling with approved aggregate or borrow material and compacting each layer to the same density criteria before restoring the subbase. Subbase depth deficiencies of half an inch or more are remedied by scarifying to three inches, blending in additional material and recompacting; if instability is widespread, the subbase is removed, the underlying subgrade repaired, and a new subbase placed.

Traffic on freshly placed or compacted layers is strictly limited. Construction traffic is prohibited on cement‑treated bases for at least three days while the surface cures under a white‑membrane compound, and no traffic, including construction vehicles, is allowed on No. OGS aggregate subbases. When a No. 2A aggregate subbase serves as a haul road or remains exposed for more than sixty calendar days, it must be retested for surface irregularities and depth before work proceeds.

All repairs are documented, verified with independent testing and recorded in the appropriate forms. Equipment condition, tire pressures, gauge calibrations and moisture adjustments are continuously checked throughout the project to ensure that compaction remains stable, non‑moving and free of rutting, displacement or shear defects.
