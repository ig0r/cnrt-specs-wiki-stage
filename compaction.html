Compaction

Part of: Pavement Structure is-subtopic-of
Compaction is a topic under Pavement Structure.
compaction
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-29

Compaction is the densification process applied to newly placed pavement layers—soil, granular base, reclaimed asphalt, roller‑compacted concrete, stone‑matrix or pervious mixes, and joint zones—to achieve prescribed density thresholds that prevent the suite of instability problems described in the specifications. By working within narrow moisture windows (typically no more than three percent below optimum for soils and ±2 % for Type 1 granules) and using rollers or other equipment that can develop the required ground pressure, each lift is compacted to at least 97 % of maximum dry density (100 % in the upper three feet) or the equivalent wet‑density percentages for asphaltic lifts, thereby eliminating permanent rutting, shoving, cracking, aggregate breakage and loss of load‑bearing capacity. The method also mandates controlled lift thicknesses, immediate rolling after placement, a limited travel speed (≈1.5 ft/s), and a minimum number of overlapping passes so that density gains fall below one percent over three consecutive passes before the layer is accepted as stable.[1][2]

PennDOT publications covering “Compaction” also include the following subtopics: Maximum Dry Density.

The Maximum Dry Density serves as the laboratory-established benchmark against which field density measurements are compared to verify that each lift meets the prescribed stability thresholds. [1][2] The reference value dictates the acceptance criteria for subgrade and fill materials, ensuring that in-place compaction achieves at least 97% of this maximum dry unit weight within the specified moisture window. [2]

Maximum Dry Density

Purpose and applicability

Compaction eliminates the instability that arises when any pavement layer – whether soil, granular base, reclaimed asphalt, roller‑compacted concrete, stone‑matrix asphalt, pervious asphalt, ATPBC mat or joint zone – is placed at an insufficient density or with moisture outside a narrow optimum band. A loosely compacted layer permits movement and deformation: permanent rutting greater than one‑half inch, shear‑wave displacement, shoving, cracking, aggregate breakage, loss of load‑bearing capacity, water infiltration and surface blemishes such as roller marks, undulations, material pickup, creases and joint discontinuities. In pervious mixes over‑compaction can close the void structure and destroy free‑draining porosity; in subbase work a depth shortfall of half an inch or more renders the layer defective, while irregularities exceeding three‑eighths of an inch at paving joints create weak zones. These deficiencies undermine structural integrity, reduce service life and make the pavement unsafe for traffic. The method therefore targets specific density thresholds – at least 97 % of maximum dry density for embankments (100 % within the top three feet), full 100 % for base‑course layers, wet‑density minima of 85 % for lifts thinner than six inches and 90 % for thicker lifts with a final compaction exceeding 96 % for all thicknesses, reclaimed materials at a minimum of 95 % of laboratory‑determined maximum, rigid concrete slabs averaging no less than 98 % of maximum wet density and none below 96 %, and longitudinal joints not less than 91 % – because failure to achieve these values is directly linked to the defects described above. By densifying each layer to its prescribed target, compaction prevents the suite of problems that would otherwise develop in an under‑dense or unevenly compacted pavement structure. [2]

Compaction is the preferred densification method whenever a newly placed pavement layer can be worked under temperature and moisture conditions that allow it to reach the densities required by the specifications, provided rollers or other compaction equipment can physically access the surface. For soils the moisture content must not be more than three percent below optimum; for Type 1 granular material the acceptable range is plus or minus two percent of optimum, and Type 2 granules are only suitable when they are surface‑damp. The material must be placed in uniform full‑width lifts that do not exceed the lift‑thickness limits dictated by the equipment: up to twenty‑four inches for tracked excavators or dozers capable of ground pressures between 625 and 1 025 lb/ft², and up to twelve inches when only a plate compactor (static weight 110–220 lb) is used. When these moisture and lift conditions are satisfied the required densities can be achieved: embankment or fill foundations deeper than three feet below the proposed subgrade elevation must reach at least 97 % of maximum dry density, while structure footings, subgrades and any material within three feet of final grade must be compacted to 100 % of maximum dry density. Base‑course layers are required to attain a minimum of 100 % of the maximum density determined by PTM No. 106 Method B (practically at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density for conventional bases). Roller‑compacted concrete and cement‑treated porous base course lifts thinner than six inches must be compacted to a minimum of 85 % of maximum wet density, lifts six inches or thicker to at least 90 %, and after rolling the overall target must exceed 96 %. For reclaimed asphalt (FDR) layers less than eight inches thick a 25‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller is used for breakdown and intermediate rolling, while layers eight inches or deeper require a 25‑ton vibratory padfoot roller for initial breakdown; finish rolling is performed with a 12‑ to 14‑ton steel‑drum static roller. Compaction using rollers is typically completed after four full passes, provided the equipment can develop the necessary pressure and the material does not exhibit permanent deformation greater than one‑half inch. When the reclaimed in‑place material can achieve the required strength simply by re‑compacting it with moisture, pulverization stabilization—using only the existing material and water—is appropriate for improving support layers rather than addressing surface distress. If the reclaimed material’s inherent strength potential is insufficient, if additional aggregate or volume is needed for structural reasons, or if the primary problems are surface‑related distresses, a different method such as mechanical stabilization (adding aggregate) or chemical stabilization (incorporating cement, lime, or fly ash) should be employed instead of pure compaction. Compaction must be abandoned whenever the prescribed equipment cannot develop the required pressure, when lift thickness exceeds the allowable limits, when full‑scale rollers would damage adjacent structures or utilities (e.g., pipe trenches, near guide rails, or in confined zones), when the foundation material is saturated, swampy, frozen or otherwise unstable, or when fine‑graded aggregate precludes roller use. In those situations the contract may allow reduction of lift thickness, substitution of hand‑operated or small dynamic compactors, placement of rock fill instead of compacted soil or granular material, or adoption of a non‑movement acceptance criterion for thin scratch or leveling courses less than one inch thick, for courses not exceeding 110 psf, for paving strips narrower than four feet, or for small placements up to five hundred tons on unstable bases where density is not critical. Acceptable alternatives when rollers cannot access the area include walk‑behind vibratory rollers, plate tampers, roller screeds, vibrating screeds, manual shovelling, hand‑brooming of fines, drilling and blasting for rock that cannot be excavated conventionally, or core testing to verify bulk specific gravity and density instead of relying on compaction acceptance. If after the normal number of passes the required density is still not achieved, additional rolling is applied; should this fail, the area must be thoroughly loosened, remoistened, reshaped and re‑compacted, or the mixture replaced. Required inputs

Compaction in pavement engineering must be matched to the material being densified, its mixture characteristics, and the prevailing site conditions. The range of acceptable fill and paving materials includes native soil, rock, shale, random fill, Type 1 and Type 2 granular materials, fine‑graded aggregate, reclaimed base courses, cement‑treated bases, cold‑recycled base mixes, pervious asphalt mixtures, stone matrix asphalt, hot‑mix paving material, binder and wearing courses for patches, subbase aggregates such as No. 2A and No. OGS, full‑depth reclamation (FDR) material that is pulverized to a maximum particle size of two inches, reclaimed asphalt pavement or millings, calcium chloride, emulsified asphalt Class E‑1, and other approved binders [2]. Roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) is treated as a very dry concrete mix composed of cement, aggregate and water with a low water‑to‑cement ratio—often as low as 0.30 and typically around 0.40—and a dense gradation whose maximum aggregate size does not exceed one inch; the mixture has virtually no slump, may contain limited air‑entraining agents or other admixtures, and is placed by a conventional or high‑density asphalt paver before densification with rollers [1]. Full‑depth reclamation compacts the reclaimed in‑place pavement itself, requiring adequate moisture to enable densification and allowing gradation improvement by adding aggregate or RAP; when higher strength is needed chemical stabilizers such as Portland cement, lime or fly ash may be incorporated, but the mix design must balance density and strength to avoid brittleness [1]. Native subgrade soils—whether coarse‑grained or fine‑grained sourced from representative cut, fill, or at‑grade locations—are governed by gradation quality (well‑graded versus poorly graded), fines content expressed as the percent passing a #200 sieve, and Atterberg limits (liquid limit, plastic limit and resulting plasticity index) which together define the AASHTO group index; maximum dry density and optimum moisture content are determined through laboratory compaction testing [1]. Moisture content is a primary mixture property: soils must be compacted at a moisture level between three percent below optimum and the optimum itself; Type 1 granular material may vary by plus or minus two percent of its optimum, while all reclaimed, cement‑treated, and fine‑graded mixes may not exceed the optimum by more than three percent. For dense‑graded asphalt mixtures the apparent moisture must not exceed 0.5 percent, and for FDR blends the water added must achieve the design optimum without deviating more than two percentage points [2]. Temperature limits are equally critical: RCC must be placed when ambient temperature is between fifty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit; hot‑mix asphalt must be compacted while its temperature remains above one hundred eighty‑five degrees; pervious asphalt mixtures are produced below three hundred twenty degrees to obtain adequate film thickness, with placement air or surface temperature not falling below fifty degrees; ATPBC placement requires at least thirty‑five degrees; cold‑recycled bases require a minimum of forty‑five degrees with a rising trend; and full‑depth reclamation forecasts must stay above forty degrees for the entire seven‑day cure period [2]. Lift thicknesses are tightly controlled: uncompacted soil lifts may not exceed six inches, Type 1 granular lifts eight inches, fine‑graded aggregate placed with tracked equipment up to twenty‑four inches, base courses when vibratory rollers are used four to ten inches, and reclaimed mixes two to five inches; FDR material is limited to lifts of two to five inches [2]. Density targets are expressed as percentages of laboratory maximum densities: soils and granular fills deeper than three feet must achieve at least ninety‑seven percent of their maximum dry density, increasing to one hundred percent within the top three feet; structure foundations, subgrades and all base‑course layers also require full density; reclaimed base courses are acceptable at a minimum of ninety‑five percent of laboratory maximum dry density; RCC lifts thinner than six inches are initially compacted to at least eighty‑five percent of the maximum wet density, thicker lifts to ninety percent, with final rolling exceeding ninety‑six percent [2]. Cold‑recycled bases must reach at least ninety‑six percent of the PTM 402 target density, pervious asphalt layers must retain at least ninety‑five percent coating of aggregate particles with no more than four percent passing a No. 200 sieve, and dense‑graded mixes must meet specified air‑void and VMA limits while staying within volumetric tolerances [2]. Site conditions governing successful compaction require a dry, unfrozen substrate free of standing water deeper than six inches, frost or ice, and any soft or muddy zones; subgrade and subbase surfaces must be cleaned, uniformly moist, and prepared at least one‑thousand five hundred feet ahead of spreading equipment, without loose debris, ruts, or irregularities greater than a half‑inch [2]. For pervious pavements the underlying subgrade must be scarified at least six inches, saturated without ponding, permeable with an infiltration capacity of two hundred inches per hour, and protected from rain during placement [2]. Ambient temperature thresholds described above apply, precipitation is prohibited until sufficient cover exists, equipment operation speeds are limited to one point five feet per second for proof rolling and no more than two miles per hour when a smooth‑drum vibratory roller works on granular material, tire pressures for loaded triaxle trucks must be maintained between one hundred and one hundred ten pounds per square inch, and steel‑wheel rollers used for asphalt layers must weigh eight to fourteen tons depending on the application [2]. Proof rolling must show no permanent rutting greater than one‑half inch, and compaction passes are limited to four for pervious mixes and fine‑graded aggregates to preserve porosity, while a minimum of two static passes is required for hot‑mix asphalt; all passes must increase density by at least one percent before work may cease [2].

Pre‑construction compaction control is anchored in laboratory moisture‑density testing performed according to PTM No. 106, which establishes the maximum dry density and optimum moisture content that must be achieved by all compacted soils, aggregates, and base layers [1][2]. In addition, a California Bearing Ratio test on soaked specimens, conducted per AASHTO T 193, is required for any material whose CBR value exceeds ten; such results must be reviewed and approved by the District Geotechnical Engineer and the Chief Geotechnical Engineer to confirm that realistic resilient‑modulus values are used in design [1]. The design specifications prescribe allowable lift thicknesses for each material type: soils and Type 1 granular fill are placed in uniform horizontal lifts not exceeding six inches, while Type 2 granular may be compacted in lifts up to eight inches, reduced to four inches where clearance is limited; base‑course layers must be between three and six inches thick, fine‑grained aggregate laid with a plate compactor may be placed in lifts as great as twelve inches, and tracked equipment may accept lifts up to twenty‑four inches provided ground pressure remains between 625 psf and 1,025 psf [2]. When a base course exceeds eight inches it must be constructed in two or more equal lifts, each lift no less than four inches and no greater than eight inches, and any single layer of subbase No. 2A or OGS material may not exceed eight inches (four inches when granulated slag is used) [2]. Density requirements are tied to material type: compacted soils must reach at least ninety‑seven percent of the laboratory maximum dry density everywhere except the upper three feet, which must achieve one hundred percent; reclaimed base and other granular layers must be compacted to a minimum of ninety‑five percent of the maximum dry density at the mix‑design optimum moisture content; structural base courses are required to meet the full (one hundred percent) maximum dry density as specified in PTM No. 106 Method B, while pavement lifts less than six inches thick must be compacted by the paver to a minimum of eighty‑five percent of the maximum wet density before any roller is used and lifts six inches or greater require at least ninety percent, with the final surface exceeding ninety‑six percent of the maximum wet density [2]. Moisture content during compaction is limited to narrow bands around the optimum value: soils may not be more than three percent below optimum; other granular layers must be kept between three percent below and the optimum, never exceeding the optimum by more than three percent; Type 1 granular material permits a plus‑or‑minus two percent window about optimum, while Type 2 may be surface‑damp [2][1]. Compaction equipment operation is constrained to a travel speed of one and a half feet per second (approximately one mile per hour) with each pass maintaining contact with the material for at least six seconds; rollers must make a minimum of four full passes, pneumatic‑tire rollers are limited to loads of twenty‑six to twenty‑eight tons and widths not exceeding ten feet, sheepsfoot or pad rollers must develop at least one hundred sixty pounds per square inch contact pressure over a pad area of fifteen square inches or more, three‑wheel power rollers must provide an unballasted load of at least three hundred pounds per linear inch of rear tread and possess a certified metal weight of ten tons, vibratory rollers must achieve a roller factor of two or greater using prescribed frequency, amplitude and drum width, and steel‑wheel power rollers used for cement‑treated bases are required to weigh eight to ten tons, be kept moist but not wet, and may operate in static mode with optional vibration when approved [2]. Pre‑construction testing includes determination of optimum moisture content and maximum dry density by PTM No. 106 before placement, proof‑rolling of the embankment foundation with a loaded 72‑000‑pound triaxle dump truck inflated to one hundred to one hundred ten psi per tire at the prescribed speed limit, and any permanent rut deeper than half an inch mandating excavation, drying and re‑compaction [2]. A Compaction Control QC Plan must be submitted at least fourteen calendar days before work or at the pre‑construction conference, specifying nuclear‑gauge moisture‑density testing; gauges are calibrated annually per AASHTO T 310 with daily standardization records, and independent gauge results must fall within plus or minus two pounds per cubic foot of wet density and plus or minus 0.8 % moisture of the contractor’s readings [2]. Field density verification follows PTM No. 402 using direct‑transmission or backscatter nuclear gauges, with at least ten random tests required for each three‑thousand square‑yard lot of reclaimed base, while ASTM C1040 testing must be completed within thirty minutes of rolling for pavement lifts, taking a minimum of five measurements behind the paver and five after final compaction for every five hundred cubic yards placed [2]. All rollers and compactors must be approved by submitting manufacturer data, measured performance data and serial numbers to the Representative; any deviation from prescribed specifications requires a written request and LTS approval [2]. Special layer controls require cement‑treated base courses to be rolled within one hour of water addition (extendable to one and a half hours with a retarding admixture) and to receive at least four roller passes, with surface irregularities after each pass limited to three‑eighths of an inch for general bases or one‑half inch for cement‑treated bases; subbase No. 2A layers must achieve a minimum compacted depth of three inches and OGS layers a minimum of four inches, with depth verification performed by drilling a six‑inch diameter test hole for each three‑thousand square‑yard area and any shortfall of one‑half inch or more deemed defective and requiring correction [2]. For pervious asphalt, a test section of at least 225 ft² must demonstrate an average infiltration rate exceeding two hundred inches per hour, and the compacted thickness may not be more than a quarter inch thinner than the design thickness [2].

Equipment and personnel

Preparation for compaction begins with establishing a sound subgrade and removing any existing pavement that must be discarded. A self‑propelled traveling rotary reclaimer or a milling machine equipped with a down‑cutting drum is used to cut and pulverize the old surface, while light equipment such as a spring‑tooth rake attached to a light tractor scarifies the underlying soil to at least six inches depth. In confined zones the subgrade is proof‑rolled using a loaded triaxle dump truck of 72,000 lb gross vehicle weight traveling no faster than 1.5 ft/s with tire pressures maintained between 100 and 110 psi; this step verifies that moisture content is within three percent of optimum before any new material is placed. For the base course a cross‑section template is positioned and string lines are installed on ample supports along each side of the alignment to control elevation and depth.

Placement of the mix follows acceptance of the processing plant and involves delivering the material in covered haul trucks, ready‑mix concrete trucks, distributor or tanker trucks equipped with recirculating pumps or agitation systems, or a material transfer vehicle for SMA or RPS pavements. The material is spread by an approved asphalt paver or mechanical spreader that includes an adjustable screed, plate vibrator and automated sensors to set profile and grade; slip‑form pavers, track pavers and form‑riding paving machines are used where specified. Where the spreader box cannot reach, hand‑held squeegees or lutes are employed, and water may be sprayed into the spreader box to keep the mix workable without harming it.

Compaction or consolidation commences immediately after placement while the mix remains within temperature limits. Soils are densified with a static‑mode padfoot roller, optionally preceded by a single pass of a smooth drum roller. Granular Type 1 and Type 2 materials and rock are compacted with a smooth‑drum vibratory roller; primary consolidation of reinforced cement concrete or other layers is achieved with a self‑propelled smooth dual steel‑drum roller having a minimum weight of eight tons, operating in static or vibratory mode. Steel‑wheel power rollers ranging from eight to ten tons are used on cement‑treated bases and permeable bases, while ten‑ton or heavier rollers compact 6.3 mm wearing courses. For deeper reclaimed lifts a twenty‑five‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller provides breakdown rolling, and a twenty‑five‑ton vibratory padfoot roller may be employed for initial breakdown on thicker layers. Final compaction of scratch courses and the last pass before traffic is permitted uses a pneumatic‑tire roller set to 40–60 psi tire pressure. Where rollers cannot access an area, mechanical vibrating hand tampers or hand‑operated vibrating tampers are used.

Finishing operations employ the same cross‑section template and string lines together with squeegees, lutes and a 10‑foot or 12‑foot straightedge to verify longitudinal irregularities and correct any deviations greater than one‑half inch before curing. For pervious concrete a form‑riding paving machine, roller screed, vibrating screed or asphalt paver provides the final surface finish while preserving voids.

Curing does not require heavy equipment beyond moisture control. Water trucks equipped with fog nozzles apply a fine mist to keep the surface damp for the minimum 72‑hour cure of reinforced cement concrete, and continuous water sprays, emulsified asphalt primer, fog seal or tack coat are used for reclaimed sections. A white membrane forming curing compound may be applied at approximately one gallon per hundred square feet (±10 %), or the surface may be covered with white polyethylene sheeting for 24 hours, extending the period if temperatures fall below forty degrees Fahrenheit. Permeable bases are allowed to cool to about 100 °F before subsequent layers, ultra‑thin wearing courses must be rolled before the temperature drops below 185 °F, and pervious concrete slabs are covered with polyethylene for at least seven days.

Testing verifies that compaction objectives have been met. Calibrated nuclear density/moisture gauges operated in direct‑transmission mode according to PTM No. 402/AASHTO T310 determine in‑place wet density and moisture, with independent gauge readings required to be within ±2.0 lb/ft³ for wet density and ±0.8 % for moisture. Field‑density tests follow AASHTO T191, and cores of six inches diameter are drilled promptly for laboratory density and asphalt‑content analysis. When required, a Superpave Gyratory Compactor confirms strength characteristics. Additional checks include infiltration measurements on pervious concrete (ASTM C1701), unit‑weight testing of trucks (ASTM C1688), and portable on‑site laboratories performing PTM 616 and PTM 100 aggregate acceptance tests. Work sequence

Before any compaction can be initiated, the entire pavement structure beneath the layer to be compacted must be fully prepared, verified and approved [1][2]. The subgrade and any sub‑base courses that were sized during the design phase are placed first and must be capable of supporting the loads imposed by rolling operations [1][2]. All standing water deeper than six inches and any ice must be removed, and the subgrade must be corrected for deficiencies as specified before sub‑base placement; the prepared subgrade/sub‑base is then maintained at least 1,500 feet ahead of the leading spreader, free of ruts, irregularities and loose material, and kept in a well‑moistened condition without standing water [2]. When a geotextile is required, a Class 4 Type A geotextile is installed as shown on the drawings before any base or subbase material is placed [2]. The aggregate or asphalt material to be placed must come from a single borrow source, with samples taken at least two weeks in advance and tested under Representative supervision; placement may proceed only after satisfactory acceptance test results are documented [2]. For asphalt mixes the vendor bill of lading must verify testing to Bulletin 25, confirm that the shipment container was free of contamination, provide compatibility or stone‑coating test results for emulsified grades, and include the required temperature‑viscosity data [2]. The optimum moisture content and maximum dry density for soils and aggregates are established in accordance with PTM No. 106 (Method B), and the material is conditioned within the prescribed limits—soil no more than three percent below optimum, Type 1 within plus or minus two percent, Type 2 kept surface‑damp, and moisture variation not exceeding two percentage points from target [2]. All paving and compaction equipment must be calibrated, and calibration is verified by constructing a 300‑foot test strip that demonstrates the full field density reduction process before full‑production work begins; the make, model, ground‑pressure data and serial numbers for trench and pad‑foot rollers are submitted to the Representative for review and acceptance at least ten working days prior [2]. A compaction control quality‑control plan (Form CS‑206) must be submitted at the pre‑construction conference or no later than fourteen calendar days before placement, and a field density reduction QC plan is required at least two weeks in advance; the Department must approve the density‑acceptance level for the mix and the Job Mix Formula within Table A limits after testing to AASHTO T‑27 [2]. When roller‑compacted concrete is used, the dry RCC mix is placed with a conventional or high‑density asphalt paver onto the prepared base before any rolling can occur [1]. For ultra‑thin wearing courses, all required crack sealing must be completed at least twenty‑four hours before paving, thermoplastic and preformed markings are removed, epoxy and polyester markings are abraded to strip glass beads and roughen the surface, and the pavement is thoroughly cleaned; only after these actions and the placement of the UTWC emulsion and layer with a self‑priming paver may compaction be applied [1]. Preliminary in‑field density measurements using an approved nuclear or electrical impedance gauge are taken with the Representative present to confirm that the material meets the required density criteria before any rollers are employed [2]. Once all of these preparatory actions—subgrade and sub‑base verification, moisture conditioning, removal of water and ice, geotextile installation, material acceptance documentation, equipment calibration and test‑strip demonstration, QC plan approval, preliminary density checks, and any special surface preparations for RCC or UTWC—are completed, compaction may commence [1][2].

Staging and logistics

Compaction work zones are first defined by preparing a sound subgrade and installing any required geotextile, after which string lines are set to control elevation and red flags are placed at the front edge of each compacted layer. Berms are constructed around the subbase to keep material confined, and when large quantities—over 1,500 tons—are anticipated a Sub‑base Delivery Plan must be submitted for departmental review. Material is delivered just‑in‑time; trucks dump only as far ahead as can be spread and compacted within the day’s progress, eliminating the need for stockpiling except where aggregates are temporarily stored in a manner that prevents segregation or degradation. All haulage uses clean, covered vehicles to retain moisture, and the mix is kept sufficiently moist from stockpile through placement to avoid segregation during compaction.

Access routes are protected by maintaining at least 1,500 ft of finished subbase ahead of the spreader, keeping those stretches free of ruts, loose material, or irregularities. Traffic on fresh granular aggregate (FGA) is limited until a geotextile and a minimum six‑inch compacted capping layer are in place; any use of the FGA area as a haul road requires an engineering evaluation of the capping thickness and departmental approval. In general only essential construction traffic—trucks delivering the next layer and rollers required for compaction—is permitted on the freshly placed mat, with a pilot car employed to control vehicle speeds when damage such as raveling is likely.

Equipment movement follows prescribed patterns that protect both the material and the subgrade. Tracked excavators or dozers are allowed only if their ground pressure falls between 625 psf and 1,025 psf, while plate compactors weighing between 110 lb and 220 lb may also be used. Rollers—steel‑wheel units of eight to ten tons or static‑mode vibratory rollers—begin at the low side or shoulders of a layer, leaving an initial unrolled strip of three to six inches along unsupported edges to prevent distortion, then advance toward the center with each pass overlapping the previous one. Four full passes covering 100 % of the surface are required for acceptance; any depth deficiency of half an inch or more triggers repair before the layer is approved. Rutting deeper than four inches caused by construction traffic must be excavated five inches below the rut, refilled, and re‑compacted with the same four‑pass regimen. [2]

Compaction can only be undertaken when ambient conditions satisfy prescribed temperature and moisture limits; the guides require that air temperature not fall below fifty degrees Fahrenheit for general operations, while additional lower thresholds of forty degrees are imposed for roller‑compacted concrete and thirty‑five degrees for pervious asphalt mixtures, reflecting material‑specific sensitivities [1][2]. Seasonal restrictions set forth in standard specifications further bound when work may proceed, and any forecast of freezing temperatures within the next twenty‑four hours precludes compaction activities [1][2]. Moisture control is equally critical: the target moisture content must lie no more than three percent below optimum and must not exceed optimum by more than three percent, with Type 1 granular material allowed a tighter tolerance of plus or minus two percent; excess water in the subgrade or base layers degrades integrity and therefore wet zones must be identified and effectively drained before any pulverization‑stabilization steps are performed [1][2]. Standing water deeper than six inches and all ice must be removed, and frozen soils are excluded from placement or compaction, necessitating thorough drying of the material in saturated areas to meet the moisture tolerances [2]. When evaporation rates exceed 0.20 lb/ft²·h, mitigation measures such as windbreaks or fog misting are required to prevent premature drying or surface hardening [2]. Space constraints dictate that rollers used for breakdown, intermediate and finish passes require a clear lane of at least eight feet in width, with the pneumatic‑tire roller itself limited to ten feet overall; in pavement strips narrower than four feet the equipment will bridge the surface and density must be accepted on a non‑movement basis [2]. In confined zones—such as pipe trenches, within one foot of manholes, utility inlets, bridge abutments or other fixed obstructions—full‑scale rollers are prohibited; instead a three‑to‑six inch buffer is left unrolled next to any unsupported edge and hand‑operated tampers or plate compactors weighing between 110 and 220 pounds are employed [2]. Where tracked excavators or dozers can access the area, fine‑grained aggregate lifts may be placed up to twenty‑four inches thick; where only a plate compactor is feasible the lift must not exceed twelve inches and ground pressure must remain between 625 and 1,025 psf, with all compacted layers limited to two to five inches in depth and four‑inch layers required in confined areas [2]. A continuous control strip of roughly three hundred feet is maintained on site so roller patterns can be established and density verified, and a subbase or subgrade extending at least one‑thousand‑five hundred linear feet ahead of the leading spreader must be free of ruts, loose material, foreign debris, ponded water or frost before compaction begins [2]. Work is normally confined to daylight hours unless an exception is granted, proof‑rolling speeds are limited to one mile per hour and special rolling may not exceed two miles per hour to ensure safety and quality control [2]. Weather imposes hard stops: material may not be placed in rain, nor if precipitation is anticipated within two hours of finishing work; light rain is only tolerated when the surface remains undamaged, while hard rain halts roller‑compacted concrete placement entirely [2]. Temperature limits for specific mixes are also prescribed: roller‑compacted concrete work must cease when descending air temperature drops to forty degrees or when mix temperature exceeds ninety degrees; asphalt‑treated bases require ambient temperatures above thirty‑five degrees; pervious asphalt mixtures demand at least fifty degrees, and ultra‑thin wearing courses must be rolled immediately after placement while the mix remains above one hundred eighty‑five degrees Fahrenheit, with rolling delayed until the mat cools to roughly one hundred degrees to avoid shoving or lateral movement [2]. Environmental stewardship requirements further constrain compaction by mandating that the subgrade remain clean and well‑drained, storm‑water be prevented from accumulating in the installation area until sufficient overburden is placed, sediment control measures such as covering detention basins with fine aggregate and geotextile be installed before compacting pervious concrete, and dust‑control practices be observed when wind conditions could cause additive drift onto traffic or adjacent properties [2].

Quality control and acceptance

Compaction inspection begins with confirming that the subgrade can attain its design density by establishing the maximum dry density and the corresponding optimum moisture content from laboratory moisture‑density testing of representative soil samples, and then comparing those values to the project specifications. The field moisture condition must be maintained within three percent below the laboratory‑determined optimum and never more than three percent above it; for granular Type 1 material the target moisture is required to stay within two percent of its optimum, while Type 2 material is simply kept surface damp. Density requirements differ by material: soils are generally required to reach at least 97 % of maximum dry density, with the upper three feet demanding 100 %; reclaimed‑as‑new fills must not fall below 95 % of the laboratory maximum; roller‑compacted concrete lifts need a minimum of 85 % of maximum wet density for layers thinner than six inches and 90 % for thicker lifts, rising to more than 96 % after the final rolling pass. Field density is measured by direct‑transmission methods in accordance with ASTM C‑1040 no later than thirty minutes after rolling, supplemented by nuclear gauge checks after each roller pass; at least five readings are taken directly behind the paver and another five after final compaction for every 500 cubic‑yard placement, and a minimum of ten random control‑strip tests are performed, with acceptance tolerances of ±2.0 pcf for wet density and ±0.8 % for moisture content. Stability is verified by proof‑rolling, and any permanent rutting or displacement greater than one‑half inch, as well as any shear‑wave bulging under the equipment, signals an unstable zone that must be excavated, reshaped, re‑moistened and recompacted. Visual examinations after each pass require a surface free of roller marks, binder flushing, shoving, cracking, aggregate breakage, loose zones, dirt contamination, or excess/deficient asphalt, and the mat must remain free‑draining to avoid over‑compaction. Geometric tolerances are checked with straightedges or templates, and any irregularity exceeding one‑half inch (three‑eighths inch for final acceptance) is corrected by loosening or adding material; thickness uniformity is confirmed by excavating a six‑inch test hole at prescribed locations—typically one per 3 000 sq yd—and verifying that the finished depth lies within a quarter‑inch of design, with a shortfall of half an inch or more requiring removal and rebuilding. When density testing is mandated, core samples (six‑inch diameter) are extracted and tested for bulk specific gravity according to PTM 715 or 716, and percent compaction must meet or exceed the lower limit of 91 % (or at least 96 % of the target density defined in PTM 402 for asphalt bases); the average compaction for a lot may not fall below 98 % of maximum wet density and no individual test may be under 96 %. Volumetric analysis of at least two laboratory specimens per AASHTO T‑312 determines bulk specific gravity, air voids and VMA, all of which must lie within the tolerances of Table B. For concrete pavements, compressive‑strength cylinders are molded in accordance with ASTM C‑1435 and PTM 604 and tested at three, seven and twenty‑eight days, with the twenty‑eight‑day strength required to be at least 3 500 psi; void content must be 20 % ± 5 %, hardened density averaged within ±5 lb/ft³ and not exceeding 130 lb/ft³. Pervious asphalt sections are limited to no more than four passes after the surface has cooled sufficiently, and infiltration testing on selected areas must yield an average rate of at least two hundred inches per hour; additionally, at least ninety‑five percent of aggregate particles must be coated with asphalt and no more than four percent may pass a No. 200 sieve. Compaction equipment itself is subject to verification: each roller must be documented with make, model, serial number and calibration tables; tracked excavators or dozers used on 12–24‑inch lifts must generate ground pressures between 625 and 1 025 lb/ft²; plate compactors must have a static weight of 110 to 220 lb; three‑wheel power rollers require an unballasted load of at least 300 lb per linear inch of tread and a certified metal weight of not less than ten tons; tire pressures may not vary more than five psi; pad‑foot rollers need a minimum contact pressure of 160 lb/in² over an area of fifteen square inches with a projection of three inches; smooth single‑drum vibratory rollers must have a drum width of at least forty‑eight inches and a roller factor of two or greater. All gauges and compaction devices must possess current calibration certificates, and operators must hold valid training records before results are accepted. [1][2]

Compaction work is accepted only when each placed layer meets a set of density, moisture, dimensional and performance limits that are specific to the material type and to the location within the pavement structure. For soils used in embankments the in‑place dry density must be at least ninety‑seven percent of the laboratory maximum dry density, while the uppermost three feet of the fill must reach the full one hundred percent. The moisture content of all soil layers must stay within two percentage points of the optimum value and may not be more than three percent below or above that optimum. Granular base material is subject to the same ninety‑seven percent density requirement and the identical moisture tolerances, but any subgrade or structural foundation that lies within three feet of the final grade must also achieve one hundred percent of its maximum dry density.

When hot‑mix asphalt or other wet mixes are placed, compaction directly behind the paver must attain at least eighty‑five percent of the maximum wet density for lifts thinner than six inches and at least ninety percent for lifts six inches thick or greater. After the final rolling pass the material must exceed ninety‑six percent of the maximum wet density regardless of thickness. If these percentages are not reached, a self‑propelled smooth dual‑drum steel roller weighing eight tons or more is used to provide additional primary compaction before the final pass.

Fine‑graded dense‑rock (FDR) material placed in three‑thousand‑square‑yard lots must be compacted to a minimum of ninety‑five percent of the laboratory maximum density at optimum moisture. Any control strip that fails this limit is re‑compacted, and if it still does not meet the requirement a backup strip must achieve at least ninety‑eight percent of the original strip’s density. The same lot must also satisfy surface‑tolerance limits of no more than one‑half inch deviation measured over a ten‑foot straightedge and must meet the prescribed strength criteria.

Dimensional tolerances for conventional cement‑treated or asphalt concrete paving courses require that surface irregularities not exceed one‑half inch when checked with a ten‑foot template; pervious asphalt surfaces are limited to three‑eighths of an inch. Finished thicknesses of subbase, base course and surface courses must be within a half‑inch of the design elevation. When a zone falls short by at least a half‑inch, the crew scarifies the area to three inches, blends in additional material, adjusts moisture if necessary and recompacts until the depth is acceptable. Persistent deficiencies after recompaction obligate removal and replacement of the defective section.

Stability is judged by permanent deflection after a rolling test: existing subgrades may not exhibit more than one‑half inch of rutting, while embankment foundations are limited to one inch. When deflection exceeds these limits the contractor must either increase compaction or undercut and excavate the deficient zone—removing one foot for deflections between one‑half and three inches and up to three feet for larger settlements—install the specified geotextile, backfill with approved material and compact again to meet density and moisture requirements.

All field‑density measurements are made with a nuclear density/moisture gauge that is calibrated against an independent standard; the wet‑density reading must agree within plus or minus two pounds per cubic foot and the moisture content within plus or minus 0.8 percent of the contractor’s values. Bulk‑density testing of every truckload is performed on site, and laboratory specimens for hot‑mix asphalt are required to verify bulk specific gravity, air voids and VMA against the tolerances in Table B. Cores taken within one day of placement must confirm bulk specific gravity within plus or minus 0.030 and density payment factors of one hundred percent for each lot; failure for two consecutive lots or three overall triggers an immediate stop to paving until corrective measures are approved.

When a layer does not meet its density target, the first corrective step is to apply additional roller passes with the approved equipment. If additional passes do not achieve the required density, the area is loosened, re‑moistened, reshaped and compacted again. Should these actions still fall short, the material must be removed and replaced with a new mix capable of attaining the specified density. For hot‑mix asphalt, any loose, broken, dirt‑contaminated or over‑/under‑asphalt area is removed, fresh mix is placed and re‑compacted to match surrounding pavement. If laboratory specimens repeatedly fail, the contractor must resample within one hundred fifty tons of production, retest, and submit results within five hundred tons; persistent failure leads to suspension of production until a written explanation, proposed solution and Department approval are received.

Equipment used for compaction must satisfy contract minimums. Rollers or pneumatic‑tire units that do not meet weight, ground‑pressure or tire‑inflation specifications are rejected; tire pressure may not vary more than five pounds per square inch, and pneumatic tires must exert at least three hundred pounds per inch of tread width on a hard surface. Tracked excavators or dozers compacting lifts between twelve and twenty‑four inches thick must apply ground pressures between six hundred twenty‑five and one thousand twenty‑five pounds per square foot, while thinner lifts are compacted with plate compactors weighing one hundred ten to two hundred twenty pounds. If substituted equipment produces nonconforming work, its use is discontinued, the deficient material is removed and replaced using the specified equipment, or other corrective action directed by the Representative is taken.

Before full production begins a three‑hundred‑foot test strip must be constructed and demonstrated to meet the minimum density required by the applicable PTM (e.g., PTM 402 for base courses). Failure of the test strip is treated as non‑conformance, requiring cessation of production, equipment recalibration or mix‑rate adjustment, and repetition of test strips until consistent compliance is achieved. Base courses must reach at least ninety‑six percent of the target density defined in PTM 402; longitudinal joint cores must attain a minimum of ninety‑one percent compaction. For pervious asphalt, infiltration rates must be two hundred inches per hour or greater, aggregate coating at least ninety‑five percent and No. 200 sieve content not to exceed four percent. Failure of any performance threshold renders the work defective and subject to removal and replacement.

Reinforced concrete pavement (RCC) lots are accepted when the overall average dry density after rolling reaches at least ninety‑eight percent of the maximum wet density and no individual test falls below ninety‑six percent. If these limits are not met, cores are taken for 28‑day compressive‑strength testing; core holes are backfilled with RCC or rapid‑set concrete. Should the lot also fail to achieve a minimum compressive strength of three thousand five hundred psi, the pavement is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced under district procedures.

In all cases, corrective actions follow a prescribed sequence: first additional rolling or moisture adjustment; if ineffective, scarification and recompaction; if still ineffective, removal and replacement of the deficient area. The contractor must determine the root cause, prepare a corrective proposal and obtain Representative approval before work may resume. No grinding or milling of cement‑treated pavement concrete is permitted during correction, and any additional roller passes are limited to eliminating creases without compromising drainage or crushing aggregate. Safety and environmental controls

Compaction work presents both occupational and public health hazards that must be managed through specific controls. Workers are exposed to mechanical danger when a loaded triaxle dump truck, weighing 72,000 lb GVW, is used for proof‑rolling; the risk of crushing or collision is reduced by restricting travel speed to no more than 1.5 ft s⁻¹ (approximately one mile per hour). The use of nuclear moisture‑density gauges introduces ionizing radiation hazards, which are mitigated by keeping a minimum separation of 30 ft between any gauge and other radioactive sources and by limiting operation to personnel who hold a current nuclear‑gauge operator certification. Additional injury risks arise from large rollers—such as pneumatic‑tire rollers of 25 ton capacity or greater, vibratory pad‑foot rollers, and steel‑drum static rollers—and are addressed through pre‑deployment inspections, calibration checks, and ongoing maintenance in accordance with Section 108.05(c). For the public, fugitive dust and airborne dry additives generated during fine‑dense‑rolling can affect adjacent properties; therefore specifications require the use of appropriate equipment and techniques to contain material, and they prohibit applying dry additive when wind conditions would cause blowing that is objectionable to traffic or nearby owners. Curing, protection, and handoff

After each lift is placed and compacted, the surface is fine‑graded and rolled with a smooth‑drum roller to promote continuous positive drainage, eliminate low spots, and prevent water infiltration; layers are shaped so that water drains during construction and furrows that would impede flow are prohibited. The freshly compacted material is then protected from mechanical disturbance and moisture loss. For reinforced cement concrete (RCC) slabs, a moist cure is started immediately after the final rolling and field‑density testing by applying misting water from a truck or an equivalent curing compound; this moisture regimen is maintained for at least 72 hours until the slab reaches a compressive strength of 3,000 psi, and vehicular traffic is barred for the entire period. Reclaimed or chemically stabilized layers are kept damp either by daily water applications or by applying an emulsified‑asphalt primer followed by a fog seal or tack coat at a residual rate of 0.02 to 0.10 gal/yd² while the material remains within three percent of optimum moisture content; these layers are cured until they satisfy the seven‑day strength requirement, during which heavy traffic is prohibited and appropriate signage posted. Fine‑grained aggregate (FGA) placed after compaction is covered with a Class 4, Type A geotextile and then overlaid with at least six inches of compacted capping material within two weeks; rollers are not allowed directly on the FGA, traffic on exposed areas is minimized, any rut deeper than four inches is excavated to five inches below the rut bottom and recompacted, ponding is prevented until sufficient overburden exists, and damaged geotextile must be removed or lap‑stitched with a minimum twelve‑inch overlap. Cementitious treated pavement base courses (CTPBC) are cured immediately after compaction by applying a white membrane‑forming compound at roughly one gallon per 100 ft² (±10 %) or by placing white polyethylene sheeting for twenty‑four hours, extending an additional twenty‑four hours if the temperature falls below 40 °F; construction traffic is prohibited for at least three days and no overlying pavement may be placed until that period expires. After an asphalt‑treated permeable base has been placed, it is allowed to cool to approximately 100 °F before any subsequent layer; rolling with a steel‑wheel or static‑mode vibratory roller is performed for four passes as soon as the temperature is reached, and traffic is limited to trucks and equipment required for the next course. The base is protected from marring or distortion, and any damaged area must be repaired and, if necessary, re‑compacted before paving resumes. Emulsified‑asphalt stabilized CRABC must remain undisturbed for a minimum of two weeks, while foamed‑asphalt stabilized CRABC is cured until the average moisture content falls below two percent; three random cores are dry‑cut before placing the binder or wearing course, and any core holes are backfilled within twenty‑four hours with the same mix, compacted and sealed. When compaction is interrupted for more than thirty minutes or at the end of a workday, a full‑depth transverse construction joint is created either by installing a temporary vertical bulkhead that extends through the entire thickness or by sawing a joint; this isolates the freshly compacted surface from contamination until the next layer or traffic is applied. Placement is prohibited if air temperature is expected to drop below 40 °F during the required seven‑day cure period, and reclamation cannot be performed in rain nor when rain is forecast within two hours after finishing; appropriate equipment and techniques are used to protect adjacent properties from fugitive dust. Longitudinal joints created after coring are backfilled no later than twenty‑four hours with the same hot‑mix asphalt, compacted and sealed, then painted and finally sealed with a hot PG‑64S‑22 binder applied at 265–320 °F in a roughly four‑inch wide band about one‑sixteenth inch thick on clean, dry joints; sealing is repeated if coverage is incomplete to prevent moisture intrusion, traffic disturbance, and loss of density while surrounding layers continue to cure under load. For concrete pavements, curing begins within twenty minutes of discharge, after which the entire surface is completely covered with polyethylene sheeting fastened at all edges and laps to prevent wind removal; the sheet remains in place for a minimum of seven days before any traffic loading. At the close of each workday the surface is sealed with smooth‑wheeled compaction equipment, protecting it from moisture and keeping the excavation graded to promote drainage, thereby retaining the achieved density and stability until final acceptance. General protection includes restricting traffic to essential construction vehicles, keeping base layers moist until covered by the next layer, promptly repairing any damage, shielding nearby structures and underground utilities (avoiding special rolling within ten feet of bridge abutments), backfilling core holes within twenty‑four hours using the same mix or a rapid‑set patching material, compacting and sealing them, and installing a safety edge immediately to preserve density while the pavement cures. After the compaction step is finished, the pavement may be placed in service for construction traffic or opened to users only when the acceptance criteria defined by the applicable guide have been satisfied [1][2]. For thin‑wear surface courses such as those used in UTWC work, the guides agree that no numeric density or strength test is required; traffic may be allowed as soon as the compacted surface has cooled to a temperature that permits safe vehicle movement, with the temperature condition being the sole requirement [1]. In contrast, for other materials the opening decision depends on material‑specific density, curing and strength requirements that must be verified before any traffic is permitted, and often requires authorization from the designated Representative [2]. Reinforced cement concrete pavement must remain moist for at least seventy‑two hours after placement and achieve a compressive strength of three thousand pounds per square inch before any vehicular traffic, including construction vehicles, may be allowed onto the surface [2]. When reclaimed fine‑graded material is used as a base, it must be compacted to no less than ninety‑five percent of its laboratory‑maximum density at optimum moisture content and this target must be confirmed by a control strip; once verified, proof‑rolling with a vehicle comparable to the heaviest anticipated traffic permits light vehicles under five tons to enter roughly two hours after construction completion provided no damage is observed [2]. Heavy or truck traffic on that reclaimed base, however, must wait until a seven‑day cure period has elapsed and the required seven‑day strength has been demonstrated, at which point unlimited traffic may be allowed [2]. General base courses are required to reach one hundred percent of the maximum density specified in PTM No. 106 Method B and confirmed by AASHTO T‑191 testing; any surface irregularities greater than a half‑inch must be corrected before a Representative can authorize opening, after which only necessary local traffic and essential construction equipment may use the section until a surface course is placed [2]. Cement‑treated base courses must remain free of construction traffic for a minimum of three days after placement; thereafter traffic is limited to trucks and equipment needed to place the next lift or an adjacent course, with any damage repaired at no cost to the Department [2]. Asphalt‑treated permeable base material, once compacted with the prescribed four roller passes (or additional passes to eliminate surface irregularities) and allowed to cool sufficiently, may be used solely by construction trucks or equipment required for the subsequent layer, while all other traffic is prohibited until the next paving operation is completed [2]. Pervious‑concrete pavements cannot be opened to any vehicular traffic, including construction vehicles, until they have cured for at least seven days and the ambient temperature has risen above fifty‑five degrees Fahrenheit during that period; after those conditions are met the surface may be placed into service [2]. For newly placed mixtures compacted with a pneumatic‑tire roller operating at forty to sixty pounds per square inch, the required set time—generally within one hour—must be achieved and the Representative must confirm that the surface is sufficiently hardened to prevent pick‑up before construction traffic or public use is permitted [2]. Across all material types, opening the compacted section to traffic typically requires documented verification of the specified density, strength or temperature condition and, where stipulated, written approval from the responsible Representative before construction vehicles or the public are allowed onto the pavement [2].

Expected performance and maintenance

When compaction does not achieve the required in‑place density, a range of distress mechanisms can develop. For lifts thinner than six inches the target is at least eighty‑five percent of the maximum wet density, while thicker lifts must reach ninety percent and a final rolling pass must exceed ninety‑six percent for any thickness; control strips are expected to show no less than ninety‑five percent of the laboratory maximum with repeat strips required to meet ninety‑eight percent, and base courses must attain a minimum of ninety‑six percent of the PTM 402 target density with lot averages not falling below ninety‑eight percent and no individual test under ninety‑six percent. Failure to meet these thresholds can manifest as tearing, cracking, deformation, uneven roller marks, surface blemishes, cold joints, objectionable undulations, settlement, rutting, displacement, shoving, and other irregularities that exceed the allowable half‑inch depth tolerance [2]. Over‑compaction—defined by applying more than four passes, using excessive pressure such as a ten‑ton steel‑wheel roller, or crushing the aggregate—eliminates the free‑draining nature of the mix and reduces porosity; in pervious asphalt this is evident when infiltration rates drop below two hundred inches per hour and may be accompanied by flushing of the binder, reduced surface drainage, and an overall decline in drainage performance [2]. Improper roller operation, including speeds faster than the prescribed one‑and‑a‑half feet per second, inadequate contact pressures or tire‑inflation tolerances (within five psi), and incorrect pattern, amplitude, frequency, or roller size, can generate high or low spots greater than a half‑inch, persistent roller marks, material pickup, surface distortion, and other defects [2]. Rolling within three to six inches of an unsupported edge may produce surface irregularities that exceed the one‑and‑a‑half inch tolerance, while leaving such edges unrolled initially helps prevent distortion [2]. Moisture conditions that deviate more than three percent below optimum for soils or plus or minus two percent for granular Type 1 material prevent a stable mass and can lead to rutting, displacement, shear‑wave bulging, push‑and‑shove, or permanent deformation; overly wet material must be dried before further work and compaction delayed beyond the one‑hour moisture window after water addition also results in low density and subsequent settlement [2]. Subgrade and subbase problems—such as placing a base on soft, muddy, frozen, or otherwise unsuitable ground, allowing depth deficiencies of half an inch or more, or mixing subgrade or subbase material into the base course—produce marred and distorted surfaces that often require removal, reshaping, re‑compaction, or full replacement; applying fine filler to wet or frozen underlying layers similarly yields unacceptable surface conditions [2]. Chemically stabilized materials that develop compressive strengths above roughly nine hundred pounds per square inch are prone to shrinkage cracking, while excessive compactive effort that crushes fine‑grained aggregate signals equipment overload and may necessitate excavation of depressions deeper than four inches [2]. Visible defects such as displacement, shoving, cracking of the asphalt layer, aggregate breakage, loose or broken sections contaminated with dirt, excess or deficiency of binder, flushing of the binder, and persistent roller marks all indicate compaction distress and require immediate corrective action such as loosening, adding or removing material, scarifying, adjusting moisture content, re‑rolling within specified limits, or excavating and replacing the affected portion [2]. After the wearing course is placed and compacted, the most common surface distresses observed are potholes and raveling, especially where reflective cracks intersect the surface, as well as general raveling of the wearing course [1].

A disciplined preventive‑maintenance program begins before any compaction work starts. All rollers, cutting drums and related equipment are inspected, calibrated and documented; manufacturers’ data sheets, serial numbers and calibration charts are supplied to the Representative. Rollers must be equipped with functioning water‑spray systems, scrapers and release agents, and their tires inflated uniformly so that pressures differ by no more than five pounds per square inch and remain between one hundred and one‑hundred ten psi on loaded triaxle trucks. Calibration of nuclear or electrical‑impedance gauges is verified annually in accordance with ASTM D2950, and a 300‑foot test strip demonstrating the final‑density‑ratio (FDR) process is constructed before production begins. Steel‑wheel rollers weighing at least ten tons are preferred; pneumatic‑tire rollers are avoided unless expressly permitted.

During placement the moisture of each material is kept within tight tolerances – soil no more than three percent below optimum, granular base within plus or minus two percent, and water added so that the mixture does not vary by more than two percentage points from the target. Moisture samples are taken at least twice daily, and at least ninety percent of the samples must remain inside the limits defined in Table A. The material is placed in lifts that meet these moisture targets before any rolling begins. Rollers operate at a maximum travel speed of 1.5 ft/s (≈1 mph) and are limited to the prescribed static or vibratory modes; steel‑drum, rubber‑tired or dual‑steel drum rollers are used as required, with an additional self‑propelled smooth dual‑steel drum roller employed when a paver cannot achieve at least 95 % field density. After each pass the in‑place moisture and density are measured at several random locations within thirty minutes using calibrated nuclear gauges or other approved devices, following AASHTO T191/T310 or ASTM C1040 procedures. Target densities are 95 % of laboratory maximum for fine‑graded reclaimed material, 96–97 % of maximum dry density for base layers, and 100 % for the upper three feet; each base‑course layer must also reach at least 100 % of the PTM 106 Method B value. For base courses a minimum of 96 % of the PTM 402 target density is required, while lot averages must not fall below 98 % of maximum wet density and no individual sublot may be under 96 %. If moisture‑density increase is less than one percent over three consecutive passes, or if any test falls short of its minimum, additional compaction passes are applied.

Continuous monitoring includes straightedge inspections for surface flatness. A ten‑foot straightedge is run parallel to the centreline in increments no greater than five feet; any deviation exceeding one‑half inch triggers corrective action. For pervious asphalt a twelve‑foot (or ten‑foot) straightedge is used and tolerances are tightened to three‑eighths of an inch. Core samples are taken within one day for each 3,000 sq yd segment; if density or depth falls short—density below the required payment factor or depth deficient by half an inch—the zone is scarified to three inches, additional material blended in, and re‑compacted. Infiltration testing of pervious pavement is performed on three locations for every 10,000 sq ft, with a minimum rate of 200 in/hr; failure leads to loosening the surface, adding or removing mix, or replacing the defective portion entirely.

When monitoring reveals rutting, permanent deflection or other instability—rut depth greater than one‑half inch in subgrade, more than one inch in embankments, or FGA rutting deeper than four inches—the area is treated as unstable. Proof‑rolling with a 72 000‑lb triaxle at the same speed limit confirms the condition; unstable zones are excavated to at least one foot depth (or five inches below the bottom of a deep rut), dried, reshaped, re‑moistened and recompacted. For fine‑graded aggregate (FGA) the repaired zone is compacted with four passes of a plate compactor; for other layers the prescribed roller sequence—often four passes of a smooth steel drum—is used. Damaged geotextile, marred or distorted pavement, and guide rails compromised during construction are repaired at no extra cost to the department. Traffic on freshly compacted surfaces is limited to essential equipment; construction traffic is prohibited for at least three days after placement, and bulk‑density checks are performed on each truckload of fill. Periodic proof‑rolling, visual inspections for excessive rutting, and independent gauge verification (agreement within ±2.0 pcf wet density and ±0.8 % moisture) constitute the ongoing quality‑control program that ensures long‑term pavement performance.
