Patching Material

Part of: Concrete Patching is-subtopic-of
Patching Material is a topic under Concrete Patching.
concrete-patching-material
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Patching material is a newly placed Portland‑cement concrete—often a rapid‑setting or accelerated mix—that fills cavities left after deteriorated slab or joint sections are removed, conforming to the specifications for concrete pavement repair [1][2]. Its primary purpose is to restore structural continuity and load‑transfer capability of rigid pavements by providing a full‑depth concrete fill that can be dowelled into existing slabs, creating a uniform base capable of supporting traffic and any subsequent overlay [1]. The mix must satisfy Class AA cement‑concrete requirements—minimum cement factor around 530 lb/yd³, water‑cement ratio not exceeding 0.42 (target 0.40), air content about 7 % ± 1.5 %, and may include supplementary cementitious material or accelerated admixtures to achieve early compressive strengths (e.g., 1,500 psi at seven hours or traffic‑opening strength in 4–7 hours) while maintaining a durability factor of at least 80 % after freeze‑thaw cycling [2].

Material purpose and applicability

Concrete patching material is a newly placed Portland‑cement concrete—often a standard or accelerated rapid‑setting mix—that replaces removed, deteriorated slab or joint areas in a rigid pavement; its primary function is to restore structural continuity and load‑transfer capability by providing a full‑depth concrete fill that can be dowelled into existing slabs, thereby creating a uniform base capable of supporting traffic and any subsequent overlay. When an accelerated mix is used it may achieve opening‑to‑traffic strength in 4 to 7 hours, allowing early traffic restoration while still meeting long‑term performance requirements. In the Pennsylvania specifications concrete patching material is described as a rapid‑setting cementitious grout or comparable high‑strength mix used to fill dowel slots and surface repairs after the bedding grout has set in full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs; it restores the slab’s surface, prevents material from entering joint gaps, and must reach a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic may be permitted on the repaired section. The same specifications define patching material for general concrete pavement patching as the rapid‑set concrete mix placed in a prepared cavity after damaged pavement has been removed; it rebuilds the lost slab section, provides proper bond and load transfer across joints, and the surface of the patch is finished so that it matches the existing pavement cross‑section. For unbonded concrete overlays an asphalt‑based, cementitious, or other approved repair mix may be used as patching material to fill shallow cracks and potholes before an overlay is placed; when a geotextile interlayer is employed it must be placed in any defect larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch and compacted to a flat, tight surface, thereby providing uniform support for the subsequent overlay and preventing premature failure. Within bonded concrete overlays the specifications require Class AA cement concrete formulated with a minimum cement factor of 530 lb/yd³, a maximum water‑cement ratio of 0.42 lb/lb (target 0.40 not below 0.38), and an air content of 7.0 % ± 1.5 %; this patching material provides a durable repair layer for accelerated patching, restoring surface continuity and extending service life. In dowel retrofit work the specifications call for concrete patching material to be placed into the dowel retrofit slots after the dowels have been positioned; its purpose is to fill the slot and, when vibrated, thoroughly consolidate around the dowel bar so that the repaired area regains structural continuity, with the surface finished to match the existing pavement profile, thereby restoring a smooth riding surface. For partial‑depth repairs the specifications prescribe a pre‑packaged Rapid‑Set Concrete Patching Material that complies with ASTM C928; it must achieve a durability factor of at least 80 % after 300 freeze–thaw cycles (ASTM C666, procedure B) and must set no later than 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F (AASHTO T 131), ensuring quick restoration of the pavement surface, and when repairs exceed two inches in depth aggregate extenders may be added according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. In slab‑stabilization applications the specifications refer to Rapid‑Set Concrete Patching Materials supplied by a Bulletin 15 manufacturer, mixed with cement, supplementary cementitious material, water and any required admixtures to form a grout that stabilizes cracked or distressed concrete slabs; these materials must be used within the shelf‑life and temperature limits set by the manufacturer. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements the specifications provide that rapid‑set concrete patching material may be used when a lot does not meet the maximum wet density and compressive strength requirements; core holes are filled either with additional RCC or with this rapid‑set material to consolidate voids, thereby restoring continuity of the slab after the defect has been identified. The specifications also allow Rapid‑Set Concrete Patching Material for partial‑depth repairs opened to traffic (Section 516.3(q)), providing a quickly hardening fill that restores the pavement surface after removal of defective work so that traffic can resume; placement is permitted only within the manufacturer’s recommended installation temperature range and when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F or greater, and the repair must be protected from freezing for at least 24 hours. In all applications the material is consolidated by vibration or tamping, worked into saw cuts that extend beyond the corners of the repair area, then struck‑off and shaped to match the surrounding pavement, including wheel ruts, while using expansion joint material and temporary forms to keep it out of existing joints. Composition and characteristics

Concrete patching material is essentially Portland‑cement concrete that must satisfy the requirements of Publication 408 Section 704, using an optimized aggregate gradation mix. Under Section 545 of Publication 408, the material is defined as Class AA cement concrete modified per Section 545.2 and consists of Portland cement—at least 400 lb/yd³ when supplemented—with optional fly ash or slag cement, provided total cementitious content stays within Table A limits and slag cement does not exceed 30 % of the cementitious material. The coarse aggregate for this mix must be Type A No. 57, No. 67, or No. 8 durable stone or gravel that is thoroughly washed; blast‑furnace‑slag aggregate is prohibited. For AA Slip‑Form Paving and Accelerated Patching the mix shall have a minimum cement factor of 530 lb/yd³ and may not exceed 600 lb/yd³, while the water‑cement ratio cannot exceed 0.42, with a target of 0.40 that will not fall below 0.38 for all Class AA concrete. Fresh concrete placed for AA Slip‑Form Paving and Accelerated Patching must contain 7.0 % air ±1.5 % at placement, and any reactive aggregate used shall have a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % only when paired with an approved cement‑supplementary material combination that reduces expansion to less than 0.1 %. Under Section 525, patching material options include Class AA cement concrete, both modified and accelerated, made with coarse aggregate Type A No. 8 and limited to a maximum water‑cement ratio of 0.42; the accelerated mix must achieve at least 1,500 psi compressive strength at seven hours. The rapid‑set concrete patching material described in Section 525 is a prepackaged product from an approved manufacturer that shall comply with ASTM C928, possess a minimum durability factor of 80 % after 300 cycles in ASTM C666 (procedure B), and have a minimum initial set time of 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F as measured by AASHTO T 131. When repairs exceed two inches in depth under Section 525, only the aggregate extenders supplied with the rapid‑set material may be used, or alternatively a No. 8 coarse aggregate with an expansion index less than 0.10 if expressly permitted by the manufacturer. For slab stabilization under Section 679, the patching material consists of one part cement mixed with three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, water adjusted so that the efflux time falls between ten and fifteen seconds, and any required admixtures such as a multiphase wetting agent, expansive agent or accelerator. The Section 679 mix must achieve an expansion of zero to ten percent, an initial set within one to six hours, bleed no more than 2.5 % of its volume, and develop at least a seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi measured on five cylinders. Under Section 704, concrete for pavement patching is produced as volumetric mixed cement concrete in a plant that stores cement, fine aggregate, coarse aggregate, water and any admixtures separately until they are metered by weight into an auger‑type mixer; the batch must be proportioned so that cement weight falls within 0 to +4 percent of the design, fine and coarse aggregates each stay within ±2 percent, water within ±2 percent and admixtures within ±3 percent. The resulting mix is required to have a uniform consistency with no segregation; during mixing the drive‑shaft speed is kept within 50 rpm of the operating speed and slump, air content, unit weight and uniformity tests are performed to verify physical characteristics. The mixture may be supplied as cast‑in‑place concrete or as precast concrete paving systems; the latter is a high‑strength Class AA cement concrete that exceeds the normal PCC specifications. When a rapid‑setting (accelerated) product is specified, it is formulated to reach the minimum compressive strength within four to seven hours after placement, whereas standard mixes typically require twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours. Dowels are embedded through the full depth of the patch and bonded along their entire length with anchoring agents such as epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders to provide load transfer. Tie bars or bolts are required for patches more than 65 feet long. High early‑strength concrete is discouraged because it can cause excessive shrinkage cracking. For shallow repairs under Section 548, the patching material may be an asphalt‑based material, a cementitious material, or any comparable pavement repair product that has been approved by the Representative prior to overlay placement. Under Section 502 for rigid pavements, concrete patching material is defined by a mix that includes cement, supplementary cementitious materials, well‑graded aggregate as specified in Table A, water, admixtures and concrete curing material, all combined according to the RCC mixture requirements; the same mix design and materials must be used throughout the project, with moisture content optimized per AASHTO T 180 Method D, and any change requires a new mix design submission. Rapid‑set patching products referenced in Section 502 must be placed within the manufacturer’s shelf‑life and temperature limits. Placement of rapid‑set concrete patching material under Sections 525 and 679 is permitted only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F or greater, and repairs must be protected from freezing for at least twenty‑four hours after placement. Consolidation of the material in Section 525 is achieved with vibration or tamping, and the material is worked into saw cuts extending beyond the repair corners to match the surrounding pavement profile; expansion joint material and pre‑formed cellular polystyrene forms are used to keep the patch out of existing joints. The Pavement Policy Manual advises that a high‑early‑strength concrete mix should be avoided because its composition tends to cause excessive shrinkage cracking[1]. In contrast, an accelerated concrete formulation—designed for rapid strength gain—can permit traffic opening after only 4 to 7 hours, whereas standard pavement concrete with optimized aggregate gradation typically requires 24 to 48 hours to reach comparable strength[1]. Thus, changes in mix type, aggregate grading, or the use of accelerated versus conventional formulations directly influence shrinkage potential, early‑strength development, and overall patch performance[1]. For Class AA paving, variations in cementitious composition are permitted only when at least 400 lb per cubic yard of Portland cement remains and the total cementitious content stays within the limits set in Table A; slag cement may not exceed 30 % of the total cementitious material[2]. These adjustments affect mix strength and durability while still meeting the minimum cement factor of 530 lb/yd³ for AA slip‑form paving and patching and the maximum cement factor of 600 lb/yd³ for AA slip‑form paving (but not accelerated patching)[2]. The water‑cement ratio is limited to a maximum of 0.42 lb/lb for all Class AA concrete, with a target of 0.40 lb/lb that shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb, and the air content at placement must be 7.0 % ± 1.5 %, both controlling workability and freeze‑thaw resistance[2]. Only durable stone or gravel that is washed thoroughly may be used as aggregate; blast furnace slag aggregate is prohibited, and reactive aggregates showing a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % can be employed only with an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material combination that reduces the expansion to less than 0.1 %, while any aggregate exceeding 0.30 % expansion is rejected[2]. Blended hydraulic cements must limit mortar expansion to 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at 8 weeks[2]. In the partial‑depth repair specification, changing the coarse‑aggregate type or water‑cement proportioning alters the Class AA mix, which is limited to a maximum water‑cement ratio of 0.42 and must meet a compressive strength target of 1,500 psi at seven hours when an accelerated design is used[2]. Selecting rapid‑set concrete patching material from an approved manufacturer and following its prescribed proportioning—including the use of aggregate extenders for repairs deeper than two inches—governs durability, requiring a minimum factor of 80 % after 300 cycles, and early set performance, demanding at least a 15‑minute initial set at 75 °F ± 2 °F[2]. Grout must be placed within 60 minutes of mixing and epoxy bonding compounds must be applied within the manufacturer’s shelf life and setting‑time limits to retain their specified properties[2]. The slab‑stabilization grout mix uses one part cement and three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, with water added so that the time of efflux falls within ten seconds to fifteen seconds, which controls flowability and bleed[2]. The mix is limited to an expansion of 0 % to 10 % and an initial setting time of one hour to six hours, defining shrinkage/expansion behavior[2]. Any change in the source of cement, supplementary material, water, or admixtures requires submitting a new mix design because such variations can affect independent laboratory testing results for compressive strength (minimum 700 psi at seven days), flowability, shrinkage, expansion and initial set time[2]. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements, any change in cement, supplementary cementitious material, aggregate, water or admixture composition—or a shift in source that alters those ingredients—requires a new mix design to preserve the intended strength and durability[2]. Uniform mixing is also critical because incomplete or non‑uniform blending, exceeding the manufacturer’s rated volume capacity for dry concrete, or using worn mixer blades can lead to inadequate consolidation and variable patch performance[2]. Rapid‑set patching material must be applied within its shelf life and temperature limits; otherwise its setting characteristics and resulting repair quality may deteriorate[2]. Because the plant must deliver each ingredient at a calibrated weight, any change in composition or source such as using different fine or coarse aggregate or cement requires recalibration to keep the mix within prescribed tolerances[2]. The batching system is required to keep cement within 0 to +4 % of the target weight, fine and coarse aggregates each within ±2 %, admixtures within ±3 % and water within ±2 %; staying inside these limits ensures consistent slump, air content and unit weight as specified by testing procedures[2]. Mixing speed must be held within 50 rpm of the set point to avoid segregation that could further alter patch performance[2].

Key material properties

The Pavement Policy Manual emphasizes that early‑age compressive strength is a key mechanical property for concrete pavement patching because accelerated concrete must reach the minimum concrete compressive strength within four to seven hours after placement as required by Publication 408 Section 501, while standard PCC with optimized aggregate gradation can develop sufficient opening‑to‑traffic strength in twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours [1]. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529) the patching material must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a minimum flexural strength of 650 psi [2]. Rapid‑set concrete patching repairs (Section 516) are judged by reaching a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi before traffic is allowed, as verified by cylinder testing [2]. Accelerated partial‑depth repair mixes (Section 525 material) shall attain at least 1,500 psi compressive strength after seven hours [2]. Slab‑stabilization rapid‑set concrete (Section 679) requires a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi based on the average of five test cylinders [2]. The durability factor for accelerated partial‑depth repair mixes must be no less than eighty percent after three hundred freeze–thaw cycles, indicating resistance to thermal cycling [2]. The Pavement Policy Manual notes that shrinkage tendency is a critical physical property, recommending avoidance of high early strength (HES) concrete because of its potential for excessive shrinkage cracking [1]. For Class AA accelerated patching concrete (Section 545) the water‑cement ratio shall not exceed 0.42, with a target range that does not fall below 0.38 and is aimed at 0.40, controlling workability and dimensional stability [2]. Air content for Class AA concrete is required to be 7.0 % ± 1.5 % at placement, influencing entrapped‑air related freeze‑thaw performance [2]. The percentage of air‑entraining admixture shall not exceed fifteen percent by mass of the total cementitious factor for all concrete patching material (Section 714) [2]. For slab‑stabilization mixes the water amount is chosen so that the time of efflux is within ten to fifteen seconds, a measure of flowability and workability [2]. The mix may be furnished with an expansion of zero to ten percent, and bleed shall not exceed two point five percent of the volume, both affecting dimensional change and surface finish [2]. Full‑depth precast slabs are cast with a sacrificial thickness of one‑quarter inch over the nominal design depth and must have a prescribed top‑surface texture, physical characteristics that aid joint performance and load transfer [2]. Class AA accelerated patching concrete (Section 545) requires at least four hundred pounds per cubic yard of Portland cement, and any supplementary cementitious material such as fly ash or slag may be used provided the total does not exceed Table A limits and slag cement is limited to no more than thirty percent of the cementitious blend [2]. Reactive aggregate is acceptable only if its fourteen‑day expansion lies between 0.10 % and 0.30 %; expansions greater than 0.30 % are rejected, ensuring chemical stability of the aggregate–cement matrix [2]. Blended hydraulic cement must limit mortar expansion to 0.02 % at fourteen days and 0.06 % at eight weeks, further controlling chemical‑induced dimensional change [2]. The mix design for full‑depth precast/prestressed slabs (Section 529) must incorporate the appropriate coefficient of thermal expansion for the site’s average daily temperatures to set correct panel sizes and joint locations, a thermal property governing long‑term movement [2]. Initial set time requirements are specified at particular temperatures; accelerated partial‑depth repair mixes cannot have an initial set time shorter than fifteen minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F, linking chemical reaction rate to ambient temperature [2]. Placement of patching material is limited to ambient air and surface temperatures of forty degrees Fahrenheit or greater (and rising), and the mixture shall be discontinued if temperatures fall below forty degrees Fahrenheit; after placement the repair must be protected from freezing for a minimum twenty‑four hours, reflecting thermal constraints on curing [2]. During curing, if ambient air temperature drops below eighty degrees Fahrenheit, insulation or heating shall be provided, and the repair area’s temperature may not change more than forty degrees Fahrenheit within any one‑hour period, ensuring controlled thermal conditions for strength development [2]. Air temperatures within the work area must remain above forty degrees Fahrenheit during production (Section 714), a thermal requirement for proper placement and finishing [2]. The Pavement Policy Manual further states that using a high‑strength, durable mix that exceeds the Class AA cement concrete requirements of precast units contributes to long‑term performance [1].

The properties of concrete patching material are primarily gauged by the minimum compressive strength required, as defined in Publication 408 Section 501, and by the elapsed time needed to reach that strength [1]. Accelerated mixes are reported to attain a traffic‑opening strength within four to seven hours, whereas standard pavement concrete typically requires twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours to achieve comparable strength [1]. The material is further classified according to its functional application into three types—full‑depth concrete pavement restoration (CPR), patching beneath an asphalt overlay, and patching beneath an unbonded concrete overlay [1]. It is also categorized by mix type, ranging from conventional portland cement concrete that meets Publication 408 Section 704 specifications to high early‑strength or accelerated concrete and precast concrete paving systems that exceed Class AA cement‑concrete requirements [1]. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, the patching mix must be verified by trial‑batch testing that demonstrates a compressive strength of at least 4,925 psi and a flexural strength of at least 650 psi before slab fabrication, with retesting permitted if these values are not met [2]. The grout used to fill dowel slots or bedding chambers is classified by its compressive strength and may not be placed for traffic until it reaches a minimum of 2,500 psi [2]. In concrete pavement patching operations, quality‑control cylinders are tested and must achieve a compressive strength of at least 1,000 psi before traffic is allowed on the repair [2]. Surface flatness of the patched area is checked with a twelve‑foot straightedge and any high point or depression exceeding three‑sixteenths of an inch is removed by grinding [2]. Dowel‑bar anchoring is inspected by coring, requiring that the perimeter voids around each dowel be less than 0.50 in, and the anchoring material must attain an initial set within five minutes of application [2]. For bonded concrete overlays classified as Class AA patching material, cement content must be at least 530 lb/yd³ for slip‑form paving, paving, and accelerated patching and may not exceed 600 lb/yd³ for slip‑form paving and paving [2]. The water‑cement ratio for all Class AA concrete is limited to a maximum of 0.42 lb/lb, with a target value of 0.40 lb/lb that shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb, and compliance is verified in the field using batch tickets together with AASHTO T 318‑02 [2]. Air content at placement for slip‑form paving and accelerated patching must be 7.0 % ± 1.5 % [2]. Aggregates are evaluated by their fourteen‑day expansion, where values between 0.10 % and 0.30 % are acceptable only when used with an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material combination that reduces the expansion to less than 0.1 %, while aggregates showing more than 0.30 % expansion are rejected [2]. In partial‑depth repair mixes, the water‑cement ratio may not exceed 0.42 [2]. Accelerated Class AA concrete designs must achieve a compressive strength of at least 1,500 psi measured after seven hours in accordance with PTM No. 604 [2]. Rapid‑set patching material is required to attain a durability factor of no less than 80 % after three hundred cycles in ASTM C666 (procedure B) and must exhibit an initial set time of at least fifteen minutes when tested at 75 °F ± 2 °F according to AASHTO T 131 [2]. The specification classifies concrete patching material into three distinct types—Class AA Cement Concrete, Modified; Class AA Cement Concrete, Accelerated; and Rapid‑Set Concrete Patching Material—and limits placement of the rapid‑set product to ambient air and surface temperatures of forty degrees Fahrenheit or greater [2]. For slab stabilization, rapid‑set concrete patching material must be documented in a laboratory mix‑design submittal that includes independent test results for one‑, three‑, and seven‑day compressive strength, flowability, shrinkage, expansion, and initial set time [2]. The mix is required to achieve an expansion between zero percent and ten percent, an initial setting time ranging from one hour to six hours, bleed no more than two point five percent of the volume, a water‑efflux time of ten to fifteen seconds, and a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of seven hundred psi based on the average of five cylinders [2]. In roller‑compacted concrete pavements, moisture content is optimized according to AASHTO T 180 Method D, ingredient proportions must meet the tolerances set out in Section 704.1(e), daily plant production records are submitted for calibration checks, and the material may only be used within the shelf‑life and temperature limits specified by the manufacturer [2]. The properties of cement concrete patching material are controlled by precise volumetric weighing on the mixing plant, with cement weight required to be within zero to plus four percent of the target, fine and coarse aggregates each within plus or minus two percent, admixtures within plus or minus three percent, and water within plus or minus two percent [2]. Behavior and mechanisms

In the design of concrete pavement patches, load transfer across transverse joints is achieved by fully bonded dowels that distribute traffic loads through the repaired area, a requirement for maintaining proper patch performance. Precast concrete paving systems used as patch material are specified to support the same range of traffic loading conditions as conventional Portland cement concrete pavement, indicating comparable load‑bearing capacity. High early‑strength (HES) concrete is avoided because it tends to develop excessive shrinkage cracking, reflecting sensitivity to moisture loss during curing. When rapid opening to service is required, accelerated concrete provides sufficient compressive strength within four to seven hours, meeting the minimum strength schedule for early traffic loading. For concrete pavement partial‑depth repairs, the rapid‑set concrete patching material must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 1,500 psi at seven hours, demonstrating its ability to sustain service loads shortly after placement. The same material is required to attain an initial set within fifteen minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F, establishing the temperature condition for early hardening. Use of the material must conform to the manufacturer’s shelf‑life and temperature limits, and when repair depth exceeds two inches aggregate extenders prescribed by the manufacturer are required. Its durability factor must be at least 80 % after three hundred cycles in the ASTM C666 freeze‑thaw test, showing resistance to moisture and temperature cycling. For slab‑stabilization applications, the rapid‑set concrete patching material shall develop a seven‑day compressive strength of not less than 700 psi, indicating capacity to carry load after curing. The mix may expand only within a range of 0 % to 10 %, limiting dimensional change under moisture or temperature variations. It may bleed up to 2.5 % of its volume, and an initial set must occur between one hour and six hours, defining acceptable setting behavior for this application. During curing of partial‑depth repairs the repair area must be insulated or heated if ambient air temperature falls below 80 °F, and the patch temperature must be monitored at least hourly; a change exceeding forty degrees Fahrenheit within any one‑hour interval renders the work defective. The mechanical load transfer through dowels must be achieved by bonding the dowels along their entire embedded length with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders, and tie bars become necessary only for patches longer than 65 feet. Mechanical behavior is also governed by physical shrinkage that occurs during cement hydration; high‑early‑strength mixes are discouraged because they may cause excessive shrinkage cracking. Chemical setting reactions dictate strength development, with accelerated concrete reaching traffic‑opening strength in 4 to 7 hours while a standard mix requires 24 to 48 hours. Physical preparation of the repair area begins with removing damaged concrete without disturbing the subbase, compacting any disturbed base and ensuring proper form placement to preserve substrate integrity. Mechanical control is provided by full‑depth saw cuts no wider than ½ inch that extend through the entire thickness, together with a ¼‑inch polystyrene board bond breaker in longitudinal joints of Type A and B patches, which together govern load transfer and movement. Traffic may be opened only after the patch attains a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi, while rapid‑set concrete must achieve a target compressive strength of 1,500 psi at seven hours. Chemical mechanisms include the application of bond‑breaker lubricants to coated dowel bars and the use of anchoring material that must reach an initial set within five minutes in accordance with manufacturers’ recommendations. For Class AA accelerated patching concrete, behavior is controlled primarily by a water‑cement ratio that must not exceed 0.42 lb/lb, with a target of 0.40 lb/lb that will not fall below 0.38 lb/lb; an air content of 7.0 % ± 1.5 % at placement provides the physical entrainment needed for freeze‑thaw resistance, and any fly ash or slag may replace Portland cement only if at least 400 lb per cubic yard of Portland cement is used and total cementitious content stays within specified limits; blended hydraulic cements are limited to mortar expansions of 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at 8 weeks. Mechanical consolidation requires placement of the patching material into a clean slot that has been air‑blasted at least 100 psi and is free of oil, moisture or contaminants, followed by vibration to thoroughly consolidate the mix around the dowel bar. Dowel retrofit specifications constrain dowel position by clearances, tilt, translation and spacing limits so that the patched material experiences controlled restraint during curing. Mechanical requirements for aggregates specify durable stone or gravel free of slate texture, while reactive aggregates showing a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % may be used only with an approved cement‑supplementary combination that reduces the expansion to less than 0.1 %. Durability and deterioration

In Section 4.2 of the Pavement Policy Manual, the patch material may suffer excessive shrinkage cracking if high early strength concrete is used, so such mixes should be avoided [1]. The manual also notes that when tie bars or tie bolts are installed in patches that are less than 65 feet long, movement of these devices in partially set concrete caused by traffic can be more detrimental to the performance of short patches than any benefit from tying the patch [1]. Patching an existing rigid pavement with a flexible material compromises the long‑term life of the pavement [1]. Section 4.3 identifies durability concerns that arise when joint performance problems such as faulting or poor load transfer develop, when spalls extend beyond the mid‑slab depth, and when water, chlorides, or incompressible materials are allowed to infiltrate the repaired area, all of which must be prevented; these deterioration mechanisms are further aggravated by an unstable subgrade or non‑functional drainage, prompting the need for support restoration, slab jacking, dowel bar retrofit, or full‑depth patching [1]. Section 516 of the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications identifies cracking, spalling and delamination as primary damage processes, and when any of these defects occur the repair must be removed and replaced [2]. The same section states that durability is further reduced by break‑back of saw cuts, moisture intrusion from rain or cooling water, voids around anchored dowel bars, and inadequate joint sealing [2]. Saw cuts must not exceed ½ inch in width nor be less than full depth before traffic exposure; excavated patch areas may not remain un‑patched for more than 48 hours (including weekends or holidays); traffic on fresh saw cuts is limited to a maximum of 72 hours; anchoring material must set within five minutes; and voids around dowels must be less than 0.50 inch [2]. After curing, grout around the patches shall be placed before opening to traffic [2]. Section 545 governs durability primarily through mix proportions and aggregate stability: the water‑cement ratio may not exceed 0.42 lb/lb, the target ratio is 0.40 lb/lb and shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb; air content at placement must be 7 % ± 1.5 %; only aggregates whose 14‑day expansion lies between 0.10 % and 0.30 % may be used with an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend that reduces effective expansion to less than 0.1 %; any aggregate showing more than 0.30 % expansion is rejected; when blended hydraulic cements are employed mortar expansion is limited to 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at eight weeks [2]. Section 527 notes that damage can occur if the slot is prepared with equipment that spalls pavement or fractures surface aggregates, therefore a power‑driven saw and jackhammer must be limited; if pavement damage occurs from a 30 lb jackhammer, the damaged area must be removed and a lighter 15 lb tool used [2]. All loose and foreign material must be cleared within 24 hours before concrete placement and final cleaning completed no later than 30 minutes before placement; when cleaning by air‑blasting, at least 100 psi of oil‑, moisture‑free air shall be used to prevent contaminants that would impair bonding and durability [2]. Section 525 requires rapid‑set concrete patching material to achieve a minimum durability factor of 80 % after 300 cycles in the ASTM C666 (procedure B) freeze‑thaw test, both with and without aggregate extenders, and it also must have a minimum initial set time of 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F [2]. The material must be used within the manufacturer’s defined shelf life and temperature limits; placement is permitted only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F or greater and within the recommended installation range, and placement shall be discontinued if temperatures fall below that threshold; repairs must be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours [2]. Section 502 reiterates that applying material beyond the shelf‑life and temperature constraints can lead to loss of strength, reduced bond performance or other degradation [2]. Section 679 states that radial cracks spreading outward from grout injection holes indicate poor quality or improper methods; transverse cracks that develop between adjacent grout injection holes require replacement of the entire slab at no additional cost; pumping must be stopped when any vertical movement is noted because continued placement under movement can affect the integrity of the patched area [2]. Additional curing requirements for rapid‑set concrete patching material used in corner repairs (Section 516) specify that a white‑membrane forming curing compound shall be applied and the temperature of the repair area must be controlled so that no curing temperature change exceeds 40 °F within any one‑hour period; if ambient air falls below 80 °F, insulation or heating must be provided to maintain the required conditions, and failure to meet this temperature control renders the work defective [2].

The use of high‑early‑strength (HES) concrete should be avoided because its propensity for excessive shrinkage cracking raises the susceptibility of a concrete patch to deterioration. Conversely, precast concrete pavement is identified as an alternative that is not subject to that excessive shrinkage, thereby lowering the risk of patch failure. Opening a repaired area to traffic before the patch reaches the minimum compressive strength also increases the chance of premature deterioration; delaying traffic until the specified strength is attained reduces this risk. When a patch is expected to remain in service for more than twenty years, specifying high‑performance dowel bars further improves durability. Placing and curing the rapid‑set mix exactly as the manufacturer’s recommendations prescribe—including the use of the specified epoxy bonding compound and a white membrane‑forming curing compound—reduces susceptibility to deterioration. Leaving an excavated patch area open for more than forty‑eight hours or allowing rain onto an open patch increases the risk of damage unless protective grading, drainage, or other approved protection is provided. Adhering to the required joint‑sealing, bond‑breaker, and curing procedures helps limit moisture intrusion and thus diminishes deterioration potential. The rapid‑set concrete patching material is limited to corner repairs and must be placed while it remains within the shelf‑life and temperature limits prescribed by the manufacturer; operating outside those limits makes the material more prone to deterioration. For bonded concrete overlay applications, maintaining a water‑cement ratio at or below the maximum of 0.42 lbs/lbs, targeting 0.40 lbs/lbs and not falling below 0.38 lbs/lbs, and providing an air content of about seven percent ±1.5 percent for Class AA slip‑form paving and accelerated patching reduces susceptibility to cracking and freeze‑thaw damage. Using durable, thoroughly washed stone or gravel and prohibiting blast‑furnace‑slag aggregate limits deleterious reactions; slag cement may not exceed thirty percent of the total cementitious content, at least four hundred pounds per cubic yard of Portland cement must be present, and only aggregates whose fourteen‑day expansion is between 0.10 % and 0.30 % may be used if an approved cement‑supplementary combination can bring the effective expansion below 0.1 %; any aggregate showing more than 0.30 % expansion must be rejected. Vulnerability is increased when slot surfaces are contaminated or improper equipment damages the surrounding pavement; loose and foreign material must be removed within twenty‑four hours before placement, all slurry eliminated, and final cleaning completed within thirty minutes prior to concrete placement. Equipment that may cause spalling or fracture of surface aggregates shall not be used—a jackhammer weighing more than thirty pounds is prohibited unless pavement damage occurs, in which case a fifteen‑pound jackhammer must be employed; air‑blasting for cleaning must use a compressed air stream of at least one hundred pounds per square inch that is free of oil, moisture and other contaminants. The rapid‑set concrete patching material must achieve a minimum durability factor of eighty percent at three hundred cycles in the ASTM C666 (procedure B) test and an initial set time of fifteen minutes at seventy‑five degrees Fahrenheit ± two degrees, and when the repair depth exceeds two inches only the aggregate extenders supplied with the prepackaged material—or, if none are included, a coarse aggregate with an expansion index less than 0.10 approved in writing—should be used to maintain durability. For slab stabilization, the material must be proportioned so that its expansion is limited to zero percent–ten percent, its initial setting time occurs between one hour and six hours, bleed does not exceed two point five percent of the volume, water content yields an efflux time within ten seconds to fifteen seconds, and a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of seven hundred pounds per square inch is required; using the material only within the shelf life and temperature limitations set by the manufacturer further reduces susceptibility. Material interactions and compatibility

In full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, the patching material is applied after the bedding grout has taken initial set but before it hardens completely; the top two inches of each grout port are removed and replaced with a rapid‑setting slot‑patching grout or other approved high‑strength material that bonds to the underlying cementitious or polyurethane bedding grout and to the dowel bars, must not flow into the joint gap, and is required to achieve a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic may be opened. The patching material must be mixed, placed and cured so that it bonds securely to the existing concrete surface in accordance with manufacturers’ recommendations for rapid‑set compounds; when dowel bars are used a bond‑breaker lubricant is applied to at least one‑half of the length of Type B coated dowels at least one hour prior to placement, rendering that portion bondless while anchoring material surrounding the dowel must achieve its initial set within five minutes, and any unsuitable subbase must be removed and replaced to provide proper support. For unbonded concrete overlays, the patching material may be an asphalt, cementitious or other approved repair mix and must receive Representative approval before placement; when a geotextile interlayer is used, all surface defects larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch are filled with the patching material and compacted to a flat, tight surface so the fabric can lie evenly, whereas when an asphalt interlayer is placed the defect may be filled with the same asphalt mix only if it can be properly compacted together with the overlay, otherwise a separate repair material is required. In accelerated (Class AA) patching, the concrete constituents must act compatibly: durable stone or gravel is washed before use and blast‑furnace slag aggregate is prohibited; reactive aggregates showing 14‑day expansion of 0.10 %–0.30 % may be incorporated only with an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend that reduces the expansion to less than 0.1 %, and any aggregate exceeding 0.30 % expansion is rejected. Portland cement may be partially replaced by fly ash or slag cement, but slag cement shall not exceed 30 % of the total cementitious material and at least 400 lb per cubic yard of Portland cement must remain, keeping overall cement content within Table A limits. The mix is required to achieve a target water‑cement ratio of 0.40 lbs/lbs that will not fall below 0.38 lbs/lbs nor exceed 0.42 lbs/lbs, with an air content of 7.0 % ± 1.5 % at placement, ensuring the paste can accommodate aggregate absorption while maintaining workability. For dowel retrofit, after cleaning slot surfaces and removing all loose material within 24 hours, a final cleaning is completed no more than 30 minutes before concrete placement; bond‑breaker lubricant is applied to Type B coated dowels at least one hour before placing the patching material, epoxy bonding compound follows manufacturer recommendations, and concrete patching material is placed in the slot, vibrated to thoroughly consolidate it around the lubricated dowel bar, the 1½‑inch non‑metallic expansion caps with ¼‑inch clearance, and supporting chairs, ensuring a sound bond while preventing any movement of the dowel. During construction, the rapid‑set concrete patching material is placed on a thin layer of grout or epoxy bonding compound brushed onto all exposed surfaces; concrete must be introduced before the grout dries or while the epoxy remains tacky to bond with the existing cementitious pavement, and placement is permitted only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F or greater—if temperatures fall below this limit the placement must be discontinued and the repair protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours. Influence of production and placement

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242) Section 4.2, mix design influences durability; high early‑strength concrete should be avoided because it may cause excessive shrinkage cracking [1]. Accelerated concrete mixes are permitted when an early opening is needed and can reach traffic‑opening strength in four to seven hours, whereas standard mixes require twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours to achieve the required compressive strength [1]. Poor work quality in the placement of concrete patches is not accepted, indicating that proper placement is essential for long‑term performance [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require that mixing practices follow the design and mix requirements set out in Section 501.3(a)‑(h), establishing the intended strength and workability of the patch material [2]. For Class AA accelerated patching the target water‑cement ratio is 0.40 lb/lb, shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb, and shall never exceed 0.42 lb/lb; a minimum cement factor of 530 lb/yd³ provides sufficient binder, and the mix shall be designed to have an air content of 7.0 % with a tolerance of ±1.5 % at the time and point of placement [2]. The rapid‑set concrete patching material is proportioned as one part cement by volume combined with three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, water being added only until the efflux time is within ten to fifteen seconds, bleed must not exceed 2.5 % of the volume, expansion may range from 0 % to 10 %, and the initial setting time shall be from one hour to six hours; the material shall achieve at least 700 psi compressive strength after seven days [2]. For rapid‑set concrete used in partial‑depth repair the material shall meet a minimum initial set time of 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F [2]. Accurate volumetric mixing shall keep the drive‑shaft speed within 50 rpm of the rated speed, and ingredient tolerances are cement weight 0 to +4 %, fine and coarse aggregates ±2 %, water ±2 %, admixtures ±3 % [2]. Placement must respect timing limits: grout or rapid‑set material shall be placed within 60 minutes of mixing, successive batches shall be placed within one half hour of each other, the mixed material shall be deposited in its final position without segregation, and placement is permitted only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F or greater and within the manufacturer’s recommended installation range; placement shall be discontinued if conditions fall below that threshold [2]. After placement the material is consolidated with vibration or tamping to eliminate voids, and in dowel‑retrofit slots the concrete shall be vibrated thoroughly to consolidate around the dowel bar [2]. The final strike‑off and consolidation steps require the patch surface to be finished to match the existing cross‑section, wheel ruts and texture so that surface irregularities do not exceed 3/16 inch, and slot surfaces shall be finished to match the existing pavement profile; water may not be added to facilitate finishing [2]. Repairs shall be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours, and rapid‑set patching material shall be cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations; test cylinders shall be cured in the same manner as the product so that the cured patch develops properties consistent with the specified material [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete the field cure may be discontinued when three‑day cylinders reach at least 3,000 psi; otherwise curing continues until a seven‑day result of at least 3,000 psi and a twenty‑eight‑day strength of at least 3,500 psi is verified, and the final average wet density for each lot cannot be less than 98 % of the maximum wet density with no individual test less than 96 % of the maximum wet density [2].

Selection, specification, and improvement

The initial step in selecting a concrete patching material is to evaluate the severity of pavement distress; fully deteriorated slabs that reach the slab bottom require full‑depth concrete patching and removal of all cracked or crushed joints, whereas partial‑depth repairs are limited to spalling confined to the top half of the slab depth without severe joint loss. The length of the repair zone also influences the choice: if a continuous stretch of approximately ±500 feet contains intermittent patches leaving ten feet or less of original pavement between them, the entire section should be removed and reconstructed for its full length, and when more than 500 feet of all lanes are to be replaced a design analysis must decide whether a thicker slab depth is needed. Traffic‑opening schedules dictate the concrete type; high early strength (HES) concrete shall be avoided because of its potential for excessive shrinkage cracking, accelerated concrete can achieve opening to traffic strength in four to seven hours when early opening is desired, and standard pavement concrete can achieve opening in twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours. When work‑zone safety or minimum‑time‑to‑traffic concerns outweigh the higher cost, precast concrete paving systems may be considered. Load‑transfer design mandates dowelling all patch joints with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders bonded along the entire embedded length, specifying new pavement joint types and spacing—thirty‑foot joint spacing for JRCP or fifteen‑foot joint spacing for JPCP—and longitudinal tie bars or bolts are required only in concrete patches more than sixty‑five feet long; for pavements expected to remain in service more than twenty years, the use of high‑performance dowel bars should be considered. For dowel‑slot patching required by Section 529 the material shall be an accepted rapid‑setting type that reaches a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is permitted and must be compatible with the slot geometry while being placed so that it does not flow into the joint gap. In unbonded concrete overlays (Section 548) the selection depends on defect size and interlayer type; surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch when a geotextile interlayer is used must be filled with an asphalt‑based, cementitious or otherwise approved repair mix, whereas shallow defects under an asphalt interlayer may be filled with the interlayer material itself only if it can be properly compacted; otherwise a repair mix is required, and Representative approval is required before overlay placement. For bonded concrete overlays (Section 545) the patching mix shall contain at least 530 lb of cement per cubic yard, have a water‑cement ratio not to exceed 0.42 (target 0.40 but shall not fall below 0.38), achieve an air content of 7 % ± 1.5 %, use only washed durable stone or gravel aggregates with slag aggregate prohibited, may include supplementary cementitious material provided that at least 400 lb/yd of Portland cement remains and slag cement does not exceed thirty percent of the total cementitious content, limit reactive‑aggregate expansion to between 0.10 % and 0.30 % unless an approved blend reduces it below 0.1 %, keep mortar expansion below 0.02 % at fourteen days and 0.06 % at eight weeks for Type IS or IP hydraulic cement blends, and field verification of the water‑cement ratio shall be performed using batch tickets and AASHTO T 318‑02. For partial‑depth repairs (Section 525) Class AA cement concrete patches shall use coarse aggregate Type A No. 8 and limit the water‑cement ratio to 0.42; when an accelerated mix is specified it shall reach a compressive strength of at least 1,500 psi after seven hours as verified by PTM No. 604, while rapid‑set concrete patching material shall be sourced from a Bulletin 15 manufacturer, conform to ASTM C928, possess a durability factor not less than eighty percent after three hundred freeze–thaw cycles (ASTM C666 Procedure B), and have an initial set time of at least fifteen minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F. Repairs deeper than two inches shall require aggregate extenders prescribed by the manufacturer or, if not supplied, a Bulletin 14‑approved No. 8 coarse aggregate with an expansion index less than 0.10 and written approval; grout mixes shall be placed within sixty minutes of mixing, epoxy bonding compounds shall meet ASTM C881 Type II or V Grade 2 and be installed according to the manufacturer’s instructions while remaining within shelf life and pot‑life temperature constraints. In slab stabilization (Section 679) the rapid‑set product shall come from a Bulletin 15 listed manufacturer, be used within its shelf‑life and temperature limits, be proportioned as one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume with any accepted admixtures, have water content adjusted so that efflux time falls between ten and fifteen seconds (ASTM C939), exhibit expansion of zero to ten percent (ASTM C940), possess an initial set time of one to six hours (AASHTO T 131), bleed no more than two point five percent of volume, achieve a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of seven hundred psi based on the average of five test cylinders, and independent laboratory results for compressive strength at one, three and seven days, flowability, shrinkage and expansion shall be submitted with the mix design; after placement patches shall be struck flush with the surrounding pavement surface. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502) the rapid‑set patching material shall follow the project’s overall mix design, have moisture content optimized according to AASHTO T 180 Method D, meet all ingredient tolerances set out in Section 704.1(e), be placed while within the manufacturer’s shelf‑life and temperature limits, and mixing operations shall not exceed the rated dry‑concrete volume capacity of the mixer nor allow hardened concrete to accumulate on mixer surfaces. Placement of any rapid‑set concrete patching material under the dowel retrofit provisions (Section 527) shall be performed while the material remains within its manufacturer‑defined shelf life and temperature range prescribed by the manufacturer. In general concrete pavement patching (Section 516) selection follows the baseline requirements of Section 501.2, a subbase must be provided as specified in Section 350.2, rapid‑set concrete patching material may be used only for corner repairs and shall be applied within the shelf‑life and temperature limits defined by the manufacturer. Repair of manufacturing defects or minor damage to precast concrete products (Section 714) shall use a material that appears on the list in Bulletin 15 unless specifically approved by the CSME; when a repair falls outside Publication 145 the project must submit the repair to the CSME for evaluation and disposition. When a partial‑depth repair is opened to traffic (Section 525) the specification shall select one of three approved materials—Class AA Cement Concrete, Modified, Class AA Cement Concrete, Accelerated, or Rapid‑Set Concrete Patching Material—as defined in Section 516.3(q); the choice is guided by whether a modified mix with dimensional constraints is required, an accelerated mix is preferred, or a rapid‑set product is needed for faster service restoration. Ambient and surface temperature conditions also govern selection between Class AA cement concrete (modified or accelerated) and rapid‑set material for partial‑depth repairs; placement is permitted only when air and surface temperatures are at least forty degrees Fahrenheit and rising, work must stop if temperatures fall below that level or outside the manufacturer’s recommended range, rapid‑set material must stay within its specified installation temperature range, all repairs must be protected from freezing for a minimum twenty‑four hours after placement, and the anticipated return‑to‑service time influences whether a rapid‑set product is chosen. The policy manual advises that high early strength concrete should be avoided because of its potential for excessive shrinkage cracking, and instead recommends the use of accelerated concrete capable of reaching required compressive strength within four to seven hours or the employment of precast concrete pavement slabs manufactured to a high‑strength Class AA mix. When rapid reopening of traffic is essential, PCPS (precast concrete pavement units) may replace accelerated concrete, providing durable support while reducing work‑zone impacts. Load transfer performance can be enhanced by dowelling all patch joints with dowels that are bonded along their entire embedded length using epoxy, polyester binders, or vinylester binders, and high‑performance dowel bars should be considered for repaired sections expected to remain in service for more than twenty years. In full‑depth precast slab repairs a sacrificial overlay one‑quarter inch thicker than the nominal design thickness is cast, and rapid‑setting dowel‑slot grout or cementitious or polyurethane undersealing grout must attain a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic may be allowed. All dowel bars and the vertical edges of the slab are required to receive a bond‑breaker coating prior to setting any concrete, and surface tolerances are controlled by grinding whenever depressions or high points exceed three‑sixteenths inch while voids around dowels must be less than 0.50 inch in radius. A quality‑control plan must be submitted that includes trial batch test data showing a minimum concrete compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a flexural strength of 650 psi, together with sampling and cylinder testing to verify that traffic may be opened only after the patch reaches at least 1,000 psi. Rapid‑set concrete patching material is required to be mixed and placed in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations, limited to corner repairs, and applied within the shelf‑life and temperature limits specified by the manufacturer. If ambient temperature falls below eighty degrees Fahrenheit during curing, insulation or heating must be provided and the curing temperature shall be monitored at least hourly to ensure that any change does not exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit in one hour. Prior to placing repair concrete a thin layer of grout or epoxy bonding compound shall be applied to all exposed surfaces to enhance bond strength. For Class AA accelerated patching concrete, Portland cement may be partially replaced with fly ash or slag cement provided that at least four hundred pounds per cubic yard of Portland cement remains and total cementitious content stays within the limits of Table A, with slag cement limited to no more than thirty percent of the cementitious blend. The target water‑cement ratio for this mix shall be 0.40 and shall not fall below 0.38, air content at placement shall be seven percent ±1.5 percent, and reactive aggregates showing fourteen‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % may be used only with an approved cement‑supplementary material combination that keeps expansion below 0.1 %. The mix may also incorporate a multiphase wetting agent, an expansive agent, or an accelerator, and supplementary cementitious material may be added at three parts per one part cement by volume; the resulting mixture shall achieve an efflux time of ten to fifteen seconds, expansion between zero and ten percent, bleed no more than two point five percent of volume, and a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi based on the average of five cylinders. When dowel retrofit work is performed, rapid‑set concrete patching material must be used within the manufacturer’s shelf‑life and temperature limits, compatible curing compounds shall be applied, and any accelerating admixture incorporated shall contain no chlorides; a bond‑breaker lubricant with a flash point of at least one hundred forty‑nine degrees Fahrenheit shall be applied to protect steel components during installation. Preformed cellular polystyrene (ASTM C578) may be employed as a lightweight filler in dowel retrofit applications, and for crack or joint widths greater than one quarter inch an expansion joint material may be used in lieu of the polystyrene with thickness matching the opening. In slab stabilization, drill holes are filled full depth with rapid‑set non‑shrink concrete patching material and finished flush with the surrounding pavement, and performance is monitored through a field quality‑control plan that requires re‑grouting and retesting any slab that deflects 0.02 inch or more after grouting. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements, acceptance requires each lot’s average density to be not less than ninety‑eight percent of the maximum wet density with no individual test below ninety‑six percent, and compressive strength must reach at least three thousand psi on a three‑day or seven‑day cylinder before cure termination and at least three thousand five hundred psi after twenty‑eight days; when these criteria are not met, deficient core holes shall be filled with rapid‑set concrete patching material. Volumetric mixed cement concrete permits the use of suitable admixtures provided their dosage is kept within plus or minus three percent by weight or volume, and mandatory quality‑control testing includes slump, air content, unit weight, uniformity, output meter calibration, and a detailed batcher‑mixer slip for each class before placement; equipment calibration shall be performed annually and whenever material sources change, and auger mixing speed shall be maintained within fifty rpm of the set point. In precast concrete product fabrication, an air‑entraining additive may not exceed fifteen percent by mass of the total cementitious factor, work‑area air temperatures must remain above forty degrees Fahrenheit during production, segregation and addition of water for finishing are prohibited, consolidation shall be achieved with approved vibration, and a minimum of four concrete test cylinders per lot shall be molded, cured in the same manner as the product, tested according to PTM No. 604, and recorded to establish a quality‑control loop. As an alternative to using concrete patching material for badly spalled joints or interior spalls, the policy manual recommends cleaning and filling such areas with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material (or equivalent) before overlay, and all joints and cracks should be cleaned and sealed with rubberized joint sealing material prior to any patching to prevent water, chloride, and incompressible‑material infiltration. Performance evaluation and implications

Laboratory evaluation of concrete patching material is performed through various test programs depending on the specification section. For full‑depth precast/prestressed slabs, trial‑batch test data must demonstrate that each concrete mix will meet the required compressive and flexural strengths; the Representative may request additional flexural‑strength results at any time, and dowel and bedding grouts are not allowed to carry traffic until they reach a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi. The slabs themselves must have a minimum concrete compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a minimum concrete flexural strength of 650 psi. For the concrete pavement patching work described in Section 516, laboratory evidence consists of compressive‑strength testing of cured cylinders, with traffic permitted only after the concrete attains at least 1,000 psi as measured per PTM No. 604. Field verification includes a surface‑tolerance check using a 12‑foot straightedge that must not exceed 3/16 inch deviation and a dowel‑bar anchoring verification in which three random cores are taken; acceptance is achieved when the summation of voids around each dowel is less than 0.50 inch on any one core. No long‑term performance monitoring is prescribed. For Class AA accelerated patching concrete, acceptance is judged by compliance with mix limits and a field verification of its water‑cement ratio. The mix must have a cement factor not less than 530 lb/yd³ and not greater than 600 lb/yd³; the water‑cement ratio may not be greater than 0.42 lb/lb, the target ratio shall be 0.40 lb/lb and shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb; air content at placement is required to be 7.0 % ± 1.5 %. Field evidence is obtained by checking batch tickets for cementitious content and applying AASHTO T 318‑02 to determine the water content of the fresh mix. For rapid‑set concrete patching material used in partial‑depth repair, laboratory criteria require a durability factor of at least 80 % after 300 cycles in ASTM C666 (procedure B) both with and without aggregate extenders, an initial set time no slower than 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F as measured by AASHTO T 131, and for the accelerated Class AA option a target compressive strength of 1,500 psi at 7 hours when tested according to PTM No. 604. No separate field or long‑term performance tests are prescribed. For rapid‑set concrete patching material in slab stabilization, independent laboratory testing submitted with the mix design must document 1‑, 3‑ and 7‑day compressive strengths, flowability, shrinkage and expansion results, and the time of initial set; the average of five test cylinders must achieve a minimum 7‑day compressive strength of 700 psi. No field or long‑term performance evidence is stipulated. For precast concrete products, evaluation relies on laboratory testing of concrete specimens; each production lot must have at least four test cylinders molded according to PTM No. 631, cured in the same manner as the product, and tested in accordance with PTM No. 604, with results recorded and provided to the Representative. Additional cylinders may be required if directed, but no specific field or long‑term performance evidence is described. [2]

Load transfer at transverse joints is essential; dowels must be bonded along their entire embedded length so that the joint can carry traffic loads. Designers avoid high early strength concrete because of its potential for excessive shrinkage cracking. The specified mix may be a standard concrete that reaches opening‑to‑traffic strength in 24 to 48 hours, or an accelerated concrete that can reach that strength in 4 to 7 hours. Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only when the patch length exceeds 65 feet, and for shorter patches their movement may be more detrimental than beneficial. When dowel detailing, joint spacing, and high‑performance dowel bar selection follow the guidelines for service lives over 20 years, the repaired slab provides uniform support, maintains ride quality, and reduces future maintenance. In full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slabs, rapid‑setting patching material placed in dowel slots and bedding chambers must attain a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is permitted, because this strength governs load transfer and prevents settlement that could create edge differentials greater than 1/4 inch; joint width shall be at least 1/4 inch and shall not exceed 1/2 inch transverse. Excavated patch areas must be filled within 48 hours, and after full‑depth saw cutting traffic may use the cut area for no more than 72 hours; the rapid‑set concrete patching material must be cured until it reaches at least 1,000 psi compressive strength before being opened to traffic. For partial‑depth repairs the rapid‑set material shall achieve a durability factor of at least 80 % after 300 cycles, an initial set time of no less than 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F, and a target compressive strength of 1,500 psi at seven hours; the mix is limited to a maximum water‑cement ratio of 0.42. In slab stabilization applications the rapid‑set material shall have a 7‑day compressive strength of at least 700 psi, an expansion ranging from 0 % to 10 %, and bleed no more than 2.5 % of its volume; it must set within 1 to 6 hours, and the proportion of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material together with any approved admixtures controls shrinkage/expansion behavior, directly influencing long‑term durability and future maintenance needs. The water‑cement ratio for AA accelerated patching is targeted at 0.40 and shall not fall below 0.38 nor exceed 0.42; air content at placement shall be 7 % ± 1.5 %; durable stone or gravel aggregates free of slate texture are required to prevent deleterious expansion, Portland cement must be retained at a minimum of 400 lb/yd³, slag cement may not exceed 30 % of total cementitious material, and reactive‑aggregate expansion shall be limited to 0.10–0.30 % only when mitigated below 0.1 %. When a geotextile interlayer is used, every surface defect larger than 4 sq in and deeper than 1 inch must be filled and compacted to a flat, tight surface with patching material to provide uniform support for the overlay and help prevent premature cracking or differential settlement. When an asphalt interlayer is employed, shallow defects may be filled with the interlayer material provided it can be properly compacted along with the interlayer; otherwise the defects must be filled with repair material before placement of the interlayer. For dowel retrofits the concrete patching material must be placed promptly after slot cleaning, with final cleaning required within 30 minutes before concrete placement; the material must be vibrated to thoroughly consolidate it in the slot and around the dowel bar so that the dowel does not move from its specified position, and the surface must be finished to match the existing pavement profile, including any wheel ruts. In unbonded concrete overlay projects the patching material used for shallow repairs shall consist of an asphalt, cementitious or similarly approved pavement repair mix and shall be accepted by the Representative before any overlay is placed, ensuring appropriate bonding with the subsequent concrete or interlayer. The volumetric mixing plant must produce the mix within tight proportion tolerances—cement weight 0 to +4 %, fine aggregate weight ±2 %, coarse aggregate weight ±2 %, admixtures weight or volume ±3 %, water weight or volume ±2 %—so that the resulting patch material has a uniform consistency and resists segregation, directly supporting pavement performance and durability. During mixing the auger drive shaft speed shall be maintained within 50 rpm of the operating speed and the interval between successive batches shall not exceed ½ hour; required slump, air content, unit weight and uniformity tests verify that the mixed concrete meets these criteria before placement. If ambient air temperature drops below 80 °F, insulation or heating shall be provided for repair areas, curing temperature shall be monitored at least hourly and shall not change by more than 40 °F within any one‑hour period; any exceedance is considered defective. Proper application of the white membrane‑forming curing compound for both Modified and Accelerated mixes directly influences the long‑term integrity of the repair and reduces future maintenance needs, and rapid‑set concrete patching material shall be cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete patching material used to fill drill holes full depth restores structural continuity and prevents post‑repair movement; striking the patches flush with the surrounding surface preserves ride quality and protects against premature deterioration, thereby supporting long‑term pavement performance and durability while meeting the construction requirement that repaired areas be ready for traffic after the specified curing period. Section 525.2(c) limits the use of rapid‑set concrete patching material to corner repairs only, and placement must occur within the shelf life and temperature limitations set by the manufacturer; installers must coordinate timing and ambient conditions, which directly influences construction scheduling and long‑term performance of the repaired area.
