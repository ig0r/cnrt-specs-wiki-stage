Patching Material

Part of: Concrete Patching is-subtopic-of
Patching Material is a topic under Concrete Patching.
concrete-patching-material
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Patching material is a newly placed Portland‑cement concrete—either a conventional or an accelerated rapid‑setting mix—used to replace removed, deteriorated slab or joint sections in rigid pavements, thereby reestablishing the structural continuity and load‑transfer capability of the pavement [1][2]. It is placed as a full‑depth fill that can be dowelled into existing slabs or backfilled into joints, creating a uniform base capable of supporting traffic and any subsequent overlay while bonding to the surrounding concrete [1][2]. The mix conforms to Class AA cement concrete specifications (minimum cement factor 530 lb/yd³, water‑cement ratio ≤0.42 with a target of 0.40, air content 7 % ± 1.5 %) and, when an accelerated formulation is used, can achieve opening‑to‑traffic strength within four to seven hours; rapid‑set versions also must meet a durability factor of at least 80 % after 300 freeze‑thaw cycles, an initial set of no less than fifteen minutes at 75 °F, and minimum compressive strengths (1,000 psi for general patches, 2,500 psi for dowel‑slot grout) before traffic is permitted [1][2].

Material purpose and applicability

In the Pavement Policy Manual, section 4.2 CONCRETE PAVEMENT PATCHING defines concrete patching material as a newly placed Portland‑cement concrete—typically a standard or accelerated rapid‑setting mix—that is used to replace removed, deteriorated slab or joint areas in a rigid pavement; its primary function is to restore the structural continuity and load‑transfer capability of the pavement by providing a full‑depth concrete fill that can be dowelled into existing slabs, thereby creating a uniform base capable of supporting traffic and any subsequent overlay, and when an accelerated mix is used it may achieve opening‑to‑traffic strength in 4 to 7 hours [1]. The same manual, in section 4.3 JOINTS, describes the material as the concrete mix employed for full‑depth repairs of pavement joints or cracks, such as when a joint is replaced or when dowel bar retrofit slots are backfilled, and it serves to restore structural continuity and load‑transfer capability by filling the repaired area with a durable, cured concrete that bonds to the existing slab [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications define patching material as a rapid‑setting grout or cementitious mix used for repairing concrete pavements. In Section 516 the rapid‑set concrete patching material is mixed and placed to replace sections removed because of cracks, spalls or other distress; its function is to restore a continuous riding surface and reestablish load transfer across the repaired joint, and it may be used for corner repairs while observing the manufacturer’s shelf‑life and temperature limits [2]. Section 529 specifies that the patching material is the rapid‑setting grout, cementitious or other accepted high‑strength mix placed to fill dowel slots and joint gaps after a slab has been set; its function is to restore continuity of load transfer at transverse joints by sealing the dowel slots in accordance with the dowel retrofit method, and it must achieve a compressive strength of at least 2,500 psi before traffic may be opened [2]. Section 545 requires Class AA cement concrete formulated for accelerated patching of rigid pavements to meet compositional limits of a minimum cement factor of 530 lb/yd³, a maximum water‑cement ratio of 0.42 lb/lb with a target of 0.40 lb/lb that shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb, and an air content of 7 % ± 1.5 % at placement; its function is to fill cracks, potholes or other surface distress, providing a durable, compatible repair that restores structural continuity and extends service life [2]. In Section 527 the concrete placed into a dowel retrofit slot after the bar and temporary joint have been positioned is described as patching material; it fills the cut slot, is vibrated to thoroughly consolidate around the dowel bar, and is finished to match the existing pavement profile, thereby restoring structural continuity and load transfer across the repaired joint [2]. Section 525 designates Rapid‑Set Concrete Patching Material from a Bulletin 15 manufacturer that complies with ASTM C928 for partial‑depth repairs; it must have a minimum durability factor of 80 % at 300 cycles when tested according to ASTM C666 (procedure B) with and without aggregate extenders, an initial set time no less than 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F as verified by AASHTO T131, and when the repair depth exceeds 2 inches aggregate extenders are required; its function is to fill cracks, potholes and other localized distress by providing a quick‑setting, durable repair that restores structural continuity of the slab [2]. Section 548 allows patching material to be an asphalt, cementitious or similar approved repair mix used to fill surface defects before an unbonded concrete overlay is placed; it creates a flat, tight surface that restores uniform support for the pavement structure, and when a geotextile interlayer is employed it must be used to fill all surface defects larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch [2]. Section 679 defines Rapid Set Concrete Patching Materials as fast‑setting cementitious grout supplied by Bulletin 15 manufacturers, mixed in a proportion of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material with water adjusted for an efflux time of 10–15 seconds and may contain approved admixtures; the mix must achieve an expansion between 0 % and 10 %, an initial setting time of 1 to 6 hours, bleed no more than 2.5 % of its volume, and a minimum 7‑day compressive strength of 700 psi, and it is used to fill and stabilize cracks or other distress, thereby restoring slab integrity and extending pavement service life [2]. Section 525.3(f) further describes the rapid‑set concrete patching material as a specially formulated cementitious mix for partial‑depth repairs that fills cracks, potholes or other localized distress to restore structural continuity of the slab; it may be placed only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F or greater and within the manufacturer’s recommended installation temperature range, must be protected from freezing for at least 24 hours, is consolidated by vibration or tamping, worked into saw cuts beyond the corners of the damaged area, struck‑off and shaped to match the surrounding pavement including wheel ruts, with expansion joint material and preformed cellular polystyrene forms used to keep the new concrete out of existing joints [2]. Section 502.3(m) specifies that rapid‑set concrete patching material is used to fill and consolidate core holes created during acceptance testing of roller‑compacted concrete pavements; when density or compressive‑strength requirements are not met, the deficient area may be repaired by placing this material instead of additional RCC, thereby restoring the pavement surface and allowing the lot to be considered acceptable [2]. Section 110.10 defines patching material as an approved concrete‑based product listed in Bulletin 15 or conforming to the approved mix design that is used to fill the voids left after coring; its function is to restore the integrity of the concrete surface by sealing those holes and maintaining a continuous pavement slab [2]. Section 679.4 treats patching material as cement grout supplied in bags, optionally with an accelerator, that fills drilled holes during slab stabilization; by filling the holes the grout stabilizes the concrete slab and addresses radial cracking as required [2]. Finally, Section 714.7 limits the use of patching material to fixing manufacturing defects and minor damage in pavement surfaces; it must be applied according to Publication 145 and must be the material listed in Bulletin 15 or otherwise approved by the CSME, thereby restoring surface continuity [2].

Composition and characteristics

Concrete patching material is essentially Portland‑cement concrete that must meet the requirements of Publication 408 Section 704 and is defined primarily as a Class AA cement concrete mix or, where specified, a rapid‑set cementitious product listed in Bulletin 15 [1][2]. For the Class AA mix the specification requires a minimum cement factor of 530 lb per cubic yard and may not exceed 600 lb per cubic yard, with at least 400 lb per cubic yard of Portland cement; fly ash or slag cement may substitute provided the total cementitious content remains within the limits of Table A and slag cement does not exceed thirty percent of the total [2]. The aggregate component shall consist of durable washed stone or gravel of Type A No.57, No.67, or No.8, with blast‑furnace‑slag aggregate prohibited; for partial‑depth repairs a Type A No.8 coarse aggregate may be used and when repair depths exceed two inches approved aggregate extenders may be added or a No.8 coarse aggregate with an expansion index less than 0.10 may be incorporated if the manufacturer approves it in writing [2]. The water‑cement ratio must not exceed 0.42, the target is 0.40 and it shall never fall below 0.38; air content for slip‑form paving and accelerated patching shall be seven percent ±1.5 percent [2]. Reactive aggregates are permitted only when their fourteen‑day expansion lies between 0.10 percent and 0.30 percent and the selected cement‑supplementary blend reduces that expansion to less than 0.1 percent, while blended hydraulic cements Type IS or IP must limit mortar expansion to 0.02 percent at fourteen days and 0.06 percent at eight weeks [2]. When a rapid‑set concrete patching material is specified the mix shall consist of one part cement by volume combined with three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, water adjusted so that the efflux time falls within ten to fifteen seconds, and any approved admixtures such as a multiphase wetting agent, expansive agent or accelerator may be used [2]. The resulting grout must achieve an expansion ranging from zero to ten percent, set initially between one hour and six hours, bleed no more than 2.5 percent of its volume, and develop a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi based on the average of five test cylinders [2]. Physical performance requirements for the rapid‑set product include a durability factor of at least eighty percent after three hundred cycles in an ASTM C666 (procedure B) freeze‑thaw test and an initial set time of not less than fifteen minutes at 75 °F ±2 °F as measured by AASHTO T 131 [2]. The mixture may be supplied as cast‑in‑place concrete or as precast concrete paving systems, the latter being a high‑strength Class AA cement concrete that exceeds the normal PCC specifications [1]. When a rapid‑setting (accelerated) product is specified it is formulated to reach the minimum compressive strength within four to seven hours after placement, whereas standard mixes typically require twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours [1]. Dowels are embedded through the full depth of the patch and bonded along their entire length with anchoring agents such as epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders to provide load transfer, and tie bars or bolts are required for patches more than sixty‑five feet long [1]. High early‑strength concrete is discouraged because it can cause excessive shrinkage cracking [1]. All rapid‑set patching materials shall be supplied by a manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15, used within the prescribed shelf‑life and temperature limits, and may be employed to fill drilled grout injection holes full‑depth with a non‑shrink concrete patching material that is struck flush with the surrounding pavement surface [2]. The overall composition of any cementitious patching material also includes water, admixtures, and concrete curing material as defined in Sections 720.1, 711.3, and 711.1–711.2, and the aggregate must be well‑graded according to Table A of Section 703 [2]. The mix design shall be consistent for the entire project, with moisture content optimized in accordance with AASHTO T180 Method D, and all produced material must meet the tolerances specified in Section 704.1(e) [2].

The Pavement Policy Manual advises that using a high‑early‑strength concrete should be avoided because its composition tends to cause excessive shrinkage cracking. In contrast, an accelerated concrete mix formulated for rapid strength gain can permit traffic opening after only 4 to 7 hours, whereas standard pavement concrete with optimized aggregate gradation typically requires 24 to 48 hours to reach the same strength. Selecting precast concrete paving systems introduces a high‑strength, durable mixture that exceeds the normal PCC requirements (Class AA cement concrete) but also raises cost and handling considerations. In the bonded‑concrete overlay specification, replacing part of the Portland cement with fly ash or slag cement is permitted only when at least 400 lb per cubic yard of Portland cement remains and the total cementitious content stays within the Table A bounds, while slag cement may not exceed 30 % of the combined cementitious material. The water‑cement ratio must be kept at a target of 0.40 lb/lb and shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb, with an absolute maximum of 0.42 lb/lb, which directly controls strength and durability. The mix for slip‑form paving and accelerated patching must achieve an air content of 7.0 % ± 1.5 %. Only durable stone or gravel that is thoroughly washed may be used; blast‑furnace slag aggregate is prohibited, and any reactive aggregate showing a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % must be paired with an approved cement‑supplementary blend to reduce the effective expansion below 0.1 %, whereas aggregates expanding more than 0.30 % are rejected. The specification limits the water‑cement ratio to 0.42 and requires a mix design that achieves at least 1,500 psi compressive strength after seven hours; altering proportioning beyond this limit would compromise the required strength. Rapid‑set concrete patching material must attain a durability factor of at least 80 % after 300 freeze–thaw cycles and an initial set time no less than 15 minutes at 75°F ± 2°F, so changes in composition such as using different aggregate extenders or coarse aggregate with an expansion index under 0.10 affect those performance thresholds. Compliance with the manufacturer’s shelf‑life, temperature limits and recommended extender usage for repairs deeper than two inches is also required to maintain the specified properties. Any change in the type or supplier of cement, supplementary cementitious material, water, admixtures or rapid‑set product requires submission of a new mix design because the source change can alter measured compressive strength, flowability, shrinkage/expansion and set time. The prescribed proportion is one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume together with an amount of water that yields a 10‑ to 15‑second efflux, which controls workability, and the mix must be limited to an expansion of 0 %–10%, an initial setting time of 1 hour to 6 hours and bleed no more than 2.5 % of its volume, with a minimum 7‑day compressive strength of 700 psi required. The roller‑compacted concrete mixture for patching must follow a single mix design throughout a project, and any alteration in cement type, supplementary cementitious material, aggregate source, water content or admixture proportion triggers the need for a new mix design to verify that the resulting concrete still satisfies the tolerances set out in Section 704.1(e). For rapid‑set concrete patching material the specification further limits use to within the manufacturer‑defined shelf life and temperature range, indicating that deviations from those processing conditions could compromise its intended properties. The composition of the patching material determines distinct preparatory and placement requirements: a Modified mix requires a grout or epoxy bonding compound applied while still tacky; an Accelerated mix follows the same bonding step but must be cured with a white membrane forming curing compound and may need insulation or heating if ambient temperature drops below 80 °F, and any curing temperature change greater than 40 °F in one hour renders the repair defective. Rapid‑Set concrete patching material is limited to placement when ambient air and surface temperatures are at least 40 °F, must be discontinued if conditions fall outside the manufacturer’s recommended installation range, and must be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours after placement. Key material properties

For concrete pavement patching the early‑age compressive strength is a key mechanical property because accelerated concrete must reach the minimum concrete compressive strength within 4 to 7 hours after placement as required by Publication 408 Section 501 [1]. Standard Portland cement concrete with optimized aggregate gradation can develop sufficient opening‑to‑traffic strength in 24 to 48 hours [1]. The physical tendency for shrinkage is also critical, so high early strength (HES) concrete should be avoided due to its potential for excessive shrinkage cracking [1]. Section 516—Concrete Pavement Patching specifies that the repaired area must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi before it may be opened to traffic [2]. Section 529—Full Depth Precast/Prestressed Concrete Pavement Slabs requires a minimum concrete compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a minimum flexural strength of 650 psi, and any bedding or dowel grout must attain at least 2,500 psi compressive strength before traffic may be permitted [2]. The mix design for full‑depth precast panels shall account for the thermal‑expansion coefficient appropriate to the site’s average daily temperatures so that panel dimensions and joint locations accommodate expansion and contraction, with a sacrificial thickness of 1/4 inch provided for surface adjustments without compromising structural integrity [2]. Section 525—Concrete Pavement Partial‑Depth Repair defines rapid‑set concrete patching material requirements that the water‑cement ratio must not exceed 0.42, the mix shall have a minimum target compressive strength of 1,500 psi at seven hours, and durability is required to reach a minimum durability factor of 80 % after 300 cycles in ASTM C666 (procedure B) testing with and without aggregate extenders [2]. The same section also mandates a minimum initial set time of 15 minutes measured at 75 °F ± 2 °F, and when repair depths exceed two inches any aggregate extender used must have an expansion index less than 0.10 to limit dimensional change [2]. Section 679—Slab Stabilization evaluates rapid‑set concrete patching material by requiring a seven‑day compressive strength of at least 700 psi, expansion between 0 % and 10 %, an initial setting time from one hour to six hours, bleeding may not exceed 2.5 % of its volume, and flowability is controlled so that the time of efflux falls in the ten‑second to fifteen‑second range [2]. Section 545—Bonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfaced Pavements mandates that the cement factor shall not be less than 530 lb/yd³ for AA Slip Form Paving, Paving, and Accelerated Patching and shall not exceed 600 lb/yd³ for AA Slip Form Paving and Paving, that the maximum water‑cement ratio shall be 0.42 lb/lb with the target kept at 0.40 lb/lb and never below 0.38 lb/lb for all Class AA concrete, and that the air content shall be 7.0 % ± 1.5 % measured at placement [2]. The same section requires durable, thoroughly washed stone or gravel aggregates with no slate texture, prohibits blast‑furnace slag aggregate, limits supplementary cementitious material so that at least 400 lb/yd³ of Portland cement remains and total cementitious content stays within Table A, restricts slag cement to no more than 30 % by weight, and limits aggregate reactivity to a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % (which can be mitigated to less than 0.1 %), rejecting any aggregate showing greater than 0.30 % expansion [2]. Blended hydraulic cements Type IS or IP are restricted to mortar expansions of no more than 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at eight weeks [2]. Section 714—Precast Concrete Products specifies that the air‑entraining admixture (AMF) must not exceed 15 % by mass of the total cementitious factor, and concrete must be placed without segregation with water shall not be added to facilitate finishing [2]. Section 527 and Section 516 require that rapid‑set concrete patching material be placed while it is still within its shelf life and only at temperatures that fall inside the limits specified by the manufacturer; air temperatures within the work area must remain above 40 °F for production, ensuring proper curing and thermal stability [2]. Section 525—Construction adds thermal sensitivity requirements that placement is allowed only when ambient air and surface temperatures are at least 40 °F and must stop if they fall below that level, the repaired area must be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours, and during curing the temperature must be kept stable with any change not exceeding 40 °F within one hour; insulation or heating is required if ambient air drops below 80 °F [2]. Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay permits the patching material to be asphalt‑based, cementitious, or a comparable approved mix, and mandates that the temperature of the underlying fabric or pavement may not exceed 120 °F at the time of placement [2]. Section 502—Roller Compacted Concrete Pavements ties performance to moisture control by requiring optimization of moisture content according to AASHTO T 180 Method D, adherence to constituent proportion tolerances set out in Section 704.1(e) to ensure consistent chemical makeup, and use within the manufacturer’s specified shelf life and temperature limits because exceeding those conditions can impair setting behavior [2]. Mechanical consolidation through vibration or tamping is required to achieve proper density of rapid‑set concrete patching material [2].

The properties of concrete patching material are primarily gauged by the minimum compressive strength required, as specified in Publication 408 Section 501, and by the time needed for the mix to attain that strength. Accelerated mixes may achieve traffic‑opening strength in four to seven hours, whereas standard pavement concrete generally requires twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours to reach comparable strength. The material is further classified according to its functional application—full‑depth concrete pavement restoration (CPR), patching beneath an asphalt overlay, or patching beneath an unbonded concrete overlay—and by mix category, ranging from conventional portland cement concrete that meets Publication 408 Section 704 specifications to high early‑strength or accelerated concrete and precast concrete paving systems that exceed Class AA cement‑concrete requirements. [1] In Section 529 the adequacy of patching material is confirmed by laboratory strength tests, requiring mix designs for precast slabs to demonstrate a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a minimum flexural strength of 650 psi; rapid‑setting grout used to fill dowel slots or surface patches must achieve at least 2,500 psi before traffic is permitted, with acceptance based on measured compressive (and where required flexural) strengths from trial batch test data. [2] Section 516 verifies performance through a quality‑control plan that requires sampling and testing before opening to traffic; concrete cylinders are tested for the minimum required compressive strength of 1,000 psi, dowel bar anchoring is accepted only when voids around the perimeter total less than 0.50 inch on any core, and surface flatness is checked with a 12‑foot straightedge, requiring any high points or depressions greater than 3/16 inch to be ground out before traffic can resume. [2] Section 525 classifies rapid‑set concrete patching material by compliance with ASTM C928 and quantifies key performance properties through prescribed test methods; the material must have a minimum durability factor of 80 % at 300 cycles when tested according to ASTM C666 (procedure B) with and without aggregate extenders, it requires a minimum initial set time of 15 minutes at 75°F ± 2°F as measured by AASHTO T 131, the mix is limited to a maximum water‑cement ratio of 0.42, and the design must achieve a minimum target compressive strength of 1,500 psi at seven hours when tested according to PTM No. 604. Behavior and mechanisms

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), section 4.2, concrete patch material must maintain load transfer across transverse joints, which is achieved by fully bonded dowels that distribute traffic loads through the repaired area. The same source notes that high early‑strength concrete should be avoided because it tends to develop excessive shrinkage cracking, indicating sensitivity to moisture loss during curing. When rapid opening to service is required, the manual specifies that accelerated concrete can provide sufficient compressive strength within four to seven hours, meeting the prescribed minimum strength schedule. Precast concrete paving systems used as patch material are stated to be designed to support the same range of traffic loading conditions as conventional Portland cement concrete pavement. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification, section 525.2, requires that rapid‑set concrete patching material achieve at least 1,500 psi compressive strength after seven hours under load. Its resistance to moisture cycling is expressed by a required durability factor of not less than 80 % after three hundred cycles in the ASTM C666 freeze‑thaw test. Temperature effects are controlled by a minimum initial set time of fifteen minutes at 75°F ± 2°F, and by compliance with the manufacturer’s shelf‑life and temperature limitations. [1][2]

The mechanical load transfer across a concrete patch is achieved through dowels that must be bonded along their entire embedded length using epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders [1]. The physical shrinkage that occurs during cement hydration influences the behavior of the patch, and high‑early‑strength mixes are discouraged because they may cause excessive shrinkage cracking [1]. The chemical setting reactions dictate strength development; accelerated concrete can reach traffic‑opening strength in 4 to 7 hours whereas a standard mix requires 24 to 48 hours [1]. Tie bars become necessary only for patches longer than 65 feet [1]. For dowel‑slot repair or crack sealing, the patching material’s behavior is governed by mechanical strength development, a chemical bond‑breaking action, and physical geometry and thermal response [2]. The grout placed in slots must attain a minimum compressive strength of 2 500 psi before traffic may be allowed [2]. A bond‑breaker coating applied to the dowels prevents the grout from adhering to the existing concrete [2]. The mix design must incorporate aggregate expansion using standard coefficient values [2]. For crack sealing, high molecular weight methacrylate is mixed according to the manufacturer’s directions, poured into the cleaned crack and sanded after roughly 20 minutes, relying on its chemical curing to seal the opening [2]. The performance of concrete patching material is chemically controlled by required curing and setting actions; rapid‑set mixes must be cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations [2]. The anchoring material used for dowels must reach an initial set within 5 minutes [2]. A bond‑breaker lubricant must be applied at least 1 hour before concrete placement to ensure a bondless dowel surface [2]. Physically, strength development is monitored by testing cylinders until the material attains a minimum compressive strength of 1 000 psi before traffic may be opened [2]. Mechanically, joint geometry and load transfer are controlled by limits such as saw‑cut widths not exceeding ½ inch, full‑depth cuts, and properly aligned dowel bars with anchoring to provide load transfer without moisture intrusion [2]. The behavior of accelerated patching concrete is primarily governed by its water‑cement ratio, which controls both workability and ultimate strength; the specification requires a maximum ratio of 0.42, a target of 0.40 that shall not fall below 0.38 for all Class AA concrete [2]. Chemical control also comes from the cementitious blend: at least 400 lb/yd³ of Portland cement must be used, supplementary material such as fly ash or slag may replace Portland cement provided total limits are observed, and slag cement is limited to no more than 30 % by weight; blended hydraulic cements are constrained to expansion of 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at 8 weeks [2]. Mechanical behavior is further regulated by air entrainment of 7.0 % ± 1.5 % at placement and by the choice of durable stone or gravel aggregate that must be washed and free of slate texture; reactive aggregates with 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % may be used only when a cement‑supplementary combination reduces effective expansion to less than 0.1 % [2]. The physical condition of the slot surface governs behavior: all loose or foreign material must be removed within 24 hours and final cleaning completed within 30 minutes before placement, using an air blast that provides at least 100 psi of oil‑free, moisture‑free air [2]. Chemically, a bond‑breaker lubricant is applied to Type B coated dowels at least 1 hour before concrete placement, and an epoxy bonding compound is added as prescribed by the manufacturer, influencing adhesion between the patch and surrounding elements [2]. Mechanically, the fresh material is vibrated after filling the slot to thoroughly consolidate it around the dowel bar, while subsequent curing of the concrete controls its strength development [2]. The performance of rapid‑set concrete patching material is governed by its water‑cement ratio, which may not exceed 0.42 lb/lb and thereby limits the mix’s workability and strength development [2]. Chemically, the hydration reaction must achieve a minimum initial set time of 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F, establishing early rigidity [2]. Mechanically, the material is required to reach a target compressive strength of 1 500 psi after seven hours and to retain at least an 80 % durability factor after 300 cycles in the ASTM C666 (procedure B) test [2]. Dimensional stability is further controlled by the use of aggregate extenders or coarse aggregate with an expansion index less than 0.10 when repairs exceed two inches [2]. The behavior of rapid‑set concrete patching material is governed primarily by the chemistry of cement hydration and any added admixtures, which together set the initial setting time to between 1 hour and 6 hours and produce a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi [2]. Physical control is achieved through water content that must give an efflux time of 10 seconds to 15 seconds, limit bleed to no more than 2.5 % of the volume, and provide the required flowability [2]. Mechanical performance is expressed by the allowable expansion of 0 % to 10 %, the shrinkage/expansion results reported in testing, and the resulting compressive strength that reflects the material’s load‑bearing capacity [2]. Work may only proceed when daytime temperatures are at least 40 °F and the subgrade is not frozen; mixed grout must be placed within one hour of mixing; injection pressure must not exceed 200 psi; upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited; and the patched area cannot be opened to traffic for less than 12 hours after grouting unless a field QC plan or manufacturer’s guidance allows a shorter period [2]. The behavior of concrete patching material is controlled by chemical adhesion from grout or epoxy bonding compounds applied to all exposed surfaces, mechanical densification through vibration or tamping, and physical temperature limits that govern placement and curing; placement occurs only within the manufacturer’s recommended installation temperature range and when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F or greater, with repairs protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours, and during curing the concrete must not experience a temperature change in excess of 40 °F within any one‑hour period [2].

Durability and deterioration

High early‑strength concrete mixes should be avoided because they may cause excessive shrinkage cracking in patches [1]. When tie bars or tie bolts are used in patches shorter than 65 feet, movement of these devices in partially set concrete under traffic can be more detrimental to performance than any benefit from tying the patch [1]. Patching a rigid pavement with a flexible material compromises the long‑term life of the pavement [1]. Joint performance problems such as faulting or poor load transfer, spalls that extend beyond the mid‑slab depth, and infiltration of water, chlorides, or incompressible materials into the repaired area must be prevented to avoid accelerated deterioration [1]. An unstable subgrade further aggravates these deterioration mechanisms, often requiring support restoration, slab jacking, dowel bar retrofit, or full‑depth patching [1]. Cracking or spalling at any location and at any time before the final inspection is considered defective work and must be removed and replaced [2]. Excavated patch areas may not remain un‑patched for more than 48 hours, and rain‑filled openings must be drained to prevent moisture intrusion [2]. If the concrete has not reached the minimum required compressive strength of 1,000 psi, traffic opening must be delayed until that strength is attained [2]. Surface irregularities greater than 3/16 inch must be ground out, saw cuts wider than ½ inch are prohibited, and insufficient protection from cooling water and dust can damage adjacent pavement [2]. The patching mix shall use durable stone that is free from slate texture or cleavage planes [2]. The water‑cement ratio may not exceed 0.42 lb/lb for the specified concrete classes [2]. Air content at placement must be 7.0 % ± 1.5 % to protect against freeze‑thaw damage [2]. Aggregates that are reactive may be used only when their 14‑day expansion lies between 0.10 % and 0.30 % and an approved cement‑supplementary blend reduces the effective expansion to less than 0.1 %; aggregates showing more than 0.30 % expansion are rejected [2]. Rapid‑set concrete patching material used for partial‑depth repair must achieve a minimum durability factor of 80 % after 300 freeze‑thaw cycles tested according to ASTM C666 Procedure B, with and without aggregate extenders [2]. The same material must attain an initial set time of at least 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F as measured by AASHTO T 131 [2]. Rapid‑set patching material shall be placed only within the shelf‑life and temperature limitations prescribed by the manufacturer; exceeding those limits can lead to loss of performance [2]. Placement is required only when ambient air and surface temperatures are at least 40 °F and within the manufacturer’s recommended installation range, and placement must be discontinued if conditions fall below that threshold [2]. Repairs must be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours, and a temperature change exceeding 40 °F in any one‑hour period renders the work defective [2]. For slab stabilization applications the mix shall have an expansion between 0 % and 10 %, an initial setting time from 1 to 6 hours, and bleed no more than 2.5 % of its volume [2]. A minimum 7‑day compressive strength of 700 psi, based on the average of five test cylinders, is required for these mixes [2]. During the opening‑to‑traffic phase, cracked, spalled or delaminated repairs are considered defective work and must be removed and replaced [2].

The use of high‑early‑strength (HES) concrete should be avoided because it is prone to excessive shrinkage cracking, which raises the susceptibility of a concrete patch to deterioration. Conversely, precast concrete pavement is described as not subject to that excessive shrinkage, thereby lowering the risk of patch failure. If traffic is allowed onto a fresh patch before the minimum compressive strength has been attained, the chance of failure increases; delaying opening until the specified strength is reached reduces deterioration. Selecting high‑performance dowel bars for patches expected to remain in service for more than 20 years further improves durability and lessens susceptibility to distress. The specification requires that excavated patch areas not be left un‑patched for more than 48 hours or over weekends or holidays, because leaving the area open for extended periods raises susceptibility of the concrete patching material. If rain occurs while the patch area is open, an outlet must be excavated through the shoulder at the lowest point of the patch as directed; failure to provide drainage can increase vulnerability of the repair. Rapid‑set concrete patching repairs shall be cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, a practice that reduces the likelihood of deterioration. For Class AA accelerated patching the mix must contain at least 530 lb of cement per cubic yard, must not exceed a water‑cement ratio of 0.42 lb/lb, the target ratio is 0.40 lb/lb and shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb, and the concrete shall be placed with an air content of 7.0 % ± 1.5 %, which provides freeze‑thaw resistance; these characteristics lower susceptibility to cracking and loss of strength. Only durable stone or gravel that has been thoroughly washed may be used, blast furnace slag aggregate is not allowed, and reactive aggregates are accepted only if their 14‑day expansion lies between 0.10 % and 0.30 % with an approved cement‑supplementary combination that brings the expanded value to less than 0.1 %; using such aggregates reduces the risk of deleterious expansion. When supplementary cementitious material such as fly ash or slag cement is used, at least 400 lb/yd³ of Portland cement must remain in the mix and slag cement may not exceed 30 % of the total cementitious content; these limits help maintain durability. In dowel retrofit work all loose and foreign material must be removed within 24 hours before concrete placement, all slurry must be removed to prevent debris from emerging once dried, final cleaning must be completed within 30 minutes before concrete placement, and when cleaning by air blasting a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi measured at the source, free of oil, moisture, and other contaminants, shall be used; applying the bond‑breaker lubricant at least 1 hour before placing the concrete around the dowel assembly also reduces the likelihood of cracking, loss of bond, and overall deterioration. Rapid‑set concrete patching material reduces susceptibility when the mix meets the maximum water‑cement ratio of 0.42 lb/lb, achieves a minimum durability factor of 80 % after 300 cycles in ASTM C666 testing, attains a minimum initial set time of 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F, and uses aggregate extenders only as supplied with the prepackaged material or, for repairs deeper than 2 inches, employs coarse aggregate whose expansion index is less than 0.10; exceeding the water‑cement ratio, failing to meet the durability factor or set‑time thresholds, using non‑approved extenders, or ignoring the manufacturer’s shelf‑life and temperature limits can increase vulnerability. For unbonded concrete overlay repairs a patching material that consists of an asphalt material, a cementitious material, or a similar pavement repair material approved by the Representative shall be used to fill all surface defects greater than 4 sq inches and deeper than 1 inch, and the filled area shall be compacted to a flat, tight surface; keeping the surface temperature of both the underlying fabric and the pavement below 120 °F before overlay placement, avoiding construction traffic on the geotextile whenever possible, and maintaining a clean, debris‑free surface all serve to reduce deterioration. Placement of rapid‑set concrete patching material shall occur only within the manufacturer’s recommended installation temperature range and when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F or greater; repairs must be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours, insulation or heating shall be provided if ambient air temperature drops below 80 °F during curing, and the repair shall not experience a curing temperature change in excess of 40 °F within any 1‑hour period; correct use of grout or epoxy bonding compounds and thorough consolidation further reduce the risk of delamination. Rapid‑set concrete patching material may be used only for corner repairs, and its performance depends on using it within the shelf life and temperature limitations set by the manufacturer; strict adherence to those limits helps keep deterioration low, whereas use after expiry or at temperatures outside the specified range can increase susceptibility to cracking, loss of bond, or other forms of distress. Material interactions and compatibility

Concrete patching material is placed on a clean, sound concrete surface prepared in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations; final cleaning must be completed within 30 minutes before concrete placement and any lubricant applied to dowels at least 1 hour prior to placement. When dowel bars are incorporated, a bond‑breaker lubricant is applied as specified, the coated dowels are embedded in an approved anchoring material that is injected into the rear of the hole before insertion, the dowel is rotated three to five complete rotations while being inserted to purge air voids, and excess anchoring paste is troweled flush, thereby providing chemical and mechanical integration with existing concrete, dowel bars, and curing compounds. For accelerated patching concrete classified as Class AA, the mix shall contain a cement factor of at least 530 lb/yd³, shall not exceed a water‑cement ratio of 0.42 lb/lb, shall target a water‑cement ratio of 0.40 lb/lb that shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb, shall achieve an air content of 7.0 % within a tolerance of ±1.5 %, may use reactive or potentially reactive aggregates only when an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend limits the combined 14‑day expansion to less than 0.1 % and must reject any aggregate showing a 14‑day expansion greater than 0.30 %, shall limit slag cement content to not more than 30 % by weight, and shall not permit mortar expansions exceeding 0.02 % at 14 days or 0.06 % at 8 weeks, thereby controlling aggregate‑cement interaction. The rapid‑set concrete patching material required for partial‑depth repair shall be mixed with a maximum water‑cement ratio of 0.42, shall attain an initial set time of at least 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F, shall achieve a minimum durability factor of 80 % at 300 cycles both with and without aggregate extenders, and when the repair depth exceeds two inches shall incorporate only the manufacturer‑provided aggregate extenders or, if unavailable, AASHTO No. 8 coarse aggregate whose expansion index is less than 0.10, thus limiting deleterious expansion within the cement matrix. In slab stabilization applications the rapid‑set patching material is proportioned with one part cement and three parts supplementary cementitious material; water is added until the efflux time falls between 10 seconds and 15 seconds, admixtures may be used to modify shrinkage and an expansion range of 0 % to 10 %, the blend shall attain an initial set between 1 hour and 6 hours, shall bleed no more than 2.5 % of its volume, and shall develop a 7‑day compressive strength of at least 700 psi, indicating compatibility with the cementitious system without exceeding specified expansion or bleeding limits. Prior to placement the repair face is coated with a grout or epoxy bonding compound; the patching material must be placed before the grout dries or while the epoxy remains tacky, ambient air and surface temperatures must be 40 °F or higher, and vibration or tamping is used to consolidate the material around dowel bars, surrounding concrete and saw cuts, thereby establishing a chemical/mechanical bond with the existing cementitious substrate and bonding agents. Influence of production and placement

The mix design influences durability, and the guidelines advise that high early‑strength concrete should be avoided because it may cause excessive shrinkage cracking [1]. Accelerated concrete mixes are permitted when an early opening is needed and can reach traffic‑opening strength in four to seven hours, whereas standard mixes require twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours [1]. For AA slip‑form paving, accelerated patching and similar Class AA cement concrete the cement content must be at least 530 lb/yd³ and not exceed 600 lb/yd³, the water‑cement ratio shall not exceed 0.42 lb/lb, the target is 0.40 lb/lb and shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb [2]. At placement the concrete must have an air content of 7.0 % ± 1.5 % [2]. The accelerated mix design includes a minimum target compressive strength of 1,500 psi at seven hours, a durability factor of at least 80 % after 300 cycles, and an initial set time of no less than 15 minutes at 75°F ± 2°F [2]. In slab stabilization the prescribed mixing proportions are one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material; water is added such that the efflux time is within 10 seconds to 15 seconds, the mix must be limited to an expansion of 0 % to 10 %, a bleed loss not exceeding 2.5 % of the volume, and an initial setting time of 1 hour to 6 hours, which together ensure that the rapid‑set concrete patching material attains at least a 7‑day compressive strength of 700 psi [2]. During slab stabilization construction dry ingredients must be weighed accurately, water metered to the day’s consumption, and the blended mix used within one hour, otherwise it must be discarded; flowability is checked at least twice each shift [2]. Proper mixing and placement as specified in Section 501.3(a)‑(h) ensures that the concrete patch meets lot acceptance criteria [2]. Continuity of placement, supported by adequate equipment and trained personnel, prevents cold joints and contributes to uniform strength development [2]. When the repair depth exceeds two inches, aggregate extenders recommended by the manufacturer are used; for shallow repairs the patching material must be placed into any defect larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch and then compacted to a flat, tight surface [2]. The surface temperature of either the geotextile fabric or the existing pavement may not exceed 120°F before overlay placement [2]. Placement of rapid‑set concrete patching material is permitted only when ambient air and surface temperatures are at least 40°F and within the manufacturer’s recommended installation temperature range; placing it outside this range or below 40°F is prohibited and the repair must be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete patching placement must deposit concrete in its final position without segregation and within one half hour of each other batch, and acceptable methods must be approved [2]. Consolidation is achieved by vibration or tamping, which densifies the material and helps it flow into saw cuts beyond the repair edges [2]. In dowel retrofit work the slot is filled with concrete patching material and vibrated to thoroughly consolidate the material in the slot and around the dowel bar [2]. Consolidation of the plastic mix is achieved by internal vibration, external vibration, or a combination of both; self‑consolidating mixtures may only be used if they were qualified within 2.0 inches of the upper production slump flow limit during trial batching [2]. After placement the surface of the slots is finished to match the existing pavement surface profile, including any wheel ruts [2]. Finishing must produce a true, even surface free of roughness, honeycombing, depressions or projections and water may not be added to aid finishing; for Type A, B, or C patches the finished surface is scribed with the placement date to preserve traceability and shaped to match existing cross‑section and texture [2]. Curing follows the manufacturer’s recommendations in all cases [2]. The Representative will determine when pavement may be opened to traffic based on when the concrete attains the minimum required compressive strength of 1,000 psi as measured by compressive strength testing of cylinders according to PTM No. 604 [2]. The curing period therefore governs when the patch can support traffic, with accelerated or standard concrete achieving required compressive strength within the specified time frames [1]. For roller‑compacted concrete patching early curing performance is judged by compressive strength: if the 3‑day test result is greater than or equal to 3,000 psi the field cure may be discontinued unless otherwise directed; otherwise the lot must remain under cure until a 7‑day test also reaches 3,000 psi or the 28‑day test confirms a minimum compressive strength of 3,500 psi [2]. For rapid‑set and slab‑stabilization repairs the cured area must be kept out of traffic for a minimum of 12 hours after grouting operations have been completed, or according to the contractor’s approved field QC plan and manufacturer recommendations [2]. Curing temperature must be controlled and monitored at least hourly to ensure the repair area does not experience a curing temperature change in excess of 40°F within any one‑hour period; failure to meet these requirements classifies the work as defective and may require repair with rapid‑set concrete patching material [2].

Long‑term performance of concrete patching material is highly sensitive to construction variations that disturb load transfer, uniform support, material properties, and proper curing. In section 4.2 of the Pavement Policy Manual dowels must be bonded along their entire embedded length; any lack of full‑length bond prevents reliable joint load transfer [1]. The manual also advises that high early strength (HES) concrete should be avoided because it can cause excessive shrinkage cracking, a defect that reduces durability [1]. Poor placement quality that fails to provide a uniformly supporting patch leads to costly leveling and reduced durability, so uniform support is essential [1]. For short patches the movement of tie bars or bolts in partially set concrete can be more detrimental than the intended benefit of tying the patch, making such movement a critical defect [1]. Section 4.3 adds that construction‑related defects impairing joint integrity—such as faulting or poor load transfer in joints, an unstable subgrade, or non‑functional drainage—must be corrected before the patch can perform reliably [1]. Missing tie bars from longitudinal contraction joints are identified as a construction error that undermines durability [1]. Failure to clean and seal joints and cracks prior to applying the patching mix compromises performance, and dowel bar retrofits require that prepared slots be filled with concrete patching material and cured for proper function [1]. In full‑depth precast or prestressed slab repairs manufacturing imperfections, handling damage, spalling of the top surface or edges, and cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than D/3 must be replaced, while cracks wider than 0.007 inches but shallower than D/3 are repaired according to ACI 224R and narrower cracks are sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate [2]. Dowel and bedding grouts used in such repairs must reach a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is allowed, otherwise the repair is considered defective [2]. In concrete pavement patching saw cuts greater than ½ inch in width or not full depth may not be opened to traffic, break‑back or spalling requires a new full‑depth transverse cut beyond the affected area, and damage to adjacent dowel bars or curbs together with improper dowel anchoring when the sum of voids around a dowel exceeds 0.50 inch in any verification core are prohibited [2]. Subbase irregularities deeper than 1 inch and surface deviations of 3/16 inch or more must be corrected by grinding, because such irregularities impair uniform support [2]. Opening the patch to traffic before the rapid‑set material reaches a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi is considered defective, and leaving cracked or spalled concrete unrepaired accelerates deterioration [2]. For Class AA accelerated patching concrete the water‑cement ratio may not exceed 0.42 lb/lb; the target ratio is 0.40 lb/lb and shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb, while air content must be 7.0 % within ±1.5 % [2]. Aggregates with a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % are acceptable only when used with an approved cement‑supplementary combination that reduces expansion to less than 0.1 %; aggregates with expansion greater than 0.30 % must be rejected, and blended hydraulic cement is limited to a maximum mortar expansion of 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at eight weeks [2]. In dowel retrofit work equipment that may spall pavement or a jackhammer weighing more than 30 lb creates broken aggregates, failure to clean slots at least twenty‑four hours before placement and within thirty minutes before concrete placement introduces contaminants, and dowel bars must be positioned with vertical tilt or horizontal skew limited to a maximum displacement of 0.25 inch at each end, longitudinal translation limited to 1 inch, and horizontal translation also limited to 1 inch; inadequate sealing of the temporary joint material permits patching material to flow into existing cracks [2]. Unbonded concrete overlays are vulnerable to non‑uniform support or unfilled voids, unstable slabs or slab fragments that move under traffic must be stabilized before any interlayer is placed, surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than 1 inch must be filled with an approved patching mix when a geotextile interlayer is used, and joint faulting greater than ¼ inch for geotextile installations or exceeding ½ inch for asphalt interlayers must be milled or scratched before overlay placement [2]. Slab stabilization requires that any change in cement, supplementary material or admixture source trigger a new mix design; water must be added so the efflux time stays within ten to fifteen seconds, expansion is limited to 0 %–10 %, bleed loss shall not exceed 2.5 % of the volume, initial setting time must be between one hour and six hours, and a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi is required, because deviations beyond these ranges promote premature deterioration [2]. Roller‑compacted concrete pavements are sensitive to changes in mix design or materials without submitting a new mix design, failure to meet ingredient tolerances, incomplete or non‑uniform mixing, exceeding the manufacturer’s rated dry‑concrete volume capacity in the mixer, using a mixer whose blades are worn enough to cause inadequate mixing, and applying material outside its shelf life or temperature limits; each of these defects undermines long‑term performance [2]. Additional construction defects that compromise durability include radial cracks spreading outward from grout injection holes, transverse cracks between adjacent holes which require slab replacement, upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 inch, drilling holes larger than 1½ inches or non‑vertical, non‑round or oversized holes, and failure to fully seal and flush rapid‑set non‑shrink concrete patches after hole removal [2]. In partial‑depth repairs grout or epoxy bonding compound must not run or puddle in low spots, the material must be placed before grout dries or while epoxy remains tacky, placement must be discontinued if ambient air and surface temperatures fall below 40 °F, repairs must be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours, any curing temperature change exceeding 40 °F within one hour is considered defective because it induces thermal stresses, and patches that become delaminated must be removed and replaced; cracks, spalling or delamination in the patch itself are primary variations that can undermine durability and must be removed and replaced [2]. Roller‑compacted concrete also requires that the final average density may not be less than 98 % of the maximum wet density and no individual test may be less than 96 % of the maximum wet density, the 28‑day compressive strength must achieve at least 3,500 psi, and a measured thickness deficient by more than ¼ inch renders the pavement depth defective [2]. Finally, precast concrete products demand that the finished surface be true and even, free from rough, open, honeycombed areas, depressions or projections; concrete must be placed without segregation and properly consolidated by vibration, water may not be added during finishing, and air temperatures within the work area must remain above 40 °F for production, because surface irregularities or compromised integrity strongly affect durability [2].

Selection, specification, and improvement

Selection of concrete patching material begins with an assessment of the severity of pavement distress; badly deteriorated pavements or slabs in which deterioration reaches the slab bottom are assigned to full‑depth concrete patching and require removal of all cracked or crushed joints, whereas partial‑depth repairs are limited to spalling confined to the top half of the slab depth without severe joint loss [1]. The length of the repair area also governs the choice of treatment: if a significant length of roadway (±500 feet) requires intermittent patches with ten feet or less of original pavement remaining between patches, the entire section should be removed and reconstructed, and when more than 500 feet of all lanes are to be replaced a design analysis must determine whether a thicker slab depth is needed [1]. Traffic‑opening requirements drive the concrete type; high early strength (HES) concrete should be avoided because of its potential for excessive shrinkage cracking, accelerated concrete can achieve opening to traffic strength in four to seven hours when early opening is desired, standard pavement concrete can achieve opening in twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours, and precast concrete paving systems are considered when work‑zone safety or minimum‑time‑to‑traffic concerns outweigh their higher cost [1]. Load‑transfer design mandates dowelling all patch joints with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders bonded along the entire embedded length, specifies new pavement joint types and spacing—thirty‑foot joint spacing for JRCP or fifteen‑foot joint spacing for JPCP—and requires longitudinal tie bars or bolts only in concrete patches longer than sixty‑five feet; for pavements expected to remain in service more than twenty years, the use of high‑performance dowel bars should be considered [1]. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs the patching material must be an approved rapid‑setting grout or mix that can achieve a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before the slab is opened to traffic, must be placed in accordance with the dowel retrofit method so that it is confined to the dowel slots and does not flow into joint gaps, and must be compatible with the rapid‑setting dowel slot grout required by the accepted PCPS system [2]. For bonded concrete overlays the patching material shall be Class AA cement concrete as defined in Section 545.2, using durable stone or gravel aggregate (slag aggregate prohibited), a cement factor of at least 530 lb/yd³ and not exceeding 600 lb/yd³ for slip‑form paving, a water‑cement ratio that shall not exceed 0.42 with a target of 0.40 that shall not fall below 0.38, air content at placement of 7 % ± 1.5 %, and when supplementary cementitious material such as fly ash or slag cement is used, at least 400 lb/yd³ of Portland cement must remain in the blend with total cementitious content not exceeding the Table A limits and slag cement limited to no more than thirty percent by weight; reactive aggregates are allowed only if their fourteen‑day expansion lies between 0.10 % and 0.30 % and an approved cement‑supplementary combination reduces the expansion to less than 0.1 %, while any aggregate showing greater than 0.30 % expansion is rejected [2]. For partial‑depth repairs the mix shall not exceed a water‑cement ratio of 0.42; accelerated mixes must achieve at least 1,500 psi compressive strength at seven hours as tested according to PTM No. 604; rapid‑set products must satisfy ASTM C928 and demonstrate a durability factor of at least eighty percent after three hundred cycles in ASTM C666 testing; the initial set time shall be no faster than a minimum of fifteen minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F according to AASHTO T 131; aggregate requirements may include Type A No. 8 coarse aggregate or, for repairs deeper than two inches, approved extenders with an expansion index less than 0.10; all materials must be used within the manufacturer’s shelf‑life, temperature limits, and installation instructions while complying with relevant ASTM or AASHTO test methods [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays shallow repairs shall use an asphalt, cementitious, or comparable pavement‑repair product that has been approved by the Representative prior to overlay placement; when a geotextile interlayer is used any surface defect larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be filled with such material and compacted to a flat, tight surface; if an asphalt interlayer is employed the patch may be placed together with the interlayer only if it can be properly compacted along with it, otherwise a separate repair material must be used before the interlayer is laid [2]. For slab stabilization rapid‑set concrete patching material shall be supplied by a manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15 and used within the shelf‑life and temperature limits specified by that manufacturer; a mix design must be submitted for District Executive approval and include independent laboratory results for one‑, three‑ and seven‑day compressive strengths, flowability, shrinkage/expansion and initial set time; the prescribed proportions are one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, with water adjusted so that efflux time falls between ten and fifteen seconds; the mix must achieve an expansion of zero percent to ten percent, an initial setting time of one to six hours, bleed no more than two point five percent of its volume, and a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi based on five cylinders; any change in the source of any material triggers a requirement for a new mix design submission [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements rapid‑set concrete patching material must be placed while still within its manufacturer‑specified shelf life and under temperature conditions approved by the manufacturer, as stipulated in Section 525.2(c) [2]. Selection among the three partial‑depth repair options is driven primarily by temperature limits and service‑time requirements: Rapid‑Set Concrete Patching Material may be used only when ambient air and surface temperatures are at least forty degrees Fahrenheit and within the manufacturer’s recommended installation range, making it suitable where a quick return to traffic is needed; Class AA Cement Concrete Modified requires grout or epoxy bonding compound to be applied while still tacky before placing concrete, so it is selected when a bonded repair is desired; Class AA Cement Concrete Accelerated can be placed under the same forty‑degree minimum but also demands that curing temperatures not fall below eighty degrees Fahrenheit and that any temperature swing during curing stay within forty degrees Fahrenheit per hour, influencing its use where controlled curing conditions are available; in all cases the return‑to‑service time must be considered before opening the repair to traffic [2]. For low‑strength cement concrete core repairs any hole left after coring must be filled with an approved patching material that comes from a manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15 or conforms to the project’s approved concrete mix design, and this approval requirement governs selection of the patching product for the repair [2]. When specifying rapid‑set concrete patching material for dowel retrofit work the selection must be governed by the requirement to use the product within the shelf‑life and temperature limitations established by the manufacturer; compliance with those limits ensures that the material will set properly and develop the intended performance [2]. Rapid‑Set Concrete Patching Material is allowed only for corner repairs, applied within the manufacturer’s shelf‑life and temperature limits, while also observing the subbase requirements of Section 350.2 and complying with the concrete curing material provisions of Sections 711.1 and 711.2 [2]. For precast concrete product repair selection follows the procedures set out in Publication 145; the patching material must appear on Bulletin 15 or have received approval from the CSME, and repairs that fall outside the scope defined in Publication 145 must be submitted to the CSME for evaluation and disposition [2]. When a partial‑depth repair is to be opened to traffic the patching material must be one of three specified types: Class AA cement concrete, Modified, provided that the repair thickness and length follow the limits in Table D; Class AA cement concrete, Accelerated as defined in Section 501.3(q); or a rapid‑set concrete patching material as described in Section 516.3(q), so selection hinges on meeting the class designation and any dimensional constraints prescribed for the chosen type [2].

The guidance advises that high early strength concrete should be avoided because of its potential for excessive shrinkage cracking, and instead accelerated concrete may be used since it can reach the required compressive strength within four to seven hours, or precast concrete pavement slabs may be employed as they are not subject to that cracking. Load‑transfer performance can be improved by dowelling all patch joints with dowels bonded along their entire embedded length using epoxy, polyester binders, or vinylester binders, and high‑performance dowel bars should be considered when the repaired section is expected to remain in service for more than twenty years. When rapid traffic reopening is critical, PCPS can replace accelerated concrete; these precast units are manufactured to a high‑strength Class AA mix and installed on a prepared bedding course, providing durable support while reducing work‑zone impacts. If a flexible base is substituted for PCC patch material, the project must meet the density acceptance sampling and testing requirements of Publication 408 Section 316 and place the base in compacted lifts that are allowed to cool before subsequent lifts. Badly spalled joints and interior spalls should be cleaned and filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material (or equivalent) before an overlay, providing an alternative to concrete patching material; additionally all joints and cracks must be cleaned and sealed with rubberized joint sealing material prior to any patching, which helps prevent water, chloride, and incompressible‑material infiltration. For full‑depth precast or prestressed slabs the mix design shall achieve a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a flexural strength of 650 psi as demonstrated by trial‑batch testing, the slabs should be cast one‑quarter inch thicker than the nominal design thickness, and high‑strength rapid‑setting grout or patching material should replace standard slot‑patching grout after the bedding grout has set; traffic must not be allowed until the grout reaches at least 2,500 psi compressive strength. A quality‑control plan specifying sampling, testing and curing procedures must be prepared and submitted for review, dowel‑bar anchorage shall be verified by random core checks with acceptance defined as perimeter voids less than 0.50 inch per core, bond‑breaker lubricant shall be applied to at least one‑half of the length of Type B coated dowels at least one hour before concrete placement unless already applied in the shop, and anchoring material formulated to reach an initial set within five minutes shall be provided. Rapid‑set patching mixes and a Type 2 white pigmented liquid membrane curing compound may be used as alternatives to standard PAMS curing when applied according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, and surface tolerances must be controlled by straightedge checks that require correction of any high or low spots of three‑sixteenths inch or more. For dowel retrofits all loose and foreign material shall be removed within twenty‑four hours before concrete placement, final cleaning must be completed within thirty minutes prior to placing the mix, a compressed‑air stream of at least one hundred psi that is free of oil, moisture and other contaminants must be used for air blasting, bond‑breaker lubricant shall be applied at least one hour before placing concrete around the assembly, fresh patching material shall be vibrated to thoroughly consolidate it in the slot and around the dowel bar, and a preformed cellular polystyrene or appropriate expansion joint material may be used as a temporary joint. In bonded concrete overlays Portland cement may be replaced with fly ash or slag cement provided that at least four hundred pounds per cubic yard of Portland cement remains and total cementitious content does not exceed the limits in Table A, with slag cement limited to no more than thirty percent of the cementitious blend; the mix should be designed for an air content of 7.0 percent ± 1.5 percent at placement and a target water‑cement ratio of 0.40 lb/lb that shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb nor exceed 0.42 lb/lb, with field verification using batch tickets and AASHTO T 318; aggregates showing a fourteen‑day expansion between 0.10 percent and 0.30 percent may be used only with an approved cement‑supplementary combination that reduces effective expansion to less than 0.1 percent, while any aggregate exceeding 0.30 percent expansion must be rejected. For partial‑depth repairs the water‑cement ratio shall not exceed 0.42, accelerated repairs should target a minimum compressive strength of 1,500 psi after seven hours as verified by PTM No. 604, rapid‑set patching material complying with ASTM C928 must achieve a durability factor of at least eighty percent at three hundred cycles in ASTM C666 (procedure B) and set no slower than fifteen minutes at seventy‑five degrees Fahrenheit ± two degrees per AASHTO T 131, repairs deeper than two inches shall incorporate aggregate extenders prescribed by the manufacturer or an approved AASHTO No. 8 coarse aggregate with an expansion index less than 0.10, and all materials must be used within their shelf‑life and temperature limits. In unbonded concrete overlays any asphalt material, cementitious material or similar repair material approved by the Representative may be used to fill defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch; such patches shall be compacted to a flat, tight surface after removal of loose debris by power broom, vacuum truck or other approved means, and when a geotextile interlayer is employed the fabric surface temperature shall not exceed 120 °F before overlay placement, with similar temperature limits for an asphalt interlayer controlled by misting, white‑pigmented curing compound, lime‑water whitewashing or other approved method. In slab stabilization admixtures such as a multiphase wetting agent, an expansive agent or an accelerator may be used when needed; the mix may contain one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume with water adjusted so that efflux time falls within ten to fifteen seconds, and the mix shall furnish an expansion of zero percent to ten percent, an initial setting time between one hour and six hours, bleed no more than two‑point‑five percent of the volume, and achieve a seven‑day compressive strength of at least seven hundred psi based on the average of five test cylinders, with rapid‑set concrete patching materials required to be used within their shelf life and temperature limitations; alternative stabilizers such as asphalt cement meeting ASTM D3141 with a minimum flash point of five hundred degrees Fahrenheit or high‑density polyurethane grout are also permitted. Construction practices for slab stabilization require that holes be filled full depth with rapid‑set non‑shrink concrete patching material mixed no longer than one hour before placement, flowability shall be measured at least twice each shift, work shall not commence when daytime temperatures are below forty degrees Fahrenheit to avoid premature setting, traffic must be kept off the repaired area for a minimum of twelve hours after grouting, and mixed material shall not be held in the mixer or injection pump sump for more than one hour. Across all repair contexts the mix design shall incorporate the specified cement, supplementary cementitious materials, well‑graded aggregate, water and admixtures, with moisture content optimized according to AASHTO T 180 Method D; uniform mixing must be ensured without exceeding the manufacturer’s rated volume capacity for dry concrete in the mixer, mixers and blades shall be kept clean and worn blades replaced, ingredient tolerances must meet limits set out in Section 704.1(e), daily plant records of material quantities shall be supplied for calibration checks, and any change in materials requires a new mix design submission. When rapid‑set concrete patching material is used it must be placed within the shelf life and temperature limitations prescribed by the manufacturer, accelerating admixtures may be used provided they contain no chlorides, and air‑entraining admixture shall not exceed fifteen percent by mass of the total cementitious factor. For partial‑depth repairs exposed surfaces shall receive grout or epoxy bonding compound immediately after final cleaning, the patching material shall be consolidated with vibration or tamping, curing shall be performed with a white membrane forming curing compound while monitoring at least hourly to ensure that the repair area does not experience a curing temperature change in excess of forty degrees Fahrenheit within any one‑hour period, placement shall occur only when ambient air and surface temperatures are forty degrees Fahrenheit or greater, and the repaired section shall be protected from freezing for at least twenty‑four hours. Quality control for each lot shall include testing for density, compressive strength and depth; the final average density may not be less than ninety‑eight percent of the maximum wet density with no individual test below ninety‑six percent, compressive strength must reach at least three thousand psi at three or seven days before field curing may stop, while a minimum of three thousand five hundred psi is required at twenty‑eight days, and cores that meet depth tolerance within one‑quarter inch are accepted; deficient core holes may be filled with additional rapid‑set concrete patching material as an alternative. For repairs involving steel dowels a bond‑breaker lubricant having a flash point of at least one hundred forty‑nine degrees Fahrenheit and free of graphite shall be used to protect the metal, and preformed cellular polystyrene lightweight fill conforming to ASTM C578 can be substituted to reduce shrinkage stresses in the repaired area. The specification permits rapid‑set concrete patching material only for corner repairs and requires it be placed within the manufacturer’s shelf‑life and temperature limitations. In slab stabilization work cement grout may be supplied with an accelerator when required, the accelerator being built into the unit price, and selecting Class AA cement concrete modified in accordance with Section 501.3(q) or the accelerated version, applying the repair thickness and length prescribed in Table D can improve performance; alternatively rapid‑set concrete patching material specified in Section 516.3(q) may be employed for faster strength gain. Quality control is reinforced by removing and replacing any cracked, spalled or delaminated repairs as outlined in Section 501.3(t), and for each lot at least four test cylinders shall be molded per PTM No. 631, cured identically to the patching product, tested according to PTM No. 604 and the results recorded for the Representative; work‑area air temperatures must remain above forty degrees Fahrenheit during production, and concrete shall be placed without segregation using internal or external vibration as required. Performance evaluation and implications

Evaluation of concrete patching material across the specifications relies on a combination of laboratory trial‑batch testing, field verification, and, where provided, limited long‑term criteria. In Section 529 the laboratory trial‑batch test data must demonstrate that each mix design will achieve the minimum required compressive strength of 4,925 psi and flexural strength of 650 psi, and field verification requires that the grout or patching material reach a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is permitted; no long‑term performance criteria are specified. In Section 516 the quality‑control plan calls for laboratory‑cured cylinders to achieve a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi before traffic, and field verification includes daily random cores through anchored dowels with acceptance when voids around the dowel perimeter are less than 0.50 inch, while surface tolerance is checked on site with a 12‑foot straightedge and any high point or depression of 3/16 inch or more must be corrected by grinding. Section 545 requires laboratory checks for Class AA accelerated patching concrete that use aggregates deemed reactive only when a 14‑day expansion between 0.10% and 0.30% is reduced to less than 0.1% by an approved cement‑supplementary material combination, and blended hydraulic cements are limited to maximum mortar expansions of 0.02% at 14 days and 0.06% at 8 weeks; field verification confirms the water‑cement ratio from batch tickets and measures air content at placement to be 7.0% ± 1.5%. Section 525 (partial‑depth repair) bases laboratory evaluation of rapid‑set concrete patching material on a minimum durability factor of 80 % at 300 cycles in ASTM C666 (procedure B) with and without aggregate extenders, an initial set time of at least 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F per AASHTO T 131, and for the accelerated Class AA mix a minimum compressive strength of 1,500 psi at 7 hours tested according to PTM No. 604; no additional field or long‑term criteria are described. Section 679 (slab stabilization) requires independent laboratory testing that must show 1‑, 3‑ and 7‑day compressive strengths, flowability measured by an efflux time of 10 to 15 seconds, shrinkage/expansion between 0 % and 10 %, initial setting time from 1 to 6 hours, bleed not exceeding 2.5 % of the volume, and a minimum 7‑day compressive strength of 700 psi based on five cylinders; field performance evidence is limited to a deflection test and hole count used for payment, with no separate long‑term data. Section 679.4 also uses the deflection test and holes drilled as field evidence for determining payment, without additional laboratory or long‑term performance data. Section 714 mandates that each production lot be evaluated in the laboratory by molding at least four test cylinders per PTM No. 631, curing them identically to the product, testing according to PTM No. 604 and supplying results to the Representative; field verification is achieved by applying the material in accordance with Publication 145 or an approved bulletin, with any repairs beyond that scope submitted for CSME evaluation. Finally, Section 525 (construction) requires laboratory curing of test cylinders under the same conditions as the repair, monitoring that curing temperature does not change more than 40 °F within any one‑hour period, and field observation that completed repairs are resounded in the presence of a Representative with delaminated patches replaced at no cost; placement is limited to ambient air and surface temperatures of 40 °F or greater and protection from freezing for at least 24 hours. The durability and performance of a concrete pavement patch depend first on achieving proper load transfer; dowels must be bonded throughout their entire embedded length so that transverse joints can carry traffic loads. Because high early strength (HES) concrete should be avoided due to its potential for excessive shrinkage cracking, designers specify either standard mix—reaching opening‑to‑traffic strength in 24 to 48 hours—or accelerated concrete, which can achieve opening to traffic strength in 4 to 7 hours. Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are only required in concrete patches more than 65 feet long, and their movement can be more detrimental to the performance of short patches than the benefits derived from tying the patch to the existing lane. When dowel detailing, joint spacing, and high‑performance dowel bar selection for service lives over 20 years follow the guidelines, the repaired slab provides uniform support, maintains ride quality, and reduces future maintenance.

The rapid‑setting patching grout used in full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs must achieve a compressive strength of at least 2,500 psi before the slab is opened to traffic, which safeguards load transfer across joints and helps prevent premature distress. In other concrete pavement repairs the opening to traffic is conditioned on the patch attaining a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi as verified by cylinder testing, while partial‑depth repairs target a compressive strength of 1,500 psi at seven hours, providing early load‑bearing capacity. For slab stabilization applications a seven‑day compressive strength of not less than 700 psi is required to ensure the repaired area can sustain traffic loads without premature failure. Cracks that are 0.007 inches wide or less are sealed with high molecular weight methacrylate, creating a durable seal that limits moisture ingress. The patching material must be capable of accommodating joint widths of at least 1/4 inch and vertical differentials not exceeding 1/4 inch; compliance with these tolerances prevents edge spalling and contributes to long‑term serviceability. Saw cuts made for full‑depth patches are limited to a maximum width of ½ inch and must be full depth before traffic may be permitted. Surface irregularities greater than 3/16 inch are corrected by grinding to meet the stricter tolerances required for pavement performance. A ¼‑inch, full‑depth polystyrene board is installed as a bond breaker in the longitudinal joints of Type A and B patches, protecting against moisture intrusion. Prior to placing the concrete patching material in dowel slots the slot must be cleaned and the cleaning completed within 30 minutes before placement; the material is then vibrated to thoroughly consolidate it around the dowel bar, and movement of the dowel from its specified position is prohibited, thereby preserving load transfer and extending pavement durability. Rapid‑Set Concrete Patching Material may be used in dowel retrofit applications provided it is applied within the shelf‑life and temperature limitations set by the manufacturer. The Class AA accelerated‑patching mix must contain a cement factor of at least 530 lb/yd³ and may not exceed a water‑cement ratio of 0.42 lb/lb; the target ratio is 0.40 lb/lb and shall not fall below 0.38 lb/lb, controlling strength development and permeability. Air content at placement must be 7.0 % ± 1.5 %, providing freeze‑thaw durability for slip‑form paving and accelerated patches. Portland cement may be partially replaced by fly ash or slag provided that a minimum of 400 lb/yd³ of Portland cement remains, total cementitious content stays within specified limits, and slag does not exceed 30 % of the blend; reactive aggregates are permitted only when their 14‑day expansion lies between 0.10 % and 0.30 % and an approved cement‑supplementary combination reduces effective expansion to less than 0.1 %, preventing deleterious swelling. The rapid‑set concrete patching material for partial‑depth repairs must achieve a durability factor of at least 80 % after 300 freeze‑thaw cycles, aiding resistance to cyclic damage, and it must also attain an initial set within 15 minutes at 75 °F ± 2 °F, allowing traffic restoration shortly after construction. For slab stabilization the rapid‑set concrete patching material is mixed as one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, with water adjusted so that the efflux time falls between ten and fifteen seconds; the mix must exhibit an expansion no greater than ten percent, an initial setting time ranging from one hour to six hours, and bleed loss not exceeding 2.5 % of its volume, these limits controlling dimensional change and early‑age cracking. The rapid‑set concrete patching material must be placed only when ambient air and surface temperatures are at least 40 °F, and placement must stop if conditions fall below that threshold; repairs must be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours, which helps prevent early deterioration and supports the intended durability of the repaired pavement. Use vibration or tamping to consolidate the rapid‑set concrete patching material, and extend the patch into saw cuts beyond the corners to ensure good bond and load transfer. Drill holes are filled full depth with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete patching material and the patched surface is struck flush with the surrounding pavement; any raised area exceeding 0.05 inch is not permitted. The work must cure before traffic, requiring a minimum of twelve hours after grouting operations have been completed. When an overlay is placed, surface defects are repaired with an approved asphalt or cementitious patching material that is compacted to a flat, tight surface; if a geotextile interlayer is used, all defects larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch must be filled before the interlayer is placed, while an asphalt interlayer may accommodate shallow defects only when they can be properly compacted together, otherwise the defects are repaired beforehand.
