Subgrade Stabilization

Part of: Subgrade is-subtopic-of
Subgrade Stabilization is a topic under Subgrade.

Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Subgrade stabilization is a treatment applied when the existing subgrade or reclaimed base does not possess adequate structural capacity, bearing strength, stiffness, or drainage to support the designed pavement thickness and anticipated traffic loads [1][2]. The method removes weak or moisture‑saturated material, verifies firmness by proof‑rolling, and then treats the remaining mass with mechanical, chemical (e.g., cement, lime, calcium‑chloride, emulsified asphalt) or geosynthetic agents that cement particles, provide confinement, increase stiffness, and improve water resistance [1][2]. The stabilized layer is compacted to at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum dry density (higher in critical zones), must achieve a seven‑day unconfined compressive strength of roughly 300–500 psi, and limits permanent deformation after special rolling to less than one‑half inch for existing subgrades or one inch for embankments [1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Subgrade stabilization is applied when the existing subgrade or reclaimed base does not possess adequate structural capacity, bearing strength, stiffness, or drainage to support the designed pavement thickness and anticipated traffic loads [1][2]. The engineering problem manifests as weak, moisture‑saturated soils with low California Bearing Ratio values—often below five—or low resilient modulus that produce excessive permanent deformation such as rutting greater than one‑half inch in existing subgrades and greater than one inch in embankment foundations, leading to differential heave, reflective cracking, frost damage, settlement, shrinkage cracking, and premature pavement failure [1][2]. Excess water within the support layer further degrades material integrity, reducing load‑bearing capacity and increasing susceptibility to freeze‑thaw deterioration [1][2]. To meet the performance need for a uniform, firm, and durable foundation, the design calls for removal of deleterious material, proof‑rolling or special rolling to verify consistent firmness, and repair of unstable zones by additional compaction, undercutting/excavation to a depth of one foot, drying, recompacting, or replacement with suitably compacted fill achieving at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum dry density at optimum moisture content [2][1]. Stabilizing agents—mechanical, chemical, cementitious, lime, fly ash, emulsified asphalt, chlorides—or the use of geogrids such as Class 2 biaxial polypropylene grids provide particle cementation, mechanical confinement, increased stiffness, higher load‑bearing capacity, improved water resistance, and a lowered freezing point [1][2]. Chemically stabilized materials must develop a seven‑day unconfined compressive strength between 300 and 500 psi (ASTM D1633) while avoiding strengths above 900 psi that could cause shrinkage cracking; traffic is not permitted until the specified strength is achieved [2]. The result of these actions is a treated subgrade that meets required CBR or design criteria, provides reliable drainage, and prevents early distress such as excessive settlement, rutting, or reflective cracking in the pavement structure [1][2].

Subgrade stabilization must achieve a strength gain sufficient to satisfy structural requirements and protect the pavement from environmental and loading effects [1][2]. Functionally the treated mass is required to behave as a stable foundation, limiting permanent deformation measured by proof‑rolling to less than one‑half inch for existing or cut subgrades and to less than one inch for embankment foundations [2]. When calcium chloride is the chosen stabilizer, a minimum 35 % solution applied at 0.1–0.15 gal/yd² per inch of depth is prescribed to cement particles and lower the freezing point, thereby improving freeze‑thaw durability [1]. If emulsified asphalt is used, the mix design must follow Bulletin 27 procedures to provide enhanced resistance to water‑induced damage and greater fatigue performance under traffic loads [1]. Structurally the material must be compacted to meet moisture‑density criteria, requiring at least 97 % of maximum dry density for deeper embankments, 100 % for structure foundations, subgrades and any layer within three feet of the proposed elevation, and no less than 95 % of laboratory‑determined maximum dry density in general [2]. Moisture content must be maintained between three percent below optimum and optimum, with verification tolerances of ±0.8 % water and wet‑density readings within ±2 lb/ft³ of the contractor’s gauge [2]. Durability is demonstrated by achieving a specified seven‑day unconfined compressive (or indirect tensile) strength typically ranging from 300 to 500 psi, and traffic may not be opened until that strength is attained [2]. Safety considerations include maintaining a minimum ten‑foot clearance for rolling equipment from bridge abutments, observing prescribed undercut depths, using appropriate geotextile classes and aggregate thicknesses when remedial excavation is performed, and limiting continuous grout‑injection pressures to 200 psi with higher pressures permitted only briefly [2]. Serviceability objectives require the finished pavement surface to conform to the intended grade line within tolerances of roughly 0.02 to 0.03 ft, to limit surface irregularities to no more than one‑half inch over a ten‑foot straightedge, and to ensure that any over‑jacking exceeding one‑tenth foot triggers removal and replacement of the pavement [2]. Together these functional, structural, durability, safety and serviceability criteria ensure that the stabilized subgrade provides reliable load support, long‑term performance, protection of adjacent structures, and a smooth, safe riding surface [1][2].

Scope and applicability

Subgrade stabilization is employed whenever the existing support layers of a pavement are judged insufficient to meet performance criteria, whether the surface is an asphalt wearing course in severe distress or any other pavement type that otherwise conforms to standard traffic design ranges after drainage problems have been corrected. [1][2] The approach is especially common in full‑depth reclamation projects, where the existing pavement material is pulverized and recycled in place to re‑establish proper drainage and add structural capacity without importing new aggregate. [1][2] Cost‑benefit considerations typically favor stabilization when the project involves at least 25 000 square yards of pavement on a Traffic Pattern Group 1‑4, or when subgrade California Bearing Ratio values fall below 5 percent. [1] For conventional soil subgrades, cuts, or spread footings, a permanent deflection greater than one‑half inch triggers stabilization, while embankments that are being raised require a permanent deflection exceeding one inch before remedial action is taken. [2] When the measured excess deflection lies between 0.5 and 3 inches, the subgrade may be recompacted or excavated to a depth of one foot; if the excess exceeds three inches, excavation depths up to three feet are permitted, after which the void is backfilled with an approved material such as selected borrow or No. 57 aggregate placed over a Class 4 Type C geotextile and covered by a Class 4 Type A geotextile before subbase placement. [2] Similar excavation‑and‑backfill criteria are applied to embankment foundations, grade‑raising of existing embankments, bridge approach embankments, and structural foundations with spread footings. [2] When the foundation consists of coarse aggregate or rock fill (AASHTO No. 1 or No. 3, R‑rock, shot rock), a geosynthetic‑stabilized bridge approach is used, involving proof‑rolling of the material, placement of a Class 4 Type A separation geosynthetic over the prepared subgrade, followed by a stabilization geosynthetic layer and appropriate backfill, with a dedicated foundation drain provided. [2] Stabilization may be achieved through several mechanisms, including pulverizing the existing material with moisture, mechanically blending aggregate or reclaimed asphalt pavement to improve gradation, or chemically amending the mix with dry or slurry applications of Portland cement, lime, fly ash, lime kiln dust, calcium chloride or magnesium chloride solutions (minimum 35 % concentration applied at 0.1–0.15 gal/yd² per inch of reclaimed depth), emulsified asphalt, or combinations thereof. [1][2] For very weak subgrades the guides also allow rock capping with one to three feet of R‑3 or equivalent rock, placement of geogrids at the subgrade‑aggregate interface, or use of Class 4 Type C geotextiles. [1][2] Fine‑graded subgrades that must be reinforced before the full‑depth subbase are treated with a wrinkle‑free Class 4 Type C geotextile overlapped at least two feet and then completely covered by subbase material so that equipment does not contact the fabric until it is fully buried. [2] When confinement of the subgrade or sub‑base is required—such as for load‑distribution pads or minor slope reinforcement—a polypropylene biaxial geogrid is specified, whereas polyester biaxial geogrids are used where the primary function is reinforcement rather than confinement. [2] Cement‑grout stabilization is employed when concrete pavements (continuously reinforced slabs, jointed slabs, or bridge‑end slabs) have settled below profile tolerances; grout is injected under pressures up to 200 psi to raise the surface within ±0.02 ft for continuously reinforced sections and within ±0.03 ft for jointed slabs. [2] When a reclaimed road is intended to support a new overlay, stabilization is considered appropriate if the treated layer can achieve an unconfined compressive strength of 300–500 psi in seven days for thin (<3 in.) surface treatments or 280–500 psi for thicker (≥3 in.) overlays, together with a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi and compaction to at least 95 % of maximum density before traffic. [2] All stabilization actions are typically preceded by a thorough pavement condition survey and in‑situ strength testing such as falling weight deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer, or light weight deflectometer, and must be coordinated with the district geotechnical engineer and reflected in design specifications. [1] Placement of chemical or asphalt binders for full‑depth reclamation is permitted only when ambient temperature will remain above 40 °F throughout the required seven‑day cure period and rain is not expected within two hours after placement. [2]

Design inputs and requirements

Designing a subgrade stabilization system begins with assembling a complete picture of the project site. The engineer must locate any low‑CBR zones, frost‑susceptible soils, rock cuts, and characterize groundwater conditions because these features directly affect the choice and amount of stabilizing agent. Traffic information is equally critical; a reliable estimate of future traffic loading is required, and a cost‑benefit analysis generally becomes favorable when the anticipated pavement quantity exceeds 25,000 square yards on a Traffic Pattern Group ranging from one to four (urban and rural interstates or other principal arterials). Material characteristics are defined through a laboratory program that evaluates representative subgrade samples for gradation, classification, liquid and plastic limits, shrinkage limit, unconfined compressive strength, dry density, sulfate, chloride, organic content, and pH when lime or cement is contemplated. Environmental factors such as the presence of freezing temperatures combined with moisture availability are identified because they can trigger frost heave in susceptible soils. Geotechnical parameters are obtained from both field and laboratory testing; in‑situ density, moisture content, resilient modulus or CBR values, and strength assessments using devices such as a falling weight deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer, or light weight deflectometer provide the stiffness profile of the existing subgrade. Existing‑condition data include a recent pavement‑condition survey that documents surface distress, current layer thicknesses, drainage adequacy, and any prior improvements, while proper sampling—ensuring sufficient number, depth, size, and handling of specimens—is essential to support accurate laboratory analysis and mix design. Subgrade stabilization for PennDOT projects is governed by an integrated set of departmental specifications, design procedures, test methods and performance criteria that together define the acceptable condition of existing subgrades, the preparation of new foundations, and the quality‑control program required for successful implementation. The process is anchored in the Full‑Depth Reclamation (FDR) best‑practice framework presented in Appendix J, which enumerates allowable mechanical, chemical, asphalt or other stabilization processes and links each method to a structure‑layer coefficient drawn from Table 9.3, ranging from subbase‑type support for simple pulverization up to higher stiffness values when cement, lime, fly ash, calcium‑chloride or emulsified‑asphalt additives are employed [1]. Design calculations must follow the standard pavement design traffic analysis procedures and incorporate condition surveys conducted in accordance with PennDOT Publication 336 or Publication 343, while the design documents must specify a minimum acceptable resilient modulus for roadbed soils and require that all borrow material meet that modulus [1][2]. In‑situ subgrade strength is verified before rehabilitation using Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, supplemented where appropriate by Dynamic Cone Penetrometer or Light‑Weight Deflectometer tests as detailed in Appendix J sections J.2.C.1 through J.2.C.3, and all material sampling follows the procedures of Appendix J section J.2.D [1]. Mix‑design requirements are set out in Publication 27 (Bulletin 27) Chapter 2, which defines test methods, quality‑control measures and permissible emulsified‑asphalt types for FDR projects; chemically stabilized mixes must achieve a minimum density of 95 % of the laboratory‑determined maximum at optimum moisture as verified on a control strip in accordance with PTM No. 106 [1][2]. For chloride stabilization, a minimum 35 % calcium‑chloride solution must be applied at a rate of 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of depth reclaimed, with the mix‑design and strength criteria described in Appendix J sections J.3.D and J.4.D; emulsified‑asphalt stabilization follows analogous guidance in Appendix J sections J.3.E and J.4.E [1]. Geotechnical approvals impose additional constraints: any California Bearing Ratio (CBR) value exceeding ten requires sign‑off by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, while values above twenty are prohibited for use in the Heukelom‑Klomp modulus correlation, and design calculations must incorporate the required resilient modulus thresholds [1]. Stability of the existing subgrade or embankment foundation is first evaluated in accordance with Section 208.3(c); any permanent deflection greater than one‑half inch on an existing subgrade or greater than one inch on an embankment triggers remedial treatment, and undercutting may be performed to a depth of one foot for deflections between one‑half and three inches and up to three feet for larger deflections, with transition slopes limited to an eight‑horizontal‑to‑one‑vertical ratio [2]. The repaired zone must receive at least eight inches of approved fill before compaction, after which a Class 4 Type C geotextile is placed at the base (optionally overlain by No. 57 aggregate drainage) and a minimum six‑inch subbase layer is installed, followed by a Class 4 Type A geotextile; each stabilization layer is built on two compacted six‑inch lifts of PennDOT No. 2A coarse aggregate placed to the elevation of the next geosynthetic and positioned within ±½ inch of the design grade [2]. Proof‑rolling of the entire embankment or fill foundation is required by Section 206.3, using a 72,000‑lb GVW triaxle dump truck with tire pressures maintained between 100 and 110 psi and travel speed not exceeding 1.5 ft/s; any permanent rutting observed that exceeds one‑half inch is considered unstable and requires corrective action [2]. In‑place moisture and density are determined in accordance with PTM No. 402 using the direct transmission method and verified with a nuclear density/moisture gauge calibrated to AASHTO T310 on an annual basis, with acceptance demanding wet‑density readings within ±2.0 pcf and moisture contents within ±0.8 % of the contractor’s reported values [2]. Compaction criteria are prescribed for all structural foundations, subgrades and any material placed within three feet of the proposed subgrade elevation, which must be compacted to 100 % of the maximum dry density; deeper embankment foundations require at least 97 % of maximum dry density, and moisture content must be maintained between –3 % and optimum and may not exceed optimum by more than three percent [2]. Strength acceptance is defined by a seven‑day unconfined compressive strength of 300 to 500 psi (or 280 psi when an asphalt overlay of three inches or more is provided) and, for asphalt‑stabilized mixes, an indirect tensile strength of at least 50 psi as specified in Bulletin 27 [2]. Geosynthetic design follows Section 200 Earthwork: a Class 2 Type A biaxial polypropylene geogrid meeting ASTM D4354 and possessing a tensile capacity of not less than 2,000 lb/ft in both longitudinal and transverse directions is used, with handling and storage conforming to ASTM D4873; the grid is backed by a Class 4 Type A geotextile whose face wrap extends at least four feet upward and two feet downward from the front edge of the grid, and adjacent strips of both geogrid and backing geotextile must overlap a minimum of twelve inches (four feet when installed perpendicular to traffic) [2]. A continuous sheet of Class 4 Type A separation material covers the prepared subgrade, overlapping adjacent sections by at least twelve inches [2]. Environmental compliance and mix‑design approval are required under Publication 242, and equipment used for pulverizing and blending reclaimed material must be capable of cutting to depths of sixteen inches, reducing particle size to no more than two inches, operating over an eight‑foot width, and incorporating a liquid proportioning system; placement is restricted by weather, requiring ambient temperature above forty degrees Fahrenheit for the seven‑day cure period, rain may not occur within two hours of completion, and cement slurries containing accelerators require written approval and cannot be placed if temperatures are expected to fall below thirty‑five degrees Fahrenheit in the first twenty‑four hours [2]. A quality‑control plan must be submitted at least two weeks before work begins; it must include calibrated equipment, a three‑hundred‑foot test strip demonstrating required application rates and densities, and verification of density per PTM No. 402 for each lot as additional performance requirements [2]. Stabilization techniques such as cement, lime, rock capping, geogrids or geotextiles must be detailed according to Publication 408 specifications (including Sections 221, 738, 210.3(b), 212, 735) and the approved construction‑material criteria of Publication 35, with laboratory testing methods referenced in Publication 293; overall project decisions are coordinated with the District Geotechnical Engineer and incorporated as special provisions within the plans, specifications and estimates (PS&E) [1]. Documentation of existing roadway profiles and post‑stabilization surveyed profile drawings is required for acceptance, while cement grout stabilization must raise pavement within grade tolerances of ±0.02 ft for continuously reinforced concrete slabs or ±0.03 ft for jointed slabs, with continuous pressure limited to 200 psi (briefly to 300 psi and up to 600 psi when bonded) and over‑jacking beyond approximately 0.10 ft necessitating removal and replacement in accordance with Section 516 [2].

Design methods and criteria

Subgrade stabilization typically begins with a field quality‑control plan that requires submission of existing roadway profiles and a post‑stabilization profile report [2]. The in‑place pavement, subbase and subgrade are first pulverized using a self‑propelled rotary reclaimer to a maximum particle size of two inches, after which the reclaimed material may be treated by one of three procedural approaches: simple pulverization with moisture, mechanical addition of aggregate or reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP), or chemical stabilization with additives such as Portland cement, lime, fly ash, lime‑kiln dust, calcium‑chloride solutions, or emulsified asphalt [1][2]. The type and dosage of chemical additive are determined from laboratory mix‑design testing that follows the procedures outlined in PennDOT Publication 27 (Bulletin 27) and the FDR Best Practices (Publication 242), which prescribe required tests such as gradation, Atterberg limits, shrinkage limit, unconfined compressive strength, dry density, sulfate/chloride content, organic content and pH for lime‑treated mixes [1][2]. For calcium‑chloride stabilization a solution of at least 35 % concentration is applied at a rate of 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of reclaimed depth; emulsified‑asphalt stabilization uses the same design framework with specified emulsion grades and aggregate proportions [1]. Design calculations are anchored in California Bearing Ratio (CBR) testing, where laboratory or field CBR values are converted to resilient modulus (M_r) using the Heukelom‑Klomp relationship, typically multiplying the CBR by 1 500, with a reduced factor of 1 000 when the in‑situ density is significantly below maximum dry density [1]. Back‑calculated M_r values obtained from Falling Weight Deflectometer data are adjusted with a correction factor of 0.25 and dynamic k values are halved to bring them into agreement with laboratory measurements [1]. Modeling of stabilized subgrades may employ a CBR value of 20 for rock‑capped sections or a structural coefficient of 0.12 for lime‑ or cement‑treated layers, while the native subgrade retains its project‑specific CBR [1]. Compaction targets are set at 97 % of maximum dry density for deep embankment foundations and 100 % within three feet of the proposed grade, with moisture contents ranging from three percent below optimum to the optimum value; special rolling is performed in the presence of the Representative using approved rollers such as pneumatic‑tire rollers, sheepsfoot or pad foot rollers delivering at least 160 psi contact pressure, smooth steel‑drum vibratory rollers providing a minimum dynamic force of 50 000 lb, or a loaded triaxle dump truck [2]. If permanent deflection after rolling exceeds allowable limits (½ in for existing subgrades or spread footings, 1 in for raised embankments), the Representative may direct undercutting and replacement of unsuitable material with Class 4 Type C geotextile, optional No. 57 aggregate drainage, backfill of approved borrow compacted per specifications, followed by a Class 4 Type A geotextile and at least six inches of subbase before final compaction [2]. When geosynthetics are specified, the stabilization sequence starts with a Class 2 Type A biaxial geogrid placed against the slope face and backed by a Class 4 Type A geotextile extending four feet above and two feet below the grid; adjacent strips must overlap at least twelve inches and each layer is backfilled with two six‑inch lifts of PennDOT No. 2A coarse aggregate compacted within ±½ inch of design elevation according to Section 350.3 [2]. The blended material, after being mixed with any required aggregates, reclaimed asphalt pavement or stabilizing additives at the mix‑design moisture content verified by ASTM D2216, ASTM D44643 or AASHTO T265, is placed with a motor grader and compacted to a minimum of ninety‑five percent (or ninety‑seven percent for deep foundations) of the laboratory‑determined maximum dry density using the procedures in PTM No. 106; nuclear density gauges calibrated per AASHTO T310 and employing the direct transmission method of PTM No. 402 are used on a control strip to confirm target densities before curing and strength testing [2]. Proof‑rolling of the completed embankment is carried out with a 72 000‑lb GVW triaxle dump truck maintaining tire pressures of 100–110 psi and travel speed not exceeding 1.5 ft/s, and any rutting greater than one‑half inch triggers remediation by excavation, drying, recompaction or replacement with approved granular fill (maximum particle size two inches) [2]. All compaction quality is documented on Form CS‑206 and moisture‑density results are recorded on Forms TR‑4276AC/AD, with daily gauge standardization required; the mix‑design process also includes a cost‑benefit analysis to select the most appropriate strengthening technique [1][2]. In cases where cement grout lifting is employed, holes are drilled as specified and grout is mixed at a plant and pumped through expanding rubber packers with pressures limited to 200 psi continuously, up to 300 psi for short periods, and brief spikes of 600 psi only when the pavement is bonded; over‑jacking beyond ten centimeters must be corrected by grinding or replacement [2].

Permanent deformation limits are tightly defined: after special rolling or proof‑rolling the allowable permanent rutting is limited to 0.5 in for existing subgrades or spread footings and to 1 in for embankment foundations, any exceedance triggering corrective undercutting of one foot for moderate settlements or three feet for severe cases [2]; proof‑rolling must be conducted with a loaded triaxle maintaining tire pressures between 100 and 110 psi, traveling no faster than 1.5 ft/s, and observed rutting greater than half an inch is deemed unstable [2]. Strength criteria after curing require an unconfined compressive strength of 300–500 psi for thin overlays (or 280–500 psi for thicker layers) measured at seven days per ASTM D1633, a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi for asphalt‑stabilized sections per Bulletin 27, and any strengths exceeding 900 psi trigger rework [2]. Environmental assumptions prohibit placement when ambient temperature is expected to fall below 40 °F during the required seven‑day cure period, and cement‑slurry mixes with accelerators may not be placed if temperatures are anticipated to drop below 35 °F within the first twenty‑four hours; work must also cease in rain or when rain is forecast within two hours of completion [2]. A quality‑control plan must be submitted at least two weeks before mobilization, and a 300‑ft test strip must demonstrate that the required density can be achieved before full production proceeds; each lot thereafter is inspected for compliance with the specified density targets, providing the documented verification that underpins the reliability requirements of subgrade stabilization [2]. All of these design criteria, assumptions, thresholds, safety factors and approval processes together constitute the reliability framework governing subgrade stabilization.

Materials and mixture design

Subgrade stabilization specifications begin by naming the stabilizing agent to be employed [1][2]. When a chloride‑based treatment is selected, the guidelines require either calcium chloride or magnesium chloride delivered as a solution containing no less than 35 % active ingredient, with an application rate of roughly 0.1 to 0.15 gallon per square yard for each inch of depth reclaimed [1]. An emulsified asphalt stabilizer may be used instead, its type and quantity being established through the mix‑design procedure prescribed for PennDOT projects [1][2]. Additional chemical or mechanical agents that may be specified include Portland cement, hydrated lime, rock capping, geogrids, or geotextiles, each with their own performance criteria [1][2]. Rock capping, when called for, shall consist of a layer ranging from one to three feet thick composed of R‑3 rock, AASHTO No. 1, or the best available rock material [1]. Geotextiles required for subgrade work must satisfy Class 4 specifications; Type A fabric is used for layer separation across the full lane width (minimum eight feet), while Type C may be employed where stabilization is desired [1][2]. When a biaxial polypropylene geogrid is incorporated, it shall meet ASTM D4354 and D4873, possess a tensile‑strength unit of at least 2,000 lb/ft in both the machine‑direction and cross‑direction, and be classified as Class 2 Type A (or B) [2]. The geogrid must be backed by a Class 4 Type A geotextile that extends a minimum of four feet above and two feet below the front edge of the grid, with adjacent strips overlapping at least twelve inches and placement tolerances held within plus‑or‑minus half an inch relative to design elevation [2]. Prior to selecting any chemical stabilizer, a laboratory testing program is required that characterizes the native soil by gradation (including hydrometer analysis), classification, liquid limit, plastic limit, plasticity index, shrinkage limit, unconfined compressive strength and dry density, and also conducts chemical analyses for sulfate, chloride, organic content, and pH when lime is considered [1]. The stabilization mix shall combine the fine‑drained‑reclaimed (FDR) material with any required coarse aggregate—specified as PennDOT No. 2A (or No. 57 for drainage layers when directed)—reclaimed asphalt pavement if used, a stabilizing additive such as Portland cement, asphalt emulsion, other chemical stabilizer, or calcium chloride, and water in proportions dictated by the approved project mix design [2]. The coarse‑aggregate component is placed in two compacted six‑inch layers for each stabilization course, providing at least eight inches of approved material over the geotextile before compaction and a minimum six‑inch subbase over the top geotextile prior to final compaction; when the existing subgrade already consists of coarse aggregate (AASHTO No. 1 or 3, R‑rock, shot rock), an additional layer of No. 2A aggregate is spread and compacted on the proof‑rolled surface to fill voids before installing the geosynthetic [2]. All material proportions must follow the mix design’s recommended optimum moisture content, with field application rates for stabilizer and water verified on a test strip and the moisture of the compacted layer not exceeding the optimum value by more than three percent [2]. Compaction shall achieve at least ninety‑five percent of the laboratory‑determined maximum dry density for the mix [2]. For chemically stabilized subgrades, the cured material is required to develop a seven‑day unconfined compressive strength between three hundred and five hundred pounds per square inch as measured in accordance with ASTM D1633 [2].

Subgrade stabilization begins with systematic assessment of existing subgrade strength, typically by mapping CBR values across the site to identify low‑CBR zones that need improvement [1]. When isolated low‑CBR areas are found, a targeted improvement is designed to raise those values to the representative project level in consultation with the geotechnical engineer [1]. Selection of stabilizing material—cement, lime, rock capping, geogrid, geotextile, calcium chloride, emulsified asphalt, or mechanical aggregate additions—is made through a formal mix‑design procedure that balances structural capacity, durability, constructability and overall performance [1][2]. The mix design is approved for the project and follows the FDR Best Practices (Publication 242), specifying which stabilizing agent(s) will be used together with reclaimed material and any supplemental aggregates or RAP required [2]. A comprehensive laboratory testing program characterizes soil gradation, moisture limits, strength indices, chemical content and other relevant properties; these results dictate both the type and quantity of stabilizer needed [1]. For cement or lime stabilization the additive rate is derived from the tests and applied using a structural coefficient of 0.12 together with the existing subgrade CBR, while rock capping is modeled with a CBR value of 20 [1]. When calcium chloride is employed it must be supplied as at least a 35 % solution and applied at 0.1–0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of reclaimed depth to achieve cementing strength and reduce freeze‑thaw susceptibility [1]. If an emulsified asphalt stabilizer is selected, the mix design follows established guidelines to determine appropriate emulsion type and dosage that provide water resistance and fatigue durability, allowing integration with other additives such as granular material, cement or lime [1]. Mechanical stabilization may involve pulverizing existing pavement, reshaping and recompacting it, then adding aggregate or RAP to improve gradation, with the proportion of each additive fine‑tuned based on laboratory results to avoid brittleness while meeting load‑bearing and long‑term durability criteria [1]. All components are blended at the moisture content recommended by the mix design, with field moisture kept within three percent of the optimum value; a machine equipped with an integral computerized liquid‑proportioning system automatically meters water and any liquid additive in proportion to depth of cut, width of cut and machine speed, records volumes delivered, and ensures thorough mixing [2]. Before full‑scale production a 300‑foot test strip is constructed to demonstrate that the selected materials and application rates achieve the target density and shaping, with compaction performed to at least ninety‑five percent of the laboratory maximum dry density determined at optimum moisture [2]. After curing, chemically stabilized sections are required to develop an unconfined compressive strength between three hundred and five hundred psi after seven days as measured by ASTM D1633, providing verification that the stabilized layer meets structural, durability, constructability and performance criteria before traffic is permitted [2]. A cost‑benefit analysis confirms that the chosen mixture satisfies the required resilient‑modulus and overall project goals while remaining practical to place and maintain [1].

Structural details and interfaces

Subgrade stabilization requires installation of reinforcement at the interface between the existing subgrade and the overlying aggregate, most commonly achieved with a biaxial geogrid system[1][2]. The geogrid must meet a minimum tensile capacity of 2,000 lb/ft in both longitudinal and transverse directions and is fabricated with stiff ribs that are either integrally formed or welded to provide continuous load‑distribution across the stabilized zone[2]. To prevent mixing of dissimilar materials, a full‑width Class 4 Type A geotextile separation layer is placed over the entire lane width on all treated sections; where the existing California Bearing Ratio falls below three, a Class 4 Type C geotextile may be substituted to also furnish lateral restraint[1][2]. At the base of any undercut, a Class 4 Type C geotextile is laid on the prepared subgrade surface, followed by a minimum eight‑inch layer of approved backfill—often incorporating a No. 57 aggregate drainage path—before compaction[2]. Over this backfill a second geotextile, Class 4 Type A, is positioned prior to placement of the subbase, which must be at least six inches thick and compacted in place[2]. The biaxial geogrid is then installed, with its face wrapped by an additional layer of Class 4 Type A geotextile that extends a minimum four feet above and two feet below the leading edge of the grid to provide slope‑face reinforcement and lateral confinement[2]. All adjacent strips of both the geogrid and its backing geotextile are overlapped a minimum of twelve inches along parallel edges to create continuous joints, while separate rolls of geotextile used on fine‑graded subgrade must overlap at least two feet and may be secured with steel pins before the subbase is placed[2]. Between each geosynthetic layer, two compacted lifts of coarse aggregate, each six inches thick, are placed up to the elevation of the next stabilization layer, forming stepped backfill that transfers loads through the stabilized zone[2]. Where abrupt changes in subgrade conditions occur, gradual transitions are provided using embankment fill or non‑frost‑susceptible granular material to maintain uniform support across the pavement structure[1]. Rock capping may be employed as an additional treatment, consisting of one to three feet of R‑3 (or equivalent) rock protected by a granular chase layer, to provide a durable surface and improve load distribution[1]. Geometric transitions at the beginning and end of any undercut are limited to side slopes no steeper than an 8 H:1 V ratio, ensuring positive drainage and preventing concentration of stresses[2]. For membrane‑type reinforcement used in bridge approaches, a continuous flat‑laid sheet is pulled tight and anchored within half an inch of the design elevation; splicing or field seams are prohibited and adjacent sheets must overlap twelve inches along parallel edges, with partial rolls laid perpendicular to traffic extending at least four feet beyond the wrapped face before another overlap can occur[2]. No vehicular traffic is permitted on any geosynthetic layer until a minimum of six inches of loose backfill cover has been placed, preserving the integrity of joints and reinforcement during construction[2]. All reinforcement, joint detailing, load‑transfer measures, and transition provisions are executed in accordance with standard drawings and Section 212 specifications to ensure proper anchorage, drainage slopes, and overall performance of the subgrade stabilization system[2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Effective subgrade stabilization starts with creating a well‑drained pavement system by first locating any wet zones in the existing subgrade and installing suitable drainage such as base drains or other appropriate measures before reclamation work begins [1][2]. Drainage paths may be excavated beneath an undercut and backfilled with No. 57 aggregate to convey excess water away from the repaired area, while the primary foundation drain is coordinated with the bridge structure plan rather than provided as a separate GSBA drain [2]. Where transverse drainage is required, the embankment is constructed according to standard drawings and the subbase surface is given a positive slope to promote runoff, further protecting the stabilized layer from moisture infiltration [2]. Moisture content of the fill material is tightly controlled by compacting only within a band that does not fall more than three percent below the optimum moisture value and does not exceed the optimum; any material outside this range must be dried through disking or scarifying before compaction [2]. In‑place verification of both moisture and density is performed with the direct transmission method prescribed in PTM No. 402, accepting results within ±2.0 pcf for wet density and ±0.8 % moisture, and if a shift in the moisture‑density relationship is detected, new target values are established for all subsequent work [2]. To further mitigate moisture effects, stabilizers that both cement particles and depress the freezing point of the reclaimed soil are specified, typically using a minimum 35 % calcium‑chloride solution applied at 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of depth reclaimed, or an equivalent magnesium‑chloride treatment [1]. Emulsified asphalt may be incorporated into the mix design to improve resistance to water‑related damage and enhance fatigue performance under repeated loading [1]. When frost‑susceptible soils are encountered, they are removed and replaced with a sufficiently thick layer of non‑frost‑susceptible material that insulates the roadbed from freezing temperatures; this replacement is commonly combined with a stronger granular base course to maintain bearing capacity during thaw periods [1]. Gradual transitions at subgrade discontinuities and the use of appropriately sized granular foundations further reduce differential heave caused by moisture or climate variations, ensuring consistent performance without necessarily increasing overall structural thickness [1]. Geosynthetic layers used in stabilization are protected from environmental exposure by storing them elevated off the ground, keeping them free of debris, shielding them from temperatures above 140 °F and direct sunlight, fine‑grading the subgrade before placement, laying the geotextile flat without wrinkles with at least two feet overlap, covering it with the full depth of subbase within two weeks, and prohibiting equipment traffic on it until fully covered [2]. These combined drainage, moisture‑control, climate‑mitigation, and environmental‑protection measures collectively address the influence of drainage, moisture, climate, and other environmental conditions on subgrade stabilization performance [1][2].

Verification and expected performance

The completed subgrade stabilization is verified through a coordinated series of compliance, constructability, performance, and consistency checks that reference the project specifications and applicable guidance documents [1][2]. Prior to construction the contractor must submit a quality‑control plan at least two weeks in advance, detailing calibrated equipment, moisture‑additive rates, weather limits, and the preparation of a 300‑foot test strip that demonstrates the full reclamation process, thereby establishing the baseline for compliance with mix‑design procedures outlined in Bulletin 27 and Appendix J [1][2]. The mix design, including stabilizer type and dosage, is confirmed against those criteria while the as‑built thickness of the reclaimed layer is measured and compared to the designed thickness; any drainage deficiencies identified during pre‑construction surveys are corrected before testing to ensure constructability and durability [1]. During construction each 3 000‑square‑yard lot is compacted to achieve at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum dry density at optimum moisture, with n=1 densities determined in accordance with PTM No. 402 and ten random nuclear gauge readings taken after each compaction pass; moisture must be within three percent of optimum, and all equipment is required to be calibrated before use [2]. The foundation is inspected and approved by the project Representative before any fill or geosynthetics are placed, and proof‑rolling with a loaded triaxle dump truck at no more than one mile per hour is performed; permanent deformation greater than one‑half inch for existing subgrade or greater than one inch for raised embankments triggers excavation, drying, undercutting to prescribed depths, and recompaction [2]. A special‑rolling pass conducted in the Representative’s presence measures permanent set, with corrective actions such as undercutting one foot for 0.5–3 inches of set and up to three feet for greater than three inches, followed by installation of the specified Class‑4 geotextile and drainage or borrow material as needed before further compaction [2]. Geosynthetic placement follows strict tolerances: the primary stabilization geosynthetic (typically a Class 2 Type A biaxial geogrid) is laid first, backed by a Class 4 Type A geotextile extending at least four feet upward and two feet downward from its front edge; adjacent strips overlap by no less than twelve inches and all layers must be positioned within plus or minus one‑half inch of the plan elevation, with damage smaller than three square feet patched using material extending three feet beyond the defect and larger damage requiring full removal and replacement [2]. Backfill above each geosynthetic consists of two compacted six‑inch layers of the specified coarse aggregate, each placed and compacted to the required density before the next stabilization layer is installed, thereby ensuring constructability and consistent material performance [2]. Upon completion a roadway profile report containing surveyed drawings of existing, design, and as‑built grades for each stabilized area is submitted; the achieved grade must be within approximately 0.02 ft for continuous concrete or 0.03 ft for jointed slabs, and any overjacking greater than about 0.10 ft mandates grinding or full removal and replacement of the pavement [2]. Post‑construction field testing includes a Falling Weight Deflectometer run to demonstrate that the rebuilt subgrade attains the required stiffness, supplemented by Dynamic Cone Penetrometer or Light‑Weight Deflectometer measurements to verify subgrade strength; laboratory and field results are used to confirm that the resilient modulus meets or exceeds the minimum value prescribed in the pavement design [1]. California Bearing Ratio values are evaluated against approved ranges, with any CBR greater than ten requiring approval by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and no CBR exceeding twenty permitted; if uncertainty exists regarding laboratory‑tested CBR, additional field testing such as FWD or DCP is performed to back‑calculate Mr for validation [1]. After a seven‑day cure, laboratory specimens of the stabilized mixes are tested, requiring asphalt‑stabilized mixes to achieve at least fifty psi indirect tensile strength and chemically stabilized mixes to reach an unconfined compressive strength between three hundred and five hundred psi, thereby confirming performance criteria [2]. Independent verification density and moisture tests are performed on a sample of the contractor’s results; independent gauge readings must be within ±2 pcf wet density and ±0.8 % moisture of the contractor’s values, with any discrepancy prompting gauge checks and corrective action, ensuring that all acceptance data are recorded on prescribed forms as documented evidence of compliance and consistency with project requirements [2]. When all of these compliance, constructability, performance, and documentation checks are satisfied and the improvement limits, quantities, and specifications have been coordinated and signed off by the District Geotechnical Engineer, the subgrade stabilization is deemed to meet the contractual and engineering standards for the project [1].

Subgrade stabilization aims to deliver a reclaimed base that satisfies defined density, deformation, surface‑smoothness and early‑age strength criteria, thereby providing long service life, resistance to cracking or rutting, and acceptable ride quality. Performance is judged successful when permanent deformation after special rolling does not exceed half an inch for existing subgrades or cuts and does not exceed one inch for embankment foundations. Density requirements are verified by in‑place moisture‑density testing according to AASHTO T191 or AASHTO 310 at several random locations, with successive compaction passes required to produce less than a one‑percent increase in density, indicating a stable mass. The target dry density must reach at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum determined on a control strip; more stringent zones call for 97 % of maximum dry density for embankments deeper than three feet and 100 % for structure foundations, subgrades or material within three feet of the proposed subgrade elevation. Nuclear‑gauge readings are taken after each compaction pass in accordance with PTM No. 402 to confirm compliance. Surface quality is measured by checking the finished stabilized base with a ten‑foot straightedge; the average surface irregularity must be no greater than half an inch both transversely and longitudinally. Early‑age strength is evaluated using laboratory specimens prepared per ASTM D1633 or Bulletin 27, requiring a seven‑day unconfined compressive strength of 300–500 psi for thin overlays (280–500 psi for thicker sections) or an indirect tensile strength of at least 50 psi. Acceptance is granted only when the deformation limits, density targets, smoothness tolerance and strength thresholds are all satisfied, after which traffic may be opened. [2]
