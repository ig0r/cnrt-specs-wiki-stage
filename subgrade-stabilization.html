Subgrade Stabilization

Part of: Subgrade is-subtopic-of
Subgrade Stabilization is a topic under Subgrade.

Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-29

Subgrade stabilization is a remedial treatment applied when the existing support layer of a pavement lacks sufficient structural capacity, bearing strength, stiffness, or drainage to sustain the designed thickness and anticipated traffic loads, typically manifested by low CBR values, excessive permanent deformation, or moisture‑saturated conditions [1][2]. The process creates a load‑bearing mass—either by excavating and replacing weak material with properly dried, compacted fill or by treating the in‑place soil with mechanical blending and chemical agents such as cement, lime, fly ash, calcium‑chloride solutions, or emulsified asphalt—that induces particle cementation, raises stiffness and strength, improves water resistance, and lowers the freezing point to produce a uniform, durable foundation [1][2]. By achieving prescribed compaction densities, moisture control, and early seven‑day unconfined compressive or indirect tensile strengths (300–500 psi or 50 psi respectively), the stabilized subgrade limits rutting to half an inch in existing sections, supports pavement geometry within tight elevation tolerances, and thereby prevents premature distress while extending service life [1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Subgrade stabilization is applied when the existing subgrade or reclaimed base does not possess adequate structural capacity, bearing strength, stiffness, or drainage to support the designed pavement thickness and anticipated traffic loads [1][2]. The engineering problem manifests as weak, moisture‑saturated soils that often exhibit California Bearing Ratio values below five or low resilient modulus, leading to excessive permanent deformation such as rutting or settlement that exceeds half an inch in existing subgrades and one inch in embankment foundations [1][2]. These conditions also generate differential heave, reflective cracking, frost damage, fatigue cracking, and loss of load‑distribution capacity, which can prevent the pavement from meeting strict elevation tolerances for concrete slabs (±0.02 ft for continuously reinforced concrete and ±0.03 ft for jointed slabs or bridge ends) [1][2]. Excess water within the support layer further compromises material integrity, reducing bearing strength and accelerating freeze‑thaw deterioration, so reliable drainage is a critical performance need addressed by stabilization [1][2]. To meet these needs, the subgrade is either excavated to at least one foot depth and replaced with suitable material that is dried and compacted to a minimum of 95 % of the laboratory maximum dry density, or it is treated in‑place with mechanical, chemical, cementitious, lime, fly ash, emulsified asphalt, chlorides, or other stabilizing agents until a seven‑day unconfined compressive strength of 300–500 psi (or an indirect tensile strength of 50 psi for asphalt‑stabilized mixes) is achieved [2][1]. The addition of stabilizers creates particle cementation that raises stiffness and load‑bearing capacity, improves water resistance, lowers the freezing point of the material, and thereby produces a uniform, durable foundation that satisfies required CBR or design criteria [1][2]. Confinement and load distribution are further enhanced by incorporating reinforcement such as polypropylene biaxial geogrids, rock capping, or full‑depth reclamation, which help prevent future settlement and maintain pavement geometry under traffic loading [2][1]. By addressing the combined problems of insufficient strength, excessive deformation, moisture susceptibility, and inadequate drainage, subgrade stabilization ensures a stable support layer that prevents early distress and extends the service life of the pavement structure [1][2].

Subgrade stabilization must create a load‑bearing mass that distributes traffic loads and limits permanent deformation, with rutting not to exceed one‑half inch in existing subgrades and not more than one inch in embankment foundations. The design therefore requires achieving prescribed compaction densities: at least 97 % of maximum dry density for all layers except the upper three feet, which must be compacted to 100 % of maximum dry density, and a minimum in‑place density of 95 % of laboratory maximum for chemically or asphalt‑stabilized material. Moisture content must be controlled within specified limits—soil no more than three percent below optimum, Type 1 granular within plus or minus two percent of optimum, and Type 2 kept surface‑damp—to ensure proper compaction and strength development. When calcium‑chloride is employed, a minimum 35 % solution applied at 0.1–0.15 gal/yd² per inch of depth provides particle cementation and lowers the freezing point, thereby enhancing freeze‑thaw durability. If emulsified asphalt is selected, the mix design follows Bulletin 27 procedures to improve resistance to water‑induced damage and to increase fatigue performance under traffic loading. Strength development must reach unconfined compressive values between 300 and 500 psi within seven days (or 280–500 psi when a three‑inch or greater overlay is present), with indirect tensile strength of at least fifty psi for asphalt‑stabilized mixes. The stabilized layer must be kept damp during curing so that moisture remains within three percent of optimum until the seven‑day strength is verified, and it must remain protected from direct sunlight, ultraviolet radiation, temperatures above 140 °F, and contamination by mud, dust or debris. Geosynthetic reinforcement, when used, shall be placed within ±½ inch of plan elevation, interlock mechanically with surrounding material, possess a tensile capacity not less than 2,000 lb/ft in both longitudinal and transverse directions, and be covered by at least six inches of loose backfill before any vehicle traffic is permitted. Safety provisions require keeping rolling equipment at least ten feet from bridge abutments and other structures, avoiding utility damage, prohibiting traffic on exposed geosynthetics until adequate cover exists, and limiting vehicle loads until the specified strength is achieved; only limited light vehicles may be allowed thereafter. Serviceability objectives demand a smooth, correctly graded riding surface with surface irregularities not exceeding one‑half inch over a ten‑foot template, cross‑slope of at least ¼ inch per foot for drainage, and slope geometry that retains its intended shape (undercut slopes no steeper than an 8H:1V ratio). Together these functional strength gains, structural density and strength criteria, durability protections against environmental and construction stresses, safety restrictions during construction, and serviceability tolerances satisfy the overall objectives for a stabilized subgrade. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

Subgrade stabilization is indicated whenever the existing support layers of a pavement are found to be deficient and require strengthening, such as when California Bearing Ratio values fall below five percent or when special rolling reveals permanent deflections exceeding one‑half inch in existing subgrades or cut sections and greater than one inch in embankment foundations; these conditions, together with a cost‑benefit analysis that typically favors treatment for projects of at least 25,000 square yards on Traffic Pattern Groups 1‑4, trigger the need for stabilization [1][2]. The approach is applicable to a broad spectrum of pavement structures, including distressed asphalt wearing courses and concrete pavements—continuously reinforced slabs, jointed slabs, and bridge‑end slabs—where it may be used to lift slabs within tight grade tolerances (±0.02 ft for continuous slabs and ±0.03 ft for jointed or bridge‑end slabs) provided lifts do not exceed ten inches, as well as to full‑depth reclamation projects on distressed roads that rely on in‑place material recycling and drainage restoration [1][2]. Project conditions that call for stabilization encompass existing subgrades, cut‑section subgrades, raised embankments, bridge approach embankments, and structure foundations with spread footings on soil; the method is also employed when a project representative judges the subgrade to be unstable or when permanent deflections range from greater than 0.5 inch up to three inches, prompting excavation of one foot depth, or larger deflections that may require up to three feet of undercutting before replacement [2][1]. Stabilization can be achieved through several mechanisms: pulverizing the existing material with moisture and mechanically blending aggregate or reclaimed asphalt pavement to improve gradation; chemically amending the mix with dry or slurry applications of Portland cement, lime, fly ash, lime kiln dust, calcium chloride or magnesium chloride solutions at a minimum concentration of 35 % applied at 0.1–0.15 gal/yd² per inch of reclaimed depth; using emulsified or foamed asphalt; or employing combinations of these additives, all of which are mixed according to the approved mix design and compacted to at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum dry density [1][2]. Additional options for very weak subgrades include rock capping with one to three feet of R‑3 (or equivalent) rock, placement of Class 4 Type A separation geosynthetics over coarse‑aggregate subgrades such as AASHTO No. 1 or No. 3, fine‑graded subgrades reinforced with Class 4 Type C geotextile fabric overlapped a minimum of two feet and protected from equipment until the full depth of subbase is placed, and use of Class 2 biaxial geogrids—polypropylene grids for confinement and polyester grids for reinforcement—to meet tensile requirements for load‑distribution pads or slope reinforcement [1][2]. The stabilized layer must attain a seven‑day unconfined compressive strength of 300 to 500 psi for chemically stabilized bases supporting overlays thinner than three inches, or an indirect tensile strength of at least 50 psi for asphalt‑stabilized bases, and curing is permitted only when air temperature remains at or above 40 °F throughout the required period with no rain within two hours of placement [2][1]. Execution typically involves special rolling to identify deflection problems, undercutting or excavating the failed subgrade to the prescribed depth, laying a geotextile free of wrinkles and overlapped as specified, backfilling in staged six‑inch lifts of PennDOT No. 2A aggregate (or approved borrow material), compacting each lift to meet density criteria, and finally placing the subbase; when rock capping or geogrid reinforcement is required, these are installed prior to backfill according to the design specifications [2][1].

Design inputs and requirements

Designing a subgrade stabilization system begins with assembling a complete picture of the project site. The engineer must locate any low‑CBR zones, frost‑susceptible soils, rock cuts, and characterize groundwater conditions because these features directly affect the choice and amount of stabilizing agent. Traffic information is equally critical; a reliable estimate of future traffic loading is required, and a cost‑benefit analysis generally becomes favorable when the anticipated pavement quantity exceeds 25,000 square yards on a Traffic Pattern Group ranging from one to four (urban and rural interstates or other principal arterials). Material characteristics are defined through a laboratory program that evaluates representative subgrade samples for gradation, classification, liquid and plastic limits, shrinkage limit, unconfined compressive strength, dry density, sulfate, chloride, organic content, and pH when lime or cement is contemplated. Environmental factors such as the presence of freezing temperatures combined with moisture availability are identified because they can trigger frost heave in susceptible soils. Geotechnical parameters are obtained from both field and laboratory testing; in‑situ density, moisture content, resilient modulus or CBR values, and strength assessments using devices such as a falling weight deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer, or light weight deflectometer provide the stiffness profile of the existing subgrade. Existing‑condition data include a recent pavement‑condition survey that documents surface distress, current layer thicknesses, drainage adequacy, and any prior improvements, while proper sampling—ensuring sufficient number, depth, size, and handling of specimens—is essential to support accurate laboratory analysis and mix design. [1]

Subgrade stabilization on PennDOT projects is directed by an integrated set of design, testing and construction specifications that stem from the Full‑Depth Reclamation (FDR) best‑practice framework in Appendix J and the Earthwork, Incidental Construction, Geosynthetic Bridge Approach and Project Manual sections, with the primary agency standard found in Publication 408 Section 344 governing allowable mechanical, chemical, asphalt or other stabilization processes and linking each method to a structure‑layer coefficient drawn from Table 9.3 [1][2]. The stabilizing agents that may be used—including cement, lime, fly ash, calcium‑chloride solutions, emulsified‑asphalt and other chemically reactive additives—are prescribed in Publication 408 as well as the related sections on geogrids, geotextiles and other reinforcement materials (Sections 221, 738, 210.3(b), 212, 735) and must conform to the approved construction‑material criteria of Publication 35 [1][2]. Design calculations follow standard pavement design traffic analysis procedures and incorporate condition surveys per PennDOT Publications 336 or 343, while the subgrade’s resilient modulus and California Bearing Ratio values are required to meet minimum acceptable levels; any CBR exceeding ten must be signed off by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and values above twenty are prohibited for use in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation [1][2]. Mix‑design requirements are set out in Bulletin 27 Chapter 2 and PTM No. 106, with laboratory mix designs verified on a 300‑foot test strip before field implementation to demonstrate that the minimum density specified by PTM No. 402 can be achieved for each lot [1][2]. Compaction of FDR material must reach at least 95 % of the laboratory‑maximum dry density at optimum moisture content, as measured by nuclear density readings after each pass, and moisture may not exceed the optimum by more than three percent; embankment foundations deeper than three feet require a minimum of 97 % of maximum dry density while the top three feet must be compacted to 100 % [2][1]. The equipment used for special rolling is limited to pneumatic‑tire rollers capable of 26–28 tons, sheepsfoot or pad rollers with a minimum foot pressure of 160 psi, and vibratory drums delivering a dynamic force of 50 000 lb, with rolling speeds not exceeding two miles per hour (approximately 1.5 ft/s) [2][1]. Proof‑rolling must be performed with a loaded 72 000‑lb GVW triaxle dump truck at tire pressures between 100 and 110 psi and vehicle speed limited to 1.5 ft/s, and any permanent deflection greater than one‑half inch in existing subgrade or one inch in raised embankments is deemed unsatisfactory, triggering remedial actions such as undercutting, geotextile installation or placement of approved fill layers [2][1]. Geosynthetic stabilization requires material submittals at least thirty days before construction, placement within ±½ inch of the plan elevation, a minimum twelve‑inch overlap along parallel edges and a two‑foot overlap between adjacent rolls, with damaged areas larger than three square feet removed and replaced; no equipment may travel on the fabric until it is fully covered by a six‑inch loose fill layer, and temperature protection above 140 °F and off‑ground storage are mandated [2][1]. When biaxial geogrids are used for confinement, they must be polypropylene Class 2 grids of stiff‑rib Type A or B meeting a tensile capacity of at least 2 000 lb/ft, conforming to ASTM D4354 and handled according to ASTM D4873 [2][1]. Environmental constraints prohibit placement of FDR materials when ambient temperature is expected to fall below 40 °F during the required seven‑day cure period, and cement slurries with accelerating admixtures require written approval if temperatures may drop below 35 °F within the first twenty‑four hours [2][1]. Post‑cure performance criteria demand an indirect tensile strength of at least 50 psi for asphalt‑stabilized mixes (per Bulletin 27) or an unconfined compressive strength between 300 and 500 psi for cementitious stabilizations, with final acceptance also requiring compliance with surface tolerance limits and the density and strength criteria outlined in Sections 344.3(e)3, (e)6 and (g) of Publication 408 [1][2]. The completed stabilized layer must be graded to within 0.02 ft (or 0.03 ft for jointed slabs) of the prescribed string line, and grout pressures are limited to 200 psi continuous (with short‑term peaks up to 300 psi and brief spikes to 600 psi only when bonded), ensuring that all stabilization work conforms to the governing specifications and project constraints.

Design methods and criteria

Subgrade stabilization begins with the preparation of an in‑place material through pulverization of the existing asphalt surface, underlying aggregate base and subgrade using a road reclaimer; the reclaimed mass can then be treated by one of three procedural approaches—pure pulverization with moisture only, mechanical augmentation with additional aggregate or reclaimed asphalt pavement to improve gradation and strength, or chemical addition of wet or dry stabilizers such as Portland cement, lime, fly ash, lime‑kiln dust, calcium‑chloride solutions, or emulsified asphalt, the type and dosage being determined from laboratory mix‑design testing—following the mix‑design methodology prescribed in PennDOT Publication 27 (Bulletin 27) which defines required laboratory tests, quality‑control steps and criteria for selecting stabilizer type and proportioning[1].

The design calculations are anchored on California Bearing Ratio (CBR) testing; laboratory or field CBR values are converted to resilient modulus (M_r) using the Heukelom‑Klomp relationship, typically by multiplying the CBR value by 1 500, although when in‑situ density is significantly below maximum dry density the multiplier is reduced to 1 000. Back‑calculated M_r obtained from Falling Weight Deflectometer data are adjusted with a correction factor of 0.25 and dynamic k values are halved to bring them into agreement with laboratory measurements; for modeling purposes a CBR of 20 may be used for rock‑capped sections while a structural coefficient of 0.12 is applied to lime‑ or cement‑treated layers, the native subgrade retaining its project‑specific CBR[1].

A field quality‑control plan documents the existing roadway profile and defines the post‑stabilization target grade; the first mechanical operation is proof‑rolling the entire embankment or fill with a loaded triaxle dump truck of 72 000 lb gross vehicle weight, maintaining tire pressures between 100 and 110 psi and limiting travel speed to one mile per hour[2]. Moisture‑density relationships are established in accordance with PTM No. 106 to determine optimum moisture content (OMC) and maximum dry density (MDD), after which in‑place moisture and density are measured with a direct‑transmission nuclear gauge as prescribed by PTM No. 402, applying AASHTO compaction criteria of at least 97 % of MDD for the full thickness except the upper three feet, which must reach 100 % of MDD; reclaimed‑material mixes require a minimum of 95 % of laboratory MDD[2].

Equipment selection follows soil type: fine‑grained or cohesive soils are treated with a sheepsfoot or pad roller delivering a minimum contact pressure of 160 psi, while granular materials receive special rolling from a smooth steel‑drum vibratory roller that must develop at least 50 000 lb of dynamic force; pneumatic rollers capable of 26–28 ton loads may also be employed where appropriate[2]. Permanent‑deflection limits govern acceptance—existing subgrades are limited to half an inch of rutting and embankments to one inch—and when these limits are exceeded the area is undercut, excavating one foot for deflections between 0.5 and 3 inches or three feet for larger settlements, followed by installation of a Class 4 geotextile with approved aggregate backfill before recompaction[2].

When Full‑Depth Reclamation (FDR) is used, a self‑propelled rotary reclaimer pulverizes pavement, subbase and subgrade to a maximum particle size of two inches while simultaneously mixing water and the selected additives—such as asphalt, Portland cement, calcium chloride or other chemicals—in accordance with the mix design approved in Publication 242; the machine’s computerized liquid‑proportioning system regulates water application based on cut depth, width and travel speed, and a 300‑ft test strip is constructed to verify that the target density can be achieved before full production proceeds[2]. Density of each 3 000‑square‑yard lot is confirmed as n = 1 using PTM No. 402, and the compacted layer is cured for seven days under damp conditions until it meets the prescribed 7‑day unconfined compressive strength of 300–500 psi (or 50 psi tensile strength for asphalt‑stabilized mixes)[2].

Geotextile installation requires fine‑grading the prepared subgrade, removing debris that could damage the fabric, laying a wrinkle‑free sheet with at least two feet of overlap between adjacent rolls, keeping the material free of traffic until it is fully covered by back‑dumped subbase placed to the design depth while maintaining a positive drainage slope; steel pins may be used for wind resistance and subbase compaction follows Section 350.3, with any wheel ruts formed during construction filled with additional subbase rather than being bladed level[2].

For reinforcement using geogrids, Class 2 biaxial polypropylene grids must meet a minimum tensile‑strength requirement of 2 000 lb/ft in both machine and transverse directions, are sampled per ASTM D4354 and handled according to ASTM D4873, and each roll must be clearly identified with product designation, manufacturer name, lot number and quantity[2].

The laboratory program supporting cement or lime stabilization includes determination of gradation, Atterberg limits, shrinkage limit, unconfined compressive strength, dry density, sulfate/chloride content, organic content and pH (for lime), after which a cost‑benefit analysis guides the selection of the most appropriate strengthening technique[1].

Subgrade stabilization designs are required to follow a mix‑design methodology that defines test procedures, quality‑control measures and strength criteria.[1] The designer must select an appropriate stabilizing material and quantity; when a chloride‑based additive is used the specification calls for a minimum 35 % calcium or magnesium chloride solution applied at a rate of 0.1 to 0.15 gal per square yard for each inch of reclaimed depth, while emulsified‑asphalt stabilization follows the same mix‑design and quality‑control protocols.[1] The stabilized subgrade must achieve a California Bearing Ratio that meets agency thresholds: any design CBR exceeding ten requires approval by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, and values above twenty are prohibited in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation, limiting allowable stress to 30 000 psi.[1] When measured or inferred CBR values fall below five, cost‑effective strengthening measures such as cement, lime, rock capping or geogrids should be considered.[1] The pavement design must also specify a minimum resilient modulus for the roadbed soil that matches the assumptions used in the structural analysis and must be verified and signed off by the District Geotechnical Engineer.[1] Compacted subgrade shall achieve at least ninety‑seven percent of the maximum dry density for embankment foundations or existing embankments deeper than three feet, while material within three feet of the proposed grade or structural foundations must be compacted to one hundred percent of the maximum dry density.[2] For reclaimed (FDR) material a minimum of ninety‑five percent of laboratory maximum dry density at optimum moisture is required and the moisture content may not exceed optimum by more than three percent; Type 1 granular fill must remain within plus‑or‑minus two percent of optimum moisture at the time of compaction.[2] Proof‑rolling with a loaded seventy‑two‑thousand‑pound triaxle truck at tire pressures of one hundred to one‑hundred ten pounds per square inch and a speed not exceeding 1.5 ft/s is required, and any permanent rutting greater than one‑half inch (or one inch for embankment foundations) renders the zone unsatisfactory and triggers excavation, drying and recompaction to the prescribed depths.[2] If deflection exceeds half an inch but is less than three inches, excavation to a depth of one foot is mandated; when deflection surpasses three inches, up to three feet must be removed before backfilling with approved material over geotextile layers.[2] Geosynthetic components used for stabilization are subject to strict placement tolerances: rolls must be positioned within plus‑or‑minus one‑half inch of the design elevation, splicing is prohibited, overlaps between adjacent rolls shall be a minimum of twelve inches (or two feet where specified), and continuous material must extend at least four feet from any wrapped edge.[2] The geotextile or geogrid must lie flat without wrinkles, be covered by the full subbase depth before equipment traverses it, and may not remain exposed for more than two weeks.[2] When polypropylene biaxial geogrids are employed they shall conform to ASTM D4873 and belong to Class 2 Type A or B (or Type C with a minimum tensile capacity of 2,000 lb/ft in each principal direction); no reduction factors apply to the specified tensile values.[2] Chemically stabilized layers must achieve an unconfined compressive strength between three hundred and five hundred psi after seven days, while strengths above nine hundred psi are prohibited because of shrinkage‑cracking risk.[2] Asphalt‑stabilized mixes must reach at least fifty pounds per square inch indirect tensile strength in the same period.[2] Cementitious stabilizers may be placed only when ambient temperature will stay above forty degrees Fahrenheit for the seven‑day cure period, and accelerating admixtures are prohibited if temperatures may fall below thirty‑five degrees Fahrenheit within the first twenty‑four hours; placement must not occur in rain or when rain is forecast within two hours of completion.[2] A detailed quality‑control plan must be submitted at least two weeks before work, a three‑hundred‑foot test strip demonstrating compliance with the minimum density criteria is mandatory, and moisture‑density acceptance testing must fall within plus‑or‑minus 2.0 pcf wet density and plus‑or‑minus 0.8 % moisture as reliability checks.[2] Surface tolerances limit irregularities to one‑and‑a‑half inches and require a minimum cross‑slope of one‑quarter inch per foot before opening to traffic.[2] Collectively these criteria, assumptions about moisture and temperature, density thresholds, deflection limits, geosynthetic installation standards, material tensile capacities, strength requirements, approvals and documented quality‑control procedures constitute the safety factors and reliability framework that subgrade stabilization must satisfy.[1][2]

Structural section and dimensions

Subgrade stabilization requires excavating a one‑foot deep undercut when permanent deflection is between 0.5 in and 3 in, or up to a three‑foot maximum depth when deflection exceeds 3 in; after placing the specified geotextile, at least eight inches of approved fill must be placed before compaction, and a minimum six inches of subbase must be laid over the geotextile prior to compaction. The source does not provide any other pavement, base, treated‑soil, slab, shoulder, edge‑support, or reinforcement dimensions. [2]

Materials and mixture design

Subgrade stabilization specifications begin by identifying the stabilizing agent, which may be a chloride‑based solution, an emulsified asphalt binder, Portland cement, hydrated lime, rock capping, or geosynthetic reinforcement [1][2]. When a chloride‑based treatment is selected the solution must contain at least 35 % active ingredient and the recommended application rate is roughly 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of reclaimed depth [1]. If an emulsified asphalt stabilizer is preferred, its type and quantity are established through the mix‑design procedure prescribed in Bulletin 27 for PennDOT projects [1]. Alternative chemical agents such as Portland cement or hydrated lime require a laboratory testing program that characterizes the native soil by gradation, liquid limit, plasticity index, shrinkage limit, unconfined compressive strength, dry density, and chemical analyses for sulfate, chloride, organic content, and pH when lime is considered; these results provide the basis for calculating the required dosage [1]. Rock capping, when used, shall consist of 1 to 3 ft of R‑3 rock, AASHTO No. 1 or the best available rock [1]. Any geotextile placed on the subgrade must satisfy Class 4 requirements and be selected as Type A for layer separation or Type C for stabilization, extending across the entire prepared subgrade [1][2]. When a biaxial polypropylene geogrid is employed it shall be a Class 2 Type A product backed by a Class 4 Type A geotextile face wrap; the grid must have stiff ribs forming apertures that permit mechanical interlock and must meet ASTM D4354 with an ultimate tensile capacity of at least 2,000 lb/ft in both machine‑direction and cross‑direction [2]. The primary confinement element may alternatively be a Class 4 Type C geotextile used without an additional wrap [2]. Fill material placed over any geotextile must be an approved borrow or other specified material; a minimum thickness of eight inches is required on a Class 4 Type C geotextile before compaction, and six inches on a Class 4 Type A geotextile when a subbase is called for [2]. Compaction targets are at least 97 % of the maximum dry density for embankment foundations or existing embankments deeper than three feet, rising to 100 % of the maximum dry density for structure foundations, subgrades, or material within three feet of the proposed grade [2]. Moisture content during compaction must be no lower than three percent below the optimum moisture content and never exceed the optimum; the optimum moisture content itself is defined by the approved mix design and may not be exceeded by more than three percent [2]. When fine‑graded reclaimed (FDR) material, optional aggregates, reclaimed asphalt pavement, stabilizing additive and water are combined, the proportions are dictated by an approved mix design that achieves the target optimum moisture content and a minimum compacted density of 95 % of the laboratory maximum dry density [2]. Chemically stabilized mixes must develop an unconfined compressive strength between 300 and 500 psi after seven days, while asphalt‑cement blends must attain at least 50 psi indirect tensile strength; calcium chloride and magnesium chloride additives also contribute to strength gain through particle cementing [1][2]. If grout injection is selected the specification shall identify either cement grout or high‑density polyurethane grout and limit continuous injection pressure to 200 psi, allowing short‑duration pressures of up to 300 psi and brief spikes to 600 psi only when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade [2]. Jacking operations for slab raising must achieve tolerances of ±0.02 ft for continuously reinforced slabs and ±0.03 ft for jointed or bridge‑end slabs [2].

Subgrade stabilization begins with a systematic assessment of existing subgrade strength by mapping California Bearing Ratio (CBR) values across the site and targeting isolated low‑CBR zones for improvement to bring them up to the project‑wide representative level [1]. The selection of the stabilizing material—such as cement, lime, rock capping, geogrid, geotextile, calcium chloride, emulsified asphalt, or mechanical aggregate additions—is made through a formal mix‑design procedure that incorporates laboratory characterization of the soil’s gradation, moisture limits, strength indices and chemical content [1][2]. For cement or lime stabilization the additive rate is derived from these tests and applied using a structural coefficient of 0.12 together with the existing subgrade CBR, while rock capping is modeled with an assumed CBR value of 20 [1]. When calcium chloride is used it must be supplied as at least a 35 % solution and applied at 0.1–0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of reclaimed depth to achieve cementing strength and reduce freeze‑thaw susceptibility [1]. If an emulsified asphalt stabilizer is selected, the mix design follows established guidelines to determine the appropriate emulsion type and dosage that provide water resistance and fatigue durability, and it may be integrated with granular material, cement or lime as required [1]. Mechanical stabilization may involve pulverizing existing pavement, reshaping and recompacting it, then adding aggregate or reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) to improve gradation, with the proportion of each additive fine‑tuned on the basis of laboratory results to avoid excessive brittleness while meeting load‑bearing and long‑term performance criteria [1]. All borrow material incorporated in the stabilized layer must satisfy the minimum resilient‑modulus requirements specified in the design documents, ensuring that structural capacity is achieved [1]. The mix design also defines the reclaimed base material, any supplemental aggregates or RAP, the chosen stabilizing additive—such as asphalt binder, Portland cement, other chemical agents, or calcium chloride per the FDR Best Practices Publication 242 and the approved project mix—and the water content required for compaction [2]. The target moisture content is set by the design and field placement must be kept within three percent of that optimum; a machine equipped with a computerized integral liquid‑proportioning system controls water and additive rates in relation to cut depth, width and travel speed, and calibration is confirmed on a 300‑foot test strip before full production [2]. Operators record each application and the stabilized blend is placed in layers and compacted to at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum dry density, a requirement verified with control strips [2]. After placement the layer is kept damp and cured for seven days, during which it must develop an unconfined compressive strength of 300–500 psi (or 50 psi indirect tensile when asphalt‑stabilized) to satisfy structural and durability criteria [2]. Geosynthetic reinforcement follows a prescribed sequence: a Class 4 Type A geotextile is laid over the prepared subgrade and any excavated layback slope for separation, and where additional confinement is required a polypropylene biaxial geogrid of Class 2 Type A or B with a minimum tensile capacity of 2,000 lb/ft in both principal directions and apertures sufficient for mechanical interlock is installed, backed at the slope face by the same geotextile [2]. Adjacent geosynthetic strips overlap a minimum of twelve inches and all components are positioned within ±½ inch of the design elevation to ensure uniform performance, while storage in accordance with ASTM D4873 protects the material from ultraviolet exposure, temperatures above 140 °F, and contamination, thereby preserving its structural strength, durability, constructability and overall pavement performance [2]. Backfill between each stabilization layer consists of PennDOT No. 2A coarse aggregate placed in two compacted lifts of six inches each, extending up to the elevation of the next geosynthetic layer, which distributes loads, maintains drainage and meets constructability requirements [2]. A cost‑benefit analysis is performed to confirm that the selected mixture fulfills the combined structural, durability, constructability and performance goals outlined in the guides [1][2].

Structural details and interfaces

Subgrade stabilization incorporates a series of structural details that begin with transition zones at the entrance and exit of any undercut, each constructed on slopes no steeper than eight horizontal to one vertical and excavated to depths between one and three feet depending on measured permanent deflection. [2] At the base of the undercut a Class 4 Type C geotextile is placed, providing both separation and lateral restraint when the existing CBR is below three, and may be followed by a drainage path backfilled with No. 57 aggregate. [1][2] Over this geotextile an approved fill material is laid so that a minimum of eight inches rests on the fabric before compaction, establishing a continuous load‑bearing layer. [2] A full‑width Class 4 Type A geotextile separation layer is then installed across the entire lane width, with adjacent rolls overlapped by at least twelve inches along parallel edges and any wrap overlap kept outside a four‑foot zone from the wrapped edge; where protective fabric is used its seams are overlapped by no less than two feet and secured with steel pins or comparable anchors to prevent wind uplift. [1][2] All geosynthetic layers must be positioned within plus‑or‑minus one‑half inch of the design elevation, and splicing of any geotextile or geogrid is prohibited, requiring continuous material placement throughout the treated section. [2] When reinforcement beyond separation is required, a Class 2 biaxial geogrid may be placed directly on the backing geotextile, extending four feet above and two feet below the front face of the grid; the grid consists of longitudinal and transverse stiff ribs whose connections are integrally formed or welded, delivering continuous load transfer through mechanical interlock with the surrounding soil. [2] Following placement of the reinforcement system, a minimum six‑inch subbase is placed over the top geotextile and compacted in accordance with prescribed compaction criteria; no construction equipment may travel on the geosynthetic until this full depth is achieved. [2] In areas where subgrade conditions change abruptly, gradual transitions are provided by using embankment fill or non‑frost‑susceptible granular material to maintain uniform support across the pavement structure. [1] Rock capping of one to three feet depth with R‑3 or equivalent rock, protected by a granular chase layer, is specified as an additional treatment option for weak subgrades. [1] A positive longitudinal slope is maintained throughout installation to promote drainage, wheel ruts are filled with additional subbase material, and existing underground utilities are protected during construction activities. In subgrade stabilization the interface between the treated subgrade and overlying layers is explicitly defined, with geogrids placed at the subgrade‑aggregate boundary to provide reinforcement and a Class 4 Type A geotextile installed as a separation fabric between subgrade and subbase to preserve each material’s performance [1]. Where abrupt changes in soil type, cut‑fill limits, or rock‑soil transitions occur, designers create gradual granular or embankment transitions—often using non‑frost‑susceptible subbase material—and retain lateral benches at rock cuts to maintain continuity of load transfer, drainage, and uniform support across the pavement structure [1]. Transition zones at the start and end of any undercut are shaped with a slope no steeper than eight horizontal to one vertical and the bottom surface is compacted to provide a smooth engineered boundary [2]. Existing underground utilities are protected throughout the operation and special rolling is prohibited within ten feet of bridge abutments or approach slabs, establishing the engineered interface between the stabilized subgrade and nearby structures [2]. The native soil is then fine‑graded to a smooth, puncture‑free condition and proof‑rolled to achieve a uniform firm surface free of unsuitable material before any geosynthetic placement [2]. A Class 4 Type C geotextile is placed on the prepared base at the bottom of the undercut, laid flat without wrinkles and positioned within ±½ inch of the design elevation, after which at least eight inches of approved backfill material are placed before compaction [2]. Over the borrow material a Class 4 Type A separation geosynthetic is laid, maintaining required vertical spacing to any underlying layer; seams are avoided and adjacent sheets are overlapped a minimum of twelve inches along parallel edges, while rolls oriented perpendicular to traffic extend at least four feet from any wrapped edge before an overlap can be made, and continuous installations require overlaps no less than two feet [2]. The geosynthetic is anchored and backfilled in two lifts of six‑inch compacted coarse aggregate placed gently so as not to damage the material, with vehicles barred until a minimum six inches of loose fill cover the fabric [2]. A minimum six‑inch subbase layer of approved material is then placed on the Class 4 Type A geotextile and compacted, providing the interface to the overlying pavement layers [2]. To further enhance interlock, a Class 2 biaxial geogrid (Type A or B) may be installed; the grid consists of a regular network of longitudinal and transverse polymer tensile elements with apertures large enough for mechanical interlock, fabricated from stiff ribs with integrally formed or welded connections, and must meet an ultimate tensile capacity of at least 2,000 lb/ft in both machine‑direction and cross‑direction [2]. All backfill and subbase material are placed by back‑dumping, spread to full design depth ahead of equipment, and compacted while maintaining a positive slope for drainage; any wheel ruts formed during compaction are filled with additional subbase to preserve the intended profile [2]. Subsurface drainage systems are installed at abutments and at the far end of the fill as shown on standard drawings, completing the controlled interface between the stabilized subgrade, backfill, adjacent structures, and the pavement system [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Effective subgrade stabilization starts by establishing a well‑drained pavement system, which requires the identification of wet zones in the existing subgrade and the installation of appropriate base drains or comparable drainage measures before reclamation work proceeds [1]. Removing excess moisture early protects the integrity of the stabilized material and preserves its structural capacity under variable environmental influences [1]. Moisture content for all compacted soils is controlled to lie between three percent below optimum and the optimum level, and any material that exceeds this range must be dried by disking, scarifying or other suitable means prior to compaction [2]. Water application during placement is regulated by equipment equipped with an integral liquid‑proportioning system that adjusts the rate based on cut depth, width and travel speed, and placement is prohibited when rain is expected within two hours of completion or when ambient temperature falls below 40 °F for the required seven‑day cure period [2]. Frozen material is not placed or compacted, and embankment or fill are never built on frozen ground to avoid adverse freeze‑thaw effects [2]. To further mitigate moisture and climate impacts, stabilizers such as a minimum 35 % calcium‑chloride solution applied at 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of depth reclaimed, or an equivalent magnesium‑chloride treatment, are used because they cement particles and lower the freezing point of the reclaimed soil [1]. Emulsified asphalt may also be incorporated into the mix design to improve resistance to water‑related damage and enhance fatigue performance under repeated loading [1]. When frost‑susceptible soils are encountered, the design calls for their removal and replacement with a sufficiently thick layer of non‑frost‑susceptible material that insulates the roadbed from freezing temperatures, typically combined with a stronger granular base course to maintain bearing capacity during thaw periods [1]. Gradual transitions at subgrade discontinuities and the use of appropriately sized granular foundations further reduce differential heave caused by moisture or climate variations, ensuring consistent performance without necessarily increasing overall structural thickness [1]. Positive drainage is achieved by shaping each layer of embankment or fill to shed water, fine‑grading low spots, and sealing the surface at the end of every workday with a smooth drum roller so that no furrows impede runoff, while maintaining a continuous positive slope away from the pavement structure [2]. Subsurface drainage systems are installed at both the abutment and the far end of the fill, and where an undercut is required a dedicated drainage path is excavated and backfilled with No. 57 aggregate to remove water from the repaired zone [2]. When geosynthetics are used, rolls are overlapped by at least two feet, protected from temperatures above 140 °F, direct sunlight and debris, stored elevated off the ground, and covered with the specified subbase within two weeks so that moisture and other environmental effects cannot damage the fabric; equipment is kept off the geotextile until it is fully covered [2]. All stabilization activities must comply with applicable environmental standards throughout design and construction [2].

Subgrade stabilization works must be staged so that construction equipment can reach the work area, which requires excavating beyond the existing line and grade without imposing additional cost to the department [2]. Throughout the operation all machinery is required to stay at least ten feet away from bridge abutments or approach slabs, and any undercut transitions must be formed with a slope no steeper than eight horizontal to one vertical [2]. Existing underground utilities must be protected during any undercut activities, and positive slopes are maintained across the work area to assure proper drainage [2][1]. The subgrade surface is fine‑graded in accordance with the applicable section and project cross‑sections, then proof‑rolled to a uniform level before placement of geosynthetic materials [2]. Geotextiles and geogrids are placed within plus or minus one‑half inch of the design elevation, overlapped by at least twelve inches (a minimum two‑foot overlap for adjacent rolls), free of wrinkles, and must remain uncovered until a minimum of six inches of loose backfill is placed; equipment may not travel directly on the geosynthetic until it is fully covered to the planned subbase depth [2]. The geogrid shall be a Class 2 biaxial product with stiff ribs that are integrally formed or welded, providing at least two‑thousand pounds per foot tensile capacity in each principal direction and an aperture geometry sufficient for mechanical interlock; it must be shielded from direct sunlight, ultraviolet radiation, temperatures above one‑hundred‑forty degrees Fahrenheit, mud, dust and debris [2]. Rolling equipment such as sheepsfoot or pad rollers is limited to a maximum speed of two miles per hour in static mode, and sudden braking or sharp turning on the stabilized fill is prohibited for all vehicles, including tracked equipment [2]. A minimum final surface cross‑slope of one‑quarter inch per foot is required, and surface irregularities may not exceed one and a half inches; smoothness must be within half an inch over a ten‑foot template, preserving the existing centerline, grades and cross‑slopes within these limits [2][1]. When raising pavement the final grade must fall within plus or minus 0.02 ft of the designed string line for continuously reinforced concrete slabs and within plus or minus 0.03 ft for jointed or bridge‑end slabs, with any overjacking beyond 0.10 ft requiring removal and replacement of the pavement [2]. Injection pressures are limited to a continuous maximum of two hundred pounds per square inch, short‑duration bursts may reach three hundred psi, and brief spikes up to six hundred psi are allowed only when the slab is bonded to the subgrade [2]. Placement of dry additive or any full‑depth reclamation material is prohibited when wind conditions would cause blowing additives to become objectionable to traffic or adjacent property owners, and materials must not be placed if air temperature is expected to fall below forty degrees Fahrenheit during the required seven‑day cure period [2]. Heavy traffic is barred from the reclaimed base throughout the seven‑day curing interval; appropriate traffic signs and approved control devices must keep trucks off the base until the required strength is achieved, after which only light local traffic may be permitted and maintenance and protection of traffic (MPT) remains in place until the road can be safely opened [2]. Subgrade stabilization also incorporates drainage provisions by re‑establishing proper cross‑slopes and installing subsurface drainage systems at abutments and at the far end of the fill, thereby limiting any large surface elevation changes that could affect vehicle operation and user access [1][2]. The construction plan must describe means of establishing and controlling the centerline, crown and any existing or proposed superelevations throughout the stabilization process [2].

Verification and expected performance

The completed subgrade stabilization is verified through an integrated sequence of compliance, constructability and performance checks that are tied to the contract specifications and the approved quality‑control plan. [1][2] First, the mix‑design procedures and stabilizer type and dosage selected during design are confirmed against the criteria set out in the applicable bulletin and construction details, ensuring that the material quantities and limits have been coordinated with the project team. [1] The as‑built thickness of the reclaimed layer is measured and compared with the designed thickness, and any drainage deficiencies identified before construction are corrected to assure constructability and long‑term durability. [1] Prior to placement of fill or geosynthetics, the ground‑stabilized base foundation is inspected by the Representative to verify that special rolling has produced a permanent deflection not exceeding one‑half inch on existing subgrades or one inch on raised embankments. [2] Compaction requirements are satisfied when dry densities reach at least 97 % of maximum for embankment foundations and 100 % of maximum for structure foundations, subgrades and chemically stabilized layers, with moisture contents within three percent below to optimum, as confirmed by AASHTO T191/T310 or PTM No. 402 direct‑transmission tests after each roller pass. [2] When test data are unavailable a 300‑foot test strip is constructed; its in‑place density must meet a minimum of 95 % of the laboratory maximum (or 98 % of the control‑strip density if a second strip is required), and subsequent random tests on each 3,000 sq yd lot confirm compliance. [2] The quality‑control plan submitted at least two weeks before work details calibrated equipment, water and additive rate records, and test frequencies; an independent nuclear density gauge must agree with the contractor’s readings within ±2.0 lb/ft³ for wet density. [2] Geosynthetics are placed within ±½ inch of the design elevation, overlapped a minimum of 12 inches along parallel edges and spaced vertically as shown on the plans, while backfill is placed in two‑to‑six‑inch lifts of approved aggregate and subsurface drainage is provided. [2] After compaction, moisture content must not exceed optimum by more than three percent and the unconfined compressive strength of chemically stabilized material must reach 300 to 500 psi after seven days as measured in accordance with ASTM D1633. [2] Post‑construction field testing includes a Falling Weight Deflectometer run to demonstrate that the rebuilt subgrade attains the required stiffness, and Dynamic Cone Penetrometer or Light‑Weight Deflectometer measurements provide additional verification of subgrade strength. [1] Laboratory and field results are used to confirm that the resilient modulus meets or exceeds the minimum value prescribed in the pavement design and that California Bearing Ratio values fall within the approved range; CBR values greater than ten require approval by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and no CBR may exceed twenty. [1] If considerable uncertainty exists regarding a laboratory‑tested CBR, supplemental testing such as additional FWD or DCP runs is performed to back‑calculate Mr for validation purposes. [1] Surface tolerances are checked with a ten‑foot straightedge; irregularities greater than one‑half inch trigger rework, and the cross‑slope is verified at a minimum of one‑quarter inch per foot. [2] The final grade is documented in a field quality‑control package that includes existing roadway profiles and as‑built surveyed drawings, demonstrating that the finished elevation lies within ±0.02 ft for continuously reinforced concrete or ±0.03 ft for jointed slabs, with any overjacking beyond 0.10 ft requiring removal and replacement. [2] Once all sampling procedures, test results, thickness checks, compaction verification, geosynthetic placement, strength criteria and grade tolerances are reviewed and signed off by the District Geotechnical Engineer, the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and the Representative, the subgrade stabilization is deemed compliant with project requirements. The stabilization process is deemed successful when the subgrade can sustain traffic without excessive permanent deformation, maintain prescribed compaction and moisture conditions, achieve required early strength, and provide a smooth riding surface. Permanent set observed during proof‑rolling must not exceed one‑half inch for existing subgrades or spread footings and may be allowed up to one inch on embankment foundations that are being raised. Proof‑rolling is performed with a loaded triaxle dump truck at a controlled speed of no more than 1.5 ft/s, and the resulting surface is inspected after each roller pass for set. Compaction requirements call for densities of at least 97 % of the maximum dry density for typical embankments and 100 % for structural foundations, with an overall minimum of 95 % of the laboratory maximum for all stabilized material; these values are verified by in‑place nuclear‑gauge moisture‑density testing according to AASHTO T 191 or T 310 after each pass and on control strips. Moisture must be within three percent below the optimum, and independent gauge readings must fall within ±2.0 pcf wet density and ±0.8 % moisture of the target. Surface smoothness is checked with a ten‑foot straightedge over any 3,000‑square‑yard area, and deviations greater than one‑half inch trigger corrective work. Early strength is evaluated at seven days: chemically stabilized mixes must reach an unconfined compressive strength between 300 and 500 psi (or 280–500 psi depending on overlay thickness) while asphalt‑stabilized mixes must achieve a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi, as determined from ASTM D1633 Proctor specimens. Satisfying these deformation limits, density and moisture criteria, smoothness tolerances, and seven‑day strength thresholds provides evidence that the stabilized subgrade will resist distress, support traffic loads, and preserve ride quality throughout its design service life.
