Asphalt Patching

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Asphalt Patching is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
partial-depth-asphalt-surface-patchingmechanized-patchmechanized-asphalt-patchingmechanized-patchingmechanized-asphalt-patches
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Asphalt patching is a partial‑depth surface repair applied to flexible or concrete‑surfaced pavements when localized distress—such as potholes, abrupt depressions, edge deterioration, spalling or joint faulting—is identified[1][2]. The treatment removes the damaged portion, cuts vertical edges down to sound material, cleans and dries the area, applies an emulsified tack coat, then fills the void with a plant‑mixed asphalt (or approved equivalent) matching the existing course; patches ≤ 3 in. deep use only a wearing‑course (Type A) while deeper defects employ a binder lift beneath a wearing layer (Type B), and are compacted to a flat, tight surface that conforms to adjacent ride quality[1][2]. By restoring structural support and providing a smooth, durable riding surface, the patch slows further deterioration and satisfies the performance tolerances required for routine‑maintenance preservation projects.

Purpose and treatment objectives

Asphalt patching is applied when a pavement exhibits localized surface distress that must be removed and replaced to restore structural integrity, targeting conditions such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling, and joint faulting identified in condition surveys. The technique is also used to repair localized surface failures that develop in the wearing course of flexible pavements, including potholes, abrupt depressions, flushing, deteriorated pavement edges, and previously temporary pothole repairs. For defects three inches or less in depth, manual patching with an asphalt wearing‑course (Type A) is prescribed; if a defect exceeds three inches, a combination of binder and wearing courses (Type B) must be used. When surface defects exceed four square inches in area and one inch in depth within traffic lanes, an approved asphalt‑based patching material is filled and compacted to a flat, tight surface before overlay placement. During profile milling operations, any spalling or dislodgement of unsound pavement identified by the Representative is excavated to match the existing surface depth, cleaned, tack‑coated, and filled with a matching asphalt mixture that is leveled and compacted. Under the 60‑month warranty provisions, partial‑depth surface patching is employed to repair localized surface distresses such as flushing and potholes identified by manual performance surveys; when these distresses exceed the specified performance criteria, the damaged portion must be removed and replaced with a square or rectangular plant‑mixed asphalt concrete patch that meets the surface tolerance. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 312–358), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

The Pavement Policy Manual places partial‑depth asphalt surface patching in the Routine Maintenance group, which is intended to address current pavement defects and therefore its chief aim is to repair damage while also helping to slow further deterioration. In the concrete‑overlay procedure (Section 548) the shallow‑repair step uses an asphalt‑based patching material so that the repaired area provides a flat, tight surface for the new interlayer, thereby repairing surface defects and restoring functionality before overlay placement. During profile‑milling operations (Section 492) patches are applied to spalled or unsound areas that are excavated to the existing depth, cleaned, tack‑coated and filled with an asphalt mix identical to the surrounding material; this restores a smooth, durable riding surface and returns the roadway to functional service. Under the 60‑month warranty provisions (Section 496) square or rectangular patches are placed on distressed sections in order to repair the distress and restore the functional performance required by the performance criteria. The manual asphalt patching described in Section 450 prepares potholes, abrupt depressions and deteriorated edges and places an asphalt plant mix either manually or with mechanical spreading and finishing equipment, directly repairing the damaged surface and restoring the roadway’s functional condition. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 312–358), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Distress characterization and causes

The distress that calls for partial depth asphalt surface patching is located on the roadway and shoulders, affecting roughly two percent of the pavement area as scheduled at the 45‑year point in the life‑cycle cost analysis. This treatment appears under Maintenance and Protection of Traffic User Delay, indicating that the primary operational consequence of not addressing the distress is increased traffic user delay. In the Pennsylvania specification, the distress is identified within a defined State Route segment approximately one‑half mile long. The annual performance surveys required under the 60‑month warranty monitor the segment, and when the year‑5 survey shows that at least 30 % of the segment area contains two or more distresses classified as low severity, remediation such as microsurfacing or a patch is mandated. The specification further requires that repair locations spaced less than 100 ft apart be combined into one continuous patch. Because surveys are conducted annually throughout the warranty period, the progression of these low‑severity defects is monitored over time. If the pavement condition is determined to be potentially harmful or unsafe, the Department may authorize immediate emergency repairs, which can result in roadway closures and associated road‑user liquidated damages. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 71–72); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), section 13.7 PAVEMENT PROJECT CRITERIA > E. Pavement Testing, partial‑depth asphalt surface patching is indicated when weak support results from an inadequate subbase, a condition that may produce premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements [1]. The same source notes that aging or chemical reactions in the aggregate—specifically concerns with Vanport Limestone and Alkali Silica Reaction (ASR)—can accelerate cracking and spalling of the surface layer [1]. Heavy traffic loading, as reflected in Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) data, contributes to fatigue distress that may necessitate patching under the criteria described in that section [1]. Deficient structural capacity identified through Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing of pavement and subgrade characteristics also signals the need for corrective partial‑depth patching according to the Pavement Policy Manual [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, SECTION 548—UNBONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF CONCRETE‑SURFACED PAVEMENTS, identify unstable concrete slabs or slab fragments that move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads as a primary distress addressed by partial‑depth asphalt patching because such instability creates non‑uniform support for an overlay and reflects inadequate or weak support beneath the surface [2]. When a geotextile interlayer is employed, SECTION 548 further requires that all surface defects larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch in traffic lanes be filled with an approved patching material and compacted to a flat, tight surface before overlay placement, indicating that such defect dimensions are considered distress mechanisms that must be remedied [2]. Under SECTION 496—ASPHALT CONCRETE PAVEMENT, 60‑MONTH WARRANTY, base condition problems become a cause for partial‑depth patching when performance surveys detect fatigue cracking or other distresses that may indicate an unacceptable base condition [2]. The same warranty section states that excessive traffic loading can also trigger the need for patching if, during the warranty period, Department traffic counts show estimated cumulative ESALs have exceeded 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life ESALs [2]. Additionally, SECTION 496 lists uncontrolled forces of nature—including floods, earthquakes, tornadoes, brush or forest fires, landslides, sinkholes, and other natural disasters—as causes of distress beyond the contractor’s responsibility that may nonetheless require partial‑depth asphalt patching to restore pavement integrity [2]. The warranty section further identifies traffic accidents that result in fuel or chemical spills, vehicle fires, and/or gouging or goring of the pavement surface as accident‑related distresses that can necessitate partial‑depth patching [2].

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Applicability and treatment selection

Under SECTION 450—MANUAL ASPHALT PATCHING, asphalt patching is appropriate on flexible pavements where localized distress appears as potholes or abrupt depressions; when the defect is three inches or less deep a manual Type A patch using only an asphalt wearing course may be applied provided each patch has a minimum area of 1 square foot and the edges are cut vertically to sound material, while defects that exceed three inches in depth call for a manual Type B patch that includes a binder‑course lift beneath a top wearing layer. The procedure also covers potholes extending into shoulders, with fill methods depending on whether the shoulder has an asphalt base or another type of surface. Under SECTION 548—UNBONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF CONCRETE‑SURFACED PAVEMENTS, asphalt patching is appropriate on concrete‑surfaced pavements when shallow surface defects that are greater than 4 square inches in area and at least 1 inch deep occur in traffic lanes and must be repaired before an overlay; the patching material may be asphalt, cementitious, or a similar approved repair mix. If an asphalt interlayer is being placed, the defect can be filled with the same asphalt material provided it can be properly compacted together with the interlayer, otherwise a separate repair fill is required. The work should be done while the surface temperature of any geotextile fabric does not exceed 120 °F. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 312–313), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Partial‑depth asphalt surface patching is prescribed only at the 45‑year point in a life‑cycle cost analysis for new construction or reconstruction and may be applied to no more than 2 % of the pavement area; therefore it is unsuitable for pavements younger than 45 years, where other maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation or repair treatments such as clean and seal with crack sealing, full‑depth patching, mill wearing courses or overlays should be selected instead [1]. Pavement testing criteria further limit the use of asphalt patching to limited‑area repairs; when the quantity of material to be removed and replaced exceeds ten percent of the pavement surface, such repairs are generally not cost‑effective or permitted within Pavement Preservation projects, prompting the selection of a more extensive treatment such as rehabilitation that may involve base repair and larger patches or overlays, or reconstruction if the functional life has been exceeded [1]. If a year‑5 performance survey determines that thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, the warranty requires remedial action by microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment rather than individual patching [2]. When potential repair locations are spaced less than one hundred feet apart, the specification directs that a single continuous repair be made instead of separate patches [2]. Patch repairs are otherwise limited to allowable crack sealing and must conform to the surface tolerance and material requirements specified in the warranty [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 71–72), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Condition assessment and timing

To decide if partial‑depth asphalt surface patching is needed, the district first relies on the annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (videologging) that record distresses such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration and raveling; these condition data are entered into the Pavement Condition/Treatment Matrices which flag sections for repair. Where skid‑related problems are suspected, a Skid Friction test performed by BOMO is requested to confirm a skid resistance issue before any treatment. Falling Weight Deflectometer testing supplies pavement and subgrade modulus values and also identifies the locations and quantities of required patching, noting that patching greater than 10 % of the pavement area is generally not cost‑effective for preservation projects. Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) traffic loading data may be reviewed as an additional factor in prioritizing any identified patching needs. A visual inspection first looks for unstable slabs or slab fragments that move or rock under normal traffic; any such condition must be stabilized or repaired before the overlay is placed. The same visual assessment measures surface defects and any defect larger than 4 square inches in area and at least 1 inch deep must be filled with an approved asphalt‑based repair material. When a geotextile interlayer is specified, joints showing faulting greater than ¼ inch are milled, and if joint faulting exceeds ½ inch a scratch course shall be placed at the joint or the joint shall be milled or ground to reduce the faulting prior to patching. The Department conducts annual performance surveys that include automated surveys identifying and measuring distresses in accordance with Publication 336 and manual surveys for flushing or potholes; the results are compared against Table A limits, for example when 30 % of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses remediation is triggered. Rut depth is verified with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width to gauge severity. Traffic classification counts are performed to confirm that ESAL estimates remain within the projected values. When fatigue cracking or other signs of base failure appear, six‑inch diameter cores are extracted from the distressed area and tested according to PTM 715/AASHTO T209 for percent unfilled voids, PTM 702 (or another approved method) for percent asphalt, and PTM 739 for gradation, providing additional data needed to locate and justify patching. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Within the framework of a fifty‑year life‑cycle cost analysis, partial depth asphalt surface patching is scheduled as a maintenance treatment when the pavement reaches approximately year 45, at which point roughly two percent of the pavement area is targeted for repair to restore surface integrity. The same manual further states that under routine‑maintenance specifications the patching—whether mechanized or spray applied—should be used whenever existing distress is present that needs to be repaired or retarded, i.e., before the pavement progresses to a more severe stage that would require preservation or rehabilitation, and the patched area should not exceed ten percent of the pavement because larger quantities are generally not cost‑effective or permitted for Pavement Preservation projects. Manual asphalt patching is triggered when a pavement exhibits localized failures such as potholes, abrupt depressions, deteriorated edges, or temporarily patched potholes that require repatching; for potholes and abrupt depressions three inches or less in depth the work shall be performed manually with an asphalt wearing‑course only (Type A), whereas defects deeper than three inches shall employ both an asphalt binder course and a wearing‑course (Type B). In addition, surface defects in traffic lanes that exceed four square inches in area and are at least one inch deep are patched as a shallow repair before an unbonded concrete overlay using an asphalt‑based, cementitious, or similar material approved by the Representative and compacted to a flat, tight surface prior to interlayer placement. Furthermore, during profile milling of asphalt pavement surfaces any spalling or dislodgement of unsound pavement identified and designated for patching by the Representative shall be excavated to match the existing depth, tack‑coated, filled with an asphalt mix of the same type, leveled and compacted to conform to the adjacent pavement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 71–72), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 312–350), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Materials and repair details

Partial depth asphalt surface patching may involve applying an asphalt tack coat, providing slab stabilization where necessary to restore support to the existing concrete pavement, installing a continuous pavement base drain, performing joint cleaning and sealing—including heavy‑duty membranes as required—and executing saw cutting and sealing of overlay joints; Type 6 or Type 7 paved shoulders are used when applicable. [1] Asphalt patching is performed by preparing and repairing potholes, abrupt depressions, deteriorated pavement edges, and repatching previously temporary patches using asphalt plant‑mix material that may be placed manually or with mechanical spreading and finishing equipment. [2] For potholes and abrupt depressions three inches and less in depth the work shall be done manually with an asphalt wearing course only. [2] Depressions deeper than three inches shall receive an asphalt binder course placed in lifts not to exceed three inches uncompacted, followed by a topmost wearing layer of about one‑and‑a‑half inches. [2] Each patch must have a minimum area of one square foot and be cut with vertical edges; milling or planing may be used to achieve the vertical cuts. [2] The prepared area shall be cleaned and dried, and a tack coat applied to the bottom and vertical surfaces before placement of the repair material. [1][2] Patches are compacted with a steel‑wheeled roller or other approved equipment so that the surface conforms to the surrounding grade. [2] Any shoulder portion of a patch shall be filled with compatible asphalt or suitable material and sealed with emulsified asphalt, Class TACK, NTT, or the PG‑Binder used in the pavement course. [2] Partial‑depth patches shall be placed using a patching material that must be an approved asphalt, cementitious, or comparable pavement repair mix. [2] When a geotextile interlayer is specified, any defect larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch in the traffic lane must be filled with this material and compacted to a flat, tight surface; the geotextile edges shall then be overlapped by eight inches ± two inches and extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge. [2] If an asphalt interlayer is specified, a nominal one‑inch layer of the approved Superpave mix design shall be placed. [2] For profile‑milled sections repair is performed only where the Representative designates a repair area; the damaged spot is excavated to the same depth as the surrounding surface, all loose material removed, an emulsified asphalt tack coat applied, and the void filled with an asphalt mixture of the same type as the existing pavement, after which the new mix is leveled and compacted so that it conforms to the adjacent pavement. [2] Under the warranty specification any patching that requires removal and replacement shall be performed with plant‑mixed asphalt concrete unless another material is expressly approved in writing by the District Executive; repaired areas are limited to square or rectangular patches, and when individual repair locations lie less than one hundred feet apart they shall be combined into a single continuous repair. [2] All such repairs must satisfy the surface tolerance defined in Section 413.3(l) and conform to the limits and performance criteria set out in Table A. [2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–72); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 312–358), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

The performance of a partial‑depth asphalt patch is fundamentally linked to the condition of the existing pavement, requiring that the damaged area be cut with vertical edges and excavated down to sound, stable material, all loose or foreign matter removed and the surface thoroughly dried before any new material is placed. The repair must extend over at least one square foot and the patch depth should match the depth of the surrounding like‑asphalt courses so that the finished surface aligns with adjacent ride‑smoothness. A tack coat is required on the bottom and on all vertical surfaces of the prepared area; the coating may be an emulsified asphalt tack coat, Class TACK or NTT, or the same performance‑grade binder used in the original pavement course such as PG 64S‑22. When a geotextile interlayer is to be placed, any surface defect larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must first be filled with an approved repair material and compacted to a flat, tight surface before the fabric is installed. If an asphalt interlayer is used, shallow defects may be filled by the interlayer material provided that the material can be properly compacted together with the interlayer; otherwise those defects must be pre‑filled with repair material. The repair mix itself must be an approved asphalt, cementitious, or other authorized pavement‑repair material capable of being compacted with any overlying interlayer, and it should be the same type as the existing pavement, matching the original PG‑binder (for example PG 64S‑22). For depressions three inches deep or less (designated Type A) only an asphalt wearing course is placed, whereas deeper depressions greater than three inches (Type B) require a binder course laid in lifts that do not exceed three inches in uncompacted depth followed by a top wearing layer, with each lift compacted as specified. All placed material must achieve a density greater than 92 % of the theoretical maximum and must meet the percent asphalt and gradation tolerances established in the contractor’s approved mix design; failure to meet these criteria is considered probable cause for distress and relieves the contractor of responsibility. The existing pavement surface temperature must not exceed 120 °F for either fabric or pavement prior to overlay placement, and joints exhibiting faulting greater than one‑quarter inch require mill preparation before geotextile installation. In addition to the above, any underlying support or drainage deficiencies should be addressed through slab stabilization and continuous pavement base drains before patch placement to ensure long‑term performance. Finally, each repaired area must satisfy the surface tolerance specified in Section 413.3(l) and be installed as square or rectangular patches using materials that meet the specification’s performance criteria. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 312–358), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Treatment procedures

In Section 450, Manual Asphalt Patching, the crew first cuts out and excavates the outlined area to sound, stable material, cutting all edges vertically; a milling or planing machine may be used and each patch must have a minimum area of 1 square foot. After debris is removed the surface and surrounding pavement are thoroughly cleaned and dried, then a tack coat is applied to the bottom and vertical surfaces in accordance with Section 460.3(b) and allowed to cure before any material is placed. For potholes three inches deep or less (Manual Asphalt Patching Type A) the asphalt wearing‑course is placed and compacted with a steel‑wheeled roller or other approved compaction equipment until the patch surface conforms to the grade of the surrounding pavement, and all excavated areas are filled before the close of each workday. For potholes deeper than three inches (Manual Asphalt Patching Type B) the same preparation is followed, but the binder course is placed in lifts that do not exceed 3 inches in uncompacted depth; each lift is leveled and compacted with an approved mechanical tamper, after which a topmost 1½‑inch layer of wearing‑course is placed and compacted with a steel‑wheeled roller, and the edges are sealed with emulsified asphalt, Class TACK or NTT as appropriate. In Section 492, Profile Milling of Asphalt Pavement Surfaces, the Representative first designates the milled spots that require patching; those areas are then excavated to match the depth of the existing surface and all loose material is thoroughly cleaned. An emulsified asphalt tack coat is applied before placing a fresh asphalt mix of the same type, which is spread, leveled, and compacted until it matches the smoothness and elevation of the adjacent pavement; no additional finishing or curing steps are required beyond achieving proper compaction and conformity. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 312–350)

Implementation of partial‑depth asphalt surface patching begins with removal of the damaged area, which may be cut out using a milling or planing machine as described in SECTION 450, and is followed by cleaning of all debris. A self‑propelled milling machine capable of milling a full‑lane width up to 12 feet in one pass, equipped with an automatic grade control system, profiler cutters and transverse cross‑slope controls, may then be used as required by SECTION 492. After the milled area is cleaned of loose material, any spalled zones are excavated, the surfaces are thoroughly dried, and a tack coat is applied; the tack coat must cure before further placement. The binder course is placed in lifts that shall not exceed three inches uncompacted depth for Type B, and each lift is compacted with an approved mechanical tamper. A topmost wearing‑course of about 1½ inches is then placed and compacted with a steel‑wheeled roller or other approved compaction equipment. All excavated areas must be filled before the close of each workday, except that the final 1½‑inch wearing course may be placed on the following day. The hot mix used to fill the patch shall be plant‑mixed asphalt concrete unless another material is accepted in writing by the District Executive, and all required materials, equipment and labor are furnished at no cost to the Department as required by SECTION 496. Sequencing of the work requires the contractor to notify the District Executive of a tentative start date and to submit a repair schedule within five days; permanent repairs may only begin after written confirmation that weather conditions permit, while temporary repairs are allowed when inclement weather prevents quality placement. Traffic must be kept off the freshly milled surface for no more than 6 calendar days, and the first overlaying asphalt course must be placed within 7 calendar days of the start of milling, with weather‑related stoppages excluded from this count. If the first course is not placed on the same day as milling, ROUGH ROAD (W8‑8) signs must be installed at a minimum of 1/2‑mile intervals and a reduced speed limit may be posted. Traffic control shall follow the approved traffic control plan, and the roadway must be returned to unrestricted use; liquidated damages are assessed if it remains closed beyond the specified period. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 312–358), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Quality and workmanship

The inspector shall verify that each patch has at least one square foot of area (Section 450—Manual Asphalt Patching). The excavated zone must be removed to sound, stable material, swept free of loose debris and dried before any tack coat is applied to the bottom and vertical faces; the tack coat must be confirmed cured prior to placing the asphalt wearing‑course or binder course for Type B (Section 450). Binder lifts shall not exceed three inches uncompacted depth and the top wearing layer shall be limited to one‑and‑half inches, with each lift compacted using an approved mechanical tamper or steel‑wheeled roller (Section 450). After compaction the patch surface must conform to the grade of the surrounding pavement, all excavated areas must be filled before the end of the workday, and edges shall be sealed with the specified emulsified asphalt or tack material (Section 450). The repair mix used for the patch must be an asphalt, cementitious, or comparable repair material that has received approval from the Representative prior to placement of any overlay (Section 548). Surface preparation requires sweeping away loose debris and filling every defect larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch with the approved material, then compacting it to a flat, tight surface (Section 548). When a geotextile interlayer is employed, the fabric must be laid clean, overlapped eight inches ± two inches on each edge, limited to no more than three layers, extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, kept damp but not saturated, and its surface temperature must not exceed 120°F before overlay placement (Section 548). For an asphalt interlayer the installation calls for a nominal one‑inch depth of the specified Superpave mix, joint faulting no greater than one‑half inch unless a scratch course or milling is applied, use of a rubber‑tire roller, and temperature control so that the pavement surface does not exceed 120°F (Section 548). The excavation shall be cut to match the existing surface depth, all loose material removed, and an emulsified asphalt tack coat applied before filling with an asphalt mixture identical to the original type; the placed mix must then be leveled and compacted so it conforms with the adjacent pavement (Section 492). The finished patch must meet profile tolerances of one‑eighth inch smoothness in twelve feet and cross‑slope within one‑eighth inch in twelve feet (Section 492). The inspector shall confirm that the patch material complies with the specification—typically plant‑mixed asphalt concrete unless a written exception is approved—and that the repaired area meets the surface tolerance required by Section 413.3(l) (Section 496). The dimensions of each patch must follow the square or rectangular limits set out in the repair criteria, and the overall work must satisfy the material and performance requirements of the warranty (Section 496). Where applicable nondestructive testing may be performed on the patched pavement and the results shall be submitted to the Department (Section 496). [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 312–358), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

For Pavement Preservation projects the Interstate Management Program Guidelines require that a partial‑depth asphalt patch be considered acceptable only when the repaired area does not exceed ten percent of the total pavement surface; any patching greater than 10 % of the pavement area is generally deemed not cost‑effective or permitted, and the decision to apply such a patch must be based on the annual condition assessments and treatment matrices that identify distresses requiring repair before they trigger a higher‑level treatment. According to the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications a satisfactory manual asphalt patch must have a minimum area of one square foot, all edges cut vertically, and be cleaned of loose material and fully dried before a tack coat is applied to the bottom and vertical surfaces; after the tack coat cures, the asphalt binder for depths over three inches is placed in lifts that do not exceed three inches uncompacted depth and compacted with approved equipment, followed by the wearing course until the patch surface conforms to the grade of the surrounding pavement and all excavated areas are filled before the close of each workday, with the final 1½‑inch wearing layer permitted to be placed the next day, and edges sealed using the specified emulsified asphalt or tack material. For shallow repairs that precede an overlay any surface defect larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be filled with a patching material approved by the Representative and compacted to a flat, tight surface; when a geotextile interlayer is used the fabric surface temperature may not exceed 120°F before overlay placement, and for an asphalt interlayer a nominal one‑inch depth of Superpave mix must be placed, joint faulting greater than one‑half inch must be reduced by a scratch course or milling, and the pavement surface temperature may not exceed 120°F prior to placement. A satisfactory partial‑depth patch must be excavated to match the depth of the existing surface, cleaned of loose material, tack‑coated with emulsified asphalt, filled with an asphalt mix of the same type, then leveled and compacted so that it conforms to the adjacent pavement; the finished patch must meet the surface tolerances of a smoothness of one‑eighth inch in twelve feet, edge matching within one‑eighth inch, and cross‑slope within one‑eighth inch in twelve feet, and it must be acceptable to the Representative. Under the sixty‑month warranty a patch is considered satisfactory only when it conforms to the surface tolerance set out in Section 413.3(l) and stays within the distress limits defined in Table A, for example if the year‑5 performance survey shows that more than thirty percent of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses then remedial treatment such as microsurfacing is required; patches must be placed with plant‑mixed asphalt concrete (or other material expressly approved) and constructed to meet the specification’s construction end‑result and performance criteria, and when individual repair locations are spaced less than one hundred feet apart they shall be combined into a single continuous patch. Performance and service life

Asphalt patching restores pavement condition by removing the damaged portion and replacing it with plant‑mixed asphalt concrete that must satisfy the same surface tolerance and performance criteria as the original structure, thereby eliminating low‑severity distresses that would trigger remedial action if they exceed the threshold of 30 % of a segment having two or more such distresses by the year‑5 survey. By conforming to the specification’s requirement for square or rectangular patches and meeting the material standards set out in the warranty, the patched area regains functional ride quality and durability comparable to the surrounding pavement, and it remains covered under the 60‑month warranty provided the contractor completes remediation within 60 calendar days of notice. Consequently, as long as the patch meets these performance criteria, its expected service life aligns with the remainder of the five‑year warranty period. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Partial‑depth asphalt surface patching is commonly triggered when spalling or dislodgement of unsound pavement occurs during a profile‑milling operation, because the milling process can damage the existing surface and leave loose material. The prescribed repair calls for excavation to match the depth of the existing surface, cleaning all loose material, applying an emulsified asphalt tack coat, filling with an asphalt mixture of the same type, and then leveling and compacting so that the patched area conforms to adjacent pavement in smoothness and durability; the finished surface must meet a smoothness tolerance of 1/8 inch in 12 feet (and cross‑slope within the same tolerance), otherwise it is deemed defective work. Under the 60‑month warranty, annual performance surveys track distresses such as flushing, potholes, rutting, fatigue cracking and base‑condition failures; if by the year‑5 survey at least 30 % of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action such as microsurfacing or an approved patch repair is required, and all repaired areas must also satisfy the surface tolerance defined in Section 413.3(l). The recurrence of these distresses is linked to factors beyond the contractor’s control, including inadequate base condition, traffic loadings that exceed the designed ESALs, routine maintenance activities by the department, destructive procedures by the department, uncontrolled forces of nature and traffic‑accident damage. When any of those influences cause distress, remediation must be performed using plant‑mixed asphalt concrete unless otherwise approved, and if repair areas are spaced less than 100 feet they shall be combined into a single continuous repair. Monitoring and follow-up

After the patch is placed, the repaired portion is subject to the Department’s ongoing performance monitoring under the 60‑month warranty; it must be included in the annual automated surveys that identify distresses per Publication 336 and in manual surveys for flushing or potholes, with rut depth verified using a 12‑foot straight edge when required. The survey results are compared against Table A limits, and if at the year‑5 performance survey thirty percent of the segment shows two or more low‑severity distresses, additional remedial action is triggered. The contractor may also conduct nondestructive testing of the patched area after providing at least seven calendar days notice and must forward those test results to the Department. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Annual automated condition surveys that detect cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling or weathering, joint spalling, or joint faulting in a patched segment indicate that routine maintenance or more extensive rehabilitation may be required. When the amount of material needed to patch an area exceeds greater than 10 % of the total pavement area, the treatment is generally considered not cost‑effective as a preservation measure and should be escalated to rehabilitation. The prescribed cyclic crack‑sealing interval of every 3 to 5 years also serves as a trigger; if distress reappears before that window, additional repair may be warranted. Under the provisions of Section 548, the presence of unstable slabs or slab fragments that move or rock under normal traffic after an asphalt patch signals the need for further maintenance because such movement would produce non‑uniform support for any subsequent overlay. Still within Section 548, any surface defect left in a lane that exceeds 4 square inches in area and 1 inch deep must be filled with an approved patching material before additional treatment; failure to do so indicates the need for corrective action. Joint faulting greater than ¼ inch requires milling of the joint, while faulting exceeding ½ inch calls for a scratch course or further grinding to reduce the fault, indicating that the original patch did not adequately address the underlying distress. Surface temperatures above 120 °F before placing an interlayer are prohibited, and exceeding this limit triggers additional corrective actions. For work governed by Section 492 profile‑milling specifications, spalling or dislodgement of unsound pavement identified by the Representative during milling signals that a surface patch is required and that the treated area may need further maintenance if not corrected. If the milled surface fails to meet the smoothness tolerance of 1/8 inch in 12 feet (or the cross‑slope tolerance), the work is classified as defective and must be repaired to match adjacent pavement. Under the 60‑month warranty provisions of Section 496, annual performance surveys that show a segment exceeding the distress limits in Table A—such as a year‑5 survey finding that 30 % of the segment area contains two or more low‑severity distresses—trigger remedial action such as microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment. Manual surveys that identify flushing, potholes, or rut depths measured with a 12‑foot straight edge indicating severity also require repair. When the distance between individual repair patches is less than 100 feet, the patches must be combined into one continuous repair. [1][2]
