Binder Course

Part of: Pavement Structure is-subtopic-of
Binder Course is a topic under Pavement Structure.
binder-coursebinder-coursessuperpave-binder-courses
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

The binder course is the intermediate asphalt layer placed between the surface (wearing) and base courses when existing pavement lacks sufficient structural capacity, a condition that must be confirmed by non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures[1]. Its primary function is to add structural thickness that raises overall load‑bearing capacity while providing a smooth riding surface and resisting permanent deformation and low‑temperature cracking; the mixture is selected according to Superpave performance‑grade binders (or friction‑treated mixes for low‑volume roads) and, for high‑traffic facilities, polymer‑modified or crumb‑rubber modified binders are prescribed[1][2]. The course must be placed to the designed depth within tight tolerances—no more than a ¼ inch short in any core and no surface irregularities greater than 3⁄16 inch—and sealed joints and a cooling period until the temperature drops uniformly to 140 °F or less are required before traffic is allowed[2].

Purpose and design objectives

The binder course is specified when existing pavement lacks sufficient structural capacity, as identified through non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures, and its purpose is to add structural layer thickness that raises overall capacity [1]. For low‑volume roads the highly flexible overlay matches the flexibility of existing structures, providing a high‑void, soft‑asphalt layer capable of accommodating movement without overstressing the underlying pavement; FB wearing courses may be used independently on roadways having an ADT of 1,500 or less [1]. On roadways with ADT greater than 1,500 a combination of binder and wearing courses must be used to achieve the required structural support [1]. In Superpave design the binder course is provided to combat permanent deformation (rutting) and low‑temperature thermal cracking that arise from projected climatic and traffic conditions, with mixture selection based on an appropriate PG‑binder and the 20‑year design life ESALs [1]. For high‑traffic, heavily loaded pavements such as Interstates and Interstate look‑alikes where traffic exceeds 25,000 ADT, cumulative loads exceed 10 million ESALs over a 20‑year design life, and the treated area is larger than 50,000 square yards, crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder courses are recommended to satisfy these performance needs, provided cost‑effectiveness can be justified [1]. The binder course also must provide a smooth riding surface; therefore it must be placed to the designed thickness within tight tolerances, and any depth shortfall greater than 1/4 inch or a pattern of shortfalls exceeding 1/8 inch in three adjacent cores is classified as defective work [2]. Surface irregularities greater than 3/16 inch are likewise deemed defective because they would impair ride quality and durability [2]. To prevent water infiltration that could degrade the binder layer, joints must be sealed with hot or emulsified asphalt to create a watertight connection [2]. Newly compacted courses must remain free of traffic until the temperature drops uniformly to 140 °F or less, ensuring adequate strength development before loading [2]. For pervious asphalt pavement systems the binder course must resist moisture‑induced stripping; this need is addressed by requiring an anti‑strip additive or hydrated lime when the mix lacks a proven dense‑graded history and by testing according to ASTM D3625 with a minimum of 90 % coating [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 5 (pp. 103–131); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 280–309)

The binder course is required to add structural layer thickness that increases the pavement’s overall capacity, a requirement that must be verified by non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures. For friction‑treated (FB) surface systems the structural objective is met by applying a structural coefficient of 0.20 in the overlay design, which adjusts the required depth because standard programs assume Superpave material. Functionally, FB binder courses may be placed as stand‑alone layers only on low‑volume roads with an average daily traffic of 1,500 vehicles or less, whereas on roadways with ADT greater than 1,500 the binder course must be combined with a wearing course. Because FB‑1 and FB‑2 surfaces have high void content, a seal coat or surface treatment shall be placed after at least three months of warm‑weather traffic densification, although this requirement may be deferred up to three years if the underlying pavement is sound; for FB Modified surfaces the seal coat may be omitted for at least four years. The binder course shall be designed as part of a paving course that resists the climatic and traffic conditions at the project site, with the mixture selected to prevent permanent deformation (rutting) and low‑temperature thermal cracking over the intended 20‑year design life using the appropriate performance grade binder and the calculated equivalent single axle loads for that period. When the projected ESALs exceed ten million, the district should specify a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder for the binder or wearing course to avoid rutting. The CRMAB binder course shall use the same structural layer coefficients as Superpave courses in the structural design, and its individual layer thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches so that serviceability is maintained while avoiding excessive stiffness that would hinder handwork. High‑traffic applications such as Interstates or Interstate look‑alikes with an ADT greater than 25,000, more than ten million ESALs in a 20‑year design life, and at least 50,000 square yards of pavement are identified as the intended context for meeting durability and safety requirements. Placement is recommended only in the upper wearing and binder layers where its superior performance can be fully utilized, and the mixture must be produced by the wet process to achieve the required binder modification. The binder course must be placed to the design depth and remain within the tolerances prescribed in Section 413.3(m); any pavement that is deficient in depth by more than ¼ inch is considered defective work, and a uniform deficiency of more than ⅛ inch in three or more adjacent cores also triggers defect status. Surface smoothness shall be such that irregularities do not exceed 3⁄16 inch when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge, otherwise the pavement is deemed defective. Newly compacted binder course may not be subjected to vehicular traffic or loads for 24 hours or until its temperature uniformly drops to 140 °F or less, thereby ensuring adequate stability and safety before opening to traffic. When a depth deficiency is identified, it must be immediately corrected starting at the point of deficiency and proceeding longitudinally and transversely until the entire repair zone is within ¼ inch of the required depth, satisfying the structural—and by implication functional and serviceability—objectives for the pavement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 5 (pp. 103–131); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 280–353)

Scope and applicability

For asphalt‑concrete pavements, a binder course is appropriate when the project evaluation shows a need for extra structural layer thickness to raise the pavement’s structural capacity; this need is identified through non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures, and the binder course then serves as the intermediate layer between the surface and base courses. [1] FB‑1 binder mixes are cold mixes that are recommended for low‑volume roads with highly flexible existing pavement structures. [1] FB‑2 binder mixes must be produced at a stationary mixing plant, may use PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement, and therefore must be mixed hot and placed hot. [1] FB Modified may be used either as a binder or wearing course but may not be allowed on Federal‑aid projects. [1] FB wearing courses may be used independently on roadways having an ADT of 1,500 or less; for roadways with ADT greater than 1,500 a combination of binder and wearing courses must be used. [1] The Superpave design approach permits a binder course as the intermediate layer on any pavement where the project’s 20‑year design life ESALs fall within the ranges defined for wearing or binder courses; specifically, when the estimated ESAL range exceeds ten million, the District should specify a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder for leveling or binder courses that are expected to experience severe rutting, and when the ESAL range is 0.3 to < 3.0 million the District may specify binder courses using binders appropriate for an ESAL range of < 0.3 million where cracking rather than rutting is the primary concern. [1] Fiber‑modified asphalt mixes may be employed on binder courses when the selected PG binder is 58S‑28, 64S‑22, or 64E‑22, provided the optional Standard Special Provision for fiber‑modified courses is applied. [1] CRMAB binder courses should be considered for projects that would normally use dense‑graded wearing or binder courses with polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder and may be used for new construction or overlays on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements, especially on Interstates and Interstate look‑alikes where the average daily traffic exceeds 25,000 vehicles, the design ESALs exceed 10 million over a 20‑year life, and the project area exceeds 50,000 square yards. [1] The mixture may contain RAP or RAS as specified; it is recommended that CRMAB be used in the upper layers of the pavement section (wearing and binder courses) and layer thickness should not exceed 2 ½ inches; the mixture must be produced using the wet process only, and if warm‑mix asphalt is employed a chemical additive is required while foaming is prohibited. [1] CRMAB should not be used where significant handwork or raking is required, in shoulders unless lane and shoulder are paved in one pass, or in temporary lanes or crossovers. [1] In flexible pavement projects that fall under Section 413, a binder course may be placed using notched wedge joints provided the nominal maximum aggregate size (NMAS) of the mixture does not exceed 19.0 mm; the constructed binder and wearing courses must also satisfy the depth verification requirements of Section 413, where any deficit greater than 1/4 inch is deemed defective work. [2] For pervious asphalt pavement systems that require a 19‑mm thick open‑graded structural layer, the binder‑course layer is specified in Section 420; the mix must use an Asphalt Cement of class PG 64S‑22, may incorporate reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) but RAP shall not exceed 10 % of the mixture, and when moisture susceptibility is a concern an anti‑strip additive must be used at a dosage not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt; if AASHTO T 305 testing indicates that a stabilizer is needed, cellulose fibers may be added at a dosage rate that must be within 0.2 % to 0.4 % by weight of the total mix. [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 5 (pp. 103–131); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 280–309)

When non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures do not demonstrate a need for additional structural layer thickness, the binder course is not selected and alternative treatments such as drainage improvements, base repair, scratch and leveling courses, surface‑friction upgrades, or shoulder work are considered instead. For roadways having an average daily traffic greater than 1,500, a combination of binder and wearing courses must be used, so relying solely on an FB binder course is not preferred; FB wearing courses may be used independently only when ADT is 1,500 or less. Additionally, FB Modified may not be allowed on Federal‑aid projects, requiring another pavement type such as Superpave. When a deflection‑based design program that assumes Superpave overlay material is applied, the FB overlay depth must be adjusted with a structural coefficient of 0.20; otherwise a conventional Superpave design would be more appropriate. If projected traffic exceeds ten million ESALs, designers should replace the standard binder course with a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder for wearing or binder layers to prevent severe rutting; conversely, when the 20‑year design life predicts an ESAL range of 0.3 to < 3.0 million, the district may lower the specified ESAL range to < 0.3 million only for wearing and binder courses provided that asphalt cracking is a regional problem, average truck speed is above thirty miles per hour, and rutting is not a concern, and smaller mix quantities (under 250 tons or under 1,000 square yards at 1½ inches) may also permit an ESAL range shift of one category to limit the number of mix designs. When the existing pavement needs its longitudinal profile or cross‑section re‑established, a separate leveling course is preferred because only a leveling course can perform that correction; it must be at least one inch thick to provide a uniform working platform and may be counted as a one‑inch paving layer in structural calculations. Combining a binder course with a leveling course is not recommended since maintaining adequate quality control of either the leveling or binder layer becomes difficult, so an alternative design such as a dedicated leveling (or scratch) course should be used instead. CRMAB binder courses are appropriate only when their premium cost can be justified; they should not be used when the mixture’s stiffness would require significant handwork or raking, when placed in shoulders unless the lane and shoulder are paved in a single pass, when applied to temporary lanes or crossovers, or when the layer thickness exceeds 2 ½ inches. Because CRMAB mixes are more expensive than conventional Superpave mixtures, they should only be used where cost‑effectiveness can be demonstrated, typically in the upper wearing and binder layers of new construction, as lower‑layer placement does not provide added benefit. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 5 (pp. 103–131)

Design inputs and requirements

For an AR‑GG binder course the design inputs include site selection limited to Interstates or Interstate look‑alikes, traffic conditions that must be greater than 25,000 ADT and greater than 10 million ESALs over a 20‑year design life with at least 50,000 square yards of pavement, material specifications that allow RAP or RAS as defined in the SSP, require wet‑process blending of the CRM with the binder, limit layer thickness to no more than 2 ½ inches and permit a chemical warm‑mix additive (but not foaming), and existing‑condition criteria that accept new construction or overlays on asphalt, concrete or composite pavements while excluding temporary lanes, shoulders unless paved in one pass, or other low‑value applications. Environmental and geotechnical information are not addressed in this section. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (p. 129)

The binder‑course design must conform to the overall project scope and be consistent with Federal policy on pavement management and design as set out in the Federal‑aid Highway Program Policy and Guidance Center, while any additional structural layer thickness is to be determined by non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures [1]. For FB surface courses the binder course is governed by the FB‑1 and FB‑2 specifications which require cold‑mix materials; FB‑2 mixing shall occur at a stationary plant using PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement, and bids must be issued under Standard Special Provisions c10000 (FB‑1/FB‑2) or C10001 (FB‑Modified), with FB‑Modified not permitted on Federal‑aid projects [1]. When a deflection‑based design is employed a structural coefficient of 0.20 shall be applied to FB courses, and traffic constraints dictate that on roadways with an ADT of 1,500 or less the wearing course may stand alone, whereas for ADT greater than 1,500 a binder plus wearing combination is required [1]. The Superpave asphalt mixture design system governs binder courses by requiring specification of a performance‑grade (PG) binder and using 20‑year ESAL estimates to select one of five ESAL ranges; if the 20‑year ESAL estimate exceeds ten million, the district should specify a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder for binder (and wearing) courses, while when the estimated range is 0.3 to < 3.0 million ESALs the district may specify the lower < 0.3 million range for binder courses [1]. Projects are generally limited to no more than three individual mix designs per project, five for major projects, and for mix‑design quantities less than 250 tons or less than 1,000 square yards at a 1½‑inch thickness the ESAL range may be shifted up or down by one range without additional approval [1]. Overlay provisions require that a binder layer be placed at a uniform depth, may not be combined with a leveling course because quality control would be difficult, and its thickness shall fall within the minimum and maximum limits shown in Table 10.5 while using an approved Superpave mix of 25 mm, 19 mm, 12.5 mm or 9.5 mm at the appropriate minimum depth; when a safety edge is provided the binder (or wearing) course shall be at least 1½ inches thick [1]. Crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder dense‑graded courses are limited to projects normally employing dense‑graded wearing or binder courses with polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, may be placed only in the upper layers of the pavement section, and shall not exceed a layer thickness of 2½ inches; structural layer coefficients for Superpave courses in Table 10.6 are to be used for design, and use is recommended on Interstates or Interstate‑look‑alikes with > 25,000 ADT, > 10 million ESALs (20‑year design life), and > 50,000 square yards, while they should not be used where significant handwork or raking is required, in shoulders unless the lane and shoulder are paved in one pass, or in temporary lanes or crossovers [1]. Under Section 413.3 of the Flexible Pavements specifications binder‑course construction is governed by joint, depth and surface tolerances; the contractor may use notched wedge joints for wearing and binder courses when the NMAS mixture size does not exceed 19.0 mm, with placement of the abutting asphalt mixture required within one working day if the joint abuts opposing traffic or within two working days if it abuts traffic moving in the same direction [2]. The pavement is considered defective if surface irregularities exceed 3/16 inch, if depth deficiency exceeds 1/4 inch at any core, or if uniform depth deficiency exceeds 1/8 inch across three or more adjacent cores, and such deficiencies must be remedied to bring the depth within 1/4 inch of the required value [2]. Newly placed binder material shall not be subjected to vehicular traffic or loads for at least 24 hours or until the course uniformly cools to a temperature of 140 °F or less [2]. Section 496.3, which governs the 60‑month warranty for asphalt concrete pavements, requires that the binder course be built as part of a combination of two or more asphalt concrete courses to achieve the specified total depth, with thickness verified by coring within 24 hours after final compaction in the presence of the inspector; any core indicating a depth deficiency greater than 1/4 inch or uniform deficiency greater than 1/8 inch across three consecutive cores that cannot be satisfactorily corrected shall be deemed defective work and must be remedied [2]. For pervious binder courses limited to a nominal aggregate size of 19 mm, Section 420.2(e) specifies that the asphalt cement shall be class PG 64S‑22, recycled asphalt pavement may be incorporated but not exceed ten percent of the total mixture, an anti‑strip additive must be blended at a dosage of no less than 0.25 percent by weight of the asphalt when moisture susceptibility is a concern, and any required stabilizer must contain cellulose fiber between 0.2 percent and 0.4 percent of the total mix; mix design shall follow AASHTO T‑209 for theoretical specific gravity and AASHTO T‑312 for bulk specific gravity, with air‑void calculations performed by any method listed in Table B, a Job Mix Formula submitted at least three weeks before production, an approved quality‑control plan per Section 106, and material certifications for asphalt content, gradation and air voids provided to the inspector within one working day after testing [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 5 (pp. 103–131); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 280–353)

Design methods and criteria

The required thickness of a binder course is established by evaluating the need for additional structural capacity through non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures. [1] When a binder course is placed over an FB surface (FB‑1, FB‑2, or FB‑Modified), design is performed with a deflection‑based design program and the overlay depth must be adjusted because the program assumes Superpave material; a structural coefficient of 0.20 is applied for FB courses. [1] The appropriate Standard Special Provision—c10000 for FB‑1/FB‑2 or C10001 for FB‑Modified—shall be used in such designs. [1] FB‑1 mixes are typically cold mixes placed by a mobile plant but may also be mixed in a stationary plant, whereas FB‑2 mixes must be produced at a stationary mixing plant and require hot mixing and placement when PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement is used. [1] For binder courses designed as Superpave paving layers, the Superpave volumetric asphalt mixture design procedure replaces the Marshall method and incorporates advanced performance testing and prediction models to address rutting and low‑temperature cracking; designers first calculate a 20‑year design life ESAL value and assign it to one of five ESAL ranges (< 0.3 M, 0.3–< 3.0 M, 3.0–< 10.0 M, 10.0–< 30.0 M, ≥ 30.0 M) to select the appropriate Performance‑Grade binder, and when the ESAL range exceeds 10 million the specification requires a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder for wearing or binder courses. [1] In overlay applications the binder course is placed over a leveling course that must be at least 1‑inch thick to provide a uniform working platform. [1] The binder layer may be constructed from Superpave mixes of 25.0 mm, 19.0 mm, 12.5 mm or 9.5 mm size provided the appropriate minimum depths are met, and design decisions rely on Tables 9.3, 9.5, 10.4 and 10.5 to obtain the structural coefficient number and allowable placement depth, while Table 10.1 or 10.2 supplies the ADT limits governing its application; a safety edge shall be incorporated with the binder course at a depth of 1½ inches or greater. [1] For an asphalt rubber gap‑graded binder course (AR‑GG), the structural layer coefficients given for Superpave courses in Table 10.6 are applied to the AR‑GG layer, and use is limited to projects that would normally require gap‑graded wearing or binder courses with polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder and that meet traffic and size thresholds of greater than 25,000 ADT, greater than 10 million ESALs over a 20‑year design life, and greater than 50,000 square yards; the layer thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches. [1] Similarly, a crumb rubber modified asphalt binder dense‑graded course (CRMAB) is designed using the Superpave structural layer coefficients listed in Table 10.6 applied to the CRMAB layer; its thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches, it must be produced by the wet process with a chemical warm‑mix additive required if WMA is employed, and it may be placed in new construction or overlays on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements but should only be used on Interstates and Interstate look‑alikes that exceed 25 000 ADT, more than 10 million ESALs over a 20‑year design life, and larger than 50 000 square yards; the mixture may contain RAP or RAS as specified in the SSP. [1] The binder‑course construction permits the contractor to use notched wedge joints when the nominal maximum aggregate size of the mixture does not exceed 19.0 mm, and the joint is to be built in accordance with the Standard Drawing; the abutting asphalt must be placed within one working day if the joint adjoins opposing traffic or within two days if it adjoins same‑direction traffic. [2] Transverse joints may be sawed, should have bulkheads that are straight and perpendicular, and the joint face is to receive a thin coating of PG‑Binder or PG 64S‑22 before fresh mix placement. [2] After compaction the finished surface is verified with a 12‑foot straightedge and any irregularity greater than 3/16 inch is deemed defective; depth compliance follows PTM No. 737 density sampling for lot‑based acceptance or mass‑per‑square‑meter calculations, and core drilling per PTM No. 1 is required when the yield indicates insufficient depth; pavement deficient by more than ¼ inch (or more than ⅛ inch in three adjacent cores) is considered defective work under this section. In the project design process the binder course is evaluated when an additional structural layer thickness is required to increase the pavement’s structural capacity, and that required thickness must be established by non‑destructive testing (NDT) or other acceptable design procedures. When a deflection‑based design program is applied to FB surfaces, the overlay depth must be adjusted because the program assumes Superpave material, and a structural coefficient of 0.20 must be used for FB courses in those designs. FB wearing courses may be used alone only on roadways with an ADT of 1,500 or less, while any roadway with an ADT greater than 1,500 requires a combination of binder and wearing courses. Under the Superpave system the binder course must be designed using a PG‑Binder selected for the project and the 20‑year design‑life ESALs, which are required for all projects regardless of intended service life; the calculated ESAL range determines the mixture, and when the 20‑year ESALs exceed 10 million the district shall specify a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder for the binder (or leveling) course to prevent rutting. For projects whose ESALs fall in the 0.3 to < 3.0 million range, the district may treat the binder course as if it were in the < 0.3 million range when asphalt cracking is a regional problem, truck speeds exceed 30 mph, or rutting is not a concern. If the mix quantity is less than 250 tons or less than 1,000 square yards at 1½ in thickness, the ESAL range may be shifted up or down by one range to limit the number of mix designs; larger adjustments require PDAU approval, and fiber‑modified courses are optional for binder courses when using PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22, or PG 64E‑22 binders but they do not permit reductions in required pavement thickness. The binder course may only be placed as a separate layer; it shall not be combined with a leveling course because quality control would be difficult, and any leveling work must be done with a distinct leveling course that can be counted as a 1‑inch thick paving course in structural calculations. The binder layer must be constructed from one of the approved Superpave mixes (25 mm, 19 mm, 12.5 mm or 9.5 mm) and installed at the minimum depth specified in Table 10.5 to achieve the required cross‑section while respecting the overall thickness limits given in that table; when a Safety Edge is provided it must be applied to both wearing and binder courses with a depth of at least 1½ inches and the total edge depth may not exceed 5 inches. Crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder (CRMAB) dense‑graded courses should be used for projects that would normally employ dense‑graded wearing or binder courses with polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder and may be placed in new construction or overlays on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements; the mixture should be confined to the upper layers of the pavement (wearing and binder courses) because placement in lower layers has not shown added benefit, and layer thickness should not exceed 2½ inches. The structural layer coefficients provided for Superpave courses in Table 10.6 should be used for the CRMAB layer in the structural pavement design, and use may be permitted on Interstates and Interstate look‑alikes that have > 25,000 ADT, > 10 million ESALs (20‑year design life), and > 50,000 square yards, provided the binder is not applied where significant handwork or raking would be required due to mixture stiffness. The pervious 19 mm binder course shall be produced using an asphalt cement that meets the PG 64S‑22 grade specified in Section 702, its aggregate gradation must follow the sizing limits of Table A and air‑void content shall be determined by any method listed in Table B while the maximum theoretical specific gravity is to be calculated in accordance with AASHTO T209, with two specimens gyrated per AASHTO T312 and their bulk specific gravities averaged for the void calculation. The mix design shall be verified in a plant laboratory against the uniformity tolerances of this specification, a Job Mix Formula that conforms to all Department requirements must be submitted at least three weeks before the scheduled start of producing the mixture, and a quality‑control plan approved by the Representative is required prior to any production; production QC tests shall follow Table C and material certifications for asphalt content, gradation and air voids must be provided within one working day after testing. During construction the binder course together with the wearing course must be placed to the design depth and remain within the tolerances set out in Section 413.3, any pavement that is deficient in thickness by more than ¼ inch at a single core location shall be deemed defective work, and a uniform deficiency of more than ⅛ inch over three or more adjacent cores also constitutes defect; surface irregularities exceeding ³⁄₁₆ inch are considered defective. Vehicular traffic or loads shall not be permitted on newly compacted binder courses for at least 24 hours or until the temperature of the course uniformly cools to 140 °F or less. For projects covered by the 60‑month warranty, no core taken within 24 hours after final compaction may be deficient in depth by more than ¼ inch and the pavement must not exhibit a uniform deficiency of more than ⅛ inch over any three consecutive cores; if either condition occurs and cannot be satisfactorily corrected, the area shall be classified as defective work and must be repaired until the depth is within ¼ inch of the required thickness. Structural section and dimensions

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), for crumb‑rubber‑modified asphalt binder courses the only dimensional limit expressed is that the layer thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches, and the manual provides no explicit thickness values for the pavement, overlay, base, subbase, treated‑soil, shoulder, edge‑support or widening [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification (Publication 408/2026, Section 496.3) states that the binder course does not have a prescribed fixed thickness; instead the installed pavement must attain the total depth called for in the design and any post‑compaction core must fall within the allowable tolerances [2]. Under those tolerances, if a single core measurement shows a depth deficiency greater than 1/4 inch that cannot be satisfactorily corrected, the area is deemed defective work [2]. Likewise, if three consecutive cores each exhibit a uniform deficiency greater than 1/8 inch that cannot be remedied, the area is also considered defective, and corrective actions must continue until the depth throughout the repair zone is within 1/4 inch of the required depth [2]. No other dimensional requirements for overlay, base, subbase, treated‑soil, shoulder, edge‑support or widening are provided in either source [1][2].

Materials and mixture design

The binder‑course specifications in the Pavement Policy Manual require that the mix type be identified; FB‑1 is a cold‑mix material while FB‑2 shall be produced at a stationary plant using hot mixing with either PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement, and no other grades are permitted. The mixture for FB courses is described as having a high void content that provides high flexibility, and when a deflection‑based design program is employed a structural coefficient of 0.20 must be used. In the Superpave paving course provisions, the binder course shall be defined by the performance‑grade asphalt binder (PG‑Binder) specified in Section 5.10 and Table 5.5 together with a 20‑year design life ESAL range that is calculated for the project; the ESAL range must be selected from one of five brackets: less than 0.3 million, 0.3 to less than 3.0 million, 3.0 to less than 10.0 million, 10.0 to less than 30.0 million, or ≥30.0 million. When the calculated ESAL range exceeds 10 million, a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder shall be specified for the binder (or wearing) course to mitigate rutting. The CRMAB binder‑course provisions require that the mixture be produced by the wet process only, employ a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder combined with crumb rubber, and may contain RAP or RAS material as defined in the SSP; if warm‑mix asphalt is used, a chemical warm‑mix additive should be utilized but foaming is not allowed, and the layer thickness should not exceed 2 ½ inches. For binder courses that incorporate notched wedge joints, the design shall limit the nominal maximum aggregate size to 19.0 mm or less, and the asphalt material placed on the joint faces shall be the same PG‑Binder grade used in the pavement course or, alternatively, PG 64S‑22. For pervious binder courses specified with a 19.0 mm nominal maximum aggregate size, the asphalt cement shall be classified as PG 64S‑22 and any recycled asphalt pavement incorporated into the mix may not exceed ten percent of the total mixture. When an anti‑strip additive is required, it must be blended with the binder at a dosage of no less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt cement. If a pervious stabilizer is specified, its cellulose fiber content must fall between 0.2 % and 0.4 % by weight of the total mix. The mixture design shall follow the aggregate sizing presented in Table A, determine air voids using the methods described in Table B, and verify bulk specific gravity and air‑void targets through the laboratory procedures outlined. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 103–131); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 280–309)

For flexible base (FB) surfaces, the binder course is specified as a cold‑mix with high void content and its flexibility makes it suitable for low‑volume roads; therefore designers select softer asphalt cement such as PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 in an FB‑2 mix that must be produced at a stationary plant and placed hot. Structural adequacy for these FB courses is addressed by applying a structural coefficient of 0.20 when the course is used in a deflection‑based overlay design. Durability is enhanced by requiring a seal coat or surface treatment after at least three months of warm‑weather traffic densification before application. Constructability considerations include the choice between mobile and stationary mixing plants, with FB‑1 mixes permissible on a mobile plant while FB‑2 mixes must use a stationary plant, and hot placement is required when PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 is used. Performance expectations are limited by traffic volume: FB wearing courses may be placed alone on roadways having an average daily traffic (ADT) of 1,500 or less, whereas a combination of binder and wearing courses is required above that threshold. When the binder course is designed using the Superpave volumetric asphalt mixture design procedure, the project designer selects a PG‑binder and an ESAL range that reflect the 20‑year traffic loading; for projects with an estimated ESAL range greater than 10 million, the District should specify a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder for the binder (or leveling) course to avoid pavement rutting. For an ESAL range of 0.3 to less than 3.0 million, the District may use the lower than 0.3 million specification for wearing and binder courses when cracking is a concern. Designers are encouraged to limit the number of distinct mix designs to no more than three per typical project and not more than five on major projects to control constructability and cost. When a binder course is placed as part of an overlay, the mix must be selected from the Superpave gradations of 25 mm, 19 mm, 12.5 mm or 9.5 mm and installed at the minimum depth prescribed in the structural coefficient tables referenced for placement depths. A separate leveling course of at least one‑inch thickness may be used to provide a uniform working platform, but the source advises that a binder course should not be combined with a leveling course because quality control becomes difficult. For projects that would otherwise require gap‑graded wearing or binder layers, asphalt rubber binders may be used in place of the polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder provided the work is new construction or an overlay and the traffic conditions exceed 25,000 ADT, 10 million ESALs over a 20‑year design life, and 50,000 square yards of pavement; the mixture shall have a layer thickness that does not exceed 2 1/2 inches and must be produced by the wet process blending the crumb rubber modifier with the binder before adding aggregates. The mixture should not be chosen for projects that demand significant handwork or raking because of its stiffness, and it must be justified as cost‑effective despite being a premium mix. CRMAB binders are appropriate only for projects that would otherwise use dense‑graded wearing or binder courses with polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder and meet high traffic criteria—more than 25,000 ADT, more than 10 million ESALs over a 20‑year design life, and an area greater than 50,000 square yards; the layer thickness should not exceed 2 1/2 inches and the mix may contain RAP or RAS as specified. The CRMAB layer is to be placed in the upper layers of new construction or overlays because its superior performance is best utilized there, structural layer coefficients from Table 10.6 for Superpave courses should be applied in the structural design, and the wet process is required; the mix should not be used where significant handwork or raking is needed, on shoulders unless paved in one pass, or in temporary lanes because of its premium cost and stiffness. For pervious asphalt binder courses designated as a 19 mm binder course, the specification requires an asphalt cement of class PG 64S‑22, limits recycled asphalt pavement (RAP) to a maximum of 10 % of the mixture, and mandates incorporation of any required anti‑strip additive at not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt. If a pervious stabilizer is needed, cellulose fibers must be dosed between 0.2 % and 0.4 % by weight of the total mix. Aggregate fractions are to be sized and uniformly graded according to Table A and air‑voids determined using any method listed in Table B, with bulk specific gravity measured per AASHTO T 209 and gyrated specimens per AASHTO T 312. The mixture design must be tested at the plant laboratory, meet all JMF production tolerances, receive QC plan approval before production, and after production certification of asphalt content, gradation, and air voids is required. Structural details and interfaces

The binder course requires a safety‑edge edge treatment on the outside edge of asphalt pavements and shoulders; it shall be provided for both wearing and binder courses with a minimum depth of 1 ½ inches, and the total thickness must not exceed 5 inches. The manual advises that a leveling course should not be combined with a binder course because adequate quality control would be difficult. Asphalt paving interlayers may be placed between two asphalt layers—including a binder layer—according to the interlayer specifications, but no mandatory joint or notch requirements are stated for the binder course. For binder courses using NMAS mixtures of 19.0 mm or smaller the contractor may employ notched wedge joints; all loose material must be removed before opening to traffic, and the joint shall be constructed per the standard drawing. The abutting asphalt mixture must be placed within one working day when the joint is adjacent to opposing traffic, or within two working days when the joint is adjacent to traffic in the same direction; if the lanes forming a longitudinal joint are not placed on the same day, additional uneven‑lane signage shall be installed. The contractor may also saw transverse joints perpendicular to the pavement centreline, install bulkheads straight and perpendicular to the surface, and paint the joint face with a thin coating of the PG‑Binder used in the course or PG 64S‑22 before placing fresh mix; painting is not required for scratch courses. Where a binder or wearing course abuts existing pavement at paving notches, lane additions or utility openings, the joint must be sealed with hot asphalt material of the class and type designated for the wearing course, applying the sealant at least 6 inches on each side; emulsified asphalt (PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT) may be used instead provided the area is cleaned, voids are completely filled to create a watertight joint, excess material is removed and the sealed area is covered with an acceptable light dry sand. [1][2]

For binder (FB) courses the interface to any seal coat or surface treatment is controlled by the high void content; a seal coat or surface treatment must be placed on the FB surface after at least three months of warm‑weather traffic densification. If the underlying pavement is structurally sound and the FB surface is in satisfactory condition, this application can be postponed for up to three years. When a deflection‑based design program is used, the overlay depth must be adjusted because the program assumes Superpave material, and a structural coefficient of 0.20 must be used for FB courses when designing FB overlays. The binder course interface is typically formed with a notched‑wedge joint when the NMAS mixture does not exceed 19.0 mm; the joint must be built according to the Standard Drawing after all loose material has been removed. For joints abutting opposing traffic, the adjacent asphalt must be placed within one working day, whereas for same‑direction traffic the placement may occur within two working days. For transverse joints the joint face shall be painted with a thin coating of the PG‑Binder (or PG 64S‑22) before fresh mixture is laid against it. Other joints that meet existing pavement are sealed with hot asphalt of the wearing‑course class applied at least 6 inches on each side and, if used, emulsified TACK or NTT/CNTT to achieve a watertight seal; excess material is removed and covered with dry sand. Drainage, environment, and accessibility

The binder course is required on roadways with average daily traffic (ADT) greater than 1,500, where it must be combined with a wearing course; on roads with ADT of 1,500 or less a wearing course alone may be used [1]. For Interstates or Interstate‑look‑alike facilities that carry more than 25,000 ADT, experience over 10 million ESALs in a 20‑year design life and have an area exceeding 50,000 square yards, the use of a crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder (CRMAB) dense‑graded course is permitted [1]. The binder layer shall be placed at a uniform depth and its cross‑slope is established by the overlying leveling or wearing course; it should not be combined with a separate leveling course because quality control becomes difficult [1]. When a safety edge is provided, it must be applied to both the wearing and binder courses and shall have a depth of at least 1 ½ inches, adding roughly one percent to material quantities [1]. The thickness of the binder course shall not exceed 2 ½ inches, and the mixture is recommended for placement in the upper layers of the pavement structure where its performance benefits are realized; lower‑layer placement provides no added benefit [1]. The binder course should not be used in shoulders unless the lane and shoulder are paved in a single pass, and it should be avoided on temporary lanes or crossovers where a premium mix is not warranted [1]. After placement, vehicular traffic or loads shall be prohibited on the freshly compacted binder course for 24 hours or until the material cools uniformly to 140 °F or less, with alternate routes provided as directed [2]. Surface irregularities of the binder course are limited to no more than 3/16 inch when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge; depth deficiencies greater than 1/4 inch constitute defective work requiring correction [2]. Additionally, depth deficiencies exceeding 1/8 inch in three or more adjacent core locations also constitute defective work [2]. When notched wedge joints are employed, any loose material must be removed before the lane is opened to traffic, and the abutting mixture shall be placed within one working day for opposing‑traffic openings or within two days for same‑direction openings, with “Uneven Lane” signage installed as required [2].

Verification and expected performance

The completed binder course is checked through a sequence that begins with verification of the mix design, continues with field construction checks, and ends with post‑placement acceptance testing. Under Section 420 the Job Mix Formula must conform to all Department requirements and be submitted at least three weeks before production; a QC Plan prepared as required in Section 106 must be approved before any paving starts. During plant production the prescribed QC tests of Table C are performed and material certifications for asphalt content, gradation and air voids are obtained, with copies provided to the Inspector‑in‑Charge within one working day after testing. This sequence ensures that the placed binder course complies with the specification, is constructible, meets performance criteria such as air‑void limits, and remains consistent with the project requirements. Section 413 requires that the finished surface be tested with a 12‑foot straightedge at any area the Representative determines may be deficient; any irregularity exceeding 3/16 inch is considered defective. Depth is then checked against the design depth either by measuring each sublot per PTM No. 737 or, when density acceptance is based on yield, by calculating mass and drilling a 6‑inch diameter core for each 500 tons placed; pavement that is deficient by more than 1/4 inch (or by more than 1/8 inch in three adjacent cores) is classified as defective work. The course must not be opened to vehicular traffic or loads for 24 hours or until it has uniformly cooled to 140°F or less, and any identified defects must be repaired or replaced as directed before acceptance. Section 496 adds that within twenty‑four hours after final compaction, in the presence of the Inspector, a core at least four inches in diameter is taken for each two thousand linear feet of lane and its depth measured according to PTM No. 737. If any single core shows a depth deficiency of more than one‑quarter inch that cannot be satisfactorily corrected, or if three consecutive cores are uniformly short by more than one‑eighth inch and the shortage cannot be corrected, the area is deemed defective work and must be repaired until the depth throughout the repair zone is within one‑quarter inch of the required thickness. Additional cores may be drilled forward and back of the deficient locations to locate the extent of the deficiency, and the Inspector may also direct loose‑mix or core samples for informational testing.
