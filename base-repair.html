Base Repair

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Base Repair is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
base-repair
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-30

Base Repair is a pavement‑preservation treatment applied when the roadway’s base layer has lost sufficient structural capacity or effective drainage, which is manifested by failures such as water‑filled “bathtub” conditions, cracking, slab movement, surface irregularities exceeding one‑half inch, or densities below required limits [1][2]. The process involves removing the compromised portion, reshaping and re‑moistening the subgrade or subbase, then reconstructing the base with a material that matches the original type and thickness—typically a standard Base Course, Superpave Base Course, or AASHTO #1 aggregate—and compacting it to at least 95 % (often 100 %) of laboratory‑maximum density while meeting prescribed seven‑day strength criteria [1][2]. By restoring a uniform, well‑drained foundation that can evenly distribute traffic loads and eliminate slab rocking, Base Repair reestablishes load‑distribution capacity, prepares the pavement for overlay, and extends service life as a cost‑effective preservation measure [1][2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Base Repair is applied when the pavement’s base layer has failed or become compromised to the point that it no longer provides sufficient structural capacity or effective drainage, a condition that may be evident in the field through obvious failure, deficient or improperly compacted material, water‑filled “bathtub” situations, cracking and seating of underlying concrete, or rubblization of a slab [1][2]. The treatment is also triggered by performance surveys that detect fatigue‑cracking or other distresses indicating an unacceptable underlying condition [2]. Surface irregularities that exceed a half‑inch in height are considered unsatisfactory and must be corrected before any overlay can be placed [2]. Likewise, when the compacted density of the base falls below the required limits—less than 100 % of maximum field density or less than 95 % of laboratory‑compacted maximum density—the base is deemed deficient and requires repair [2]. Thickness deficiencies of a half‑inch or more also necessitate removal of the inadequate material and replacement to restore the original design thickness, with an emphasis on matching the existing type and thickness of the base course [1][2]. Contamination of the base by subgrade or subbase material is another condition that calls for removal of the mixed material, reshaping and recompaction of the clean subgrade or subbase, and reconstruction of a satisfactory base layer [2]. Strength shortfalls, such as an indirect tensile strength below 50 psi or an unconfined compressive strength outside the 300–500 psi range at seven days, are identified as triggers for Base Repair because they compromise load‑bearing capacity [2]. Shrinkage cracking and visible movement or rocking of slabs under normal traffic or roller loading also indicate a loss of uniform support that must be stabilized or repaired before overlay placement [2][1]. Surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch, which would prevent proper interlayer or surface course installation, are required to be filled with appropriate patching material and compacted to a flat, tight surface [2]. In all of these cases the prescribed action involves removing the failed sections, reshaping, re‑moistening, and recompacting the area, or fully replacing it with a base course that matches the original material—such as Base Course/Superpave Base Course or AASHTO #1 aggregate—to restore uniform load distribution, structural performance, proper drainage, and smoothness prior to overlay placement [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–60), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 209–229), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 347–358), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–471)

Base repair is undertaken after an evaluation identifies the causes of base failure and involves removing failed areas and replacing them with material matching the original type and thickness to provide adequate structural capacity and proper drainage. [1] The treatment also requires cleaning, stabilizing, and backfilling with approved subbase such as Subbase No. 2A, followed by patching with flexible‑base or cementitious/asphalt repair mixes that meet performance criteria like at least 95 % of laboratory‑compacted maximum density and the seven‑day strength requirement before traffic is allowed. [2] By removing unsuitable or unstable material and restoring the base to its designed load‑distribution capacity, the primary objective is to repair damage and restore functionality of the pavement. [1][2] Because it is classified as a pavement‑preservation activity, the work is also intended to be cost‑effective and to retard further deterioration, thereby slowing the progression of distress while maintaining or improving roadway condition. [1] Although preventing future deterioration is not the primary purpose, the restored structural capacity contributes to extending the pavement’s useful service life. [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–60), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 209–229), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 347), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 360–471)

Distress characterization and causes

The distress that triggers a base‑repair is found in the base course itself, where surface irregularities such as high or low spots develop on the finished layer. When any deviation exceeds about one‑half inch, it is classified as severe enough to require immediate correction by blading, loosening, reshaping and recompacting, or full removal and reconstruction of the affected portion. The source does not quantify how quickly these irregularities worsen nor does it describe specific safety or operational impacts, only that traffic on a damaged base should be limited to essential construction equipment. Consequently, the location is the base layer surface, the extent is any area with >½‑inch deviation, severity is high enough to mandate repair, while progression rate and safety consequences are not detailed in the excerpt. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 212–214)

The distress that prompts a Base Repair is fundamentally driven by water infiltration and inadequate drainage, which erode the base layer’s ability to distribute loads and create zones of failure that must be removed. [1][2] When surface or subsurface moisture is not effectively expelled, it accumulates within the pavement structure, reducing structural support and producing visible distress such as premature mid‑slab cracking in jointed concrete pavements, a symptom of weak subbase material. [1][2] Additional degradation arises from aggregate reactions, including those associated with Vanport Limestone and alkali‑silica reaction, which further compromise the integrity of the base before they are detected by routine surveys. [1] The guides note that mixing subgrade or subbase material into the base course contaminates the layer, weakening support and diminishing structural capacity. [2] Placing the initial fine‑material layer on a wet, frozen, or otherwise unsuitable subgrade permits water to infiltrate, creating voids that lower bearing strength. [2] Inadequate compaction—densities below 95 % of the laboratory maximum dry density—or failure to achieve the specified depth by at least a half‑inch leaves unfilled spaces that deform under traffic loads. [2] Premature or excessive traffic, especially before the surface course is placed or before a seven‑day cure period for reclaimed material, can mar, distort, or otherwise damage the base layer. [2] Chemically stabilized material tested to strengths exceeding 900 psi may induce shrinkage cracking, while compaction control strips that fall short of the minimum density require additional compaction cycles. [2] Unstable or unsuitable material beneath concrete slabs, combined with insufficient drainage that allows water ingress, undermines subgrade support, produces vertical deflection of fractured slabs, and can cause visible rocking or movement of slab fragments under normal traffic, resulting in non‑uniform support for any new overlay. [1][2] Joint faulting greater than one‑quarter inch typically necessitates milling, whereas faulting exceeding one‑half inch requires a scratch course, both conditions reflecting compromised base integrity. [2] Finally, exposure of the pavement surface to temperatures above 120 °F before overlay placement can exacerbate these mechanisms and accelerate distress. [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–60), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 209–229), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–471)

Applicability and treatment selection

Base Repair is indicated when a concrete slab cannot be fully seated, as shown by continued vertical deflection under the pneumatic roller, or when slabs or fragments move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads, creating non‑uniform support for an overlay. The treatment is also required where joint faulting exceeds a quarter‑inch and where surface defects in traffic lanes larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch are present. Unstable or unsuitable material beneath the slab must be removed; the void is then backfilled to the bottom of the slab with Subbase No. 2A and topped with a flexible‑base replacement before any further work. The repair must be performed under dry conditions, with all traffic prohibited after fracturing until the base has been restored, and roller seating operations are not permitted when wet. Prior to placement of an interlayer or overlay, the pavement surface must be swept clean and its temperature kept below 120 °F to protect geotextile or asphalt materials. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–471)

Base Repair is intended as a preservation‑type fix, suitable only when the required patching remains limited in extent [1]. It becomes unsuitable once the amount of pavement that would need to be patched exceeds ten percent of the surface area, because such extensive patching is not cost‑effective or permitted for preservation projects [1]. In addition, during the warranty period the agency explicitly excludes routine maintenance activities such as crack sealing and base repairs from being performed on warranted pavement; therefore base repair is inappropriate in that timeframe [2]. When a five‑year performance survey shows that at least thirty percent of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses, the required remedial action shifts to microsurfacing or another department‑approved method rather than simple patching [2]. If the observed distress indicates that structural capacity must be increased—such as fatigue cracking or other signs of an unacceptable base condition—the treatment should move beyond preservation to rehabilitation, which may incorporate more extensive base repair together with overlays to augment structure [1][2]. When the functional life of the pavement has been exceeded, reconstruction becomes necessary as the appropriate alternative to base repair [1].

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Condition assessment and timing

The first step in deciding whether base repair is required is an annual automated pavement condition survey, commonly called videologging, which records distress types such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling, joint spalling and joint faulting; the resulting data are entered into treatment matrices that flag sections for minimal or full‑depth base repair. Complementary performance surveys conducted in accordance with Publication 336, either automated or manual, also identify and quantify distresses like fatigue cracking that may indicate an unacceptable base condition. Where the condition‑survey results suggest structural inadequacy, a Falling Weight Deflectometer test is performed to back‑calculate pavement and subgrade stiffness and confirm whether the identified distress warrants base replacement rather than surface‑only treatment. Rut depth is measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge across the lane width, and any longitudinal deviation greater than one‑half inch is recorded as excessive and used to prioritize further investigation. Traffic loading verification through classification counts ensures that accumulated equivalent single axle loads remain within the design limits for the existing structure. When distress indicators reach the thresholds above, six‑inch diameter cores are extracted from the affected zones and sent to a laboratory where void content, asphalt percentage and gradation are determined in accordance with PTM 715 and AASHTO T 209. Field density of each base layer is then verified against the maximum density specified in the mix design by applying the procedures of PTM No. 106 Method B and AASHTO T 191; a calibrated nuclear gauge may be used on control strips, and at least ten random in‑place density tests are required to establish an average value. The moisture content of the existing material is measured using ASTM D2216, D44643 or AASHTO T 265 and compared with the optimum moisture specified for the mix design. Surface regularity is checked by moving a ten‑ or twelve‑foot straightedge longitudinally and cross‑sectionally at intervals not exceeding twenty‑five feet; deviations greater than one‑half inch trigger immediate correction of the surface irregularity. Visual inspection also looks for marred, distorted or unstable slabs that rock or move under normal traffic loads, and any slab exhibiting such movement is flagged for stabilization before base repair proceeds. Joint faulting is measured, with faults exceeding a quarter‑inch prompting preparation of a geotextile interlayer and faults over a half‑inch requiring milling or the placement of a scratch course. Depth adequacy is verified by excavating test holes to the full compacted thickness at least once per three‑thousand square‑yard segment; a shortfall of one‑half inch or more initiates removal and replacement of the deficient section. Strength verification may involve coring specimens for indirect tensile or unconfined compressive testing, with acceptance criteria of three hundred to five hundred pounds per square inch after seven days as specified in ASTM D1633. For fractured pavements, a visual check of vertical deflection under the roller confirms that the substrate does not move; if movement is observed, base repair—including removal of unsuitable material, backfilling with Subbase No. 2A and patching with flexible‑base replacement—is required. Falling Weight Deflectometer testing is also employed on fractured layers to determine elastic modulus values, and any modulus outside the acceptable range dictates that base repair be performed. [1][2]

Materials and repair details

Base repair projects start with a thorough evaluation of the failure cause and selection of a repair type that uses material matching the existing base in type and thickness. The material is generally a standard Base Course or Superpave Base Course, and where additional support is required an AASHTO #1 aggregate may be incorporated. All repairs must be placed with plant‑mixed asphalt concrete unless another material receives written approval from the district authority. Repairs are executed as square or rectangular patches that meet the specification’s performance criteria, and when individual locations lie less than one hundred feet apart they are combined into a single continuous patch. Prior to placement the existing surface is swept clean, unsuitable or unstable material is removed, and the remaining pavement may be pulverized to the required depth and particle size. If calcium chloride is specified it is added at seven pounds per ton for a Type 1 mix or five point seven pounds per ton for a Type 2 mix. A fine‑material bedding layer is spread uniformly, two inches thick over subgrade or one inch thick over subbase or existing pavement, to serve as a bed and filler. The coarse aggregate base is then placed in layers whose compacted thickness does not exceed three to six inches; when the total depth exceeds six inches the placement is divided into two or more approximately equal layers, with minimum layer thickness increasing to four‑eight inches for depths over eight inches and five‑ten inches for depths over ten inches, especially when vibratory compaction is employed. Each layer is spread with adjustable self‑propelled mechanical spreaders operating in echelon or alternating lanes to retain moisture, and compacted with rollers—vibratory equipment where required—to at least ninety‑five percent and up to one hundred percent of the laboratory maximum density. After compaction the surface is broomed to a granular texture, high spots are bladed off, low spots are corrected by loosening, reshaping and recompacting, and any construction joints are scarified, moistened and blended with fresh mix so that no joint remains. The compacted base receives a single prime coat of asphalt material not exceeding zero point fifteen gallons per square yard, and a minimum cross‑slope of one quarter inch per foot is provided to ensure proper drainage. Surface irregularities must not exceed one and a half inches overall and must be less than one half inch when measured over a ten‑foot straightedge; any waviness or depth deficiency greater than half an inch is removed, reshaped, recompacted, or replaced before traffic is permitted. A leveling course of at least one inch thickness is incorporated, including cross‑slope correction and a continuous pavement base drain to prevent bathtub conditions. When reclaimed (FDR) material is used it is mixed with the required aggregates, recycled asphalt pavement, stabilizing additive and water according to the mix design, keeping moisture within three percent of optimum, placed and compacted within four hours of water or additive addition, and compacted to a minimum density of ninety‑five percent of laboratory maximum. A control strip of three hundred feet is used to establish roller patterns, leaving an unrolled margin of three to six inches near unsupported edges; after shaping the base is cured for seven days while being kept damp with water and an emulsified asphalt primer or fog seal applied at rates between zero point zero two and zero point one gallons per square yard. The cured material must achieve at least fifty psi indirect tensile strength for asphalt‑stabilized mixes or three hundred to five hundred psi unconfined compressive strength for chemically stabilized mixes before traffic is allowed, with proof rolling and verification of density, surface tolerance and strength required prior to light traffic. For fractured rigid pavements where seating is insufficient all unsuitable material is removed, the void is backfilled to the bottom of the cracked slab with Subbase No. 2A, and a patch of Flexible Base Replacement is placed on top of the slab to restore the base layer. Surface defects larger than four square inches or deeper than one inch are filled with an approved patching material—either asphalt‑based, cementitious, or comparable—and compacted to a flat, tight surface. When a geotextile interlayer is specified it is laid flat over the repaired surface, overlapped by eight inches plus or minus two on each side and extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, with no more than three layers in any location. If an asphalt interlayer is required a nominal one‑inch depth of Superpave mix is placed over the prepared surface. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (p. 60), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 209–229), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–471)

Base‑repair performance is fundamentally linked to the condition of the existing pavement, requiring that any area exhibiting obvious failure be removed before new material is placed [1][2]. The underlying subgrade and especially the subbase must be sound because an inadequate subbase can precipitate premature mid‑slab cracking and undermine load distribution [1][2]. Adequate drainage must be retained, so the replacement material should match the original base in type and thickness to avoid creating a “bathtub” condition that traps water within the pavement structure [1]. Aggregate‑related problems such as Vanport Limestone or Alkali Silica Reaction, which may not be evident from routine surveys, must be identified and mitigated because they can compromise the repaired section [1]. The repair material must provide sufficient structural capacity, meeting or exceeding the design thickness and density requirements specified for the original base layer [1][2]. Field cores taken from distressed zones are examined for void content, asphalt percentage, and aggregate gradation, and the reconstructed section is required to achieve a density greater than 92 % of the theoretical maximum with all mix‑design tolerances satisfied [2]. When reclaimed or fine‑graded material is used, compaction must reach at least 95 % of the laboratory‑determined maximum dry density at optimum moisture content, and the moisture level may not exceed the design optimum by more than three percent [2]. The cured repair should develop a seven‑day unconfined compressive strength between 300 and 500 psi as measured in accordance with ASTM D1633 to prevent shrinkage cracking [2]. Compatibility is ensured through strict control of moisture, temperature, and material uniformity throughout mixing, transport, spreading, and placement, requiring that the aggregate blend be proportioned correctly and kept uniformly moist [2]. Positive bonding between new and existing layers demands maintaining proper moisture at all stages, avoiding segregation, and prohibiting placement on frozen surfaces or when ambient temperature falls below 40 °F or freezing is forecast within the next 24 hours [2]. For permeable bases the surface temperature must be kept below 115 °F, and for geotextile or asphalt interlayers it may not exceed 120 °F before overlay placement, with water sprinkling used as needed to achieve these limits [2]. Plant‑mixed asphalt concrete is the default repair material unless a different product receives written approval, reinforcing the need for material uniformity across the repaired area [2]. Layer thickness is limited to a minimum of four inches and a maximum of eight inches per lift (or five to ten inches when alternative methods are employed), and any deviation greater than one‑half inch must be corrected before final compaction [2][1]. If the required depth exceeds eight inches, the repair is constructed in two or more layers of approximately equal depth, each complying with the minimum and maximum lift limits [2]. Prior to placement, the subbase or subgrade must be free of ruts, loose material, frost, standing water, or contamination with base‑course material; any such unsuitable material is removed, reshaped, and recompacted before reconstruction [1][2]. After placement, traffic is restricted to essential local movements until the surface course is installed, ensuring that the repaired base is not disturbed before it attains the specified strength [2].

Treatment procedures

Base repair begins with a thorough preparation of the existing pavement, which includes cracking, rubblizing, and seating any concrete slab to provide a stable substrate while simultaneously marking the sections slated for replacement, removing the asphalt surface course within the plan limits using an acceptable method, and trimming the remaining edges to a clean vertical line [1][2]. All loose debris is swept from the exposed base by power broom, vacuum truck or other approved means, and any unstable slabs or fractured material are stabilized through concrete patching, slabjacking, or similar approved techniques; if instability persists, all unsuitable material is removed [2]. The subbase or subgrade ahead of the spreader—at least 1,500 ft in advance—is finished to be free of ruts, irregularities, and loose material, providing continuous pavement base drainage [1][2]. A uniform fine‑material bed is then spread over the prepared subgrade or subbase, typically two inches thick on subgrade or one inch on subbase or existing pavement, to serve as a filler layer [2]. The coarse base mix, which may be a Base Course/Superpave Base Course or AASHTO #1 aggregate, is proportioned with aggregates, water and, when required, calcium chloride, blended without segregation, and delivered to the site for placement [1][2]. If the compacted depth of the base exceeds the allowable single‑layer thickness, it is placed in two or more layers of approximately equal depth, with each layer no thinner than three inches and no thicker than six inches; when rollers alone are used, the permissible range expands to four through eight inches per layer [2]. Each layer is kept moist, and the next layer is placed immediately after compacting the previous one to ensure proper bonding [2]. Compaction of each coarse layer must achieve at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum density and not less than 100 % of the field maximum dry density as defined in PTM No. 106 Method B, with a minimum of three inches per layer [2]. After a coarse layer is fully compacted, a fine‑material overlay is spread uniformly over its surface and sweep‑rolled to fill any voids; water may be sprinkled and rolled again to improve bonding [2]. The repaired base surface is then shaped so that irregularities do not exceed one and a half inches from the intended grade and cross‑slope, with tolerances verified using templates or straightedges and corrected as needed [1][2]. A leveling course of at least one inch, incorporating any necessary cross‑slope correction, is applied before any overlay or surface treatment [1]. The cleaned base is broomed and a prime coat of asphalt is applied at a rate not exceeding 0.15 gal per square yard [2]. If an interlayer is specified, a geotextile fabric is laid flat on the prepared surface with edges overlapped about eight inches (± 2 in), extending four inches beyond the pavement edge, kept damp but not saturated, protected from traffic, and maintained at a temperature below 120 °F; alternatively, a one‑inch Superpave mix may be placed as an asphalt interlayer under similar temperature controls [2]. After placement, the repaired area is kept damp, traffic is restricted, and curing continues until the seven‑day strength requirement is satisfied; additionally, all mixed material must be rolled within four hours of mixing to achieve the required density [2]. Throughout the operation continuous base drainage is maintained to ensure proper moisture conditions for the repaired section [1][2].

Base‑repair work must be executed with a full complement of materials, equipment, labor and an approved traffic‑control plan provided at no cost to the Department. The contractor first sweeps the pavement with a power broom or vacuum truck to remove loose debris, then pulverizes the existing surface to the specified depth before any stabilizing additives are applied. If a fractured slab is encountered, it is seated using a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller and vertical deflection is verified; unsuitable material is removed, the void is backfilled with Subbase No. 2A and the top is patched with Flexible Base Replacement. A processing or mixing plant proportioning aggregates, reclaimed asphalt pavement, RAP and water must produce a mix at the design moisture content, which may be delivered as a slurry in ready‑mix concrete trucks equipped with recirculating pumps to prevent settling. The fine‑material bed is placed only on dry, unfrozen subgrade; it cannot be laid on wet or frozen surfaces. Adjustable self‑propelled spreaders lay and screed the base material without segregation, spreading it in layers no thinner than three inches and no thicker than six inches (or four to eight inches, extending to five to ten inches when vibratory rollers are used). Each layer is tested for irregularities, compacted with standard or vibratory rollers as specified until at least 95 % of the laboratory‑determined maximum dry density is achieved within four hours of mixing, and then a subsequent layer is placed. Shoulders must be built up and compacted either before the base or simultaneously with each layer’s compaction. After placement, controlled water sprinkling maintains moisture not more than three percent above optimum, and curing proceeds until the seven‑day strength requirement is satisfied; heavy traffic is prohibited during this period while only light vehicles may use the lane after proof rolling. All operations are confined to daylight hours and cannot be performed when air temperature falls below 40 °F or when freezing temperatures are forecast within the next 24 hours, nor on frozen surfaces. The surface temperature of any geotextile used must stay below 120 °F, and construction traffic is avoided on the fabric until just before concrete placement. Traffic control must keep the roadway open; if closure exceeds the stipulated period, liquidated damages accrue for each calendar day the road is not unrestricted. During base placement only essential local vehicles and construction equipment are permitted on the fresh surface, with heavy traffic barred until the surface course is installed. [2]

Quality and workmanship

Base‑repair inspections begin by confirming that all material constituents meet the design specifications. Aggregate gradations are verified through daily sieve analysis and the proportions of cementitious binders, water and any calcium chloride additive are measured for accuracy and blended uniformly; stockpiled aggregates must remain free of segregation before mixing. When reclaimed pavement material is used, it is required to be pulverized to the specified size and its original moisture content adjusted to stay within three percent of the optimum value, and the overall mix design—including RAP, stabilizing additives and water—is checked for compliance prior to placement. Any unsuitable or unstable existing material must be removed, and the prepared subgrade or treated base is inspected for cleanliness, appropriate moisture, and an acceptable temperature before work proceeds.

During preparation the fine‑material bedding is spread uniformly over a suitable subgrade, forms and shims are set correctly to maintain grade and depth, and all required sawcuts, removal of overlying asphalt, loose joint seals and other preparatory steps are verified. For projects employing geotextile interlayers, inspectors confirm that the fabric is placed flat on the cleaned surface with edge overlaps of eight inches plus or minus two inches, no more than three layers in any location, proper free‑edge extension, suitable moisture condition, and a surface temperature not exceeding 120 °F.

Installation checks focus on layer placement and compaction. Each lift of base course is placed to a thickness of three to six inches, with immediate bonding between adjacent lanes observed, and the spreader operation monitored for uniformity. Compaction must achieve at least the full maximum density as defined by PTM No. 106 (or AASHTO T 191), which translates to not less than 100 % of the laboratory maximum for conventional mixes and a minimum of 95 % for reclaimed‑material mixes; nuclear gauge readings are taken after each pass, with a minimum of ten random density tests per lot. The pneumatic 50‑ton roller is required to make at least three passes for the Crack‑and‑Seat method or five passes for the Break‑and‑Seat method, and visual observation must show no vertical deflection of underlying layers, indicating proper seating. Joints are scarified and moistened, a prime coat is applied at a rate not exceeding 0.15 gal/yd², and any geotextile fabric temperature limits are respected.

After placement the finished base is examined for surface tolerance and texture. Surface irregularities greater than one‑half inch are corrected by removal and replacement of the deficient area, and overall surface levelness is measured with a straightedge in accordance with Section 413.3(l). Rut depth is assessed using a twelve‑foot straight edge across the lane width. Core samples—six‑inch diameter cores drilled from any area showing fatigue cracking or other distress—are tested for void content, asphalt percentage and gradation according to PTM 715, AASHTO T 209, PTM 702 and PTM 739, with in‑place density required to exceed 92 % of the theoretical maximum. Strength testing of cured material is performed after seven days using indirect tensile or unconfined compressive tests on Proctor specimens, and acceptance is based on meeting prescribed density, surface and strength thresholds. Joint integrity is verified by wipe tests and timely sealing, curing temperature is monitored, and Falling‑Weight Deflectometer measurements are taken to confirm that the elastic modulus of any fractured concrete falls within the specified range. All identified distress types must remain within the limits set for the warranty period. [2]

A satisfactory base repair begins with the complete removal of all visibly failed portions of the existing base layer and their replacement by a material that restores the required structural capacity while preserving proper drainage [1]. The new material must be of the same type and thickness as the surrounding base to maintain continuity of load‑distribution characteristics and to avoid creating bathtub conditions that could impede water removal, a requirement confirmed during field inspection [1]. Dimensional acceptance is governed by a surface tolerance that limits irregularities measured with a 10‑foot straightedge to no more than one‑half inch; any deviation greater than this must be corrected by loosening, removing, or adding material until the finished surface meets the half‑inch limit [2]. Depth checks performed in test holes across the repaired section must also fall within a half‑inch of the design elevation, and areas deficient by that amount are required to be removed and replaced [2]. When individual repair patches are spaced less than 100 feet apart they are merged into one continuous repair to satisfy continuity criteria [2]. Each layer of newly placed base material must achieve at least one hundred percent of the maximum dry density determined according to PTM No. 106 Method B, while reclaimed or fully‑dense recycled (FDR) material is required to reach a minimum of ninety‑five percent of its laboratory‑maximum dry density at optimum moisture content [2]. A compaction control strip of not less than three hundred feet is established and ten random nuclear‑gauge tests are performed; the average in‑place density obtained from these tests must meet the same minimum percentages specified for the individual layers, and any test showing a deficiency mandates additional compaction or full replacement [2]. Strength acceptance depends on the stabilizing agent: asphalt‑stabilized mixes must demonstrate an indirect tensile strength of at least fifty pounds per square inch when tested in accordance with Bulletin 27, whereas chemically stabilized mixes must develop an unconfined compressive strength between three hundred and five hundred psi (two hundred eighty to five hundred psi for thicker overlays) after seven days, and the repaired base may not be opened to unrestricted traffic until those strengths are attained, with only light traffic permitted during curing [2]. Structural seating is verified visually by confirming that no vertical deflection occurs under a minimum fifty‑ton pneumatic roller, and falling‑weight deflectometer testing of the repaired concrete layer must produce an average elastic modulus within the range prescribed in Table A; failure to meet either condition requires removal of unstable material and replacement with the appropriate subbase and flexible base replacement before further work proceeds [2]. All surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch are required to be filled with an approved patching material and compacted to a flat, tight surface [2]. Where geotextile interlayers are employed their edges must overlap by eight inches plus or minus two inches, be limited to three layers, and extend at least four inches beyond the pavement edge [2]. Joint faulting exceeding one‑quarter inch is milled, and for asphalt interlayers any fault greater than one‑half inch necessitates a scratch course or milling to reduce the faulting [2]. The temperature of fabric or pavement surfaces must not exceed one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit before overlay placement [2]. Long‑term performance acceptance requires that a year‑five survey show no more than thirty percent of the repaired segment containing two or more low‑severity distresses; if this threshold is exceeded, additional remedial treatment such as microsurfacing is required [2]. Finally, all repair work must be completed within the prescribed time frames and using materials that meet the specification’s performance criteria, ensuring that the repaired base continues to function as an integral component of the pavement structure [1][2].

Performance and service life

Base repair involves removing sections of the base that have failed and replacing them with engineered material designed to restore structural capacity and provide effective drainage [1][2]. The reclaimed or new material is compacted to at least 95 % of the laboratory‑maximum density, and when stabilizing additives are used it must achieve an unconfined compressive strength of 300–500 psi within seven days [2]. By re‑establishing a sound, well‑drained foundation that can uniformly distribute traffic loads, the treatment directly improves the pavement’s physical condition and reduces water‑related distress such as pumping or frost heave [1][2]. Restoring uniform load‑distribution capacity also eliminates slab movement, allowing the surface to remain flat and preventing premature cracking or rutting, which enhances functional performance [2]. When applied as a preservation measure, base repair addresses minor distress in the base layer, retaining the existing functional condition of the roadway and slowing further deterioration [1]. In a rehabilitation context where the repair is more extensive, the added structural capacity strengthens the pavement structure, increasing its durability [1][2]. The requirement to meet density and strength criteria before reopening to unlimited traffic ensures that the repaired section can sustain design loads without early failure, further contributing to long‑term durability [2]. Because the restored base provides effective drainage and a stable support for subsequent surface layers, the expected service life of the pavement segment is extended relative to an unrepaired condition [1][2].

Base repair is typically required when underlying distresses such as premature mid‑slab cracking appear, often stemming from inadequate subbase material or aggregate‑related problems like Vanport Limestone concerns or alkali‑silica reaction, which can escape routine condition surveys and demand targeted inspection. [1] The most frequently observed post‑repair failures reflect an insufficiently prepared base and include flushing, potholes, rutting, fatigue cracking, surface irregularities larger than one‑half inch, and depth deficiencies of the same magnitude. [2] These symptoms usually indicate that the required density or thickness was not achieved, with core testing commonly revealing densities below 92 % of theoretical maximum for conventional mixes or below 95 % for reclaimed, chemically stabilized material. [2] Moisture conditions that exceed the optimum by more than three percent, placement of fine material on wet, frozen, or otherwise unsuitable subgrade or subbase, and mixing of subgrade or subbase materials with the base course all promote segregation, poor bonding, and low‑strength zones. [2] Chemically stabilized mixes that develop compressive strengths above about 900 psi are prone to shrinkage cracking, while premature traffic loading before the seven‑day cure period—or after cumulative ESALs surpass the projected twenty‑year design—accelerates the development of flushing, potholes and fatigue cracks. [2] Joint faulting greater than a quarter‑inch, surface defects exceeding four square inches in area and one inch in depth, and unstable slabs or slab fragments that rock under normal traffic further signal inadequate base preparation. [2] Additional factors such as excessive traffic loads, uncontrolled forces from accidents, adverse environmental conditions, and destructive maintenance actions like coring or milling can exacerbate these distresses. [2] When thresholds are exceeded—such as more than 30 % of a segment exhibiting two or more low‑severity distresses after five years, repair patches spaced less than one hundred feet apart, surface irregularities or depth deficits of at least one‑half inch, or joint faulting beyond the prescribed limits—the specifications call for remedial actions ranging from additional compaction and moisture adjustment to full removal and reconstruction of the affected area. [2] In summary, recurring base‑repair failures are driven by material quality deficiencies, improper moisture control, insufficient compaction, premature loading, and traffic or environmental stresses that together prevent the base layer from providing adequate load distribution and structural support. Monitoring and follow-up

After a Base Repair is placed, the Department initiates its warranty performance‑survey program, conducting an automated survey each year in accordance with Publication 336 and supplementing it with manual surveys whenever the automated results approach the limits set out in Table A; additional surveys may be ordered if any criteria appear unmet. Survey findings are communicated to the Contractor within fourteen calendar days, and the Contractor may witness the manual inspections while also being allowed to perform nondestructive testing of the repaired pavement after giving at least seven‑day notice to the District Executive. Concurrently, each layer of the base course must be compacted to not less than 100 % of its maximum density, with nuclear gauge readings taken after every pass of the compaction equipment and a minimum of ten random tests on the control strip to establish the average in‑place density. Surface evenness is verified by running templates or a straightedge along the base at intervals no greater than twenty‑five feet; any irregularity exceeding one‑half inch must be corrected by loosening, adding or removing material and re‑compacting. Depth is confirmed by cutting or digging a test hole to the full compacted depth for each three thousand square yards (or as directed) and measuring against the design thickness, with deficiencies of half an inch or more requiring replacement. The repaired layer is kept damp daily and cured until the required seven‑day strength is achieved; heavy traffic is prohibited during this cure period and a proof roll with a vehicle comparable to the heaviest expected traffic—or a strength measurement of the finished dense‑rated (FDR) material—must be performed before opening to traffic. Visual inspection for vertical deflection under the fifty‑ton pneumatic roller ensures that no movement occurs; if movement is observed, additional repair is required. After final seating, the surface is swept clean and a falling‑weight deflectometer test is conducted to verify that the elastic modulus of the fractured concrete layer remains within the approved range before any asphalt overlay proceeds. Throughout this period the base course is protected from traffic and freezing conditions until the next construction step. [2]

Annual condition surveys that continue to record distress such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling or weathering, joint spalling and faulting beyond the thresholds defined in treatment matrices indicate that the base repair is insufficient and trigger additional work ranging from further base repair to full rehabilitation. [1] When the amount of patching required after a base repair exceeds roughly ten percent of the pavement area, this level of remedial effort is generally considered uneconomical for preservation projects and signals the need for more extensive maintenance or structural rehabilitation. [1] The appearance of premature mid‑slab cracking in jointed concrete pavements, often linked to inadequate subbase material, also serves as an early warning that the repaired base is not performing adequately. [1] Surface irregularities that exceed one‑half inch are deemed unsatisfactory and must be corrected by reshaping and recompacting the affected portion before any surface course can be placed. [2] Similarly, if the compacted depth of the base is deficient by at least one‑half inch, the zone should be loosened, remoistened and re‑compacted, or removed and replaced entirely. [2] Visible marbling, distortion or other damage to the pavement surface likewise indicates that the repair is no longer adequate and requires repair or replacement of the damaged area. [2] Density measurements on the compaction control strip that fall below the specified minimum trigger additional compaction when the base appears uniform, but any lot failing to meet acceptance criteria must be identified for rework. [2] Chemically stabilized bases that develop strengths greater than nine hundred pounds per square inch are prone to shrinkage cracking and therefore require remedial action. [2] Unstable slabs or slab fragments that move or rock under normal traffic loads, surface depressions larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch, and joint faulting exceeding a quarter‑inch (or half‑inch when asphalt interlayers are present) are additional performance trends that demand repair, replacement, or rehabilitation of the base layer.
