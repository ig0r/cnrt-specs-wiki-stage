Base Repair

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Base Repair is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
base-repair
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-29

Base Repair is a pavement‑preservation treatment applied when an existing base course no longer provides a sound, uniformly compacted and adequately thick foundation for load transfer, as indicated by visible distress such as cracking, water‑filled “bathtub” conditions, surface irregularities, or density shortfalls that exceed specified limits [1][2]. The procedure involves removing the failed portion, reshaping and recompacting the subgrade or subbase if needed, and reconstructing the base with a material that matches the original type and thickness while meeting moisture‑content and density requirements—typically 95 % to 100 % of the laboratory maximum dry density [1][2]. By restoring proper drainage, uniform support, and structural capacity, Base Repair re‑establishes load‑distribution capability and extends the pavement’s service life before any overlay is placed [1][2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Base Repair is required whenever the existing base course no longer furnishes a sound, uniformly compacted and adequately thick foundation for load transfer, indicating that the layer has failed or become compromised beyond acceptable limits. [1][2] The failure may be evident in the field as obvious deterioration, water‑filled “bathtub” conditions, cracking and seating of underlying concrete, rubblization of a slab, or other visible distress that prevents proper load distribution and drainage. [1] Observable deficiencies that trigger the treatment include surface irregularities exceeding one‑half inch, depth shortfalls of at least one‑half inch, densities falling below the specified minimum—typically 95 % to 100 % of the laboratory maximum dry density—and mixing of subgrade or subbase material into the base course. [2] Additional signs are visible marbling or distortion of the pavement surface, instability revealed by proof‑rolling or vertical deflection under rolling loads, and damage caused by local traffic, weather exposure, prolonged use as a haul road, or other environmental factors. [2][1] When any of these conditions are present, the compromised portion must be removed and replaced with a base course that matches the original type and thickness—such as Base Course/Superpave Base Course, AASHTO #1 aggregate, Subbase No. 2A, or Flexible Base Replacement—to restore uniform load distribution, structural performance, and proper drainage before any overlay is placed. [1][2] If the existing material cannot achieve the required density through additional compaction, the area should be thoroughly loosened, remoistened, reshaped, and recompacted to meet the minimum density criteria. [2] Likewise, when depth deficiencies of one‑half inch or more are identified, the deficient section must be removed and rebuilt to the proper thickness using suitable material. [2] Any mixing of subgrade or subbase into the base course requires removal of the mixture, reshaping and recompaction of the subgrade/subbase, and reconstruction of the unsatisfactory base area with appropriate material. [2] The overall objective of Base Repair is to re‑establish a structurally sound, uniformly compacted, adequately thick, and well‑drained base layer that can effectively transfer loads and support subsequent pavement layers. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–60), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 204–227), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 340), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–415)

Base Repair is undertaken after an evaluation identifies the causes of base failure and involves removing the failed sections and replacing them with material that matches the original type and thickness, thereby re‑establishing adequate structural capacity and proper drainage. [1] The treatment repairs the damaged base and restores the pavement’s functional performance, ensuring that the load‑distribution capability of the structure is regained. [2][1] Because it is classified as a pavement‑preservation activity, the objective includes cost‑effective measures to retard further deterioration and slow the progression of distress while maintaining or improving roadway condition. [1] The specifications require cleaning the surface, eliminating unstable sub‑base material, and repairing or replacing compromised areas; when necessary full‑depth reclamation with stabilizing additives is performed, compacted to required density and cured until the 7‑day strength criterion is satisfied. [2] Throughout the operation the base must be kept at proper moisture content, protected from traffic, and limited to essential local equipment until it can support the surface course, thereby preserving the repaired condition. [2] Patching of surface defects may employ asphalt‑based or cementitious repair materials, further contributing to a uniform, stable support condition before placement of an overlay. [2] By restoring a uniform, stable support condition and addressing existing damage, Base Repair not only restores functionality but also contributes to extending the service life of the pavement structure. [2][1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–60), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 204–227), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 340), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462)

Distress characterization and causes

The distress addressed by base‑repair occurs in existing concrete pavements where slabs or slab fragments move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads, typically within the traffic lanes; it also includes surface defects larger than about four square inches and deeper than one inch. Such instability creates non‑uniform support for any overlay, a severe condition that can lead to premature failure of the new pavement and unsafe riding conditions, so the specification requires these areas be stabilized or patched before the interlayer is placed. The document does not quantify a progression rate, but untreated movement will persist under traffic, compromising safety and operational performance. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462)

The primary mechanisms that generate the distress necessitating base repair are water infiltration and inadequate drainage, which erode the load‑distribution capacity of the base layer. [1] When surface or subsurface water is not effectively removed it accumulates within the pavement structure, reducing structural support and creating visible failure zones that must be excavated and replaced. [1] Weak subbase material, often manifested as premature mid‑slab cracking in jointed concrete pavements, signals insufficient underlying support and a lack of adequate structural capacity. [1][2] Aggregate‑related degradations such as reactions associated with Vanport Limestone or alkali‑silica reaction further compromise base integrity, accelerating deterioration before routine surveys detect it. [1] Construction deficiencies also play a central role; if reclaimed or newly placed base material is not compacted to at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum density its structural capacity is reduced. [2] Moisture imbalance during placement and failure to achieve the specified indirect tensile strength of about 50 psi (as defined in Bulletin 27) further weaken the layer. [2] Allowing traffic, including construction traffic, before the seven‑day cure period or imposing premature heavy loads on a material that has not attained the required strength promotes shrinkage cracking and other damage. [2] Excessive stabilizer content can make chemically stabilized fine‑dense reclaimed (FDR) mixes overly stiff, detrimentally affecting ultimate strength. [2] Environmental exposure compounds these problems; leaving a subbase uncovered for more than sixty days during winter shutdowns or exposing it to weather without protection leads to surface irregularities, loss of depth and overall instability. [2] Use of unsuitable aggregates such as No. 2A haul‑road material or No. OGS base accelerates degradation, especially when traffic is permitted on these layers. [2] Proof‑rolling that reveals instability requires removal of the failing subbase and repair or replacement of the underlying subgrade before a new base can be placed. [2][1] Consequently, each base‑repair project begins with a site‑specific evaluation to identify these causative factors, selection of repair material that matches the original type and thickness, and restoration of proper drainage to avoid “bathtub” conditions. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–60), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 222–227)

Applicability and treatment selection

Base Repair is called for when the underlying base layer fails to provide a stable, uniformly supporting substrate under the anticipated loading conditions. For flexible pavements, if a No. 2A aggregate subbase has been employed as a haul road or has remained exposed to weather for more than sixty calendar days, the surface must be retested for irregularities and depth; any deficiencies identified must be corrected before the final pavement is placed. Proof‑rolling of both flexible and rigid pavements is required at this stage, and if proof‑rolling reveals instability—whether localized or pervasive—the unstable subbase must be removed, the underlying subgrade repaired or replaced, and a new base installed prior to resuming paving. Construction traffic is prohibited on a No. OGS aggregate subbase to avoid premature damage that would trigger these repairs. For rigid concrete pavements that have been fractured and seated, the repair is required when the final seating does not achieve a non‑moving substrate, which is judged by the absence of visible vertical deflection under the roller. If such movement is observed, base repair must be performed after traffic has been restricted following the fracturing operation; the work may not be undertaken under wet conditions and sonic vibratory equipment must be avoided where it could cause subgrade liquefaction. The same restriction applies in areas where fracturing is prohibited because of proximity to utilities, structures, or other fixed objects. In addition, base repair is appropriate wherever existing rigid pavement exhibits slabs or slab fragments that visibly rock or move under normal traffic loads, creating non‑uniform support for an overlay. Surface defects larger than four square inches in area and deeper than one inch within the traffic lanes must be filled with an approved patching material—such as asphalt, cementitious, or a comparable repair mix—and compacted to a flat, tight surface before any interlayer is placed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 227), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–462)

Base Repair is appropriate only as a preservation‑type fix; it becomes unsuitable when the amount of required patching exceeds the preservation limit—specifically when more than ten percent of the pavement surface would need to be patched—or when the distress indicates that structural capacity must be increased. In those cases the treatment should shift to rehabilitation, which uses more extensive base repair together with overlays to boost structure, or ultimately to reconstruction if the functional life of the pavement has been exceeded. [1]

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Condition assessment and timing

The determination of a needed base‑repair begins with the annual automated pavement condition survey, commonly called videologging, which records distress types such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration and joint faulting and feeds those observations into the systematic treatment matrices that flag sections for minimal or full base repair [1]. Complementary to the survey, a thorough visual inspection of the pavement surface and underlying subbase is performed to identify any instability, including slabs that rock or move under normal traffic loads or vertical deflection observed while the roller seats the surface; such movement signals an unstable base that must be addressed before further work [2]. The moisture content of the existing material is then measured in accordance with ASTM D2216 (or equivalent) and compared to the mix‑design optimum to verify suitability for repair [2]. In‑place density is verified by taking nuclear gauge readings after each compaction pass, and a minimum of ten random field‑density tests are conducted on a control strip, with every layer required to achieve not less than 100 % of its maximum dry density as defined by AASHTO T191 or PTM 106 Method B [2]. Surface irregularities are checked using a 12‑foot straightedge at intervals no greater than 25 feet, and any longitudinal or transverse deviation exceeding one‑half inch is flagged for correction [2]. Defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be filled before overlay placement, and joints offset by more than one‑quarter inch (or more than one‑half inch when an asphalt interlayer is present) are identified for repair or additional treatment such as a scratch course [2]. Depth verification is performed by cutting a test hole to the full compacted depth for each 3,000 square‑yard area and measuring the thickness; a deficiency of half an inch or more triggers removal and replacement of that portion of the base [2]. Prior to placing the final pavement layer a proof roll is conducted, and any instability observed during this roll requires removal of the subbase in the affected zones [2]. Where structural capacity remains uncertain after visual assessment, a Falling Weight Deflectometer test is administered on fractured or seated pavement, and the back‑calculated elastic modulus values are compared to prescribed limits; values outside those limits call for corrective actions such as removal of unsuitable material and replacement with an appropriate base layer [1][2]. Strength testing is carried out by coring the base and preparing at least three standard Proctor specimens per 3,000 square‑yard lot, which are then tested for unconfined compressive or indirect tensile strength according to ASTM D1633; any lot that fails the specified strength thresholds is flagged for rework [2]. The combined results of the automated condition survey, visual inspections, dimensional measurements, moisture and density checks, surface‑irregularity assessments, depth verification, proof rolling, FWD testing, joint evaluation, and laboratory strength tests provide a comprehensive basis for locating and specifying the required base‑repair treatments [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 204–227), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–462)

Base Repair is applied when the base layer shows clear failure that has reduced its structural capacity or impaired drainage, requiring removal of the deteriorated material and replacement with suitable fill [1]. The guides also state that the treatment is warranted at an earlier stage where distress is visible but not yet advanced, allowing a minimal repair as part of routine preservation to halt further damage [1]. Additionally, Base Repair must be performed when a fractured pavement has not achieved a stable, non‑moving condition after final seating, which is identified by observable vertical deflection under the roller [2]. The presence of any slab or slab fragment that moves or rocks under normal traffic loads also triggers the need for repair because such instability would produce non‑uniform support for an overlay [2]. Surface defects larger than four square inches in area and deeper than one inch must be filled with approved patching material and compacted to a flat, tight surface before any interlayer is placed, constituting another condition for Base Repair [2]. Across the guides, selecting Base Repair before extensive deterioration or instability sets in is emphasized as a preservation strategy that avoids more costly rehabilitation or full reconstruction [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–462)

Materials and repair details

Base repair projects commence with a thorough evaluation of the failure cause, after which the field inspection team selects a repair type and specifies methods that employ a material matching the existing base in both type and thickness. The material is typically a standard Base Course or Superpave Base Course, and an AASHTO #1 aggregate may be incorporated when additional support is required. Prior to reconstruction any unsuitable or unstable material, including mixed subgrade or subbase that has become incorporated in the base course, must be removed, and the underlying subgrade and/or subbase reshaped and re‑compacted. The new base is then reconstructed using a well‑graded coarse aggregate mix combined with measured water; when specified calcium chloride may be added at 7 lb per ton for Type 1 or 5.7 lb per ton for Type 2, and any reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) or stabilizing additives required by the mix design are incorporated at this stage. The base is placed in compacted layers whose individual thicknesses fall within prescribed limits: if the total compacted depth exceeds six inches, no layer shall be thinner than three inches nor thicker than six inches, and where depths exceed eight inches each layer must be between four and eight inches, with layers of approximately equal depth when multiple layers are required. Each layer is spread, shaped with templates, string lines, GPS or laser‑controlled equipment, scarified and moistened at joints, then compacted to at least 100 % of the maximum density determined by PTM No. 106 Method B (or a minimum of 95 % of laboratory maximum density when reclaimed material is used). High spots are removed by blading and low spots are corrected by loosening the surface, adding correctly graded fill material and recompacting. After compacting a layer, a fine‑material filler bed is spread to fill voids before the next coarse layer: two inches over subgrade or one inch over subbase or existing pavement. A 300‑foot control strip is constructed during initial reclamation, leaving a three‑to‑six‑inch unrolled margin near any unsupported edge, and the surface is broomed to produce a granular texture free of loose material. The repaired area must include a leveling course of at least one inch thickness, incorporate cross‑slope correction, and feature a continuous pavement base drain to ensure proper drainage and prevent bathtub conditions. A prime coat of asphalt is applied over the prepared base at a rate not exceeding 0.15 gallon per square yard before any overlay or shoulder work is installed. Surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch are patched with an approved asphalt‑based, cementitious, or other representative repair mix and compacted to a flat, tight surface. When a geotextile interlayer is used it must be laid flat, overlapped by eight inches (± 2 in) on all edges, limited to three layers in any spot, and extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge. After placement the base is cured for seven days under damp conditions; traffic is restricted to essential construction vehicles until strength criteria are met (50 psi indirect tensile for asphalt‑stabilized or 300–500 psi unconfined compressive for chemically stabilized). Surface tolerances of ≤½‑inch irregularity over a ten‑foot template and a cross‑slope of at least ¼‑inch per foot are verified, proof rolling is performed, and any marred, distorted, or otherwise damaged pavement encountered during repair is either patched with the appropriate material or replaced before the final flexible (asphalt) or rigid (concrete) pavement is placed. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (p. 60), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 204–227), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–462)

Base repair performance is fundamentally linked to the condition of the existing pavement; any area exhibiting obvious failure must be removed before new material is placed [1]. The underlying subgrade and especially the subbase must be sound because an inadequate subbase can cause premature mid‑slab cracking and other distress [1][2]. The repair mix must possess appropriate intrinsic qualities—well‑graded aggregate providing a structural skeleton of coarse particles together with a uniform fine filler, an optimum moisture content that does not exceed the design value by more than three percent, and sufficient density achieved through compaction to at least ninety‑five percent of the laboratory maximum dry density [2]. When calcium chloride is required, it must be blended uniformly into the mix at a rate of seven pounds per ton for Type 1 mixes or 5.7 lb/ton for Type 2 mixes before water is added [2]. Frost‑containing or frozen material may not be incorporated, and placement on a frozen surface, when ambient temperature is below 40 °F, or when freezing is forecast within the next twenty‑four hours is prohibited [2]. Each layer of base must be placed while still moist, compacted promptly after the preceding layer, and ultimately develop an unconfined compressive strength between three hundred and five hundred psi within seven days [2]. Compatibility between new and existing materials requires that the replacement material match the original base in type and thickness so that drainage characteristics are retained and a “bathtub” condition is avoided [1]. Proper drainage must be provided by the repair material, and any aggregate‑related problems such as Vanport Limestone or Alkali Silica Reaction, which may not be evident from routine surveys, should be identified and mitigated before placement [1]. The subbase or subgrade should be prepared at least one‑thousand five hundred feet ahead of the spreader, cleared of ruts, loose material, and other irregularities; any segregated or unsuitable material must be removed and replaced with well‑graded aggregate [2]. The fine initial course may not be placed on a wet, frozen, or otherwise unsuitable subbase, and if subgrade or subbase material becomes mixed with the base course it must be excavated, reshaped, re‑compacted, and the base rebuilt [2]. Existing pavement slabs must be sound; unstable slabs or fragments that move under traffic must be stabilized before any interlayer is installed, and surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be filled with an approved asphalt, cementitious, or similar repair material and fully compacted to a flat, tight surface [2]. When a geotextile interlayer is used it must be clean, overlapped correctly, limited to three layers, kept damp but not saturated, and its temperature must remain below one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit until the overlay is placed; an asphalt interlayer requires a one‑inch Superpave mix applied at a surface temperature not exceeding one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit and compacted appropriately [2]. The type of subbase aggregate influences performance: No. OGS aggregates may not be subjected to traffic, while No. 2A aggregates used as haul roads or left exposed for more than sixty days must be retested for depth and surface irregularities and corrected if necessary; uncovered subbases during winter shutdowns require a protective asphalt (for flexible pavements) or cement concrete (for rigid pavements) layer, otherwise they are proof‑rolled to detect instability and any unstable zones are removed before reinstalling a new base [2]. Final repair steps include backfilling beneath fractured slabs with Subbase No. 2A, patching the top surface with a flexible‑base replacement material, and confirming satisfactory seating by observing that vertical deflection under a roller indicates a non‑moving substrate [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–60), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 204–227), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–462)

Treatment procedures

Preparation begins with a careful layout of the repair area, marking the required section and cutting the existing pavement back to neat, straight lines as defined in the applicable specifications. [2] Where the existing concrete slab is cracked or deteriorated, rubblizing and seating of the slab are performed to create a stable substrate before any base material is placed. [1] All unsuitable, unstable, or loose material within the marked zone is removed; when reclaimed pavement is used it is first pulverized to the specified size and depth, with calcium chloride optionally added at this stage. [2] The underlying subbase or subgrade is then protected—traffic on No. OGS aggregate subbases is prohibited, any No. 2A subbase exposed for more than sixty days is retested and corrected, and a proof roll is conducted to identify instability; unstable portions are removed and the subgrade repaired or replaced as needed. [2] Continuous base drainage must be maintained throughout the repair, and the subbase is built up ahead of the spreader at least 1,500 ft, cleared of ruts, irregularities, and loose material. [2][1] A proportioned mix of aggregates—such as a Base Course/Superpave Base Course, AASHTO #1 aggregate, or a blend of reclaimed material with stabilizing additives—is blended with water at the mix‑design moisture level in a plant, optionally incorporating calcium chloride, and delivered in clean covered trucks to prevent moisture loss. [1][2] Prior to placement an initial fine‑material bed is spread uniformly over the prepared surface—two inches thick on subgrade or one inch on existing subbase or pavement—to serve as a filler layer. [2] The coarse aggregate is then placed across the full width in lifts whose compacted thickness does not exceed six inches; when greater depth is required the material is laid in two or more lifts of approximately equal depth, each lift being no less than three inches and no more than six inches thick. [2] Each lift is spread using templates and string lines, kept moist, and compacted to at least 100 % of the maximum density specified by PTM No. 106 (Method B) or a minimum of 95 % of laboratory‑determined maximum density at optimum moisture content. [2] After each coarse lift is fully compacted, a fine material layer is spread uniformly to fill any voids, the surface is swept, water is sprinkled, and the layer is rolled again to bond and set the course; this sequence is repeated for subsequent lifts. [2] Once the base material has been placed, a leveling course of at least one inch with cross‑slope correction is applied before any overlay or surface treatment, ensuring that deviations do not exceed one‑half inch during construction and no more than 1½ inches from the design grade in the final profile. [1][2] Surface irregularities greater than one‑half inch are bladed, reshaped, or reconstructed as they appear, and the repaired surface is swept to remove loose debris before applying any interlayer material. [2] The repaired base is kept damp and cured for seven days, maintaining moisture until the specified 7‑day strength is achieved; curing materials may be applied after finishing and within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation. [2][1] Prior to opening to traffic a prime coat of asphalt is applied at a rate not exceeding 0.15 gallon per square yard, and geotextile fabric is placed flat on the cleaned surface with edges overlapped by eight inches plus or minus two inches where required. [2][1] When fractured concrete slabs are encountered, the void beneath the slab is backfilled with Subbase No. 2A and patched with Flexible Base Replacement up to the top of the slab before any further work.

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 204–227), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–462)

Base repair must be carried out with a fully equipped processing and mixing plant that includes automatic cutoff devices, separate aggregate bins, and adjustable self‑propelled spreaders capable of placing and screeding material without segregation. The spreading operation is guided by templates cut to the required cross‑section or by GPS/laser‑controlled devices, while string lines are used to maintain elevation. Prior to placement a pulverizer may be employed to break up the existing pavement, after which reclaimed material, aggregates, RAP and any stabilizing additives are blended in a ready‑mix concrete truck fitted with a recirculating pump or agitation system to keep slurries from settling. The sequence begins with preparing the subbase or subgrade at least 1,500 ft ahead of the leading spreader, laying an initial fine layer on a dry, stable surface, followed by uniform placement of coarse material, compaction with standard, vibratory or minimum‑50‑ton pneumatic‑tired rollers, and then additional fines. If the compacted depth exceeds eight inches, the work is divided into two or more layers each between four and eight inches thick, never exceeding six inches per lift for the base mix, and moisture must be retained until the next layer is placed to ensure prompt bonding to adjacent lanes. Each layer is compacted to 100 % of its maximum density, or at least 95 % of the laboratory‑determined optimum as required by PTM No. 106, using compaction control strips and verified with nuclear density gauges. After placement the cured surface must be kept damp and protected from adverse weather; curing time varies with rain, sunshine, humidity and temperature, and heavy traffic is prohibited for a seven‑day period while only light vehicles may traverse after a proof roll confirms strength. All operations are confined to daylight hours and cannot be performed on frozen surfaces, when air temperature falls below 40 °F, or when freezing temperatures are forecast within the next 24 hours; roller seating must not occur under wet conditions, and FWD testing is barred when pavement is frozen or when temperature is below 45 °F or above 90 °F. Where a geotextile interlayer is required, it is laid after sweeping debris with a power broom or vacuum truck, overlapped correctly, kept damp but not saturated, and protected from construction traffic whenever possible; the fabric surface temperature must stay below 120 °F, and insulating blankets are provided when ambient temperature is 40 °F or lower, with removal of insulation limited to a concrete‑surface temperature change of less than 40 °F per hour. Traffic on the repaired base is restricted to essential construction equipment and necessary local traffic until a surface course is installed; no traffic, including construction traffic, may use a subbase constructed of No. OGS aggregate, and any No. 2A aggregate subbase used as a haul road or exposed for more than sixty days must be retested and corrected. Subbases left uncovered during winter shutdowns must receive an asphalt base (for flexible pavements) or cement concrete (for rigid pavements) before the season ends, otherwise a proof roll is required prior to pavement placement. The repaired lane remains closed to traffic until the overlay is placed, with only limited crossing allowed for construction access. [2]

Quality and workmanship

During a base‑repair project the inspector must first confirm that the processing and mixing plant has been accepted and is operating correctly before any work begins, and that all aggregates, water and any calcium chloride are measured and blended in the proper ratios with no frost or frozen material entering the mix. The coarse and fine base materials, including reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) or other recycled material, must be free of segregation, properly graded, and, where required, pulverized and mixed with the specified stabilizing additives at the mix‑design moisture level; the original moisture content of any reclaimed material is verified before blending. The subgrade or prepared subbase must be dry, stable, and free of ruts or loose material, and the aggregate type used for the subbase must be appropriate – traffic is prohibited on a No. OGS subbase, while a No. 2A subbase that serves as a haul road or remains exposed for more than sixty days must be retested for surface irregularities and depth. Construction joints are scarified and moistened before blending, and a prime coat not exceeding 0.15 gallon per square yard is applied.

Each lift of base course is placed in three‑ to six‑inch layers on the prepared subgrade, spread uniformly to the required thickness, and kept at the required moisture content, ensuring that moisture does not exceed optimum by more than three percent. The inspector checks cross‑sectional shape with templates or GPS/laser equipment at intervals no greater than twenty‑five feet and verifies longitudinal flatness with a straightedge every few feet. Compaction is verified for each layer: new base material must reach at least one hundred percent of the maximum dry density as determined by PTM No. 106, Method B and AASHTO T 191, while reclaimed or stabilized mixes must achieve a minimum of ninety‑five percent of the laboratory maximum dry density using a nuclear‑density control strip with at least ten random tests per PTM No. 1. After compaction, surface tolerances are inspected; any irregularity exceeding one‑half inch is corrected by loosening, adding or removing material, and depth is confirmed by digging a test hole for each three thousand square yards of work. A proof roll is performed before placement of the final pavement layer, and any zones indicating instability require removal of the subbase, repair of the underlying subgrade, and replacement of the subbase.

Completed work is examined for grade and smoothness tolerances, and cured strength is tested – requiring at least fifty pounds per square inch indirect tensile strength for asphalt‑stabilized mixes or the prescribed unconfined compressive strength for chemically stabilized mixes. The fractured slab, when present, must be properly seated; visual inspection for vertical deflection confirms non‑movement before proceeding. All surface defects larger than four square inches and one inch deep are filled with an approved patching material and compacted to a flat, tight surface. Loose debris is swept from the pavement, and any geotextile interlayer is placed clean and flat with edges overlapped eight plus or minus two inches, limited to three layers, and its temperature kept at or below one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit before overlay placement. Finally, the repaired base is protected from traffic until the surface course is installed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 204–227), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–462)

A satisfactory base repair is achieved when every visibly failed portion of the existing base layer has been removed and replaced with a material that matches the original in type and thickness, thereby restoring the required structural capacity while preserving proper drainage and avoiding bathtub conditions that could impede water removal [1][2]. The replacement material must be placed to meet the density targets prescribed for the project, typically 100 % of the laboratory maximum dry density for new aggregate or at least 95 % when reclaimed material is used, and these values are verified by a control strip of nuclear‑density tests [2]. Compaction must occur at the optimum moisture content defined in the mix design, with the actual moisture not exceeding the optimum by more than three percent [2]. Surface smoothness is controlled using straightedge measurements; any longitudinal irregularity greater than one‑half inch over a twelve‑foot template or an average irregularity exceeding one‑half inch on any three‑thousand‑square‑yard area must be corrected, and if correction is not possible the deficient portion must be removed and replaced with new material [2]. Thickness is checked by test holes, and a compacted depth that is short by half an inch or more also mandates removal and replacement to maintain continuity of the base layer thickness [2][1]. The repaired base must demonstrate the specified structural strengths, such as an indirect tensile strength of at least fifty pounds per square inch for asphalt‑stabilized mixes and an unconfined compressive strength in the range of three hundred to five hundred psi (or two hundred eighty psi for thicker overlays) for cementitious bases after seven days of curing; when a fractured rigid slab is involved, the elastic modulus of the repaired concrete must fall within the limits set by the applicable tables [2]. Stability is verified through a proof roll prior to placement of any flexible or rigid surface, and any indication of instability requires removal of the unstable subbase and repair of the underlying subgrade, with complete removal required if most of the layer exhibits instability [2]. Subbases that have been exposed to weather for more than sixty days are retested for depth and surface irregularities, and all deficiencies identified in this retest must be corrected before proceeding [2]. All patching materials used in the repair must be approved by the responsible representative, and surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be filled and compacted to a flat, tight surface; when interlayers such as geotextiles or asphalt are employed, the fill material must be capable of proper compaction with the interlayer, otherwise prior repair is required [2]. Final acceptance is confirmed through field inspection that verifies the repaired section meets the similarity requirements for type and thickness, exhibits the required structural properties and drainage performance, and complies with all density, moisture, surface‑smoothness, thickness, strength, stability, and material‑approval criteria outlined in the guides [1][2].

Performance and service life

Base repair involves removing failed sections of the base layer and replacing them with engineered material that restores structural capacity and provides drainage, thereby improving the pavement’s physical condition.[1] The process can also be performed by pulverizing the existing base, mixing it with stabilizing additives and water, and compacting the reclaimed mix to at least 95 % of laboratory‑maximum density, which reestablishes load‑distribution capability.[2] After compaction, the repaired layer is cured for seven days until it attains an unconfined compressive strength between three hundred and five hundred psi, after which traffic is limited to light vehicles until full strength is verified.[2] By meeting these density and strength criteria, the base regains its ability to distribute traffic loads, reducing water‑related distress such as pumping or frost heave and thus enhancing functional performance.[1][2] When applied as a preservation measure, base repair addresses minor distress, retains the existing functional condition of the roadway, and slows further deterioration, contributing to sustained durability.[1] In a rehabilitation context where the treatment is more extensive, the added structural capacity further strengthens the pavement structure, increasing overall durability and extending the expected service life.[1] Across both preservation and rehabilitation applications, the restored, well‑drained foundation supports the intended service life without premature distress, provided that traffic restrictions are observed until the required strength is achieved.[2][1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 222–224)

Base repair failures often begin with premature mid‑slab cracking, a distress that is frequently traced to an inadequate subbase material and to aggregate‑related problems such as Vanport Limestone concerns or Alkali Silica Reaction, conditions that may escape detection by routine condition surveys and therefore require targeted inspection before work proceeds [1]. In addition to cracking, the most commonly observed deficiencies appear as surface irregularities—high or low spots, excessive roughness, and waviness—that exceed the allowable half‑inch tolerance, depth shortfalls of at least one‑half inch, and depressions larger than roughly four square inches and deeper than an inch [2]. Insufficient compaction also recurs, with compacted layers that fail to reach 100 % of the laboratory maximum density (or, for reclaimed stabilized material, less than 95 % of the maximum) being routinely reported, as are moisture‑content errors where the water content deviates more than three percent from the optimum [2]. These distresses develop when the base mixture loses moisture or is placed on a frozen or otherwise unsuitable subgrade—air temperatures below 40 °F are prohibited—and when improper spreading allows segregation of coarse aggregate or the inadvertent mixing of subgrade or subbase material into the base course [2]. Inadequate compaction or vibration, failure to achieve prompt bonding between successive layers, and premature exposure to traffic—including construction or haul‑road traffic on a newly placed subbase—further promote void formation, waviness, and instability of the repaired layer [2]. Chemical over‑stabilization can produce shrinkage cracking when strengths exceed roughly 900 psi, while excessive stiffness of fractured slabs, indicated by an elastic modulus above the upper limit in Table A, leads to re‑fracturing under load [2]. Prolonged weather exposure beyond sixty days, winter shutdown without a protective overlay, and surface fabric temperatures that rise above 120 °F also undermine the integrity of the repaired base, accelerating the development of the aforementioned distresses [2]. When any of these conditions are observed—irregularities greater than half an inch, depth deficiencies of a half‑inch or more, unstable or rocking slab fragments, or mixtures that fail density, moisture, strength, or modulus acceptance criteria—the specifications require removal of the defective portion and reconstruction to meet the prescribed tolerances, reinforcing the importance of controlling these failure mechanisms during base repair operations [2].

Monitoring and follow-up

After the base repair has been placed and compacted, the inspector conducts a comprehensive post‑treatment inspection that addresses density, surface profile, and thickness. Density is first verified by constructing a minimum 300‑foot compaction control strip; nuclear density readings are taken after each roller pass until no further increase occurs, and then at least ten random tests are performed to confirm that the average in‑place density meets or exceeds the specified minimum, which must be not less than 100 % of the maximum density. Surface tolerances are checked with a template cut to the required cross section using a straightedge at intervals no greater than 25 feet; any irregularity exceeding one‑half inch is corrected immediately by loosening the surface and adding or removing material, blading, reshaping, or rebuilding the spot. Thickness is confirmed by drilling a test hole for each 3,000 square yards (or less); the measured compacted depth is compared to design, and any area that is short by one‑half inch or more is removed and rebuilt. The repaired section is then cured for the required seven‑day period with heavy traffic prohibited; light traffic may be permitted once the material stabilizes, but a proof roll using a vehicle equal to the heaviest expected load—or strength testing of core specimens—must verify that the 7‑day strength has been achieved before unlimited traffic is allowed. Throughout this period, any damage observed is repaired immediately and ongoing monitoring of surface tolerance and density continues until all acceptance criteria are satisfied. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 204–224)

The guides state that when ongoing condition surveys—such as annual videologging—continue to record distress types including cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling or weathering, joint spalling and faulting at levels that surpass the thresholds defined in treatment matrices, the base‑repair applied is judged insufficient and additional work ranging from expanded base repair to full rehabilitation must be initiated. [1] They also note that if the amount of patching required after a base repair exceeds roughly ten percent of the total pavement area, this proportion signals that preservation alone is no longer cost‑effective and further maintenance or structural rehabilitation is warranted. [2] In addition, field density measured on a compaction control strip that falls below the minimum specified value, or laboratory cores that do not achieve the required compressive or tensile strength limits, flag the repaired section for recompaction or replacement.
