Overlay Thickness

Part of: Overlay Design is-subtopic-of
Overlay Thickness is a topic under Overlay Design.
overlay-thicknesseffective-existing-thickness
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Overlay thickness is the prescribed depth of a new rigid or flexible pavement layer placed over an existing surface to supply the additional structural capacity needed to remedy distress, eliminate rutting, and restore acceptable rideability. The required depth is determined by the condition of the underlying pavement and the design method—thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete overlays are limited to 2.5–4 in, bonded concrete overlays to 4–8 in, while asphalt overlays must equal the extra pavement thickness calculated from the AASHTO design procedure, often incorporating a minimum 1‑inch leveling course—and it also governs panel dimensions (no larger than eighteen times the thickness with an aspect ratio ≤1.5) and joint‑cutting depths[1][2]. Compliance is verified through core testing and strength criteria such as a minimum 3,600 psi compressive strength and an average bond strength of 175 psi before traffic reopening[1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Overlay thickness is selected to provide the additional structural capacity required to correct existing pavement problems such as surface distress, rutting, or poor rideability. For bonded concrete overlays on pavements that are in good to fair condition, the thickness is intended to add structural capacity and eliminate surface distress. Unbonded overlays rely on sufficient depth to restore structural capacity on moderately to significantly deteriorated pavements. In asphalt overlays, the required thickness equals the amount of additional pavement needed to achieve the structural strength prescribed by the AASHTO design method. Thin‑bonded JPCP overlays of 2.5 to 4 in are considered to correct surface deficiencies and eliminate rutting, whereas bonded JPCP overlays of 4 to 8 in are considered to add structural capacity. Overlay thickness is also chosen so that the overlay can remediate surface deterioration and restore acceptable rideability by supplying enough depth to support traffic loads while creating a smooth, uniform riding surface. For an AC overlay placed over an existing AC/PCC pavement, the thickness provides sufficient structural depth to accommodate anticipated future traffic; the design first determines Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic and then adjusts it by calculating Effective Existing Thickness from a condition survey, yielding the actual asphalt overlay thickness required. In a bonded PCC overlay, the specified thickness supplies the structural capacity required for anticipated future traffic while respecting the condition of the existing concrete slab; the calculated thickness must be sufficient to carry projected truck loads after accounting for effective existing thickness and must be rounded up to the nearest half‑inch so that the final depth lies between the minimum and maximum limits given in Table 10.10, and it is appropriate only when the existing concrete does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting. Overlay thickness may be selected to provide extra structural capacity for high‑traffic, heavily loaded pavements while permitting a relatively thin slab—typically 3 to 6 in—to meet performance goals; increasing thickness together with fiber reinforcement is intended to limit crack widths, maintain joint or crack load‑transfer efficiency, and extend the pavement’s serviceability under variable support conditions. For bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 540), overlay thickness serves as a control parameter for joint layout and panel sizing so that panels are limited to dimensions no greater than eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and an aspect‑ratio of at most 1.5, directly addressing the need to prevent premature cracking and keep the overlay structurally sound. For unbonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 548), the same thickness limitation is used to limit panel size and joint depth in order to prevent premature cracking; saw cut depths must be at least D/3 within 12 inches of the pavement edges and at least D/4 elsewhere, reinforcing the crack‑prevention objective. For bonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545), overlay thickness is used to remediate a rigid pavement that has lost structural capacity or an undesirable elevation, allowing the engineer to lower the pavement grade and replace deteriorated material with a new concrete layer; construction requirements include scarifying the existing surface to the depth indicated in Section 591.3, cutting off loose reinforcement, removing all full‑depth asphalt patches, and then placing a uniform overlay that will achieve the required bond strength (average not less than 175 psi at seven days) and compressive strength (minimum 3,600 psi) before traffic can be reopened. In the measurement and payment provisions for bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 540.4), overlay thickness is used to prevent a deficiency in pavement thickness depth that would trigger payment adjustments; if cores show the overlay does not meet the required thickness, contract payment for the bonded concrete overlay and cement concrete material is reduced as specified, and when the measured depth is deficient by more than ¼ inch the Department will not pay for any extra core samples. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 10 (pp. 183–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 458–474)

Overlay thickness shall be selected so that the overlay can correct structural deficiencies, surface deterioration, and rideability as required for rehabilitation overlays; it must supply the structural strength called for by the design method so that a bonded system functions as one structure or an unbonded system provides a stable platform, while also respecting functional limits such as roadway grade, which may lead to using inlays when raising the road is undesirable, thereby ensuring safety and acceptable ride quality. The chosen thickness provides the required structural capacity, and a leveling course of at least 1‑inch may be included in the pavement’s structural calculations as a 1‑inch paving layer; when the existing surface is sound, drainage is good or will be upgraded, less than 2 % requires patching, and no structural upgrade is required, thin asphalt overlays are limited to a minimum depth of 1½ inches. Durability objectives are satisfied by an interlayer material that must be compatible with the asphalt, must be thermally stable and physically durable, and must not exhibit property or performance breakdown over time, thereby helping mitigate reflective cracking and moisture damage. Safety requirements include a Safety Edge applied to the outside edge of wearing and binder courses with a depth of 1½ inches or greater and should not be more than 5 inches. Fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays provide additional structural capacity while achieving durability and serviceability goals such as reduced crack widths, maintained joint or crack load‑transfer efficiency, and extended pavement service life through less crack deterioration; a thinner slab—typically 3 to 6 in.—is useful where higher traffic volumes, variable support conditions, or increased design or service life are required, and macrofibers can increase load‑transfer efficiency by about 30 % compared with plain concrete when cracks exceed >1.0 mm. The overlay must be placed when the concrete temperature is between 65 °F and 90 °F and the existing surface is below 120 °F to ensure proper bonding and durability, and it must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 3,600 psi and an average bond strength of at least 175 psi (with each individual test not less than 160 psi) before opening to traffic. To maintain safety and serviceability, edge slump may not exceed ¼ inch next to the shoulder or ⅛ inch next to an adjacent lane; saw cuts must extend the full overlay thickness plus ½ inch without damaging dowel load‑transfer devices, and intermediate transverse joints shall be spaced so that overlay panels do not exceed 15 feet in length. For bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements, structural panels shall not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter side ratio shall not exceed 1.5, which helps prevent premature cracking; similarly, for unbonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements, panel dimensions are limited to no more than eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and an aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5, thereby maintaining structural integrity, durability and serviceability, while saw cuts must be at least D/3 within 12 inches of the pavement edges and at least D/4 anywhere, providing sufficient stress relief and safety against random cracking. The pavement may be opened to traffic when the concrete attains the minimum required compressive strength of 3,000 psi, providing the necessary safety and serviceability performance. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

Overlay thickness is defined for both rigid and flexible overlays placed over existing pavements. For a bonded concrete overlay on an existing rigid (concrete‑surfaced) pavement that is being rehabilitated by scarifying, grade correction, or replacement of deteriorated patches, the design calls for an overlay thickness of 4 to 8 inches and requires ambient concrete temperature between 65 °F and 90 °F, a prepared surface temperature below 120 °F at placement, edge slump in the outer six inches adjacent to shoulders not exceeding 1/4 inch, joint cutting that extends through the full thickness of the overlay plus ½ inch, and bond‑strength testing with an average of at least 175 psi and no individual test below 160 psi [2]. An unbonded concrete overlay on a rigid pavement is appropriate when panel dimensions are limited to no more than eighteen times the overlay thickness, the longer‑to‑shorter side ratio does not exceed 1.5, saw cuts are no less than one‑third of the thickness within twelve inches of the pavement edges and no less than one‑quarter elsewhere [2]. For existing asphalt‑surfaced pavements receiving a Class AA cement‑concrete bonded overlay, the asphalt surface shall be milled to the plan‑specified depth, cracks larger than ¾ inch filled with approved grout, the cleaned surface must be dry and below 120 °F before concrete placement, final cleaning shall occur no more than thirty minutes prior to placement, protection from oil, grease, or standing water shall be provided, joint layout shall be controlled by the overlay thickness so that panel dimensions do not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and the aspect ratio does not exceed 1.5, and the design calls for an overlay thickness of 4 to 8 inches [2]. A thin bonded concrete overlay on an asphalt‑surfaced pavement may be placed with a typical thickness of 2.5 to 4 inches and is bonded directly to the asphalt surface after milling and preparation as described above [2]. Thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays applied over existing asphalt or composite pavements are specified at 2.5 to 4 inches in depth and are used to correct surface deficiencies, eliminate rutting, and are especially suitable for local streets and intersections with high average daily traffic [1]. When a bonded JPCP overlay is intended to add structural capacity to such pavements, the thickness should be 4 to 8 inches [1]. Unbonded concrete overlays may be applied to moderately to significantly deteriorated pavements and require a minimum 1‑inch asphalt separation layer or, alternatively, a non‑woven geotextile interlayer; in this case the overlay depth is not fixed [1]. For asphalt pavements that are structurally sound—defined as requiring less than 2 % patching—and that have good or upgraded surface and subsurface drainage, a Superpave wearing‑course overlay of at least 1½ inches is appropriate on non‑Interstate routes; on Interstates a thickness less than 1½ inches may be used only with prior PDAU approval [1]. A separate leveling course of minimum 1‑inch thickness may be placed to provide a uniform working platform before the binder or wearing layer, and thin Superpave overlays such as 6.3 mm or 9.5 mm Fine Grade may be applied within the prescribed depth limits [1]. The Safety Edge treatment shall be used as a standard pavement edge treatment on the outside edge of asphalt pavements and shoulders and should be used for both wearing and binder courses with a depth of 1½ inches or greater [1]. When an asphalt‑concrete (AC) overlay is placed over existing jointed plain concrete pavement, the design uses the Condition Survey Method with STAMPP condition data from the Roadway Management System and, when required, a field view by the district PME/PM to determine effective existing thickness; this calculation is appropriate for AC overlay applications on JPCP where the existing concrete condition must be quantified [1]. For an AC/PCC (composite) pavement that already has an asphalt overlay, an additional AC overlay without removing the existing surface is appropriate provided the existing asphalt is not suspected of material problems; if material problems are suspected, cores shall be obtained and analyzed, and the design proceeds by determining future‑traffic pavement thickness and then calculating effective existing thickness using the condition‑survey method [1]. Bonded Portland cement concrete (PCC) overlays are appropriate for existing PCC pavements that exhibit only limited cracking or faulting; the overlay is placed as a monolithic slab bonded directly to the underlying concrete, and thickness is determined according to PennDOT’s procedures using rigid‑pavement truck factors and DARWin calculations within the limits shown in Table 10.10 [1]. Fiber‑reinforced concrete (FRC) overlays may be applied over asphalt‑surfaced or composite pavements with typical thicknesses ranging from 3 to 6 inches; they are appropriate where a thinner slab is required, in higher traffic areas with heavy repeated loadings, under variable support conditions, or on projects that need an increased design or service life, and the presence of synthetic or steel macrofibers providing residual strengths between 100 and 225 psi enables these benefits [1]. A bonded FRC overlay can be designed if the existing asphalt pavement is in fair to good condition, whereas a poor and deteriorated underlying surface generally calls for an unbonded overlay [1].

When the existing pavement is in poor condition—such as significant structural deterioration, unstable support, stripping of asphalt, or unresolved drainage problems—a bonded concrete overlay is not appropriate and a different design approach, such as an unbonded overlay, should be used; similarly, if slabs are rocking, pumping, or faulted, stabilization or alternative treatments like cracking/breaking, seating, or rubblizing may be preferred before applying any overlay. When a thin asphalt overlay less than 1 1/2 inches is being considered, another design approach must be used unless the existing pavement surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than 2% requiring patching, both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded to good by the project, and no structural upgrade of the pavement is required; heater planing or milling should replace an overlay when the pavement base is in stable condition and removal of material would not affect performance, or when major curb reveal, shoulder, guide‑rail, or drainage adjustments are needed. The AC Overlay of AC/PCC composite pavement is only appropriate when a concrete road that already has an asphalt overlay can receive another asphalt layer without removing the existing overlay; if the current asphalt overlay is suspected of material problems or deficiencies, cores must be taken and analyzed, and any identified deficiency requires complete removal of that overlay and the use of the alternative AC Overlay of PCC Pavement design instead. When the existing concrete pavement shows extensive cracking or faulting, a bonded PCC overlay is not recommended; under such conditions another design option should be considered because significant distress in the underlying slab will transmit through a bonded overlay unless it is repaired. When the existing asphalt surface is only fair to good, a bonded concrete overlay can be used; however, if the underlying pavement is poor or deteriorated, an unbonded overlay becomes the preferred design because the decision between bonded and unbonded overlays depends chiefly on the condition of the existing pavement rather than on fiber reinforcement, and adding macrofibers does not justify converting an unbonded design to a bonded one. Design inputs and requirements

The overlay design must be based on a site assessment that confirms the existing pavement surface is asphalt and that both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded, with no structural upgrade required; traffic data must include the average daily traffic limits identified in the ADT tables to verify eligibility of Superpave thin overlays; material information requires confirming a sound pavement, less than 2 % patching, and specifying the appropriate Superpave wearing‑course mix such as PG 64E‑22 binder for 6.3 mm courses; and existing‑condition data must document pavement type, structural soundness, and the percentage of area needing patching. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

Overlay thickness is governed by a series of state specifications and design methodologies that prescribe limits, calculation procedures, and construction constraints. For thin‑bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements, the thickness shall be between 2.5 and 4 inches [1][2]. Bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements are limited to a range of 4 to 8 inches [1][2]. When an unbonded concrete overlay is placed, a minimum one‑inch asphalt separation layer—or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile—must be incorporated between the existing concrete and the new overlay [1]. The required thickness of an asphalt overlay shall be calculated to provide the structural strength required by the AASHTO Design Method described in Chapter 10 [1]. A minimum leveling course of 1 inch may be counted as a 1‑inch paving course in structural calculations [1]. On Interstate facilities, Superpave wearing‑course overlays shall not be placed with a thickness of less than 1½ inches without prior PDAU approval; on non‑Interstate routes the same minimum applies only when the existing surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than 2 % patching, and both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded [1]. All overlay depths must also comply with the placement limits shown in Table 9.5 and with the weather limitations of Publication 408 Section 413.3(b) [1]. The Safety Edge shall be used on wearing and binder courses whose depth is 1½ inches or greater but shall not exceed 5 inches, as required by Publication 408 Section 413.3(h)1e [1]. For an asphalt‑concrete (AC) overlay of an AC/PCC composite pavement, the design must first establish the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic using Table 10.8, then compute the Effective Existing Thickness with the Condition Survey Method according to Table 10.9, and finally apply the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors listed in Table 7.1 for ESAL calculations [1]. A bonded PCC overlay of a PCC pavement must observe the minimum and maximum concrete overlay thicknesses prescribed in Table 10.10; the calculated depth shall be rounded up to the nearest half‑inch and verified to fall within those limits [1]. Fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays are generally limited to a thickness ranging from 3 to 6 inches, and the inclusion of fibers is mandated by Publication 408 Section 541 [1]. For bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements, panel dimensions may not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and the ratio of the longer to shorter panel dimension shall not exceed 1.5; the existing asphalt surface must be milled to the depth indicated on the plans (Section 492) [2]. When cutting transverse or intermediate joints for bonded concrete overlays over concrete‑surfaced pavements, the cut depth shall equal the full thickness of the overlay—including any additional thickness used for grade corrections—plus ½ inch and the width must at least match the underlying joint [2]. Any overlay found defective in depth shall be repaired according to the limits shown in Table A of Section 545.4(c) [2]. Measurement and payment provisions require core testing to verify installed depth; where a deficiency is identified, the contractor’s payment for both the overlay and cement concrete material will be adjusted according to Table B, and the Department will not pay for additional cores when the depth shortfall exceeds ¼ inch [2]. For thin bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements, work shall be divided into lots of no more than 3,000 square yards per traffic lane with ramps placed in separate lots; one coring location per lot is selected at random according to PTM No. 1 and cores are taken while the inspector is present, or two cores (or nondestructive measurements) for projects under 3,000 square yards, with any alternative nondestructive method requiring written approval from the District Executive [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays over concrete‑surfaced pavements, panel dimensions follow the same limits of not exceeding eighteen times the overlay thickness and an aspect ratio not greater than 1.5; saw cuts used to create joints shall be at least D/3 within twelve inches of the pavement edges and at least D/4 elsewhere, where D is the overlay thickness [2]. Curing‑compound application rates are defined by whether the overlay thickness is greater than six inches (minimum 1 gallon per 150 sq ft) or less than or equal to six inches (minimum 1 gallon per 125 sq ft), or as recommended by the manufacturer, whichever is greater [2]. For asphalt overlays installed with an interlayer system, the overlay thickness must conform to the contract drawings and documents, and the first lift of compacted overlay in any area where the interlayer system is installed may not be less than 1½ inches [2].

Design methods and criteria

Overlay thickness is determined through prescribed procedures that vary according to the type of existing pavement and the selected overlay material, requiring specific testing, calculations, and design tools. For existing Portland cement concrete pavements (PCCP), a falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD) test is required and should be conducted prior to any overlay design, with the resulting structural information feeding subsequent calculations. When the overlay material is asphalt, the required thickness is derived from the Chapter 10 procedure grounded in the AASHTO Design Method and incorporates values such as the Structural Coefficient, minimum and maximum placement depths, and scratch and leveling‑course thicknesses taken from Tables 9.3, 9.5, 10.4, and 10.5, while traffic‑volume limits are obtained from Table 10.1 or 10.2; a pre‑overlay surface‑preparation sequence is performed, coring must be carried out during the design phase to verify an adequate bond, and a leveling course of at least 1 inch is placed to provide a uniform working platform. For concrete overlays, the specifications in Publication 408 prescribe thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays to be 2.5 to 4 inches deep and bonded JPCP overlays to be 4 to 8 inches deep. When overlay thickness is expressed directly rather than as a structural number, the Condition Survey Method is employed to calculate the effective existing thickness by using STAMPP condition data from the Roadway Management System and input guidance from Table 10.9; the values are entered into the DARWin software and the required overlay depth is obtained by selecting the “Calculate” button. In an asphalt‑concrete (AC) overlay of a jointed plain concrete pavement, the pavement thickness required for future traffic is first established and then the effective existing thickness is computed with the Condition Survey Method, after which DARWin supplies the actual overlay depth. For an AC overlay of an AC/PCC composite pavement, the design begins by determining the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic using Table 10.8, proceeds with the Effective Existing Thickness calculation per Table 10.9, and obtains the asphalt overlay depth from DARWin; equivalent single‑axle load (ESAL) calculations employ the rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors listed in Table 7.1. The design of a bonded PCC overlay follows the same sequence—calculating Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic and Effective Existing Thickness in DARWin using the rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1—and after entering the inputs, selecting “Calculate” yields the required concrete thickness, which is rounded up to the nearest half‑inch and must lie between the minimum and maximum values shown in Table 10.10. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, the residual tensile strength f₁₅₀ of the macro‑fiber mix is determined by ASTM C1609 testing (normally 100 to 225 psi) and added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture using the equation feff = MOR + f150 to obtain an effective flexural strength, which is entered into a Residual Strength Estimator spreadsheet that estimates the required f₁₅₀ based on pavement conditions and overlay inputs; the resulting feff is then used in mechanistic‑empirical design programs such as BCOA‑ME, ACPA Pavement Designer, or AASHTOWare Pavement ME to compute the necessary concrete overlay thickness, with typical fiber dosages achieving the specified residual strength ranging from 0.2 % to 0.5 % by volume. Section 540 of Publication 408 specifies that a bonded concrete overlay over an asphalt‑surfaced pavement shall have a thickness of 4 to 8 inches, and this thickness governs empirical limits on panel size such that maximum panel dimensions may not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and the ratio of the longer to the shorter panel dimension shall not exceed 1.5; joint layouts are developed accordingly and the sawing schedule may be determined with FHWA’s HIPERPAV or a similar software approved by the Department as a design tool for establishing the project‑specific sawing window. For unbonded concrete overlays described in Section 548, the overlay thickness denoted D is linked to several procedural limits: panel dimensions must not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter side ratio shall not exceed 1.5; saw cuts must be no less than D/3 within twelve inches of the pavement edges and no less than D/4 anywhere, with a minimum cut width of ¼ inch; joint layout follows the approved plan and FHWA’s HIPERPAV or similar software may be used to determine the project‑specific sawing window. Verification of overlay thickness for thin bonded concrete overlays in Section 541 is performed by dividing the pavement into lots representing not more than 3,000 square yards per traffic lane, selecting one random coring location in each lot according to PTM No. 1, drilling cores in accordance with PTM No. 606 in the presence of the Inspector, and for intersections or projects smaller than 3,000 square yards either drilling two cores or conducting non‑destructive thickness measurements per PTM No. 605; after coring, core walls are thoroughly cleaned of laitance and loose material and the holes are filled with the same mix used to place the overlay or with a premixed non‑shrink grout. [1][2]

Overlay thickness is selected according to the condition of the existing pavement and the structural capacity required for rehabilitation (section 4.9) [1]. Thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays over an asphalt or composite base are specified at a depth of 2.5 to 4 inches, whereas bonded JPCP overlays over the same bases are designed at a depth of 4 to 8 inches [1]. When the overlay material is asphalt concrete, its thickness shall be the amount of additional pavement required to achieve the structural strength prescribed by the AASHTO Design Method in Chapter 10, and a falling‑weight deflectometer test should be performed on the existing plain cement concrete pavement prior to designing the overlay [1]. Under the overlay specifications (section 5.12), a leveling course shall be at least 1 inch thick and may be counted as a 1‑inch paving layer in structural calculations [1]. Thin asphalt overlays using a Superpave Wearing Course shall not be placed thinner than 1½ inches on Interstates without prior PDAU approval; on non‑Interstate routes the thickness may be less than 1½ inches only if the existing surface is asphalt, structurally sound with less than 2 % patching, and both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded [1]. When milling an existing asphalt layer over a concrete or brick base, at least 1½ inches of the original asphalt should remain to retain structural integrity and bond [1]. The Safety Edge shall be provided with a depth of 1½ inches or greater but not exceeding 5 inches total [1]. For an AC overlay of an AC/PCC composite pavement (section 10.5), design thickness begins by determining the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic as guided by Table 10.8, then calculating the Effective Existing Thickness using the Condition Survey Method with inputs from Table 10.9; the resulting thickness is obtained from DARWin as the actual asphalt overlay required, and ESAL calculations must employ the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors listed in Table 7.1 [1]. When designing a bonded PCC overlay of PCC pavement (section 10.6), thickness is obtained by first calculating the future‑traffic pavement thickness in DARWin and the effective existing thickness using the condition‑survey evaluation method, then computing the required concrete thickness; that thickness must be rounded up to the nearest half‑inch and must fall within the minimum and maximum overlay depths listed in Table 10.10, with the design assuming the use of rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 for ESAL calculations and presuming the existing concrete does not exhibit extensive cracking or faulting so that a monolithic slab can be formed [1]. For fiber‑reinforced concrete pavement overlays (section 10.10), design thickness is normally limited to a range of 3 to 6 inches, and the required residual strength (f150) must fall between 100 and 225 psi; this residual strength is added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength that is entered into mechanistic‑empirical design programs to verify that the chosen thickness meets structural performance objectives [1]. Overlay thickness must be sufficient to accommodate the scarification depth specified for grade correction when a bonded concrete overlay is placed over a concrete‑surfaced pavement, and saw‑cut joints are required to penetrate the entire overlay; consequently the cut depth shall equal the full thickness of the overlay—including any extra thickness used for grade corrections—plus ½ inch while protecting dowel devices [2]. At the pavement edges the finished surface may not slump more than ¼ inch in the outer six inches adjacent to a shoulder and may not slump more than ⅛ inch in the outer six inches adjacent to an adjoining lane [2]. For projects that employ an asphalt paving interlayer system, overlay thickness shall meet the requirements set out in the contract drawings; the first lift of the asphalt overlay will not be less than 1½ inches compacted, and geotextile paving fabric may not be installed in areas where the overlaid asphalt tapers to a minimum compacted thickness of less than 1½ inches [2]. When a bonded concrete overlay is applied to an asphalt‑surfaced pavement, Portland cement concrete shall be placed with a thickness ranging from 4 to 8 inches; that thickness controls the allowable panel size so that each concrete panel shall be limited to a maximum dimension that does not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the ratio of its longer side to its shorter side shall not exceed 1.5, with joint spacing following the project plans or an approved Department layout [2]. Core sampling is used to verify the thickness, and if a deficiency in depth exceeds ¼ inch the contractor receives no credit for additional cores and payment for both the overlay and the cement concrete material is reduced as specified, while any defective pavement left in place is compensated at only five percent of the contract unit prices unless a written exception is issued [2]. For thin bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements, the thickness is limited to a range of 2.5 to 4 inches; the overlay is divided into testing lots representing not more than 3 000 square yards per traffic lane, with ramps placed in separate lot(s). Within each lot the inspector shall randomly select one coring location and have cores drilled according to PTM 606 in the presence of the inspector; if the project is at an intersection or the total area is less than 3 000 square yards, two cores—or a non‑destructive thickness measurement per PTM 605—must be obtained, and after coring the vertical surface of each hole shall be thoroughly cleaned of laitance and loose material before being filled with the same mixture used to construct the overlay or an approved premixed non‑shrink grout [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements, panel dimensions shall not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio should never exceed 1.5 [2]. Saw cuts that form transverse and longitudinal joints must be cut to a depth of at least D/3 within twelve inches of any edge and to at least D/4 elsewhere, where D is the overlay thickness [2]. When a white‑pigmented liquid membrane forming curing compound is used, it shall be applied at a minimum rate of one gallon (± 0.1 gallon) per 150 ft² for overlays thicker than six inches and per 125 ft² for overlays six inches or thinner [2].

Overlay thickness is first determined by evaluating existing pavement condition and intended performance[1]. For concrete pavements, a thin‑bonded jointed plain cementitious pavement (JPCP) overlay of 2.5 to 4 inches is recommended when only surface deficiencies or rutting need correction, while a bonded JPCP overlay of 4 to 8 inches is used to add structural capacity and reduce maintenance cycles; unbonded concrete overlays require at least a 1‑inch fine‑graded asphalt separation layer (or a non‑woven geotextile) to act as the platform[1]. For flexible overlays, the required thickness equals the amount of additional pavement needed to provide the required structural strength as determined by the AASHTO Design Method[1]. The selection of material—rigid Portland cement concrete for bonded or unbonded concrete overlays, or asphalt concrete for flexible overlays—is driven by the need to satisfy structural, durability, constructability and performance goals[1]. When an asphalt overlay is designed, the engineer first consults the structural tables (9.3, 9.5, 10.4, 10.5) to obtain the required structural coefficient and the allowable minimum and maximum placement depths for the selected Superpave mix; a leveling course, when used, shall be at least 1‑inch thick and may be counted as a structural layer[1]. Thin functional overlays such as the 6.3 mm fine‑grade wearing course are designed only if the existing surface is asphalt, the existing pavement is structurally sound, less than 2 % of the pavement requires patching, and both surface and subsurface drainage are good (or will be improved); the appropriate binder PG 64E‑22 is specified for the 6.3 mm wearing course[1]. Any overlay on an Interstate thinner than 1½ inches requires prior PDAU approval[1]. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, the pavement engineer first determines the residual flexural strength (f150) required for the project and then selects a macrofiber type and dosage that will achieve that residual strength; typical fiber content ranges from 0.2 % to 0.5 % by volume[1]. The chosen fiber content together with the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture yields an effective flexural strength that is entered into mechanistic‑empirical design programs to size a thinner slab—often in the 3 to 6 in range—for higher traffic or variable support conditions[1]. Because adding fibers can lower slump by up to 4 in, water‑reducing admixtures and air‑entraining adjustments are used in trial batches to meet fresh‑property specifications, thereby ensuring constructability while preserving durability through reduced crack widths and maintained joint load transfer[1]. To satisfy the same four goals for an asphalt overlay that incorporates an interlayer system, the asphalt material must be applied at rates that fully saturate the chosen reinforcement; the required application rate is 0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard for geotextile paving fabric, 0.02 to 0.05 gallons per square yard for fiberglass mesh grid, and 0.19 to 0.28 gallons per square yard for composite reinforcing grid, with the width of placement extending six inches beyond each side of the reinforcement as approved by the manufacturer’s technical representative[2]. The material temperature shall be at least 290 °F and shall not exceed 320 °F for performance‑grade liquid asphalt binders to ensure proper bonding without damage to the interlayer system[2]. The first lift of the asphalt overlay shall not be less than 1½ inches in areas where an asphalt paving interlayer system is installed, providing the required structural capacity[2].

Structural details and interfaces

For unbonded concrete overlays the joints are installed in the same manner as for a new concrete pavement, and they need not be aligned with existing transverse joints; their spacing is determined by the overlay type and thickness [1]. A fine‑graded asphalt separation layer at least 1 inch thick, or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile, must be placed between the old slab and the new concrete to achieve the unbonded condition [1]. When an asphalt overlay is placed over a concrete pavement, the contractor must saw and seal the overlay directly over all underlying transverse joints and any patch joints [1]. Bonded concrete overlays are constructed with smaller slab dimensions but no special joint alignment beyond standard practice is required [1]. Overlay thickness may include a separate leveling course of at least 1 inch to provide a uniform working platform, which can be counted as a 1‑inch paving layer in the structural calculations [1]. At the pavement edge where a transition is desired, a Safety Edge shall be provided on the outside edge of asphalt pavements and shoulders with a depth of not less than 1 ½ inches and not greater than 5 inches [1]. Reinforcement of an overlay can be achieved by installing an asphalt paving interlayer—such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grid, or composite reinforcing grid—that must be compatible with the asphalt mix and thermally stable to maintain bond performance [1]. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays the design calls for properly timed and adequately deep saw‑cut contraction joints so that the FRC joint activates early and prevents premature cracking; macrofibers serve as reinforcement within the slab and can maintain load‑transfer efficiency across those joints similarly to tie bars, although they do not replace dowel bars where required [1]. In a bonded concrete overlay over a concrete‑surfaced pavement, all existing transverse joints must be filled with joint backing or an approved filling material placed within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface and protected from displacement during placement; the joint locations are to be accurately marked for subsequent saw‑cutting [2]. The transverse joints are then saw‑cut directly over the existing joints to a depth equal to the full thickness of the overlay plus ½ inch, with a cut width at least as wide as the underlying joint while avoiding damage to any dowel load‑transfer devices, and intermediate transverse joints must be spaced so that no panel exceeds 15 feet in length [2]. Longitudinal joints receive a sealant reservoir ¼ inch wide cut to the full overlay depth plus ½ inch, and joint faces are sealed as specified; edge faces along both sides of the overlaid pavement must be neat and free of honeycomb or segregation, with edge slump not exceeding ¼ inch next to the shoulder or ⅛ inch next to an adjacent lane [2]. Any exposed reinforcement or steel plates encountered during scarification shall be removed before overlay placement [2]. When an asphalt paving interlayer system is incorporated, joints are required to have an overlap of at least 1 inch in the direction of placement, and all reinforcement such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grid, or composite reinforcing grid must be fully saturated with asphalt material at manufacturer‑approved rates prior to placing the overlay; the first lift of the asphalt overlay must achieve a minimum compacted thickness of 1 ½ inches in areas where the interlayer system is installed [2]. For a bonded concrete overlay over an asphalt‑surfaced pavement, joints are laid out according to the project plans or an approved layout, with panel dimensions not exceeding eighteen times the overlay thickness and a length‑to‑width ratio not greater than 1.5; longitudinal joints should be avoided in wheel paths, and both transverse and longitudinal joints must be created only by sawing with a green‑cut saw to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness (D/3) and a minimum width of ⅛ inch once the concrete has hardened sufficiently, while any joint located within 1.5 ft of an existing crack is omitted [2]. Transitions are required to be constructed at the overlay limits as shown on the plans; where a transition overlies treated base or existing concrete pavement a geotextile debonding layer is placed in accordance with Section 212, and construction of transitions together with joint sawing and sealing constitute the required structural details for the overlay [2]. For an unbonded concrete overlay over a concrete‑surfaced pavement, existing transverse expansion joints are identified and precut in the plastic concrete; two full‑depth saw cuts are then made on each side of the precut joint with a depth no less than D/3 within 12 inches of the pavement edges and no less than D/4 elsewhere, after which debris is removed before sealing [2]. New joints need not align with existing ones but must be spaced as indicated on the project plans or according to an accepted layout plan; longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths, panel dimensions may not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and the aspect ratio may not exceed 1.5, and saw cuts are made only with early‑entry saws to achieve at least D/3 depth and a minimum width of ¼ inch after curing compounds have been removed within a maximum of ½ hour before sawing [2].

In rehabilitation projects the interface is defined by whether the overlay is intended to be bonded or left unbonded, because a bonded overlay requires a continuous bond that makes the existing pavement an integral part of the structural system while an unbonded overlay relies on a separation layer and does not require such a bond [1]. For bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements the new Portland‑cement concrete layer is specified at a thickness of four to eight inches, and panels are limited so that no dimension exceeds eighteen times the overlay thickness and the ratio of longer to shorter side does not exceed 1.5, with longitudinal joints avoided in wheel paths [2]. For AC over AC/PCC composite pavements the existing surface is evaluated by coring; if material deficiencies are found the old overlay shall be completely removed before the new asphalt layer is placed, otherwise the design proceeds by determining Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic, calculating Effective Existing Thickness with the Condition Survey Method, and using the DARWin output to obtain the actual required overlay thickness [1]. In bonded PCC overlays the interface is a direct bond so that the two act as a monolithic slab; this method is used only when the underlying concrete does not exhibit extensive cracking or faulting, and the required overlay thickness is calculated in DARWin, rounded up to the nearest half‑inch, and checked against the minimum and maximum depths specified in Table 10.10 [1]. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays the decision to bond or leave unbonded is driven by the condition of the existing asphalt; a fair‑to‑good pavement may receive a bonded FRC overlay with typical thickness between three and six inches, while a poor deteriorated surface calls for an unbonded design, and cores must be taken to verify that stripping is not prevalent before bonding to a milled surface [1].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

In the design of an overlay, drainage and moisture considerations are satisfied by requiring that both surface and subsurface drainage be good or will be upgraded to good as part of the project before a thin asphalt overlay is placed; this condition applies when the existing pavement is sound and no structural upgrade is needed. The specification also mandates a minimum overlay thickness of 1 ½ inches for thin Superpave wearing courses on non‑Interstate routes, which provides sufficient depth to maintain structural integrity while allowing the use of interlayers that act as moisture barriers and help mitigate freeze‑thaw damage in harsh climates. When an interlayer is selected, its ability to block water intrusion and its compatibility with the overlay are evaluated alongside climate and traffic conditions, and a thicker overlay may be recommended where severe freeze‑thaw cycles are expected. In Section 545 of the Pennsylvania specifications, any standing water on the prepared pavement must be drained or removed with Department‑approved measures before the concrete overlay is placed, directly addressing surface moisture and drainage concerns. The surface temperature must be kept below 120°F immediately prior to placement, and concrete may only be placed when its temperature lies between 65°F and 90°F; fog‑misting, protective covers, or blankets are used to maintain these limits. If the forecasted air temperature is 45°F or less, insulating blankets shall be provided to prevent rapid heat loss, and removal of those blankets must not cause a temperature change exceeding 40°F within any one‑hour period. After finishing, curing material must be applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation at a minimum rate of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 ft² to protect moisture and aid strength development. In Section 466, overlay thickness design is coordinated with surface drainage by requiring the interlayer to be placed on a drainable surface that contains no depressions, and the first lift of asphalt overlay shall not be less than 1½ inches where an interlayer is used. Materials must be kept dry during shipping and storage, and installation may proceed only when weather conditions satisfy the manufacturer’s recommendations; if rain falls on the interlayer before overlay placement, the surface must be allowed to dry prior to placing the asphalt overlay. When a leveling course is required to provide a uniform working platform and to establish the necessary cross‑slope, its thickness shall be at least one inch [1]. For Interstate facilities the wearing or binder courses applied as an overlay shall not be thinner than one and one‑half inches unless a prior exception is granted [1]. For non‑Interstate facilities the same one‑and‑half‑inch minimum applies only when the existing surface is asphalt, structurally sound, has adequate surface and subsurface drainage, and does not require a structural upgrade [1]. Any Safety Edge incorporated into an overlay shall have a depth of at least one and one‑half inches but shall not exceed five inches, and it must be placed on both wearing and binder courses along pavement edges and shoulders [1]. In asphalt paving interlayer systems the overlay thickness must conform to the values shown on the contract drawings, and the first lift of an asphalt overlay may not be less than one and one‑half inches when compacted [2]. A pre‑placement meeting shall identify slopes and sharp curves so that the selected overlay thickness can accommodate those geometric features [2]. During placement, the paver and any other vehicles must turn gradually and avoid abrupt starts or stops in order to protect the thin overlay, and no traffic except construction vehicles may use the pavement until the overlay has cured [2]. For a bonded concrete overlay over a concrete‑surfaced pavement, edge slump shall not exceed one quarter inch on the shoulder side and shall not exceed one eighth inch next to an adjacent lane [2]. Intermediate transverse joints in such overlays must be spaced so that no panel exceeds fifteen feet in length, and saw cuts for those joints shall extend through the full thickness of the overlay plus a half‑inch without damaging underlying dowel load transfer devices, with a width at least equal to the existing joint [2]. The pavement may be opened to traffic only after the concrete has attained a compressive strength of 3,600 psi and the bond strength satisfies the minimum values specified for the overlay [2]. For bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements, panel dimensions shall not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the length‑to‑width ratio shall not exceed 1.5, while longitudinal joints shall be avoided in wheel paths [2]. Unbonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements must also keep panel dimensions no larger than eighteen times the overlay thickness and maintain an aspect ratio as close to 1.0 as feasible but not exceeding 1.5, with longitudinal joints kept out of wheel paths and joint spacing following the project plan [2]. Saw cuts that form the joints in unbonded concrete overlays shall be at least D/3 deep within twelve inches of the pavement edges and at least D/4 deep elsewhere, where D represents the overlay thickness [2].

Verification and expected performance

After the DARWin calculations provide a required concrete thickness for the bonded PCC overlay, the designer rounds that value up to the nearest half‑inch and then verifies that the resulting overlay depth falls between the minimum and maximum limits listed in Table 10.10, confirming compliance with design specifications and dimensional requirements. For projects that include an asphalt paving interlayer system, once the interlayer is installed the completed overlay thickness is checked by confirming that the measured asphalt thickness satisfies the contract drawing specifications. The first lift in any area with an interlayer system must achieve a compacted thickness of not less than 1 1/2 inches. Asphalt material temperature is verified against the minimum required by the manufacturer’s technical representative as documented in the field quality‑control plan, ensuring constructability and performance. All paving over the interlayer must be completed on the same day with no construction traffic before curing, and joints and overlaps are executed according to the specified overlap requirements, thereby maintaining consistency with project requirements. For bonded concrete overlays the completed thickness is verified through a post‑placement coring program that divides the pavement into lots of not more than 3 000 square yards per lane, with the inspector selecting one random core per lot (two cores for intersections or smaller areas). Each core is drilled in accordance with PTM No. 606, cleaned, and its depth measured with a device conforming to PTM No. 614; if any core measurement is deficient by more than 1/4 inch the inspector directs additional cores at 100‑foot intervals until the deficiency is reduced to no more than 1/4 inch. The pavement is considered defective when any measured depth remains beyond the 1/4 inch limit as shown in Table B, and such deficient pavement triggers payment adjustments according to Table B while the Department will not pay for additional cores drilled after a deficiency exceeds 1/4 inch. For thin bonded concrete overlays the same lot size limitation applies; the inspector selects one random coring location per lot (or two cores or non‑destructive measurements at intersections or where the lot is less than 3 000 square yards), drills the core in the presence of the inspector, cleans the vertical surface of laitance and loose material, and then fills the hole with the mixture used to construct the course or with a premixed non‑shrink grout.
