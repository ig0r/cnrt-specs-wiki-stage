Overlay Thickness

Part of: Overlay Design is-subtopic-of
Overlay Thickness is a topic under Overlay Design.
overlay-thicknesseffective-existing-thickness
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Overlay thickness is the engineered depth of a new pavement layer—whether Portland cement concrete or Superpave asphalt—that is added to an existing surface to supply the additional structural capacity required to remedy surface distress, rutting, or inadequate rideability and to accommodate anticipated traffic loads[1]. The required thickness is derived from the AASHTO design method (or DARWin condition‑survey calculations for concrete) and may include a minimum one‑inch leveling course that counts as structural pavement while ensuring a smooth, uniform riding surface[1][2]. For thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete overlays the depth is limited to 2.5–4 inches, bonded concrete overlays to 4–8 inches, and fiber‑reinforced or unbonded systems typically range from 3 to 6 inches, whereas asphalt overlays are sized to meet the structural number prescribed by the design tables and never less than 1½ inches on non‑Interstate facilities without special approval[1]. In both bonded and unbonded concrete designs the overlay thickness also governs joint layout, restricting panel dimensions to no more than eighteen times the slab depth and an aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5, thereby preventing premature cracking and preserving durability[2].

Purpose and design objectives

Overlay thickness is selected to provide the additional structural capacity needed to correct existing pavement problems such as surface distress, rutting, or poor rideability [1]. For bonded concrete overlays the thickness is intended to add structural capacity and eliminate surface distress on pavements that are in good to fair condition, while unbonded overlays rely on sufficient depth to restore structural capacity on moderately to significantly deteriorated pavements [1]. In asphalt overlays the required thickness represents the amount of additional pavement needed to achieve the structural strength prescribed by the AASHTO design method, and thin‑bonded JPCP overlays of 2.5 to 4 in are considered to correct surface deficiencies and eliminate rutting, whereas bonded JPCP overlays of 4 to 8 in are considered to add structural capacity [1]. Overlay thickness is also chosen so that an overlay can remediate surface deterioration and restore acceptable rideability by supplying enough depth to support traffic loads while creating a smooth, uniform riding surface [1]. For an AC overlay placed over an existing AC/PCC pavement the thickness provides sufficient structural depth to accommodate anticipated future traffic loads; the design first determines the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic and then adjusts it by calculating the Effective Existing Thickness from a condition survey, resulting in the actual asphalt overlay thickness required [1]. In a bonded PCC overlay the specified thickness supplies the structural capacity required for anticipated future traffic while respecting the condition of the existing concrete slab; the calculated thickness must be sufficient to carry projected truck loads after accounting for the effective existing thickness and must be rounded up to the nearest half‑inch so that the final depth lies between the minimum and maximum limits given in Table 10.10, and it is appropriate only when the existing concrete does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting [1]. Overlay thickness may be selected to provide extra structural capacity for high‑traffic, heavily loaded pavements while permitting a relatively thin slab—typically 3 to 6 in—to meet performance goals; increasing thickness together with fiber reinforcement is intended to limit crack widths, maintain joint or crack load‑transfer efficiency, and extend the pavement’s serviceability under variable support conditions [1]. In SECTION 548—UNBONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF CONCRETE‑SURFACED PAVEMENTS the concrete overlay thickness serves as a key control for panel size and joint layout so that panels are not subject to premature cracking, with maximum panel dimensions limited to no more than eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and joint spacing and aspect ratios required to keep panels from becoming overly large or elongated [2]. In SECTION 540—BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF ASPHALT‑SURFACED PAVEMENT the bonded concrete overlay design similarly limits panel sizes relative to the overlay thickness, specifying that maximum panel dimensions should not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and that the length‑to‑width ratio of a panel should not exceed 1.5, thereby preventing premature cracking and preserving structural performance [2]. In SECTION 466 – ASPHALT PAVING INTERLAYER SYSTEM the overlay asphalt thickness must satisfy the contract‑drawing requirements, and the first lift of overlay is required to have a compacted thickness of at least 1.5 in wherever an interlayer system is installed in order to meet the specified design performance criteria [2]. In SECTION 541—THIN BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF ASPHALT‑SURFACED PAVEMENT overlay thickness is used to remedy situations where core testing shows the existing pavement does not meet the required thickness depth; when a deficiency is identified, payment for the bonded concrete overlay and cement concrete material is adjusted to ensure adequate structural support and ride quality [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 10 (pp. 183–188); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 309–311), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 446–462)

For rehabilitation overlays, overlay thickness must be selected so that the overlay can correct structural deficiencies, surface deterioration, and rideability as required for rehabilitation overlays, and it must supply the structural strength called for by the design method (e.g., the AASHTO Design Method) so that either a bonded system functions as one structure or an unbonded system provides a stable platform, thereby extending service life, reducing maintenance cycles, and limiting reflective cracking; functional limits such as roadway grade may lead to using inlays when raising the road is undesirable, thus ensuring safety and acceptable ride quality. In the overlay design, the chosen thickness provides the required structural capacity, and a leveling course of at least 1‑inch may be included in the pavement’s structural calculations as a 1‑inch paving layer. When the existing surface is sound, drainage is good or will be upgraded, less than 2 % requires patching, and no structural upgrade is required, thin asphalt overlays are limited to a minimum depth of 1½ inches. Durability objectives are satisfied by an interlayer material that must be compatible with the asphalt, must be thermally stable and physically durable, and must not exhibit property or performance breakdown over time, thereby helping to mitigate reflective cracking and moisture damage. Safety requirements include a Safety Edge applied to the outside edge of wearing and binder courses with a depth of 1½ inches or greater and should not be more than 5 inches. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, overlay thickness provides additional structural capacity while also achieving durability and serviceability goals such as reduced crack widths, maintained joint or crack load transfer efficiency, and extended pavement service life through less crack deterioration; a thinner slab—typically 3 to 6 in.—is used where higher traffic volumes, variable support conditions, or projects needing an increased design or service life are present. Use of macrofibers can increase load‑transfer efficiency by about 30 % compared with plain concrete when cracks exceed >1.0 mm, further supporting durability and safety objectives. For bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavement, overlay thickness must be incorporated into the structural design so that panel dimensions are limited to no more than 18 times the concrete overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio does not exceed 1.5, which helps prevent premature cracking. For functional safety, the milled asphalt surface must be below 120 °F immediately before placing the concrete overlay. Durability is further ensured by controlling curing temperature so that the rate of temperature change at the concrete surface does not exceed 40 °F within any 1‑hour period. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

For thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays placed over existing asphalt or composite pavements, the specified depth is 2.5 to 4 inches and they are intended for correcting surface deficiencies and are especially suitable for local streets and intersections with high ADT [1]. When a bonded JPCP overlay is used to add structural capacity to such pavements, the required thickness is 4 to 8 inches [1]. Unbonded concrete overlays may be applied to moderately to significantly deteriorated pavements; they require a minimum 1‑inch asphalt separation layer or, alternatively, a non‑woven geotextile interlayer, and no fixed overlay depth is prescribed in this case [1]. For asphalt pavements that are structurally sound—defined as requiring less than 2 % patching—and that have good or upgraded surface and subsurface drainage, a Superpave wearing‑course overlay of at least 1½ inches is appropriate on non‑Interstate routes; on Interstates a thickness less than 1½ inches may be used only with prior PDAU approval [1]. A separate leveling course of minimum 1‑inch thickness may be placed to provide a uniform working platform before the binder or wearing layer is applied [1]. The Safety Edge treatment shall be used on the outside edge of asphalt pavements and shoulders, and it should be provided with a depth of 1½ inches or greater for both wearing and binder courses [1]. When an AC overlay is placed over existing jointed plain concrete pavement, the design employs the Condition Survey Method using STAMPP condition data from the Roadway Management System and may require a field view by the district PME/PM to determine effective existing thickness; this approach is appropriate for AC overlay applications on JPCP where the existing concrete condition must be quantified [1]. For an AC/PCC (composite) pavement that already has an asphalt overlay, an additional AC overlay without removing the existing surface is appropriate provided the existing asphalt is not suspected of material problems; if material problems are suspected, cores shall be obtained and analyzed before proceeding [1]. Bonded Portland cement concrete (PCC) overlays are appropriate for existing PCC pavements that exhibit only limited cracking or faulting; they are placed as a monolithic slab bonded directly to the underlying concrete and thickness is determined according to PennDOT procedures using rigid‑pavement truck factors and DARWin calculations within the limits shown in Table 10.10 [1]. Fiber‑reinforced concrete (FRC) overlays may be applied over asphalt‑surfaced or composite pavements with typical thicknesses ranging from 3 to 6 inches; they are appropriate where a thinner slab is required, in higher traffic areas with heavy repeated loadings, under variable support conditions, or on projects that need an increased design or service life, and a bonded FRC overlay can be designed if the existing asphalt pavement is in fair to good condition whereas a poor and deteriorated underlying surface generally calls for an unbonded overlay [1]. In SECTION 548 of the specifications, an unbonded concrete overlay is appropriate for existing concrete‑surfaced (rigid) pavements after unstable slabs are stabilized and surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch are repaired; the interlayer selection depends on joint faulting, with a geotextile used when joints have greater than ¼ inch faulting and an asphalt interlayer material placed to a nominal depth of 1 inch when joint faulting exceeds ½ inch [2]. For such unbonded overlays the panel layout shall be governed so that maximum panel dimensions do not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and the aspect ratio of any panel should be as close to 1.0 as feasible and shall not exceed 1.5 [2]. In SECTION 540, a bonded concrete overlay (Class AA cement concrete) is appropriate when an existing asphalt‑surfaced pavement is milled to the plan‑specified depth, cleaned, and the surface temperature is below 120°F before placement; joint layout must keep panel dimensions no greater than eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and maintain a length‑to‑width ratio not exceeding 1.5, and where the overlay terminates over an existing treated base or concrete a geotextile debonding layer is required under the transition [2]. The design for that bonded overlay calls for an overlay thickness ranging from 4 to 8 inches [2]. SECTION 541 specifies that a thin bonded concrete overlay is appropriate when the existing pavement is asphalt‑surfaced and the design calls for a Portland cement concrete layer that is thin—specifically between 2.5 and 4 inches in thickness [2]. SECTION 515 requires saw‑cutting and sealing procedures when an asphalt overlay is placed over existing transverse joints on a rigid pavement and the total depth of the overlay reaches 3½ inches or more; this triggers an initial 1/8‑inch‑wide saw‑cut to a depth of 1½ inches or one‑third of the total overlay thickness, whichever is greater, with any subsequent cuts located within ± 1 inch of the joint, and sealing may only be performed when air temperature is at least 40°F and not above 90°F, with the sealant placed within 7 days after the wearing course has cooled below 140°F [2].

When the existing pavement is in poor condition—such as significant structural deterioration, unstable support, stripping of asphalt, or unresolved drainage problems—a bonded concrete overlay is not appropriate and a different design approach, such as an unbonded overlay, should be used; similarly, if slabs are rocking, pumping, or faulted, stabilization or alternative treatments like cracking/breaking, seating, or rubblizing may be preferred before applying any overlay. When a thin asphalt overlay less than 1 1/2 inches is being considered, another design approach must be used unless the existing pavement surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than 2% requiring patching, both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded to good by the project, and no structural upgrade of the pavement is required; heater planing or milling should replace an overlay when the pavement base is in stable condition and removal of material would not affect performance, or when major curb reveal, shoulder, guide‑rail, or drainage adjustments are needed. The AC Overlay of AC/PCC composite pavement is only appropriate when a concrete road that already has an asphalt overlay can receive another asphalt layer without removing the existing overlay; if the current asphalt overlay is suspected of material problems or deficiencies, cores must be taken and analyzed, and any identified deficiency requires complete removal of that overlay and the use of the alternative AC Overlay of PCC Pavement design instead. When the existing concrete pavement shows extensive cracking or faulting, a bonded PCC overlay is not recommended; under such conditions another design option should be considered because significant distress in the underlying slab will transmit through a bonded overlay unless it is repaired. When the existing asphalt surface is only fair to good, a bonded concrete overlay can be used; however, if the underlying pavement is poor or deteriorated, an unbonded overlay becomes the preferred design because the decision between bonded and unbonded overlays depends chiefly on the condition of the existing pavement rather than on fiber reinforcement, and adding macrofibers does not justify converting an unbonded design to a bonded one. Design inputs and requirements

The overlay design must be based on a site assessment that confirms the existing pavement surface is asphalt and that both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded, with no structural upgrade required; traffic data must include the average daily traffic limits identified in the ADT tables to verify eligibility of Superpave thin overlays; material information requires confirming a sound pavement, less than 2 % patching, and specifying the appropriate Superpave wearing‑course mix such as PG 64E‑22 binder for 6.3 mm courses; and existing‑condition data must document pavement type, structural soundness, and the percentage of area needing patching. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

For thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays placed over an existing asphalt or composite base, the allowable thickness is limited to 2.5 to 4 inches according to Publication 408 Section 541, and the same range is defined for thin bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavement in Section 541.1 of the 2020 Specifications [1][2]. For bonded concrete overlays over an asphalt‑surfaced pavement, the permitted thickness ranges from 4 to 8 inches as stated in Publication 408 Section 540 and reiterated in Section 540.1 of the 2020 Specifications [1][2]. Unbonded concrete overlays on a concrete‑surfaced pavement must include at least a 1‑inch minimum asphalt separation layer—or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile—per Publication 408 Section 548, and their panel dimensions shall not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness with the longer‑to‑shorter side ratio limited to 1.5 as required by Sections 548.3 and 540.3(i) of the 2020 Specifications [1][2]. When the concrete overlay thickness exceeds six inches, a curing compound must be applied at a minimum rate of one gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 150 ft²; for thicknesses of six inches or less the minimum rate is one gallon per 125 ft², as prescribed in Section 540.3 of the 2020 Specifications [2]. Asphalt overlay thickness is not fixed but shall be calculated to provide the structural strength required by the AASHTO Design Method described in Chapter 10 of the Pavement Policy Manual [1]. A minimum leveling course of one inch may be counted as a one‑inch paving course in the structural calculations, per the Policy Manual [1]. On Interstate facilities, Superpave wearing‑course overlays shall not be placed with a thickness less than 1 ½ inches without prior PDAU approval; on non‑Interstate routes the same minimum applies only when the existing surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than 2 % patching, and both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded, as stipulated in the Policy Manual [1]. For overlays that involve an asphalt paving interlayer system, the overlay thickness must conform to the contract drawings, and the compacted thickness of the first lift shall not be less than 1.5 inches, according to Section 466(i) of the 2020 Specifications [2]. When a total overlay depth reaches three‑and‑a‑half inches or more, an initial saw cut one‑eighth inch wide to a depth of one‑and‑a‑half inches or one‑third of the overlay thickness (whichever is greater) must be made directly above each existing transverse joint within plus or minus one inch, as required by Section 515.3 [2]. All overlay depths must also comply with the placement limits shown in Table 9.5 and with the weather limitations of Publication 408 Section 413.3(b), as indicated in the Policy Manual [1]. The Safety Edge shall be used on wearing and binder courses whose depth is 1 ½ inches or greater but shall not exceed five inches, per Publication 408 Section 413.3(h)1e [1]. Thickness verification for thin bonded concrete overlays requires dividing the overlay into lots not exceeding 3,000 square yards per traffic lane with one random coring location selected in each lot according to PTM No. 1; projects at intersections or where the area is less than 3,000 square yards require two cores or nondestructive thickness measurements per PTM No. 605, and any alternative nondestructive method must be approved in writing by the District Executive, as set out in Section 541.3 [2]. Cores that show a measured depth deficient by more than one‑quarter inch will not be paid for additional coring; any deficiency triggers an adjustment of payment for both the bonded concrete overlay and the cement concrete material as specified in Table A, and if the District Executive authorizes leaving defective pavement in place, final payment for that portion is limited to five percent of the contract unit prices for the overlay and cement concrete, per Section 541.4 [2].

Design methods and criteria

For existing Portland cement concrete pavements a falling‑weight deflectometer test is required and should be conducted before any overlay design; the resulting structural information feeds subsequent calculations. When the overlay material is asphalt, the required thickness is obtained from the procedure in Chapter 10 that is based on the AASHTO Design Method. For concrete overlays thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays are specified to be 2.5 to 4 inches deep and bonded JPCP overlays are specified to be 4 to 8 inches deep according to Publication 408 specifications. Prior to thickness selection a pre‑overlay surface‑preparation sequence is performed, including placement of a leveling course of at least 1 inch to provide a uniform working platform for the binder and/or wearing course. Design tables are consulted for structural coefficient numbers, minimum and maximum placement depths, and scratch and leveling‑course thicknesses as given in Tables 9.3, 9.5, 10.4 and 10.5. Traffic‑volume limits are obtained from Table 10.1 or 10.2. Coring must be performed during the design phase to verify that an adequate bond exists between the existing base and the new material. When overlay thickness is expressed directly rather than as a structural number, the Condition Survey Method is used to calculate the effective existing thickness using STAMPP condition data from the Roadway Management System; inputs are guided by Table 10.9 and the calculation is executed in DARWin software by selecting the Calculate button. For an AC overlay of a jointed plain concrete pavement, the pavement thickness for future traffic is first established, then the effective existing thickness is computed with the Condition Survey Method, and DARWin provides the required asphalt overlay depth. For an AC overlay of an AC/PCC composite pavement, the design begins by determining the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic using Table 10.8, follows with Effective Existing Thickness calculation per Table 10.9, and obtains the asphalt overlay depth from DARWin; equivalent single‑axle load calculations employ the rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors listed in Table 7.1. The design of a bonded PCC overlay proceeds similarly: Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic is calculated in DARWin, Effective Existing Thickness is obtained with the Condition Survey evaluation method within DARWin using the same truck factors, and after inputs are entered the Calculate button yields the required concrete thickness, which is rounded up to the nearest half‑inch and must fall between the minimum and maximum values shown in Table 10.10. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, the residual tensile strength f150 of the macro‑fiber mix is determined by ASTM C1609 testing (normally between 100 and 225 psi) and added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture using the equation feff = MOR + f150; the effective strength is entered into a Residual Strength Estimator spreadsheet, and mechanistic‑empirical design programs such as BCOA‑ME, ACPA Pavement Designer, or AASHTOWare Pavement ME are then used to compute the necessary concrete overlay thickness. In Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfac ed Pavements, an empirical rule requires that maximum panel dimensions shall not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness; joint layout plans must satisfy this limit and FHWA’s HIPERPAV or similar software approved by the Department may be used to determine a project‑specific sawing window. In Section 540—Bonded Concrete Overlay of Asphalt‑Surfac ed Pavement, the same eighteen‑times rule applies to joint layout and additionally the ratio of the longer to shorter panel dimension shall not exceed 1.5; FHWA’s HIPERPAV or comparable approved software may again be used for the sawing window, and removal of insulation blankets during curing may be controlled by the maturity method applying degree‑hour criteria as specified in Section 540.3(q). In Section 466—Asphalt Paving Interlayer System, overlay thickness is controlled by procedural checks rather than analytical formulas; the contractor must verify that the asphalt material temperature meets the minimum specified by the manufacturer’s technical representative and documented in the field QC plan, must ensure that all areas where an interlayer system has been placed are paved on the same day, must confirm that the overlay thickness complies with the contract drawings, and the first lift of the asphalt overlay shall have a compacted thickness not less than 1.5 inches wherever an interlayer system is installed. In Section 515—Sawing and Sealing of Asphalt Overlays, a QC plan must be prepared and approved before any joint referencing, each transverse joint shall be re‑located within ±1 inch with saw cuts placed within the same tolerance after the asphalt has cooled below 140 °F, and when the total depth of overlay is 3 ½ inches or greater an initial saw‑cut 1/8 inch wide shall be made to a depth of either 1 ½ inches or one‑third of the total thickness, whichever is greater; if the wearing course will not be placed in the same season a similar cut of at least 1 inch or one‑third of that layer’s thickness must be provided. In Section 541—Thin Bonded Concrete Overlay of Asphalt‑Surfac ed Pavement, verification of overlay thickness is performed by dividing the concrete overlay pavement into lots no larger than 3,000 square yards per traffic lane (ramps treated as separate lots); within each lot the inspector randomly selects one coring location according to PTM No. 1 and cores are drilled in accordance with PTM No. 606 while the inspector is present; at intersections or when a lot is smaller than 3,000 square yards two cores are taken or nondestructive thickness measurements are performed per PTM No. 605, after which the vertical surface of each hole is cleaned and the void filled with the same mixture used for the overlay or with a premixed non‑shrink grout. [1][2]

Overlay thickness is selected based on the condition of the existing pavement and the structural capacity needed. Thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays over an asphalt or composite pavement are 2.5 to 4 inches thick, whereas bonded JPCP overlays over the same bases are designed at 4 to 8 inches thick. When the overlay material is asphalt concrete, its thickness shall be the amount of additional pavement required to achieve the structural strength prescribed by the AASHTO Design Method in Chapter 10, and a falling‑weight deflectometer test should be performed on the existing PCCP before the overlay design. Under the overlay specifications, a leveling course shall be at least 1 inch thick and may be counted as a 1‑inch paving layer in structural calculations; thin asphalt overlays using a Superpave Wearing Course shall not be placed thinner than 1½ inches on Interstates without PDAU approval, while on non‑Interstate routes the thickness may be less than 1½ inches only if the existing surface is asphalt, structurally sound with less than 2 % patching, and both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded. The Safety Edge shall be provided with a depth of 1½ inches or greater but not exceeding 5 inches total. For fiber‑reinforced concrete pavement overlays the design thickness is normally limited to a range of 3 to 6 inches, and the required residual strength (f150) must fall between 100 and 225 psi; this residual strength is added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength that is then entered into mechanistic‑empirical design programs. In designing an AC overlay of an AC/PCC composite pavement, the first step shall be to determine the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic as guided by Table 10.8, after which the Effective Existing Thickness must be calculated using the Condition Survey Method; the resulting thickness is obtained from DARWin as the actual asphalt overlay required, and ESAL calculations must employ the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors listed in Table 7.1. When designing a bonded PCC overlay of PCC pavement, the thickness shall be obtained by first calculating the future‑traffic pavement thickness in DARWin and the effective existing thickness using the condition‑survey evaluation method, then computing the required concrete thickness; that thickness must be rounded up to the nearest half‑inch and must fall within the minimum and maximum overlay depths listed in Table 10.10. For unbonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements, panel dimensions are limited so that, in general, the maximum dimension does not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness; the same eighteen‑times‑thickness limit applies to bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements, and the ratio of the longer to the shorter panel side shall not exceed 1.5. Joint spacing shall follow the distances shown on the project plans, and where no joint spacing is indicated a joint‑layout plan that satisfies these dimensional limits must be prepared and accepted by the Department before placement; longitudinal joints are to be avoided in wheel paths. Curing compound must be applied at a minimum rate of one gallon per 150 ft² for overlays thicker than six inches and at least one gallon per 125 ft² for overlays six inches thick or less, or according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, whichever is greater. When an asphalt paving interlayer system is used, the first lift of compacted asphalt overlay shall not be less than 1.5 inches, and geotextile fabric or composite reinforcing grid may not be installed in locations where the overlaid asphalt tapers to a compacted thickness of less than 1.5 inches. If the total depth of an asphalt overlay is three‑½ inches or greater, an initial saw‑cut 1/8 inch wide shall be made to a depth equal to the greater of one‑and‑a‑half inches or one‑third of the total overlay thickness; all saw‑cuts shall be placed directly above existing transverse joints within ± 1 inch. The indicated overlay depths do not include scratch or leveling courses less than one inch. Thin bonded concrete overlays are verified by cores, and a deficiency greater than one‑quarter inch triggers a payment adjustment; the Department will not pay for additional cores if the depth is deficient by more than one‑quarter inch. Structural section and dimensions

For thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays placed over existing asphalt or composite pavements, the design calls for a depth of 2.5 to 4 inches [1]. Standard bonded JPCP overlays over the same types of existing pavements require a depth of 4 to 8 inches [1]. When an unbonded concrete overlay is used, the underlying pavement must be separated by at least a 1‑inch fine‑graded asphalt layer or an equivalent geotextile before placing the new concrete [1]. The panel dimensions for both bonded and unbonded concrete overlays are limited so that the longest dimension of any panel shall not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the aspect ratio of any panel shall not exceed 1.5 [2]. Saw cuts defining the overlay joints must be at least D/3 deep within twelve inches of the pavement edges and at least D/4 deep elsewhere, where D is the overlay thickness [2]. Thin asphalt overlays on both Interstate and non‑Interstate facilities must not be placed with a thickness of less than 1½ inches unless prior approval or listed conditions are satisfied [1]. When milling an existing asphalt layer over concrete or brick, the remaining asphalt should retain a minimum of 1½ inches to preserve structural integrity [1]. The first lift of an asphalt overlay over any interlayer system must have a compacted thickness not less than 1.5 inches [2]. Geotextile or composite reinforcing grids may not be installed where the overlaid asphalt tapers to a minimum compacted thickness of less than 1.5 inches [2]. If a fiberglass mesh grid is placed on a leveling course, that course must be at least two times the Nominal Maximum Aggregate Size (NMAS) [2]. The Safety Edge edge‑support treatment shall be provided on pavement edges and shoulders with a depth of 1½ inches or greater but the total depth shall not exceed 5 inches [1]. No explicit dimensions are given for base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab, or reinforcement layers in either source [1][2].

In selecting overlay thickness for concrete overlays, the Pavement Policy Manual specifies that thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete (JPCP) overlays placed over existing asphalt or composite pavements are limited to 2.5 to 4 inches in depth, whereas bonded JPCP overlays for the same bases are limited to 4 to 8 inches [1]. When an unbonded overlay is required, the manual requires a separation layer of at least a 1‑inch fine‑graded asphalt or a non‑woven geotextile to be placed beneath the concrete overlay [1]. Joint spacing for these concrete overlays follows the same criteria as new concrete pavements and depends on the type and thickness of the concrete being constructed, without further numeric limits provided in this section [1]. For asphalt treatments, the manual directs that a separate leveling (or scratch) course of at least 1 inch be provided first to correct the profile or re‑establish the cross‑section, and this course must remain distinct from any binder or wearing course so that quality control is preserved [1]. A Superpave Wearing Course overlay then must be placed with a thickness of not less than 1 ½ inches unless prior approval is obtained, and thin asphalt overlays are subject to the same minimum depth limits [1]. The cross slope for the pavement is established by the tonnage item associated with the leveling course, while the wearing and/or binder courses are placed at a uniform depth [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications add that overlay thickness design incorporates joint layout and panel sizing rules in which the aspect ratio (longer dimension divided by shorter dimension) of any panel should be as close to 1.0 as feasible and should not exceed 1.5, and the longer dimension of any panel must be no more than 18 times the concrete overlay thickness [2]. Joints are to be spaced according to the project plans; if the plans do not indicate joint spacing, a department‑approved joint layout is developed before concrete placement, with longitudinal joints avoided in wheel paths [2]. Transitions are constructed at the limits of the concrete overlay as shown on the plans, and where a transition overlies an existing treated base course or concrete pavement, a geotextile debonding layer is installed beneath the overlay [2].

Materials and mixture design

Overlay Thickness specifications must first identify whether the new layer will be a Portland cement concrete (rigid) or an asphalt concrete (flexible) system. For flexible overlays the specification shall name the exact Superpave asphalt mix to be placed, and acceptable mixes include 25.0 mm, 19.0 mm, 12.5 mm, 9.5 mm, and the 6.3 mm thin overlay; the required binder grade for standard 6.3 mm wearing courses shall be PG 64E‑22, which shall not be used for a 9.5 mm Fine Grade. For rigid overlays that employ fiber‑reinforced concrete the mix shall provide a residual strength (f150) within the range of 100 to 225 psi and shall contain macrofibers at a volume fraction normally required between 0.2 % and 0.5 %. When the overlay is bonded, the thickness is typically limited to a range of 3 to 6 inches. In Section 548 an unbonded concrete overlay shall be accompanied by a definition of any interlayer material; if an asphalt interlayer is used the contract shall require a nominal one‑inch depth of a Superpave mix design as referenced in Section 410.3, and if a geotextile interlayer is employed all surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be filled with an approved patching material that may be asphalt, cementitious, or a similar pavement repair material. The curing‑compound application rate shall be tied to the overlay thickness, requiring at least one gallon per 150 square feet for overlays thicker than six inches and one gallon per 125 square feet for overlays six inches or less, or the greater of these rates and the manufacturer’s recommendation. Overlay Thickness specifications must identify the reinforcement type and prescribe the corresponding asphalt material application rate: for a geotextile paving fabric the rate shall be between 0.22 and 0.28 gallons per square yard; for a fiberglass mesh grid the rate shall be between 0.02 and 0.05 gallons per square yard; and for a composite reinforcing grid the rate shall be between 0.19 and 0.28 gallons per square yard, each applied over a width equal to the reinforcement plus six inches on each side. The asphalt binder temperature must be kept at or above 290 °F but not exceed 320 °F during placement, and the first lift of the overlay shall achieve a minimum compacted thickness of 1.5 inches wherever an interlayer system is used. For a bonded concrete overlay the specification shall call for a Portland cement concrete layer whose thickness shall be between four and eight inches. A thin bonded concrete overlay over an asphalt‑surfaced pavement shall require a Portland cement concrete overlay whose thickness shall be between 2.5 and 4 inches and shall be bonded to the existing asphalt surface. Overlay thickness is first determined by evaluating the existing pavement condition and the intended performance [1]. For concrete pavements a thin‑bonded jointed plain cementitious pavement (JPCP) overlay of 2.5 to 4 inches is recommended when only surface deficiencies or rutting need correction, while a bonded JPCP overlay of 4 to 8 inches is used to add structural capacity and reduce maintenance cycles [1]. Unbonded concrete overlays require at least a 1‑inch fine‑graded asphalt separation layer (or a non‑woven geotextile) to act as the platform [1]. For flexible overlays the required thickness equals the amount of additional pavement needed to provide the required structural strength as determined by the AASHTO Design Method, and the overlay may be placed over either unbonded or bonded concrete depending on the condition of the existing slab [1]. When an asphalt overlay is designed, the engineer consults the structural tables (9.3, 9.5, 10.4, 10.5) to obtain the required structural coefficient and the allowable minimum and maximum placement depths for the selected Superpave mix; a leveling course, when used, shall be at least 1‑inch thick and may be counted as a structural layer [1]. Thin functional overlays such as the 6.3 mm fine‑grade wearing course are limited to existing asphalt surfaces that are structurally sound, have less than 2% of the pavement requiring patching, and possess good surface and subsurface drainage; the appropriate binder PG 64E‑22 is specified for the 6.3 mm wearing course [1]. Any overlay on an Interstate thinner than 1½ inches requires prior PDAU approval, while non‑Interstate thin overlays must meet the same drainage and soundness conditions [1]. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays the residual flexural strength (f150) required for the project is first determined, after which a macrofiber type and dosage ranging from 0.2% to 0.5% by volume are selected to achieve that strength; the resulting effective flexural strength is entered into mechanistic‑empirical design programs to size a thinner slab often in the 3 to 6 in range for higher traffic or variable support conditions [1]. Because adding fibers can lower slump by up to 4 in, water‑reducing admixtures and air‑entraining adjustments are used in trial batches to meet fresh‑property specifications, thereby ensuring constructability while preserving durability through reduced crack widths and maintained joint load transfer [1]. When an overlay includes an asphalt paving interlayer system, the material mix and proportion are driven by the type of reinforcement specified; a geotextile fabric requires saturating application of 0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard with a target width extending six inches beyond each side of the fabric [2]. A fiberglass mesh grid uses 0.02 to 0.05 gallons per square yard, and a composite reinforcing grid uses 0.19 to 0.28 gallons per square yard, all as approved by the manufacturer’s technical representative [2]. The asphalt binder temperature must be at least 290 °F and must not exceed 320 °F (or between 40 °F and 140 °F for fiberglass mesh placement) to achieve a uniform spray pattern and proper bonding, which supports structural integrity and durability [2]. Overlay thickness must meet the requirements of the contract drawings, and the minimum compacted thickness of the first lift shall not be less than 1.5 inches in areas where an interlayer system is installed, ensuring constructability and performance requirements are satisfied [2].

Structural details and interfaces

In an unbonded concrete overlay the transverse and longitudinal joints are cut as they would be for a new concrete pavement and need not line up with any existing joints, the spacing being governed by the overlay type and thickness [1]. A fine‑graded asphalt separation layer at least 1 inch thick, or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile placed flat on the repaired surface overlapping edges by 8 inches ± 2 inches and extending at least 4 inches beyond the pavement edge (no more than three layers), satisfies the unbonded condition [1][2]. When joint faulting exceeds ½ inch, an asphalt interlayer of nominal 1‑inch depth may be used in addition to the geotextile [2]. The overlay may include a separate leveling course of at least 1 inch thickness to provide a uniform working platform, and this layer can be counted as a 1‑inch paving layer in the structural calculations [1]. For unbonded concrete overlays anchored dowel bar assemblies are required, with their baskets fastened to the substrate so that movement does not exceed ¼ inch in any direction [2]. The saw cuts for transverse and longitudinal joints must be no less than one‑third of the slab thickness (D/3) within 12 inches of the edges and at least one‑fourth of the thickness (D/4) elsewhere, with joint spacing limited so that panel dimensions do not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the length‑to‑width ratio does not exceed 1.5; all joints are to be sealed after cleaning [2]. Bonded concrete overlays are built with smaller slab dimensions and follow standard joint practices without a requirement to align with existing joints [1]. The specification further requires that joint spacing follow the plan, or an approved layout if none is shown, and that panel dimensions not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness nor have a length‑to‑width ratio greater than 1.5; saw cuts are made to a depth of D/3 and at least ¼ inch wide, with early‑entry sawing permitted when concrete hardness allows, and cuts may be omitted if an existing crack lies within 1.5 feet of the intended joint location [2]. Reinforcement for overlays can be provided by installing an asphalt paving interlayer such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grid, or composite reinforcing grid that must be compatible with the mix and thermally stable; each reinforcement material shall extend six inches beyond its edges on both sides when the tack coat is applied, and it may not be placed where the overlaid asphalt tapers to a compacted thickness less than 1.5 inches [1][2]. Macro‑fibers incorporated in fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays act as internal reinforcement and can maintain load‑transfer efficiency across saw‑cut contraction joints similarly to tie bars, although they are not considered a substitute for dowel bars where faulting control is required [1]. In asphalt overlay applications the contractor must saw and seal the overlay directly over all underlying transverse joints and any patch joints; each end of every existing transverse joint shall be located and referenced before placement, cuts are made directly above the joints within ± 1 inch, with an initial cut width limited to ¼ inch and a depth equal to either 1½ inches or one‑third of the total overlay thickness whichever is greater when the overlay depth is 3½ inches or more; sealing must be completed within seven days using clean dry reservoir faces, a tape bondbreaker not extending more than ¼ inch up the face, and filling the reservoir to a level ⅛‑¼ inch below the pavement surface, provided air temperature is between 40 °F and 90 °F unless otherwise authorized [2]. All joints in an asphalt paving interlayer system must be overlapped by at least 1 inch of the interlayer material, with end joints oriented in the direction of overlay placement; any wrinkles larger than 1 inch are to be sliced and flattened before compaction [2]. Where a transition occurs at the limits of a concrete overlay and existing treated base or concrete lies directly beneath, a geotextile debonding layer must be placed as part of the transition construction [2]; transitions are also constructed at overlay limits for both bonded and unbonded overlays as indicated in the plans [1][2]. For asphalt pavements a Safety Edge shall be provided on the outside edge of the pavement and shoulders, with a depth of at least 1½ inches and not exceeding 5 inches [1].

In rehabilitation design the interface between an existing pavement and a new overlay is treated according to whether the overlay is bonded or unbonded, with bonded overlays requiring a continuous bond so that the two layers act as one structure and joints placed as for new concrete pavements rather than being forced to line up with underlying transverse joints, while unbonded overlays deliberately separate the old surface from the new layer by a fine‑graded minimum 1‑inch asphalt separation layer or a non‑woven geotextile interlayer so that no bond is required and joint spacing follows the criteria for the type and thickness of concrete being placed [1]. For an unbonded concrete overlay the existing surface must first be repaired by fixing unstable slabs and patching shallow defects larger than 4 sq inches and deeper than 1 inch with approved material, after which all debris is swept away; a separation layer may then be provided either as a geotextile interlayer placed flat on the repaired surface with overlaps of 8 inches ± 2 inches, limited to three layers in any location, extended at least 4 inches beyond the pavement edge, kept clean and dampened but not saturated and installed when the surface temperature does not exceed 120°F, or as an asphalt interlayer of nominal 1‑inch depth placed while the surface temperature is also ≤120°F [2]. Joint layout for concrete overlays follows a panel size rule that limits maximum panel dimensions to no more than 18 times the overlay thickness and requires an aspect ratio not to exceed 1.5; saw cuts are made at least one‑third of slab depth within 12 inches of edges and at least one‑fourth of slab depth anywhere, with a minimum cut width of ¼ inch [2]. When a bonded concrete overlay is placed over an asphalt‑surfaced pavement the interface is prepared by milling to the plan‑specified depth, cleaning all debris, filling cracks larger than 3/4 inch with approved non‑shrink grout, and ensuring the milled surface is dry and below 120°F before placement; joints are then cut with a green‑cut saw to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness and at least 1/8 inch wide while avoiding longitudinal joints in wheel paths, and transitions are constructed at the overlay limits with a geotextile debonding layer placed where underlying treated base or concrete exists [2]. For bonded overlays the existing pavement becomes an integral part of the structural design and a leveling or scratch course at least 1‑inch thick is required to provide a uniform working platform and correct profile before the binder or wearing course is placed, this course may be counted as a 1‑inch paving layer in structural calculations [1]. When an asphalt overlay is placed over concrete or brick base the design calls for retaining a minimum of 1½ inches of the existing asphalt to preserve structural integrity and ensure adequate bond, and any debonded areas identified by coring are addressed with surface‑replacement items; additionally the overlay should be sawed and sealed directly over all underlying transverse joints [1]. If reflective cracking or moisture intrusion is a concern an asphalt paving interlayer may be installed between the existing surface and the new overlay, the interlayer being placed on a dry, clean pavement free of dirt, gravel, vegetation and oil after cracks are sealed and potholes repaired; a tack coat of asphalt material is applied uniformly at 0.22 to 0.28 gal/yd² for geotextile fabric with a target width equal to the fabric width plus six inches on each side, the asphalt material temperature must be maintained between a minimum of 290°F and a maximum of 320°F (or between 40°F and 140°F for fiberglass mesh grid) so that the surface remains tacky when the interlayer is laid, all joints and overlaps must have at least a one‑inch overlap of asphalt material, wrinkles larger than one inch are trimmed and overlapped flat, and the first lift of the overlay shall achieve a minimum compacted thickness of 1.5 inches wherever an interlayer system is installed [2]. For AC over AC/PCC composite pavements the interface is evaluated by coring any existing asphalt overlay; if material deficiencies exist the old overlay must be completely removed before the new asphalt layer is placed, otherwise the design proceeds by determining the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic and then calculating the Effective Existing Thickness with the Condition Survey Method, with the DARWin output providing the actual thickness of the asphalt overlay required [1]. In a bonded PCC overlay the interface is designed as a direct bond so that the new concrete layer and existing pavement act as a single monolithic slab, a condition that is limited to situations where the underlying concrete does not exhibit extensive cracking or faulting; the required overlay thickness is calculated in DARWin, rounded up to the nearest half‑inch and checked against the minimum and maximum depths specified for the project [1]. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays the decision to bond or leave unbonded is driven by the condition of the existing asphalt surface, with fair‑to‑good pavements eligible for a bonded FRC overlay and poor, deteriorated surfaces calling for an unbonded design; cores are taken to verify that stripping is not prevalent before bonding to a milled surface, typical bonded overlay thickness ranges from 3 to 6 inches, macrofibers are limited to 0.2 %–0.5 % by volume to achieve residual strengths of 100‑225 psi, and proper timing and depth of sawcut contraction joints are required so that the FRC joints become active early and prevent premature cracking [1].

Verification and expected performance

After the DARWin calculations provide a required concrete thickness for the bonded PCC overlay, the designer rounds that value up to the nearest half‑inch and then verifies that the resulting overlay depth falls between the minimum and maximum limits listed in Table 10.10; this check confirms compliance with the design specifications and ensures the overlay meets the project’s dimensional requirements. For sections that include an asphalt paving interlayer system the overlay thickness is measured after placement and compared against the contract drawing specifications, the first lift must have a minimum compacted thickness of not less than 1.5 inches, and this check must be completed on the same day the interlayer was installed to verify constructability and performance and to ensure the overlay meets all project requirements. Compliance for thin bonded concrete overlays is verified by coring the pavement and measuring its thickness with the apparatus supplied under the contract. The pavement is divided into lots no larger than 3,000 square yards per traffic lane (ramps are treated as separate lots) and the Inspector randomly selects a coring location in each lot according to PTM No. 1; cores are drilled following PTM No. 606 while the Inspector is present. For intersections or areas under 3,000 square yards two cores or nondestructive thickness measurements per PTM No. 605 are required, and any alternative nondestructive method must have written approval from the District Executive. After each core is removed the vertical surface of the hole must be thoroughly cleaned of laitance and loose material, then the void is filled with the same mixture used to place the overlay and consolidated, or a premixed non‑shrink grout may be used. If the measured depth is deficient by more than 1/4 inch the Department will not pay for additional cores and lot payment for both the bonded concrete overlay and the cement concrete material will be adjusted as specified in Table A; when the District Executive approves leaving defective pavement in place final payment for this pavement is limited to five percent of the contract unit prices of the concrete overlay and cement concrete. For a 6.3 mm thin asphalt overlay, the guidelines state that this thickness is expected to provide a longer service life and to improve ride quality relative to emulsified‑asphalt alternatives; an accompanying asphalt paving interlayer is intended to retard reflective cracking and thereby extend pavement life, although no specific measurement procedure for these outcomes is prescribed [1]. In the case of a thin bonded concrete overlay applied over an asphalt‑surfaced pavement, performance is judged primarily by achieving the specified thickness, with compliance verified through drilling concrete pavement cores; any core that shows a depth deficiency greater than 1/4 inch triggers a payment adjustment as defined in Table A and the Department retains the measuring apparatus and will not fund additional cores when such a deficiency occurs [2]. Ride quality of the completed concrete overlay is also evaluated, with incentives linked to the assessment described in Section 507.4 [2].
