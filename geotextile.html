Geotextile

Part of: Pavement Structure is-subtopic-of
Geotextile is a topic under Pavement Structure.
geotextilegeotextilesgeotextile-properties
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Geotextile is a synthetic, permeable fabric made from long‑chain polymeric filaments such as polyethylene, polypropylene, polyamide or polyester that are arranged into a stable, needle‑punched non‑woven network containing at least 85 % by weight of these polymers and heat‑set on one side to resist asphalt bleed‑through and chemical attack [1][2]. In pavement construction the material functions as a mechanical separator and filter, isolating compacted subgrade from overlying aggregate or base while allowing water to drain through its open structure, and, when specified as a Class 4 Type C product, it also provides lateral reinforcement that reduces load stresses on weak soils [1][2]. Its performance is defined by high tensile strength (minimum 560 lb/in), limited elongation, a melting point above 420 °F, and strict handling requirements that keep the fabric dry, flat and protected from temperatures exceeding 140 °F until it is covered by backfill or overlay [1][2].

Material purpose and applicability

Geotextile is a synthetic permeable fabric composed of long‑chain polymeric filaments or yarns that are bonded into a stable network, most often supplied as a non‑woven, needle‑punched material, which allows the fabric to retain its shape while permitting water to pass through [1][2]. In pavement construction the geotextile is placed as a continuous sheet over a prepared subgrade—or over a hydrocarbon‑resistant geomembrane—by rolling it out flat and overlapping adjacent sheets at least twelve inches for general coverage or eight inches ± two inches when used as an interlayer on repaired pavement, with seams sealed using the manufacturer’s recommended seaming adhesive and care taken to keep the material free of wrinkles until it is covered by aggregate, sub‑base or overlay [2]. The primary function of the geotextile in the pavement system is layer separation, isolating compacted soil from overlying aggregate, base or concrete so that each layer retains its integrity and preventing intermixing while still allowing water to drain through the fabric [1][2]. When installed around prefabricated pavement base drains or foundation pipe bedding, the geotextile also provides filtration, permitting water to reach the drain while retaining fine particles and preserving drainage capacity [2]. If the geotextile is saturated with tack coat at a rate of 0.22–0.28 gal/yd² and laid on still‑tacky asphalt of at least one‑and‑a‑half inches thickness, it reinforces the overlay and ensures bond between the fabric and the pavement surface [2]. In addition to separation and filtration, geotextiles may serve a stabilization function by providing lateral restraint along the base‑subgrade interface, which reduces load stresses transmitted to the subgrade; this dual role is classified as Class 4—Stabilization (Type C) in certain specifications [1]. The guides also recognize ancillary benefits such as erosion and sediment control on construction sites, protection of underlying membranes, and acting as a debonding layer at concrete‑overlay transition zones where an existing treated base or slab lies directly beneath new concrete [1][2].

Geotextiles are appropriate when a permeable fabric is needed to separate the subgrade from the subbase across the full lane width, especially in weak soils where the subgrade CBR is less than three; in those cases a Class 4‑Stabilization (Type C) geotextile provides both separation and lateral reinforcement, while a Class 4‑Layer Separation (Type A) fabric is used for general layer separation on all completed subgrades. When the subgrade has higher strength (CBR ≥ 3) or when other functional requirements such as load‑bearing capacity are met without reinforcement, a granular base or alternative material may be selected instead of geotextile. [1]

Composition and characteristics

Geotextiles are permeable fabric systems composed of long‑chain polymeric filaments such as polyethylene, polyamide, polyvinylidene‑chloride, polypropylene or polyester that are formed into a stable network where each strand retains its relative position to the others. [1][2] The fabrics may be produced either as woven or as non‑woven needle‑punched sheets; the latter is required for pavement applications and must consist of at least 85 % by weight of polyolefins, polyesters or polyamides derived from virgin resin unless an approved alternative is documented. [2] This network can be created by weaving filaments into a fabric or by needle‑punching the fibers to interlock them, resulting in a durable, tear‑free sheet that provides characteristic permeability and mechanical integrity. [1][2] The material is classified according to functional classes; for pavement work Class 4‑Layer Separation (Type A) specifies separation of dissimilar soils such as subgrade and subbase, while Class 4‑Stabilization adds lateral restraint and reinforcement functions. [1] For the most common pavement use, Class 1 fabrics are specified, and slit‑film woven constructions are permitted only for Class 3; chemically or thermally bonded fabrics are prohibited for any class. [2] The geotextile must be chemically inert to construction chemicals, resistant to rot, mildew and other chemical attack, and protected from direct sunlight and temperatures above 140 °F during handling, shipment and storage. [2] One side of the sheet is heat‑set to prevent asphalt bleed‑through and to reduce pickup by equipment during placement, and seams are typically positioned longitudinally on the shoulder side when the fabric wraps an inert plastic core for drainage functions. [2]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 317), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 518–521), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 716)

The performance of a geotextile hinges on its makeup and manufacturing steps. Because the fabric must be a stable, needle‑punched, nonwoven material containing at least 85 % by weight of polyolefins, polyesters or polyamides, any change in polymer type or proportion can affect its resistance to chemical attack, rot, mildew and its overall tensile behavior. The requirement for no tears or defects makes processing critical; needle‑punching provides stability while heat‑setting one side limits asphalt bleed‑through and reduces fabric pick‑up during installation. Thus variations in composition, source material quality, or processing parameters directly influence durability, chemical stability, and interaction with the pavement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 317)

Key material properties

The overall ability of a geotextile to separate subgrade and subbase layers, filter water, and provide lateral reinforcement is governed by an interrelated set of physical, mechanical, chemical and thermal properties identified across the guides. [1][2] Physically the fabric must be permeable yet remain dry or only slightly dampened so that a tack coat can wet it without becoming saturated, and it should be installed on a well‑drained surface to prevent water entrapment. [2] The material itself is required to consist of long‑chain polymeric filaments such as polyethylene, polyamide, PVC, polypropylene or polyester that are formed into a stable woven or needle‑punched nonwoven network, with at least eighty‑five percent of the weight contributed by polyolefins, polyesters or polyamides, thereby ensuring that the filaments retain their relative positions under load. [1][2] Mechanically the geotextile must exhibit a minimum tensile strength of five hundred sixty pounds per inch in each principal direction with elongation limited to roughly three percent, providing resistance to tearing and puncture throughout manufacture, transport, storage and installation. [2] It must retain its integrity when handled or subjected to construction traffic, be kept flat, protected from folding, tearing or puncturing, and any damaged section is required to be removed and replaced. [2] Overlap seams for repair or joining are limited to a maximum of three layers in any location, with each overlap extending at least twelve inches in all directions and secured using a manufacturer‑approved spray adhesive that does not bond the fabric to adjacent geomembranes. [2] Chemically the geotextile must be inert to the range of construction chemicals it may encounter, resistant to chemical attack, rot and mildew, and chemically‑ or thermally‑bonded fabrics are prohibited because they compromise durability; one side is often heat‑set to reduce asphalt bleed‑through and equipment pick‑up. [2] Thermally the fabric’s surface temperature must remain below one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit before overlay placement and generally below about one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit during handling, storage and exposure to sunlight, while its melting point must exceed four hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit to avoid degradation at installation temperatures. [2] Additionally, asphalt placed over the geotextile may not exceed three hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit, and protection from direct ultraviolet radiation, precipitation, mud, dust, debris and sparks is required throughout shipment, storage and installation to preserve its functional properties. [2][1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 182–204), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 317–319), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 716)

Designers determine the relevant geotextile properties for a pavement project on a case‑by‑case basis by consulting FHWA Publication 408, especially Sections 212 and 735, together with data from the National Transportation Product Evaluation Program and the manufacturer’s published product information [1]. Pavement specifications classify geotextiles through a class‑and‑type system that assigns a numeric functional class and an alphabetic type, linking the intended role to a performance level [2]. Functional classes include Class 1 for subsurface drainage, Class 3 for sedimentation control, and Class 4 for layer separation, erosion control, stabilization or GRS abutment reinforcement, each further distinguished by fabric types A, B, or C [2]. In the pavement context geotextiles are designated as Class 4 fabrics and may be specified either as a Type A layer‑separation material or as a Type C stabilization material, consistent with the classification criteria set out in Publication 408 and related bulletins [1][2]. The class‑type designation appears on every roll together with the manufacturer’s model, plant and lot numbers, providing a clear label of performance level and functional role [2]. The required quantity of geotextile is expressed as installed area measured in square yards to cover separation layers and end‑wrapping, rather than by field measurements of material properties [2]. Acceptance of the geotextile relies on documented compliance with the prescribed class/type; contractors submit certified test data, manufacturer data sheets and identification samples that are reviewed against the physical requirements listed for each construction class and type [2][1]. Tensile strength of the constituent strands is verified according to ASTM D6637, requiring a minimum component strength of 560 lb/in (or 460 lb/in for composite fabrics) and an elongation not exceeding 3 % (5 % for composites) [2]. Melting point is determined by ASTM D276 with minima of 420 °F for mesh‑grid fabrics and 320 °F for composites [2]. Mass per unit area is checked by ASTM D5261, demanding at least 10 oz per square yard [2]. The fabric must contain a minimum of 85 % polyolefins, polyesters or polyamides, be needle‑punched, heat‑set on one side, and exhibit resistance to chemical attack, rot and mildew with no tears that could alter its properties [2]. For mesh‑grid fabrics an aperture size of at least 0.5 in by 0.5 in is required, linking pore dimensions to the filtration function [2]. Only slit‑film woven fabrics are permitted for Class 3 geotextiles, reinforcing the connection between fabric structure and classification [2].

Behavior and mechanisms

The geotextile functions as a permeable fabric that must retain its structural integrity throughout construction and service. Under permanent loads it is required to keep its shape so that the drainage capacity of any underlying core is not reduced; testing or spot‑bonding procedures are called for to verify that continuous loading does not cause deformation sufficient to diminish flow. Live loads from equipment are not tolerated until the fabric is covered by at least six inches of backfill, and construction traffic, sharp turns, or sudden braking must be avoided to prevent tearing or folding.

Moisture control is equally critical. The material should be dampened before placement to aid handling, but it must never become saturated; excess water impairs its separating and reinforcing functions. During storage and installation the fabric is kept dry, wrapped, and elevated off the ground, and wet pavement is expressly prohibited because water can compromise bonding with the asphalt binder.

Temperature limits are specified at several stages. The surface temperature of the geotextile may not exceed 120 °F prior to overlay placement, and exposure to temperatures above 140 °F or direct sunlight must be avoided, as heat degrades the polymer matrix. In hot‑mix paving operations the adjacent asphalt temperature is limited to 320 °F, a threshold that protects the interlayer system; one side of the fabric is heat‑set to withstand the elevated temperatures and to reduce asphalt bleed‑through during placement.

Chemical resistance is built into the material composition. The non‑woven, needle‑punched fabric—comprising at least 85 % polyolefins, polyesters or polyamides from virgin resin—is required to resist chemical attack, rot and mildew, ensuring that exposure to aggressive chemicals does not deteriorate its performance. Nevertheless, during handling the geotextile is stored protected from chemicals, precipitation, UV radiation and sparks.

Other service conditions emphasize protection against physical damage. Defective, torn or punctured sections must be removed and repaired by overlapping a minimum of twelve inches in all directions and sealing with a manufacturer‑approved seaming adhesive; the fabric must not be bonded to an underlying geomembrane. Throughout construction it is kept flat, unwrinkled, and free from debris, with storage elevated and covered to shield it from weather, UV light and mechanical injury. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 182–204), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 317–319), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 518–521)

Geotextiles function in pavement by providing a permeable fabric network that separates the subgrade from the subbase while allowing water to pass, thereby preserving the integrity of each layer [1][2]. The separation function is achieved because the fabric consists of long‑chain polymeric filaments or yarns that are arranged into a stable network, keeping the individual strands in fixed relative positions [1][2]. Physically, the material must retain its structural integrity during handling, storage, and placement; any tears, punctures or other defects are rejected and damaged sections are removed, with repairs performed by overlapping at least twelve inches beyond the defect and securing the lap with an approved spray adhesive [2]. To preserve its low water absorption—measured at about 0.01 % after 24 h—and to maintain filtration capability, the geotextile is dampened but not saturated before overlay placement, and its non‑woven needle‑punched structure provides the required permeability while resisting fungal growth [2]. Chemically, the fabric is composed primarily of long‑chain polymeric filaments such as polyethylene, polypropylene, polyamide, polyester or PVC, which together constitute at least 85 % of the material by weight and render the geotextile inert to common construction chemicals, resistant to rot, mildew and chemical attack [2]. The polymeric composition also gives the fabric a high melting point exceeding 420 °F and a heat‑set surface that reduces asphalt bleed‑through and minimizes pick‑up by equipment during hot‑mix placement [2]. Mechanically, the geotextile must meet minimum tensile strength requirements of 1 200 psi (ASTM D638) or a component strand strength of 560 lb/in in each direction, with only limited elongation, ensuring that it can sustain ground pressures from compaction equipment ranging between 625 and 1 025 lb/ft² without crushing the fibers [2]. The fabric provides lateral restraint at the base‑subgrade interface, reducing load stresses transmitted to the subgrade and thereby reinforcing the pavement structure [1]. During installation the material is laid flat, pulled tight, anchored, graded without wrinkling, and rolled with brooming or rubber‑tire rollers to achieve uniform contact pressure while avoiding direct roller loads that could crush the fibers [2]. Storage considerations such as keeping the geotextile away from flood‑prone areas, protecting it from direct sunlight, ultraviolet radiation and temperatures above 140 °F further preserve its physical and chemical integrity until placement [2]. The combined physical separation and filtration, chemical inertness and thermal stability, and mechanical strength and restraint together control the behavior of geotextiles in pavement applications [1][2].

Durability and deterioration

The long‑term performance of geotextile fabrics in pavement systems is compromised by a combination of mechanical injury and adverse environmental exposure that can occur at any stage from manufacture through service life. Physical damage may be introduced during production, shipment, storage, or on‑site handling when the material is dragged across the ground, folded, torn, punctured by sharp objects, rocks, or aggregate dropped from haul trucks, or subjected to abrupt turns of tracked equipment that pull and tension the sheet. Construction traffic and heavy machinery placed directly on an exposed fabric also generate tears, shear failures, and abrasion, especially when the backfill has not yet provided a minimum six‑inch compacted cover. Once installed, the geotextile must be protected from prolonged exposure to weather; if left uncovered for more than two weeks it is vulnerable to ultraviolet radiation, precipitation, and temperature extremes that degrade the polymer matrix. Direct sunlight and ambient temperatures above 140 °F accelerate fading and loss of tensile strength, while surface temperatures exceeding 120 °F before overlay placement further threaten integrity. The heat‑set side of the fabric is particularly sensitive to asphalt bleed‑through, requiring that hot mix temperatures be limited to 320 °F and that a protective capping layer be placed promptly. Chemical attack, rot, mildew, and abrasion from mud, dirt, dust, and other debris also erode the material unless a polymer that is inert to typical construction chemicals is selected. Flooding or storage in wet conditions must be avoided, and the fabric should be kept elevated off the ground and shielded from sunlight, rain, chemicals, sparks, and other agents during storage. When any defect—such as cuts, tears, punctures, or manufacturing flaws—is identified, it must be repaired or replaced according to manufacturer recommendations at no additional cost, with repairs overlapping a minimum of twelve inches in all directions and secured by an approved seaming adhesive applied over at least six inches of lap (or by sewing or heat bonding). The repaired zone should then be reshaped and re‑compacted before the overlay is placed. Throughout installation, visual inspection is required to reject any geotextile that exhibits deterioration, and strict handling protocols—including limiting traffic until adequate backfill is in place, removing potential puncturing objects, and preventing overspray of adhesives onto adjacent geomembranes—are essential to preserve durability. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 171–204), SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 231–232), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 317–319), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 521–522), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 716)

The tendency of a geotextile to deteriorate is governed chiefly by its composition and the conditions it encounters from manufacture through placement. Fabrics that are non‑woven, needle‑punched and made from virgin polymer resins—such as long‑chain polyethylene, polypropylene, polyamide, PVC or polyester—exhibit markedly lower susceptibility because virgin polymers resist chemical and biological attack. Specifications call for a Class 4, Type A fabric whose fibers consist of at least 85 % by weight of polyolefins, polyesters or polyamides and that is heat‑set on one side; this construction limits asphalt bleed‑through, reduces pick‑up by equipment, and provides inherent resistance to rot, mildew and the chemicals normally encountered on a pavement site. By contrast, the use of unapproved or recycled resins, water‑soluble adhesives, or any adhesive capable of leaching introduces pathways for chemical breakdown and loss of flow capacity, especially when continuous heavy loading causes deformation.

Exposure to heat and ultraviolet radiation accelerates polymer oxidation. Surface temperatures above roughly 120 °F before the overlay is placed, temperatures exceeding 140 °F in general storage, and the much higher limit of 320 °F encountered during hot‑mix asphalt placement have all been shown to damage the fabric. Keeping geotextile shaded, storing it off the ground on elevated platforms, and covering it with heavy‑duty protective sheeting prevent direct sunlight and heat sources from raising its temperature beyond these thresholds.

Moisture is another critical factor. Standing water deeper than six inches, prolonged wet conditions, or placement on a wet pavement promote hydrolysis and loss of strength. The material should be kept dry, dampened only enough to aid handling, and promptly covered with at least six inches of compacted aggregate or capping material. Extended exposure—more than two weeks without cover—or leaving the underlying subgrade exposed for more than sixty days (or an entire winter season) permits water infiltration; sealing the subgrade each night with a smooth‑drum roller in static mode and applying the required capping layer mitigates this risk.

Mechanical damage dramatically raises susceptibility. Physical injuries such as dents, holes, abrasions, tearing from dragging or folding, puncture from rocks dropped more than one foot, and direct traffic or construction equipment riding on the fabric all accelerate deterioration. Heavy construction traffic, sharp turning loads, abrupt turns of tracked equipment that pull or tension the fabric, and rollers placed directly on the geotextile should be avoided; if rolling is required it must be performed in a static mode without contact with the fabric. Limiting handling, overlapping seams correctly, using pins to prevent wind uplift, and promptly repairing any torn sections further reduce damage.

Chemical exposure, particularly vapors from organic solvents (acetone, benzene, mineral spirits) or petroleum‑based fuels (gasoline, kerosene, diesel), can break down the polymer matrix. Storing geotextile blocks away from heat sources, flame‑producing activities, and solvent vapors, as well as keeping the fabric in sealed protective coverings until installation, limits this hazard. Cleanliness is essential; the fabric should be free of loose debris, mud, dirt or dust before placement.

By adhering to these material specifications—virgin polymer composition, non‑woven needle‑punched structure with a minimum 85 % polyolefin/polyester/polyamide content, heat‑set surface—and by controlling exposure conditions—temperature limits (≤120 °F pre‑overlay, ≤140 °F storage, ≤320 °F during paving), moisture management, mechanical protection, chemical avoidance, and proper storage and covering—the susceptibility of geotextile to deterioration can be substantially reduced. [2]

Material interactions and compatibility

In pavement construction the geotextile functions primarily as a mechanical separator, filter and reinforcement layer that interacts with cementitious patches, hot‑mix asphalt binders, aggregates and subgrade soils without any chemical reaction. When installed over a cleaned concrete surface it is laid flat after all surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch have been filled and compacted; the fabric is then kept clean, lightly dampened (but not saturated) and protected from temperatures above 120 °F before the overlay is placed. Overlaps of roughly eight inches are used to maintain continuity. In asphalt applications the geotextile is fully saturated with a tack coat applied at a rate that covers the material and extends beyond its edges; this saturation creates adhesion to the underlying binder, which must remain tacky by being laid while still warm and before it cools. Any gaps in the coating are corrected by hand‑placement of additional binder to preserve a uniform bond. When placed over prepared subgrade, the geotextile acts as a separation membrane that prevents fine or deleterious soils from mixing with coarse backfill aggregate; therefore aggregate is introduced by hand placement, light rain from an excavator bucket, or gradual blading rather than direct dumping, and the surface is graded without wrinkling the fabric. The material itself is a stable, needle‑punched nonwoven composed of at least 85 % polyolefins, polyesters or polyamides, engineered to resist chemical attack, rot and mildew, and heat‑set on one side to limit asphalt binder bleed‑through and prevent pickup by equipment. Finally, when positioned beneath a concrete overlay over an existing treated base course or old concrete slab, the geotextile serves as a debonding layer that separates fresh cementitious material from the substrate, allowing independent slab movement, controlling stress transfer and reducing the risk of premature cracking. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 202–204), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 317–319), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 471–474)

Influence of production and placement

Mixing of the surrounding granular material influences geotextile performance primarily by controlling moisture content; sufficient moisture during stockpiling, hauling and placement prevents segregation and limits additional water added during compaction, thereby providing a uniform support condition for the fabric. Placement is the dominant factor: the fabric must be stored protected from weather and UV, inspected visually before use, and laid on a clean, repaired surface in a loose, unstretched condition without folds, wrinkles or dragging across the ground. Overlaps are required to maintain continuity—edges should overlap by at least six inches, with eight‑inch (±2 in) overlaps commonly specified, and damaged sections must be removed and replaced, with new seams extending a minimum of twelve inches in all directions and secured either by manufacturer‑approved spray adhesive applied over at least six inches of lap, sewing, heat‑bonding or simple overlapping. No more than three layers may be stacked at any location, the fabric should not be saturated (only lightly dampened) and its surface temperature must remain below 120 °F before overlay placement. During installation of an asphalt tack coat interlayer, the coating is applied at a rate of roughly 0.22–0.28 gal/yd² to keep the fabric tacky; the fabric is placed before the cement cools, and any rain that falls on the wet interlayer must be allowed to dry prior to overlay placement. Compaction of overlying layers must avoid direct contact with the geotextile—pad‑foot rollers are prohibited—and should be limited to a non‑movement condition, typically achieved with hydraulic lifts not exceeding nine inches and water‑sprayed each lift, or by restricting roller passes to four when compacting pervious asphalt so that aggregate crushing does not impair drainage. The first backfill lift is placed immediately after the fabric is positioned to ensure full contact with surrounding soil, and subsequent lifts are compacted gently to preserve permeability while providing load transfer. Finally, the installed geotextile should be covered within two weeks to prevent prolonged exposure, after which it functions as a durable separator, filter, reinforcement or drainage element. [2]

The long‑term performance of a geotextile in pavement construction is governed almost entirely by the condition it possesses when placed into service. Any physical defect that originates during delivery, storage, handling or placement—such as tears, punctures, cuts, folds, wrinkles, crushing, stretching or any loss of continuity—immediately compromises tensile strength and filtration capability, thereby reducing its ability to separate, reinforce, protect or drain. Consequently, all damaged sections must be removed and replaced, and any repaired seams must follow the manufacturer’s prescribed method, using a non‑water‑soluble adhesive and extending at least twelve inches in every direction with a six‑inch extension beyond the perimeter. Overlaps that are not properly secured, that use inadequate adhesive, or that are bonded to an underlying geomembrane must be avoided because they lead to seam failure and loss of function.

Surface preparation is equally critical. The subgrade must be dry, clean and free of dirt, gravel, vegetation, oil and any debris; irregularities greater than one‑half inch must be corrected, mill joints with faulting exceeding a quarter‑inch must be filled, and surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be repaired before the fabric is laid. No more than three layers of geotextile may be stacked at any location, as excess thickness can trap debris and impede drainage.

During installation the material must remain loose and unstretched, without folds or wrinkles. Direct traffic, construction equipment, rollers, blading, abrupt turns of tracked equipment that tension the sheet, or dumping aggregate directly onto an exposed fabric are prohibited because they create tears or folds that degrade separation and reinforcement functions. The fabric should be protected from ambient conditions: exposure to temperatures above 120 °F (and certainly above 140 °F), direct sunlight, ultraviolet radiation, mud, dust or debris must be prevented, and the material may not remain uncovered for more than two weeks before being covered with subbase or other required cover. When a tack coat is applied, it should be placed at a rate of 0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard on a dry surface to ensure adequate bonding without damaging the geotextile.

All geotextiles must conform to the specified type—non‑woven, needle‑punched, made from virgin resin unless otherwise approved—and be chemically resistant to rot, mildew and attack. Visual inspection before placement is required to confirm that no defects are present; any damaged material must be repaired according to the manufacturer’s recommendations or replaced at no additional cost to the department. By eliminating construction‑induced defects and adhering strictly to these handling, preparation, installation and protection measures, the long‑term durability and functional performance of the geotextile are preserved. Selection, specification, and improvement

The selection of a geotextile begins with matching the product to its intended function—drainage, sediment control, separation, or reinforcement—and then confirming that the project documents prescribe the appropriate class‑type for that function [2]. The chosen class‑type must be verified against the project‑specific design parameters found in FHWA Publication 408, the NTPEP database, the manufacturer’s product data, and any other applicable technical references, because only those functions approved by PennDOT—drainage, layer separation, erosion or sediment control, and reinforcement of soft subgrade or aggregate layers—are permitted for use without experimental status [1][2]. For a layer‑separation application (Class 4 Type A) the fabric must extend across the full lane width, defined as at least eight feet, and special provisions apply when the lane is narrower; similarly, patching sections longer than twenty feet and at least eight feet wide require geotextile placement after subbase removal [1]. When reinforcement is required (Class 4 Type C) the geotextile is selected for subgrades whose California Bearing Ratio is less than three, providing lateral restraint and load reduction, and it must be installed on a positive drainage slope [1]. The class‑type itself is taken directly from the drawings—such as Section 212.3(b) for base drains or Section 735 for stabilization geosynthetics—and the manufacturer must appear on the approved Bulletin 15 list [2].

Material composition is governed by performance standards that require all acceptable fabrics to be formed from long‑chain polymeric filaments such as polyethylene, polyamide, PVC, polypropylene, or polyester arranged in a stable network; chemically‑ or thermally‑bonded fabrics are excluded for every class, while slit‑film woven structures may be used only for Class 3 geotextiles [2]. For pavement applications the fabric must meet AASHTO M 355 Type II, meaning it is a needle‑punched non‑woven material containing at least 85 % by weight of polyolefins, polyesters or polyamides, resistant to chemical attack, rot and mildew, free of tears or defects, and heat‑set on one side to prevent asphalt bleed‑through and reduce pick‑up during placement [2]. Each roll must be supplied with complete manufacturer identification—including product model, manufacturing plant and lot number—to ensure traceability, and acceptance rests on certified test data submitted by the manufacturer or verified by LTS before inclusion in Bulletin 15 [2].

Installation criteria further refine selection. Overlaps are prescribed according to application: for rigid pavement overlays edges are overlapped eight inches plus or minus two inches with no more than three layers at any point; other earthwork seams require a minimum twelve‑inch lap secured by sewing, heat‑bonding or similar methods, and adjacent rolls must overlap at least two feet [2]. The free edge of an interlayer must extend a minimum of four inches beyond the pavement edge, while transverse limits for lane‑width separators are set between six and twelve inches from the interior edge of the base drain [2]. During placement the fabric must be kept clean, damp but not saturated, protected from construction traffic, heat, UV exposure, mud and excessive handling; surface temperature may not exceed 120 °F before concrete overlay, and storage must avoid flood‑prone areas [2]. Any damage incurred during installation must be repaired at no cost to the department, with the geotextile remaining continuous in the longitudinal direction throughout subsequent base‑drain work [2].

Improving the performance of geotextiles in pavement applications begins with a disciplined material‑selection program that specifies the appropriate class and type, such as Class 1 for base drains or Class 4 Type A for lane‑width separators. The fabric should be a stable, needle‑punched nonwoven constructed from long‑chain polymeric filaments—polyethylene, polyamide, PVC, polypropylene or polyester—and must contain at least eighty‑five percent by weight of polyolefins, polyesters or polyamides. Virgin resin is required unless an approved alternative is documented in the certification statement, and the material should be heat‑set on one side to limit asphalt bleed‑through and reduce the likelihood of equipment pick‑up during placement. Where additional stiffness, tensile capacity, or drainage is needed, high‑strength open fiberglass mesh grids, composite reinforcing grids, or a biaxial geogrid backed with a Class 4 Type A geotextile may be employed as alternative reinforcement systems.

All shipments must arrive with complete certification data—including lot number, thickness and weight—accompanied by manufacturer test results that are verified through laboratory acceptance testing before the material is listed. During storage the fabric should remain dry, wrapped in heavy‑duty coverings, elevated off the ground, and protected from direct sunlight, ultraviolet radiation, temperatures above one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit, mud, dust and debris. Handling must be minimized throughout manufacture, shipment, storage and construction to avoid physical damage; any material that exhibits tears, punctures, flaws or other defects is to be rejected at the time of installation, and damaged areas larger than three square feet require complete removal and replacement at no additional cost to the project owner.

Prior to placement the existing pavement surface must be repaired, cleaned and kept free of debris. The geotextile is laid flat without stretching, with edges overlapped by eight inches plus or minus two inches—or a minimum of twelve inches where specified—and no more than three layers are permitted in any location. Overlaps are secured using a manufacturer‑approved non‑water‑soluble seaming spray adhesive applied over at least six inches of the lap; alternative seam methods such as sewing or heat‑bonding may also be used provided the same overlap length is maintained. Damaged sections that are smaller than three square feet are patched with the same material, extending several feet beyond the flaw and bonded in the same manner. Pad‑foot rollers are prohibited on the geotextile, traffic and construction equipment are barred from contacting the fabric until it is covered, and exposure of the uncovered fabric must not exceed two weeks.

A light dampening—never saturation—is permitted before the overlay is placed. When an asphalt interlayer is used, a liquid binder is applied at a rate of 0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard while maintaining a temperature between two hundred ninety and three hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit to achieve a tacky surface that bonds the geotextile; overlaps receive at least a one‑inch coat of asphalt. Alternative interlayers such as fiberglass mesh or composite reinforcing grids may be substituted where additional strength or drainage is desired. Subgrade preparation also influences performance: the subgrade must be fully compacted, proof‑rolled and sealed to prevent water infiltration, surface irregularities greater than half an inch are corrected, and after prolonged exposure the subgrade is re‑compacted before geotextile installation. End‑of‑day sealing of disturbed surfaces with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode promotes continuous positive drainage and minimizes infiltration. By adhering to these material specifications, handling precautions, inspection regimes, seam treatments, subgrade controls and timely coverage requirements, the inherent weaknesses of geotextiles are mitigated and their long‑term effectiveness in pavement systems is enhanced. Performance evaluation and implications

Evaluation of a geotextile intended for pavement applications is based on a combination of laboratory certification, field inspection, and ongoing performance monitoring. The primary laboratory evidence consists of manufacturer‑provided certified test data that must demonstrate compliance with the specified non‑woven, needle‑punched construction (Table A) and meet defined physical property limits: a minimum component strand tensile strength of 560 lb/in measured in accordance with ASTM D6637, an elongation not exceeding 3 % also per ASTM D6637, a melting point of at least 420 °F verified by ASTM D276, and a mass of no less than 10 oz/yd² as determined by ASTM D5261. These data may be supplemented or independently confirmed by the Laboratory Testing Section (LTS), which can perform additional testing to verify the same criteria. Before installation the geotextile is subject to field‑based verification. An acceptance sample, sized and packaged according to Publication 2 Part B Section 6, is submitted electronically together with identification samples and all required test documentation for review by the Representative. The foundation on which the geosynthetic will be placed is inspected and formally approved prior to placement of fill or the material itself. For prefabricated pavement base drains, flow tests are performed on the assembled drain under load, either with a spot‑bonded shoulder side face or with the geotextile unattached, to confirm that continuous loading does not cause deformation sufficient to reduce the prescribed minimum flow rate. Visual inspection at installation checks for defects, deterioration, or damage incurred during manufacture, transport, or storage; any geotextile exhibiting such conditions is rejected. During service, performance evidence continues to be gathered through routine visual inspections. Small damaged areas are patched, while sections with damage exceeding three square feet must be removed and replaced entirely. Although the cited guidance does not prescribe specific long‑term laboratory monitoring, these ongoing field observations provide the practical evidence of acceptable performance over time. [2]

Geotextiles are permeable polymeric fabrics that serve to separate dissimilar pavement layers, convey water, control erosion and sediment, and when required provide lateral restraint to reinforce weak subgrades. Their effectiveness is governed primarily by intrinsic properties such as permeability, tensile strength, chemical resistance, durability against rot and mildew, and a heat‑set surface that limits asphalt bleed‑through and equipment pickup. High tensile capacity and durability preserve the individual strengths of subgrade and sub‑base by preventing intermixing while allowing drainage, which enhances overall pavement performance and extends service life. For soils with very low bearing capacity (CBR < 3), a Class 4 Type C geotextile provides lateral support that reduces load stresses on the subgrade, permitting designers to adopt thinner or more economical structural layers without sacrificing reliability. Consequently, design specifications across the guides require a continuous full‑width separator of at least eight feet across the pavement width—typically a Class 4 Type A fabric for standard applications and a Type C material wherever reinforcement is needed—with special provisions for narrow widenings or patch repairs to maintain continuity. Because geotextile performance varies with product type and site conditions, design parameters must be established on a project‑by‑project basis using FHWA Publication 408, NTPEP data, manufacturer technical sheets, and other appropriate references. When the material is employed beyond its standard functions, such as structural reinforcement of soft subgrade, it is classified as an experimental item that requires additional approvals and stricter construction controls to ensure proper placement and integration. The Class 4 Type A fabric is a needle‑punched nonwoven composed of at least eighty‑five percent polyolefins, polyesters or polyamides, which provides the high tensile strength and chemical resistance needed for pavement service. To retain its permeability and dimensional stability, the geotextile must be protected from temperatures above one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit, direct sunlight, debris, flood‑prone storage, and exposure longer than two weeks before backfill coverage. During handling the fabric should remain flat, unstretched, free of folds or wrinkles, and no traffic or construction equipment may be placed on the exposed material. Installation practices call for continuous placement across the full lane width (minimum eight feet) with transverse edges positioned six to twelve inches from any pavement‑base drain, and overlaps of roll ends and adjacent edges of at least six inches, while general edge overlaps are specified as eight inches ± two. Seams are sealed with a manufacturer‑approved spray adhesive applied to a minimum of six inches of joint, taking care not to bond the geotextile to underlying membranes so that layer separation is preserved; damaged areas must be lapped at least twelve inches in all directions and secured similarly. No more than three layers of fabric may be stacked at any location to avoid excessive stiffness. Immediately after placement the first lift of backfill is placed while the geotextile remains in the trench, ensuring full contact with the trench wall and preserving drainage pathways. In prefabricated base‑drain applications the fabric completely wraps the plastic core, retains at least fifty percent open area on the pavement side, and resists deformation under permanent traffic loads so that flow capacity is not diminished. When used as an interlayer beneath asphalt, the geotextile is saturated with a tack coat of roughly 0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard before the cement cools, creating a strong bond that prevents delamination; the overlying asphalt must have a minimum compacted thickness of one and a half inches. By isolating flowable backfill from structural elements such as foundation drains and pipe bedding, the geotextile prevents intrusion and contamination of drainage paths, while its filtration action excludes fines yet allows water to pass, thereby maintaining the intended load‑carrying capacity of the pavement system over time. The separation of subgrade soil from aggregate layers eliminates soil‑aggregate mixing, distributes loads more evenly, limits settlement and rutting, and reduces differential stresses that could cause premature cracking in concrete overlays when the geotextile functions as a debonding layer. These combined material behaviors directly shape design specifications—mandating the use of Class 4 Type A or C fabrics, defined overlap lengths, inclusion of debonding layers, and appropriate thicknesses—and they improve long‑term durability, reduce the frequency and extent of corrective maintenance, and dictate construction sequencing and quality‑control procedures. Consistent installation that respects required width, overlaps, anchorage, and protection from damage minimizes settlement and material intermixing, thereby reducing future maintenance demands and preserving pavement integrity throughout its design life.
