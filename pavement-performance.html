Pavement Performance

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Pavement Performance is a topic under Pavement Design.

Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Pavement Performance is a systematic evaluation of an existing roadway’s surface condition and its supporting structure that quantifies distress such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage deficiencies while simultaneously measuring subgrade stiffness with in‑situ tests like Falling Weight Deflectometer, Dynamic Cone Penetrometer or Light Weight Deflectometer [1]. The results are used to answer whether projected traffic loads are compatible with the proposed design, what structural layer thicknesses are required, and whether drainage or base problems must be corrected before rehabilitation actions such as full‑depth reclamation, Hot‑In‑Place Recycled treatment, or overlay placement [1][2]. Ongoing performance monitoring—annual automated/manual distress surveys, core‑depth verification, and traffic classification counts—provides the data that trigger remedial thresholds (e.g., 30 % low‑severity distresses or core deficiencies exceeding ¼ inch) and guide maintenance, design adjustments, and warranty compliance [2].

Purpose and investigation objectives

For full‑depth reclamation candidate pavements (section 5.15) Pavement Performance evaluates the existing pavement surface and its supporting layers by measuring the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and any drainage deficiencies, and by quantifying subgrade stiffness with in‑situ tests such as Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer. The investigation must answer whether future traffic loads are compatible with the proposed design, which distress mechanisms are currently active and why they occurred, how strong and dry the subgrade is, what layer thicknesses are required to meet structural needs, and whether excess moisture or drainage problems need correction before reclamation [1].

For hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt treatments (section 5.19) Pavement Performance evaluates the surface distress condition of an existing asphalt pavement to determine its suitability for a Hot‑In‑Place Recycled treatment. The investigation must answer whether rutting severity is less than 1 ½ inches, whether transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth is less than 1.0 inch with average crack width not exceeding the Medium Rating of ≤ ½ inch, whether raveling or weathering does not exceed the Medium Rating, whether any corrugation, shoving or slippage has an average depth less than 1 ½ inches, and whether asphalt thickness is at least 1 ½ inches greater than the milling depth while confirming that prohibited materials such as rubber or fiber are absent from the top 2 inches. Additional questions include whether the base, subbase and subgrade are sound, whether paving fabric lies at least 1 ½ inches below the cut depth, and whether markers, large mastic patches or extensive sealants have been removed [1].

Under the 60‑month asphalt‑concrete pavement warranty (section 496) Pavement Performance evaluates the condition of the paved surface—including flushing, potholes, cracks, rut depth and other distresses identified by automated or manual surveys—as well as the underlying base quality when fatigue cracking suggests an unacceptable base. The investigation must determine whether measured distress levels remain within the limits set in Table A, whether a threshold such as 30 % of a segment having two or more low‑severity distresses is exceeded, and whether any identified problems can be attributed to factors beyond the contractor’s control, for example poor base condition, excess traffic loading or natural forces [2].

Within the same warranty framework Pavement Performance also evaluates the constructed thickness of the binder and wearing courses by coring and measuring each core; the investigation must determine whether any single core depth is deficient by more than 1/4 inch or whether three consecutive cores are uniformly deficient by more than 1/8 inch, and it must identify the start and end points of such deficiencies so that corrective placement can bring the depth within 1/4 inch of the required value [2].

For extended‑season paving (section 413) Pavement Performance evaluates newly placed asphalt concrete by assessing both material quality and workmanship. The investigation examines the presence and severity of fatigue cracking, transverse or miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond or delamination, and it must answer whether these observed distresses meet the Department’s acceptance criteria or require remedial action or repair [2].

Performance information gathered from pavement testing and surveys is a primary driver of engineering decisions throughout the life‑cycle of a roadway. In section 4.9 of the Pavement Policy Manual, Falling Weight Deflectometer testing is required and should be conducted on existing Portland cement concrete pavements before designing an overlay, because the results indicate whether the structure is in good‑to‑fair condition suitable for a bonded concrete overlay or moderately to significantly deteriorated and therefore better served by an unbonded overlay [1]. The identified presence of slab rocking, pumping, faulting, or inadequate drainage then dictates surface preparation steps such as full‑depth patching, slab stabilization, or installation of subdrainage prior to any overlay placement [1]. When the existing pavement is asphalt or a composite and structurally sound with less than 2 % patching required, thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete overlays of 2.5 to 4 inches may be used, whereas thicker bonded overlays of 4 to 8 inches are considered when additional structural capacity is needed [1]. For severely distressed concrete sections the manual recommends cracking and breaking, rubblizing to 12‑inch or 8‑inch pieces, or providing a minimum 1‑inch asphalt separation layer for unbonded overlays to achieve a stable platform for new pavement [1].

The Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (LLCP) option is promoted in section 4.12 because high‑performance dowel bars and reduced water‑cement ratios deliver longer service life, allowing planners to justify LLCP over conventional concrete where durability is critical; the incentive shall be 4 percent of the estimated total LLCP SY paving cost and a mandatory ride‑quality incentive under Section 530 links surface smoothness testing directly to payment, thereby influencing construction quality decisions [1]. Since LLCP is not appropriate for thin (less than 8 inches) or bonded concrete pavement applications, engineers can screen suitable sections during rehabilitation planning and use the decision tree to select the proper item‑of‑work classification based on site constraints such as a central mix plant requiring approximately 4 acres and a slip‑form paver needing a minimum clearance of 3 feet on each side [1].

Overlay design is further refined in section 5.12, where performance evaluation that pinpoints structural deficiencies, surface deterioration, or ride‑ability issues guides the selection of overlay type and preparation; if the existing surface is asphalt, structurally sound, and has good surface and subsurface drainage, a thin Superpave wearing course as thin as 6.3 mm or 9.5 mm may be placed instead of the standard 1½‑inch minimum, otherwise a leveling course of at least 1 inch or a full‑depth overlay is required [1]. When reflective cracking or moisture damage is observed, an interlayer such as geotextile fabric or fiberglass grid can be incorporated and overlays over interlayers shall be at least 1½ inches thick for milling; the presence of edge drop‑offs calls for inclusion of a Safety Edge with a depth of 1½ inches to 5 inches, improving driver recovery while adding roughly 1 % material [1].

Full‑depth reclamation techniques in section 5.15 begin with a reliable estimate of future traffic loading and a recent pavement condition survey that records the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues to identify underlying damage mechanisms; Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements before rehabilitation supply valuable information on existing stiffness, post‑construction tests confirm the new pavement’s structural capacity, and additional DCP or LWD tests may be used for subgrade evaluation while sampling reclaimed materials follows detailed criteria to verify composition and suitability for the chosen stabilization method [1]. These findings together guide project selection, determine appropriate layer thicknesses and drainage corrections, and dictate whether pulverization, mechanical or chemical stabilization is required, thereby supporting planning, design, maintenance and rehabilitation decisions [1].

The Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) asphalt framework in section 5.19 provides explicit thresholds—rutting severity less than 1½ inches, transverse or miscellaneous cracking depth less than 1.0 inch with average crack width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, raveling/weathering not exceeding the medium rating, and minor corrugation, shoving or slippage with an average depth less than 1½ inches—to qualify a candidate pavement for HIR treatment; design must provide asphalt thickness at least 1½ inches more than the milling depth and any paving fabric shall be a minimum of 1½ inches below the specified HIR cut depth, while composite pavements require a minimum of 1 to 2 inches of asphalt beneath the HIR layer (2 to 3 inches for full‑depth asphalt) [1]. Confirmation of these thresholds through assessment, sampling and testing enables engineers to select projects where HIR will remediate surface distress, improve performance and allow traffic reopening once the surface cools, thereby supporting rehabilitation planning, design specifications and maintenance scheduling [1].

Subgrade considerations in section 6.2 influence design decisions by requiring improvement of isolated low CBR areas to achieve values representative of overall project conditions; all CBR values greater than 10 for use in pavement designs shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and no CBR value greater than 20 may be used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation [1]. When large areas of a project have significantly different soil types and CBR values, it may be permissible to divide the project into a limited number of subsections with different pavement design sections, optimizing material use and construction sequencing [1]; for rehabilitation overlays where the existing pavement will not be removed, the in‑situ density condition requires multiplying the CBR by 1,000 instead of 1,500 when using the Heukelom and Klomp equation to obtain a more conservative Mr estimate, and if back‑calculated Mr from FWD data yields a value above 15,000 psi it must be verified by another form of testing prior to its use in the pavement design process, thereby increasing confidence in planning, design, maintenance and rehabilitation decisions [1].

The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications reinforce the role of performance monitoring through annual surveys required for the 60‑month asphalt concrete warranty; when a year‑5 survey shows that 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, the warranty mandates microsurfacing or an approved alternative, while any combination of deviations that exceeds 20 % of a segment’s surface triggers full removal and replacement [2]. The Department’s annual analysis together with traffic classification counts ensures that design ESAL assumptions remain valid—if cumulative ESASL exceed 100 % of the projected 20‑year design life, rutting and base failures are treated as beyond the contractor’s responsibility, prompting redesign or more robust materials [2]. Depth‑measurement and sampling requirements in Section 496.3 give engineers concrete data on whether an asphalt concrete pavement meets its warranty performance criteria; when any one core depth measurement indicates a deficiency of more than 1/4 inch that cannot be satisfactorily corrected, the affected area is considered defective work, and when uniform deficiency exceeds 1/8 inch in any three consecutive cores the area is likewise deemed defective [2]. Mandatory loose‑mix and core samples—one per paver pass and one 6‑inch core per 1,000 tons placed—allow quality‑control testing that confirms material properties and informs future design specifications [2].

Performance testing prescribed for high‑friction surface treatment (HFST) provides concrete data that directly inform engineering actions; after installation the Department conducts a calibrated skid test between 45 and 120 days using ASTM E274 procedures, and acceptance payment is tied to the results, while any untreated areas, poor aggregate retention, delamination or color discoloration must be removed, prepared and retreated at no additional cost [2]. Post‑paving evaluations required for work placed in the fall portion of an extended season are performed by a spring manual survey conducted by May 1, documenting distress types such as fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond/delamination; these findings directly inform whether the pavement is accepted or remedial action is required, allowing engineers to adjust maintenance schedules, plan rehabilitation, and refine future design specifications [2]. The requirement to submit documentation and measurements within 24 hours of paving completion further enables timely decision‑making for maintenance planning and ensures that any required repairs are performed at no additional cost before a final acceptance certificate is issued [2].

Preplacement quality‑control plans, density acceptance criteria and the weather limitation that asphalt may not be placed when air or surface temperature is 40 °F or lower generate performance thresholds that guide planning of construction timing and mix design to meet durability targets [2]. Deflection test results required by Section 679.3 give quantitative criteria that directly drive maintenance and rehabilitation actions; when a joint or crack shows a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more, the measured joint efficiency determines whether the element is stabilized outright if efficiency is at least 65 % or patched and then stabilized if efficiency is less than 65 %, and because the test data must be provided to the Representative fifteen working days before work begins and testing may only be performed when air temperature is between 40 °F and 70 °F, these performance findings also shape scheduling and planning decisions for slab‑stabilization projects [2].

Scope and applicability

Pavement Performance assessment is appropriate for Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (LLCP) when the pavement uses high‑performance dowel bars, is installed as a full‑depth replacement with new concrete, and all aspects of subgrade preparation, mixing, joint placement, finishing, curing and sawing are tightly controlled; the slab must be at least 8 inches thick and may not be a bonded concrete pavement. The site for an LLCP project must be able to accommodate a central mix plant occupying approximately 4 acres and a slip‑form paver that requires a minimum clearance of 3 feet on each side. Pavement Performance assessment is also appropriate for Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) mixtures applied to surface‑distressed asphalt pavements provided the underlying base and subgrade are sound. The allowable distresses for HIR projects are limited to rutting less than 1½ inches, transverse or miscellaneous cracking with a depth less than 1 inch and an average crack width not exceeding ½ inch, raveling or weathering rated no more than medium, and minor corrugation, shoving or slippage with an average depth under 1½ inches; bleeding may be present only in isolated areas and low friction numbers are acceptable. The existing asphalt layer must be at least 1½ inches thicker than the milling depth, any paving fabric must lie at least 1½ inches below the HIR cut, and pavements that contain rubber or fiber material in the top 2 inches are excluded from Pavement Performance assessment. Raised markers, durable markings, large mastic patches greater than 5 ft², and extensive crack sealant or over‑banded rubberized joint seal must be removed before HIR work is performed. Suitable projects for the HIR approach include structurally sound, well‑drained composite or full‑depth asphalt pavements, concrete decks with stable joints, and specialty mixes such as stone matrix asphalt or open‑graded friction courses. The Pavement Performance approach under Section 496 is limited to asphalt‑concrete pavements that are covered by the 60‑month warranty and consist of two or more different asphalt concrete pavement courses, specifically a binder course and a wearing course. Core depth testing must be performed within 24 hours after final compaction; any single core deficient in depth by more than ¼ inch or a uniform deficiency of more than ⅛ inch in any three consecutive cores that is not satisfactorily corrected designates the area as defective work. The performance assessment for these warranty‑covered pavements evaluates flushing, potholes, low‑severity and medium‑severity cracking, and rut depth measured with a 12‑ft straight edge; automated surveys conducted in accordance with Publication 336 (with manual surveys used for flushing and potholes) provide the data that trigger remedial actions when the year‑5 survey shows that thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, or when combined deviations exceed twenty percent of the surface area. The assessment also recognizes site conditions beyond the contractor’s control, including an unacceptable base condition revealed by core testing, traffic loadings that exceed the projected 20‑year ESAL design, routine maintenance activities performed by the department, destructive procedures such as coring or milling, uncontrolled natural forces such as floods or earthquakes, and damage resulting from traffic accidents. Section 413 extends Pavement Performance assessment to flexible pavements that use PG 64E‑22 wearing courses, wearing courses designed for more than ten million ESALs, 4.75‑mm wearing courses, or any other wearing course placed at a compacted depth of less than 1.5 inches, as well as all other asphalt concrete courses. When paving is performed outside the normal season or after extended‑season work, a spring field survey examines fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling or weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond or delamination. Placement is prohibited on wet surfaces or when the air or surface temperature is 40 °F or lower, and it must occur within the prescribed paving windows of April 1 through October 15 for the listed wearing courses (or April 1 through October 31 for other courses) unless a specific extension is granted. Density acceptance for mixtures placed at the minimum compacted depths is achieved by pavement cores, while other mixes rely on an optimum‑rolling pattern to ensure confidence that optimum density has been attained throughout the course. Existing information and records

The Pavement Performance review should begin by obtaining a reliable estimate of future traffic loading on the road, because traffic level drives design and evaluation. It must include a recent pavement condition survey—typically following Publication 336 or 343—or an equivalent distress procedure such as MicroPaver™, which documents current distress types such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues together with their causes. In‑situ testing results are required, especially Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements taken before rehabilitation together with dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer data to assess subgrade stiffness and support conditions. Appropriate material sampling—covering the number of samples, locations, depth, size, handling and identification of layers—should be collected according to the outlined sampling criteria. The review should also assess existing pavement, base, subbase and subgrade conditions, conduct sampling and testing of the pavement, and study the construction and maintenance history and traffic data. For projects considered under Section 5.19 HOT IN-PLACE RECYCLED ASPHALT > C. Characteristics of Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) Mixtures, surface distresses must meet candidate thresholds such as rutting severity less than 1 ½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth less than 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding ≤ ½ inch, raveling/weathering severity not exceeding a medium rating, minor corrugation, shoving and slippage with average depth less than 1 ½ inches, low friction numbers, and asphalt thickness at least 1 ½ inches greater than the milling depth. The review should examine the automated and manual condition surveys required by Section 496.3(c), including distress identification, measurement and extent data reported in accordance with Publication 336 for each segment. Rut‑depth measurements must be verified using a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width to determine severity and extent. Manual flushing and pothole surveys are performed according to Publication 336 when needed. Annual performance analysis reports that compare observed distress percentages—such as the year‑5 finding that 30 % of a segment area has two or more low‑severity distresses—must be incorporated into the review. Any combination of deviations exceeding 20 % of a segment’s surface triggers full replacement under the warranty provisions. Traffic classification counts are used to verify that ESAL estimates remain within projected values. Access to all prior performance survey results, traffic count data, and records of any emergency or remedial repairs issued by the Department should be included in the review. [1][2]

Visual inspection and condition assessment

Pavement Performance examinations should record transverse joint faulting, the presence of fines near joints or cracks on traffic lanes or shoulders, and small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, as these indicate loss of slab support. Corner breaks and any slab movement or faulting, including rocking slabs, are also noted as signs of deteriorating slab stability. Pumping of water beneath concrete slabs and surface spalling are documented because they signal subgrade consolidation or loss of material integrity. Cracking that has progressed to a level where patching is uneconomical, as well as alligator cracking, should be recorded along with any associated drainage problems such as inadequate subdrainage. The distress survey must assess the severity of rutting, bleeding, raveling, potholes, and other drainage issues because they help identify damage mechanisms. For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) candidate pavements the visible distress inventory is limited to surface‑level problems; load‑related base distress is excluded. Rutting severity shall be less than 1½ inches, and transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth shall not exceed 1.0 inch with an average crack width not greater than ½ inch. Raveling or weathering may not surpass the medium rating, which is described as a rough, pitted surface that may have loose particles, while minor corrugation, shoving and slippage are acceptable provided their average depth is less than 1½ inches; isolated bleeding is permissible. Under Section 496—Asphalt Concrete Pavement, 60‑month warranty, the performance examination focuses on flushing, potholes, rutting measured as rut depth, and cracking, with low‑severity and medium‑severity cracks—including fatigue cracking—identified and measured according to Publication 336. Flushing and pothole results are determined by manual surveys, and rut depth is verified using a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width. In slab stabilization assessments visual verification of pumping or other poor joint performance is required, and inspectors should look for radial cracks radiating from grout injection holes, transverse cracks that appear between adjacent injection holes, and any panel displacement where the surface is raised beyond the 0.05 inch allowable tolerance. For High Friction Surface Treatment surface preparation, visible cracking that is one‑quarter inch or wider must be located, loose material removed with compressed air, and the cracks pre‑treated with the mixed binder; overfilled cracks are to be scarified flush with surrounding pavement after curing, and areas showing poor aggregate retention, delamination, or color discoloration are to be removed, prepared, and retreated. The spring evaluation for Superpave mixture design includes a manual survey of fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond or delamination to determine acceptance or need for remedial action. Field measurements and testing

In the Rehabilitation section (4.9) of the Pavement Policy Manual, a Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) test is required and should be conducted on existing Portland cement concrete pavements before any overlay design because it provides data on the current pavement condition that guides selection of the appropriate rehabilitation strategy [1]. The Long‑Life Concrete Pavement section (4.12) calls for an Evaluation of Concrete Pavement Ride Quality, as defined in Publication 408 Section 507, which offers a nondestructive indication of the pavement’s smoothness and user comfort [1]. When a water‑to‑cement ratio incentive is specified for LLCP projects, an Evaluation of Overall Lot Pay Factor is performed to verify that the lower W/C ratio has been achieved, supporting the durability objective [1]. For Full Depth Reclamation project selection (section 5.15 B), a recent pavement condition survey prepared according to Publication 336, Publication 343 or a distress procedure such as MicroPaver™ is important because it provides information about the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues and insight into the causes of visible distress [1]. In‑situ testing with an FWD before rehabilitation supplies valuable information about the stiffness of existing pavement materials, and post‑construction FWD testing determines the stiffness of the new pavement; the dynamic cone penetrometer (DCP) and light weight deflectometer (LWD) are also useful for evaluating the subgrade [1]. The Hot‑In‑Place Recycled Asphalt section (5.19 C) requires a visual distress survey that records rutting severity, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth, average crack width, raveling/weathering rating, corrugation/shoving depth, bleeding presence, friction numbers, and pavement thickness relative to the milling depth [1]. Rutting severity must be less than 1 ½ inches; transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth must be a depth less than 1.0 inch and the average crack width may not exceed the Medium Rating of ≤ ½ inch; corrugation, shoving and slippage should have an average depth less than 1 ½ inches; and asphalt pavement thickness must be at least 1 ½ inches more than the milling depth [1]. These measurements indicate whether the existing surface is suitable for Hot‑In‑Place Recycled treatment by confirming limited rutting, shallow and narrow cracking, moderate surface loss, minor longitudinal distress, acceptable skid resistance, and sufficient structural thickness after milling [1]. The Subgrade Soils section (6.2) prescribes FWD testing that records pavement deflections under a 9,000‑lb load and permits back‑calculation of the subgrade resilient modulus (Mr) to evaluate structural capacity [1]. Dynamic Cone Penetration (DCP) testing uses a 17.6‑lb hammer to drive a metal cone repeatedly, providing an in‑situ resistance‑to‑penetration profile that can be converted to Mr via the CBR correlation [1]. California Bearing Ratio (CBR) testing of subgrade, subbase or base materials assesses potential strength and supplies the values needed to develop Mr and k for design, and when laboratory CBR results are uncertain additional field testing with FWD or DCP may be performed to back‑calculate Mr for validation [1]. Annual automated distress surveys conducted in accordance with Publication 336 identify and quantify cracking, rutting, flushing and potholes, while hand‑surveys are used for verification of flushing and potholes when required [2]. Rut depth is measured with a 12‑ft straight edge laid across the lane width to determine severity and extent of rutting [2]. Yearly traffic classification counts are made to confirm that accumulated Equivalent Single Axle Loads remain within the projected design values [2]. The contractor may also apply nondestructive testing at any time during the warranty, provided a seven‑day notice is given [2]. Within 24 hours after final compaction, a core at least 4 inches in diameter is drilled for each 2,000 linear feet of lane and the depth of binder and wearing courses is measured according to PTM No. 737; a single core showing a deficiency greater than ¼ inch or three consecutive cores uniformly low by more than ⅛ inch signals defective work that must be corrected [2]. When directed, one loose‑mix box sample from behind the paver before compaction and one 6‑inch diameter core for every 1,000 tons placed are collected and delivered to the inspector for laboratory evaluation [2]. For high‑friction surface treatments, a calibrated skid test is performed between 45 and 120 days after placement using a smooth‑tire lock‑wheel apparatus prescribed in ASTM E274 (AASHTO T 242); this nondestructive test quantifies surface friction or skid resistance to determine whether the treatment meets safety criteria [2]. Concrete surfaces are also inspected with profile chips to visually confirm compliance with the International Concrete Repair Institute CSP 5 roughness requirement, providing a qualitative measure of texture needed for proper binder adhesion [2]. In‑place density is verified by pavement core sampling for mixes placed at the minimum compacted depths listed in Table G; where cores are not required, an optimum‑rolling pattern is used to infer density [2]. After extended‑season paving, a spring manual performance survey conducted according to Publication 336 visually evaluates fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond/delamination to decide acceptance or remedial action [2]. Deflection testing performed in accordance with ASTM D 4695 provides a nondestructive measure of slab corner deflection under load; when the loaded deflection reaches 0.02 inch or more, evaluation is triggered [2]. Joint efficiency, calculated as unloaded deflection divided by loaded deflection times 100, must be at least 65 % for stabilization, while lower values require patching [2]. Testing is not performed if air temperature is below 40 °F or exceeds 70 °F, nor during spring thaw or when the subgrade is frozen; in those conditions visual verification of joint performance is used instead [2].

Structural and functional performance

Evaluation of structural capacity for rehabilitation of existing Portland cement concrete pavement begins with Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing on the existing pavement before any overlay is designed; the test is performed at midslab locations that are not cracked using a 9,000‑pound load in accordance with ASTM D 4694/D 4695 and the resulting deflections are back‑calculated to obtain a subgrade resilient modulus [1]. Bearing strength of the subgrade, subbase or base course is assessed through California Bearing Ratio (CBR) testing, and any CBR value greater than 10 shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer while values greater than 20 are not allowed to be used in the Heukelom‑and‑Klomp correlation [1]. When the back‑calculated resilient modulus exceeds 15 000 psi it must be verified by another form of testing, and additional field resistance information may be obtained with a Dynamic Cone Penetration (DCP) test that can be converted to resilient modulus values via the CBR conversion [1]. In‑situ testing also includes dynamic cone penetrometer and Light Weight Deflectometer tests to assess subgrade strength and to determine existing layer thicknesses while confirming adequate drainage conditions [1]. Functional condition is documented through a pavement condition survey that records the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues; measurable distress criteria for Hot‑In‑Place Recycled asphalt projects require rutting severity to be less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth to be less than 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding the Medium Rating of ≤ ½ inch, raveling or weathering severity not to exceed the Medium Rating, and minor corrugation, shoving or slippage to have an average depth less than 1½ inches; low friction numbers are tolerated and the existing asphalt pavement thickness must be at least 1½ inches more than the milling depth [1]. For Long‑Life Concrete Pavement the performance assessment focuses on evaluating concrete pavement ride quality, which is required by the acceptance criteria and underpins the ride‑quality incentive specified in Publication 408 [1]. The warranty evaluation combines automated and manual distress surveys with a 12‑foot straight edge run across the lane width to verify rut depth as an indicator of functional surface condition [2]. Structural capacity is checked by extracting six‑inch diameter cores from distressed zones, measuring each core’s depth against minimum thickness requirements; a deficiency is recorded when any single core is short by more than ¼ inch or when three consecutive cores are uniformly short by more than ⅛ inch [2]. Laboratory analysis of the extracted cores must show density greater than 92 % of theoretical maximum density and percent asphalt and gradation within the tolerances of the contractor’s mix design [2]. Traffic classification counts are routinely performed so that accumulated Equivalent Single Axle Loads remain within the projected values in the contract documents, providing a load‑related capacity indicator [2]. For high‑friction surface treatments a calibrated skid test conducted between 45 and 120 days after installation according to ASTM E274 (AASHTO T 242) with a smooth‑tire lock‑wheel apparatus is used to assess friction as a functional performance measure [2]. In slab‑stabilization work, deflection testing performed in accordance with ASTM D 4695 measures loaded and unloaded slab‑corner deflections; stabilization is required when the loaded deflection reaches 0.02 inch or more together with a joint efficiency of 65 % or higher (or below 65 % for other actions), and testing may only be carried out when air temperature is between 40 °F and 70 °F [2].

The Pavement Policy Manual identifies inadequate drainage as a primary contributor to poor pavement performance because water that enters the pavement and is not removed by subsurface drains creates pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement that generate voids beneath concrete slabs, leading to loss of slab support and excessive deflections [1]. The same manual notes that weak underlying support may result from these voids, active pumping indicating a poor subgrade, or insufficient subgrade stiffness as revealed by in‑situ testing, all of which reduce the structural capacity of the pavement [1]. Material deterioration is linked to surface spalling, cracking, rutting, raveling and potholes; the manual specifies that rutting severity greater than 1½ inches, transverse or miscellaneous cracking depth exceeding 1.0 inch, or raveling/weathering rating above medium are indicators of such degradation [1]. Construction deficiencies cited in the manual include failure to seal joints and cracks, lack of adequate base drains, and improper slab stabilization methods; radial cracks spreading from grout injection holes or transverse cracks between adjacent injection holes are described as signs of poor quality work that may require full‑depth slab replacement [1]. The manual further attributes distress to environmental exposure such as freeze‑thaw cycles and frost action that cause differential heaving, loss of bearing capacity during spring thaw, and material damage, while alligator cracking that extends across the entire surface is attributed to age oxidation of the pavement and general aging [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specifications point to a weak supporting base as a likely cause when performance surveys detect fatigue cracking, which may signal an unacceptable base condition [2]. Excessive traffic loading is also implicated because estimated cumulative ESALs have exceeded 100 % of the projected 20‑year design life, suggesting overload beyond the pavement’s intended capacity [2]. Environmental exposure beyond the contractor’s control, such as floods or other natural disasters, is listed among factors that can produce distress independent of construction quality [2]. Material deterioration under the specifications may arise when placement occurs on wet surfaces or at air or surface temperatures of 40 °F or lower, conditions prohibited because they increase the risk of fatigue cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, or loss of bond/delamination [2]. Construction defects are highlighted by insufficient pavement thickness: any single core depth measurement that is short by more than ¼ inch, or three consecutive cores each short by more than ⅛ inch, triggers a defective‑work determination requiring correction [2]. For high‑friction surface treatment, improper surface preparation, binder contamination, or placement when wind speeds exceed 15 mph are identified as causes of poor aggregate retention, delamination and subsequent material deterioration [2]. Slab stabilization work may exhibit construction defects indicated by radial cracks from grout injection holes, transverse cracks between adjacent injection holes, or any observed vertical movement during pumping, each requiring immediate remedial action rather than being attributed to drainage, support strength, overload, environmental exposure, or aging [2].

Findings, confidence, and next actions

The performance assessment permits the use of rigid or flexible overlays to correct structural deficiencies, surface deterioration, or rideability problems, and it allows thin Superpave wearing courses only when the existing surface is asphalt, structurally sound, less than 2 % of the pavement requires patching, and both surface and subsurface drainage are good, with a minimum thickness of 1 ½ inches on Interstates (or where approved) and the same minimum on non‑Interstates under those conditions. The Safety Edge shall be used as a standard edge treatment for wearing or binder courses with a depth of at least 1 ½ inches and not exceeding 5 inches, adding roughly 1 % to material quantities. Interlayer systems may be incorporated to mitigate reflective cracking and provide moisture barriers, but they are limited to addressing crack‑related distress and do not normally treat rutting, bleeding, raveling, or water intrusion from underground sources, leaving uncertainty about long‑term performance under severe climate or traffic conditions. Hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) asphalt mixtures can remediate rutting and other surface distress, recover and improve pavement performance, enhance appearance, and prolong service life, and they are considered suitable for any level of average daily traffic; however, the treatment is described as a relatively short‑term interim wearing surface that must be followed by a permanent surface course before the construction season ends. Acceptable candidate pavements for HIR treatment are limited to those whose rutting severity is less than 1 ½ inches, whose transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth is under 1 inch with an average crack width not exceeding a medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and whose raveling or weathering does not exceed the medium rating; they must also have at least 1 ½ inches of asphalt below the HIR depth, and pavements containing rubber in the top layers, fiber materials within the upper 2 inches, or extensive mastic patches are excluded. Pavement performance assessment can support a design that uses the lowest subgrade CBR value to control pavement thickness, but any CBR value greater than 10 must be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and no CBR value greater than 20 may be used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation; when laboratory CBR results are uncertain additional laboratory or field testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer or Dynamic Cone Penetrometer should be performed, yet back‑calculated resilient modulus from Falling Weight Deflectometer data is not an exact science and values above 15 000 psi must be verified by another test before they can be used. Under the 60‑month warranty for asphalt concrete, annual automated surveys performed in accordance with Publication 336 allow a conclusion of satisfactory performance when the fifth‑year survey shows that thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses; at that point remedial microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment is required. If deviations beyond the Table A limits combine to exceed twenty percent of the surface area of any segment, this triggers full removal and replacement of that segment, with a medium‑severity crack counted as a deviation affecting six inches on either side of the crack for its entire length and repair areas spaced less than one hundred feet apart merged into a continuous repair. Core‑depth testing conducted within twenty‑four hours after final compaction on a four‑inch (or larger) core for each two thousand linear feet provides another basis for concluding compliance: no single core may be deficient by more than one‑quarter inch and no three consecutive cores may show a uniform deficiency of more than one‑eighth inch; any such uncorrectable shortfall is deemed defective work. For High‑Friction Surface Treatment, friction performance can be concluded as satisfactory when a calibrated skid test conducted between forty‑five and one hundred twenty days after installation using the ASTM E274 (AASHTO T 242) lock‑wheel method meets the specified criteria, and any sections exhibiting poor aggregate retention, delamination or discoloration are to be re‑prepared and retreated at no extra cost, provided that binder components are mixed within ± 2 percent of the manufacturer’s volume specifications and surface preparation is performed under wind speeds below fifteen miles per hour (gusts under twenty miles per hour). For extended‑season asphalt courses, post‑construction inspection that includes a manual survey of fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond or delamination enables the Department to conclude acceptance or need for remedial action after the spring evaluation, with a Final Acceptance Certificate withheld until any required repairs are completed. The conclusions drawn from performance data remain limited by uncertainties in traffic forecasts, the snapshot nature of distress surveys, and the representativeness of sampled materials, as well as the need to correctly identify and remediate drainage issues before reclamation; additionally, while increasing chemical stabilizer generally raises strength, excessive amounts may cause brittleness, introducing further performance uncertainty. Project selection and proper sampling/testing for HIR treatment remain critical sources of uncertainty for achieving the expected performance gains because removal of durable markings and certain sealants is required and candidate pavements must meet several distress thresholds. Uncertainties also persist regarding subgrade strength where CBR variability is wide or field conditions differ from laboratory specimens, since back‑calculated resilient modulus values above 15 000 psi must be verified by another test before they can be used. Under the warranty regime, distresses may be attributed to factors beyond the contractor’s control—such as inadequate base condition, traffic loadings exceeding design ESALs, routine Department maintenance, natural disasters or accident‑related damage—which are excluded from responsibility and can obscure the cause of performance failures. The reliability of conclusions also depends on survey accuracy, possible weather‑induced delays to remediation, and resolution through a Conflict Resolution Team. Core‑depth testing is limited to inspector‑selected locations and does not address other performance attributes, leaving uncertainty about conditions not captured by the prescribed sampling regime. The HFST assessment is confined to the early post‑curing period and relies on strict compliance with surface preparation, binder mixing tolerances and wind limits; consequently longer‑term durability and ride quality remain uncertain. Finally, the extended‑season acceptance conclusion applies only after the spring evaluation for work placed during that season, so pavements not subject to that survey or pending repair retain uncertainty. Following a pavement‑performance assessment districts are required to continue annual automated and manual surveys that document fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond or delamination, with a spring‑time manual survey to be completed by May 1 for acceptance or remedial decision [2]. When the most recent performance survey shows that at least thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, the warranty calls for microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment, and if combined deviations exceed twenty percent of the segment surface area the entire segment must be removed and replaced, with repair locations spaced less than one hundred feet merged into a continuous patch [2]. Rut depth shall be verified using a twelve‑foot straight edge across the lane width to determine severity and extent [2]. In addition, annual traffic classification counts are required to ensure that ESAL estimates remain within projected values, and nondestructive testing may be performed by the contractor with prior notice [2].

If base condition concerns arise six‑inch cores shall be extracted from distress zones and tested in accordance with PTM 715, AASHTO T‑209, PTM 702, and PTM 739 to confirm that density exceeds ninety‑two percent of theoretical maximum and that asphalt content and gradation meet mix‑design tolerances; any exceedance of design ESALs or identified base failures shifts responsibility for rutting repairs away from the contractor [2]. Within twenty‑four hours after final compaction, in the presence of the inspector, a four‑inch (or larger) core shall be drilled through all lifts and courses for each two thousand linear feet of lane and measured; when any one core depth measurement indicates the pavement is deficient in depth by more than ¼ inch that cannot be satisfactorily corrected, the affected area shall be considered defective work and must be immediately corrected, extending longitudinally and transversely until all depths are within ¼ inch of the required value [2]. When core depth measurements indicate a uniform deficiency greater than ⅛ inch in any three consecutive core locations that cannot be satisfactorily corrected, the same immediate correction procedure applies, with additional coring forward and back to determine actual extent; holes shall then be backfilled, compacted and sealed with matching mix, and the department may also direct the contractor to provide one loose‑mix box sample taken behind the paver for each direction and one six‑inch diameter core per 1 000 tons placed for laboratory testing [2].

For existing Portland cement concrete pavements a falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD) test shall be performed before any overlay design is prepared, providing valuable information about the stiffness of the existing pavement materials; supplemental Dynamic Cone Penetrometer or Light Weight Deflectometer tests shall also be performed for subgrade strength assessment, and after construction testing shall be repeated to verify new stiffness [1]. When low or uncertain CBR values are identified additional laboratory and/or field testing such as FWD or DCP shall be performed to back‑calculate the resilient modulus, and any Mr value above 15 000 psi must be verified by another form of testing before it is used in design [1].

Overlay design shall include coring of the existing structure during the design phase to verify bond quality; if profile correction is required a separate leveling course shall be applied, and thin Superpave Wearing Course overlays shall be limited to a minimum thickness of 1½ inches only when the surface is asphalt, less than two percent of the pavement requires patching, and both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded, with the overlay using PG 64E‑22 binder and meeting the ADT limits in Table 10.1 [1]. When edge safety is a concern a Safety Edge of at least 1½ inches depth shall be incorporated on the outside edge of asphalt pavements and shoulders, to be used for both wearing and binder courses [1]. If reflective cracking, moisture intrusion, or other distresses are identified an appropriate interlayer—geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh, or composite grid—shall be selected based on the listed distress types, climate, traffic, and cost considerations [1].

When moderate to severe distress is observed corrective actions such as cracking/breaking and seating or rubblizing may be required before placing an unbonded concrete overlay; the unbonded overlay shall employ a fine‑graded one‑inch minimum asphalt separation layer or geotextile interlayer, whereas bonded overlays are reserved for fair‑to‑excellent pavements and unbonded overlays are used when structural deficiencies are moderate to significant [1]. For hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) asphalt the pavement shall be sampled and laboratory tested, including core extraction, to determine the required rejuvenator dosage; rutting severity must be less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth under one inch with an average crack width not exceeding a medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and raveling/weathering shall not exceed the medium rating [1]. Design checks for HIR mixtures require that asphalt pavement thickness be at least 1½ inches more than the milling depth, and for composite pavements a minimum of one to two inches of asphalt must remain below the HIR layer with any paving fabric located at least 1½ inches beneath the cut; corrective actions include milling before the HIR process to treat deeper cracks, removal of raised pavement markers, durable markings, and all mastic patches larger than five square feet, while pavements containing rubber or fiber in the top two inches shall be excluded, and HIR is treated as a relatively short‑term interim wearing surface with a permanent surface course planned before the end of the construction season [1].

Full‑depth reclamation shall begin with a detailed distress survey that records the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues; in‑situ testing such as FWD shall be performed before rehabilitation to gauge existing stiffness and again after construction to verify new stiffness, supplemented by Dynamic Cone Penetrometer or Light Weight Deflectometer tests for subgrade strength, and representative material samples shall be collected according to prescribed number, size, depth and handling criteria for laboratory analysis; the resulting data shall be used to determine appropriate layer thicknesses and to correct any identified drainage problems before implementing full‑depth reclamation, while a stabilization mechanism—pulverization, mechanical or chemical—shall be selected based on design checks and laboratory‑determined stabilizer content [1].

Long‑Life Concrete Pavement projects shall trigger an Evaluation of Concrete Pavement Ride Quality and an Evaluation of Overall Lot Pay Factor to verify compliance with Section 507 and to determine eligibility for incentive payments; districts that specify a lower water‑to‑cement ratio must include contract item 0530‑9999 so that the incentive can be paid after those evaluations, and the incentive amount is set at four percent of the estimated total LLCP SY paving cost unless district experience suggests a lower amount [1].

When excessive deflection or low joint efficiency is identified the contractor shall perform ASTM D4695 deflection testing on every transverse joint and crack while air temperature is between 40 °F and 70 °F, providing results to the representative at least fifteen working days before work; slabs showing a loaded‑slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more with joint efficiency of 65 % or higher shall be stabilized, whereas those below 65 % shall be patched and stabilized as directed, and after grouting a repeat test shall be required twenty‑four hours later; any slab still exceeding the 0.02 inch deflection must be regrouted and retested, with removal and full‑depth concrete replacement possible after two unsuccessful groutings, and additional corrective actions include grinding panels that rise more than 0.05 inch above tolerance, replacing entire slabs if transverse cracks appear between injection holes, and patching all drill holes with rapid‑set non‑shrink concrete flush with the surface [2].

After high‑friction surface treatment (HFST) installation a second aggregate sweep shall be performed between fifteen and thirty days, and any untreated spots, poor aggregate retention, delamination or discoloration shall be prepared and retreated with fresh HFST at no extra cost; the department will conduct a calibrated skid test between forty‑five and one hundred twenty days after completion using the ASTM E274 lock‑wheel apparatus, and the results of that skid test together with visual inspections shall constitute the performance testing and acceptance criteria for the pavement treatment [2]. Density verification for mixtures placed at the minimum compacted depths shall be accomplished by pavement cores regardless of quantity, and deviation from core‑based acceptance may be waived only at the representative’s discretion when optimum density is demonstrated throughout; all post‑paving documentation, including Form CS‑413EQC, must be submitted within twenty‑four hours of paving completion to support final acceptance [2].
