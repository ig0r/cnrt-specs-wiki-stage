Pavement Performance

Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

Pavement Performance is a systematic evaluation of an existing roadway that quantifies both its functional distresses—such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage deficiencies—and its structural capacity through in‑situ testing (e.g., Falling Weight Deflectometer, Dynamic Cone Penetrometer, Light Weight Deflectometer) and laboratory sampling (cores, CBR, mix‑design checks) [1][2]. The investigation determines whether current traffic loads are compatible with the proposed rehabilitation, identifies active distress mechanisms, assesses subgrade stiffness, required layer thicknesses, and any moisture or drainage problems that must be corrected before treatments such as full‑depth reclamation, hot‑in‑place recycling, or overlay placement [1][2]. Performance criteria are expressed through defined severity limits (e.g., rut depth < 1½ in., crack depth < 1 in. with average width ≤ ½ in., raveling no greater than a medium rating) and structural thresholds such as core density > 92 % of theoretical maximum, ensuring that the selected repair strategy meets design life and warranty obligations [1][2].

Purpose and investigation objectives

For full‑depth reclamation candidate pavements (section 5.15 of the Pavement Policy Manual) the Pavement Performance investigation evaluates the existing surface and its supporting layers, focusing on the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage deficiencies while also quantifying subgrade stiffness through in‑situ tests such as Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer. The investigation must answer whether future traffic loads are compatible with the proposed design, what distress mechanisms are currently active and why they occurred, how strong and dry the subgrade is, what layer thicknesses are required to meet structural needs, and whether excess moisture or drainage problems need correction before reclamation. For hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt treatments (section 5.19 of the Pavement Policy Manual) the evaluation examines the surface distress condition of an existing asphalt pavement to determine its suitability for a Hot‑In‑Place Recycled treatment. It must verify that rutting severity is less than 1 ½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth is less than 1.0 inch with average crack width not exceeding the Medium Rating of ≤ ½ inch, raveling or weathering does not exceed the Medium Rating, and any corrugation, shoving or slippage has an average depth less than 1 ½ inches; it also must confirm that asphalt thickness is at least 1 ½ inches greater than the milling depth and that prohibited materials such as rubber or fiber are absent from the top 2 inches. Additional questions include whether the base, subbase and subgrade are sound, whether paving fabric lies at least 1 ½ inches below the cut depth, and whether markers, large mastic patches or extensive sealants have been removed. For asphalt‑concrete pavement covered by the 60‑month warranty (section 496.3 of the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications) the Pavement Performance investigation evaluates the post‑construction condition of the pavement segment by surveying for distresses such as flushing, potholes and rutting in accordance with Publication 336. The investigation asks whether more than 30 % of a segment area exhibits two or more low‑severity distresses, whether any required repair spacing is less than 100 ft which would trigger a continuous repair, and whether rut depth measured with a 12‑ft straight edge indicates severity. It also probes underlying causes by asking if fatigue cracking or other signs point to an unacceptable base condition that is confirmed by six‑inch cores showing a density not greater than 92 % of the theoretical maximum or asphalt content out of tolerance, and whether cumulative ESALs have exceeded 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life. The investigation further evaluates the depth condition of the binder and wearing courses by taking cores within 24 hours after final compaction and asks whether any single core shows a depth deficiency greater than 1/4 inch that cannot be satisfactorily corrected, or whether a uniform deficiency greater than 1/8 inch exists across three consecutive core locations that also cannot be satisfactorily corrected; affirmative answers designate the pavement area as defective work. For asphalt‑concrete courses placed under the extended‑season paving provisions of Section 413.3(o) (section 413.3 of the same specification) Pavement Performance evaluates the post‑construction condition by surveying for fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling or weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond or delamination, and must determine whether each observed distress type meets the acceptance criteria in Publication 336 to decide if remedial action is required. In the Pavement Policy Manual, section 4.9 Rehabilitation, performance data obtained from a Falling Weight Deflectometer test that is required and should be conducted on existing PCCP before designing an overlay directs the selection of rehabilitation strategy by indicating whether the structure is in good‑to‑fair condition suitable for a bonded concrete overlay or moderately to significantly deteriorated and therefore better served by an unbonded overlay. The same manual notes that when the assessment shows slab rocking, pumping, faulting, or inadequate drainage, surface preparation steps such as full‑depth patching, slab stabilization, or installation of subdrainage are prescribed before any overlay can be placed. Overlay thickness is also tied to the identified condition; thin‑bonded JPCP overlays of 2.5 to 4 inches may be used on asphalt or composite pavements to correct surface defects, while thicker bonded overlays of 4 to 8 inches are considered when additional structural capacity is needed. For severely distressed existing concrete, the manual recommends methods such as cracking/breaking and seating, rubblizing to 12‑inch or 8‑inch pieces, or providing a minimum 1‑inch asphalt separation layer for unbonded overlays to achieve a stable platform for the new pavement. In section 4.12 Long‑Life Concrete Pavement, the finding that LLCP delivers longer service life when high‑performance dowel bars are used and the water‑cement ratio is reduced allows planners to justify selecting LLCP over conventional concrete where durability is critical. The specification that the incentive shall be 4 percent of the estimated total LLCP SY paving cost provides a quantitative lever for budgeting and design decisions, while the mandatory ride‑quality incentive under Section 530 ensures that performance testing of surface smoothness directly influences payment and therefore construction quality. Because LLCP is not appropriate for thin (less than 8 inches) or bonded concrete pavement applications, engineers can screen suitable sections during rehabilitation planning, and the decision tree outlined in the guidelines helps districts choose the appropriate item‑of‑work classification based on site constraints such as a central mix plant needing approximately 4 acres and a slip‑form paver requiring a minimum clearance of 3 feet on each side. Section 5.12 Overlays links pavement performance evaluation to the selection and design of an appropriate overlay; when the existing surface is asphalt, structurally sound, requires less than 2 % patching, and has good surface and subsurface drainage, a thin Superpave wearing course as thin as 6.3 mm or 9.5 mm may be placed instead of the standard 1½‑inch minimum. If those conditions are not met, a leveling course of at least 1 inch or a full‑depth overlay is required. When reflective cracking or moisture damage is observed, an interlayer such as geotextile fabric or fiberglass grid can be incorporated, with the recommendation that overlays over interlayers be at least 1½ inches thick for milling. The presence of edge drop‑offs calls for inclusion of a Safety Edge with a depth of 1½ inches to 5 inches, improving driver recovery while adding roughly 1 % material. In section 5.15 Full‑Depth Reclamation Techniques, the performance assessment begins with a reliable estimate of future traffic loading, which ensures that any full‑depth reclamation design remains consistent with standard traffic analysis procedures, and a recent pavement condition survey that records the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues to identify the underlying damage mechanisms. Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements before rehabilitation supply valuable information on existing stiffness, while post‑construction tests confirm the new pavement’s structural capacity; additional DCP or LWD tests may be used for subgrade evaluation. Sampling of reclaimed materials follows detailed criteria to verify composition and suitability for the chosen stabilization method, together guiding project selection, determining appropriate layer thicknesses and drainage corrections, and dictating whether pulverization, mechanical or chemical stabilization is required, thereby supporting planning, design, maintenance and rehabilitation decisions. Section 5.19 Hot In‑Place Recycled Asphalt defines a decision framework in which candidate pavements should have rutting severity < 1½ inches, transverse or miscellaneous cracking depth < 1.0 inch with average crack width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, raveling/weathering severity not exceed the medium rating, and minor corrugation, shoving or slippage with an average depth < 1½ inches. Design must provide asphalt pavement thickness at least 1½ inches more than the milling depth, and any paving fabric must be a minimum of 1½ inches below the specified HIR cut depth; for composite pavements designers should specify a minimum of 1 to 2 inches of asphalt beneath the HIR layer (and 2 to 3 inches for full‑depth asphalt). By confirming these thresholds through assessment, sampling and testing of existing pavement, engineers can select projects where HIR will remediate surface distress, improve performance and allow traffic reopening once the surface cools, thereby supporting rehabilitation planning, design specifications, and maintenance scheduling. Section 6.2 Subgrade Soils states that all CBR values greater than 10 for use in pavement designs shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, and in no case will a CBR value greater than 20 be allowed to be used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation, which directs designers to seek approval before incorporating higher strength subgrades. If the lowest CBR values are located in isolated areas of the project, designers consider improving the subgrade in those areas to achieve CBR values that are representative of the overall project conditions. When large areas of a project have significantly different soil types and CBR values it may be permissible to divide the project into a limited number of subsections with different pavement design sections, optimizing material use and construction sequencing. For rehabilitation overlays where the existing pavement will not be removed, the in‑situ density condition requires multiplying the CBR by 1,000 instead of 1,500 when using the Heukelom and Klomp equation to obtain a more conservative Mr estimate. If back‑calculated Mr from FWD data yields a value above 15,000 psi it must be verified by another form of testing prior to its use in the pavement design process, thereby increasing confidence in planning, design, maintenance and rehabilitation decisions. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 Specifications, Section 496—Asphalt Concrete Pavement, 60‑Month Warranty requires annual performance surveys; when the year‑5 survey shows that 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, the warranty mandates microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment, directly guiding rehabilitation planning. Those same surveys together with traffic classification counts are used to verify that cumulative ESALs have not exceeded 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life, informing design adjustments such as mix changes if overload is detected. Rut depth measurements verified with a 12‑foot straight edge and core testing of suspected base failures provide quantitative evidence for maintenance decisions and determine contractor responsibility, while distances between repair areas less than 100 feet trigger a single continuous repair, guiding the scheduling of maintenance activities. Core depth measurements that indicate a deficiency in depth greater than 1/4 inch for a single core, or a uniform deficiency greater than 1/8 inch in any three consecutive cores, classify the pavement area as defective work and trigger immediate corrective placement, which can be used to prioritize maintenance or rehabilitation actions. The mandated collection of one loose‑mix box sample per 1,000 tons placed and one 6‑inch core provides laboratory data on mix quality that can be fed back into design specifications and future planning to ensure warranty compliance and long‑term durability. Section 659—High Friction Surface Treatment requires a second aggregate cleanup between 15 to 30 days after placement and a calibrated skid test a minimum of 31 days after installation; the results identify sections with inadequate friction or visible defects, which are then removed, prepared and retreated at no extra cost, reinforcing design decisions about cure periods and confirming that the treatment will perform as intended over its service life. Section 679—Slab Stabilization uses deflection testing (ASTM D4695) to provide quantitative measures of slab distress; when a loaded slab corner deflection reaches 0.02 inch or more, slabs with joint efficiency at least 65 % are slated for stabilization, while those with efficiency less than 65 % receive patch‑and‑stabilize treatment, directly dictating repair actions and informing project scheduling. Because testing is prohibited when air temperature is below 40°F or exceeds 70°F, the timing of data collection also informs project scheduling and ensures reliable performance metrics for planning, design and maintenance actions. Scope and applicability

Pavement Performance assessment is appropriate for Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (LLCP) when the pavement employs high‑performance dowel bars and is placed as a full‑depth replacement with new concrete under tightly controlled subgrade preparation, mixing, joint placement, finishing, curing and sawing; the pavement must be at least 8 inches thick and cannot be a bonded concrete pavement [1]. The site for an LLCP project must be capable of accommodating a central mix plant occupying approximately 4 acres within the project limits and a slip‑form paver that provides a minimum clearance of 3 feet on each side [1].

For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) mixtures, Pavement Performance is appropriate when the treatment is applied to surface‑distressed asphalt pavements whose underlying base and subgrade are sound; the distresses must be limited to rutting less than 1½ inches, transverse or miscellaneous cracking with a depth less than 1 inch and an average crack width not exceeding ½ inch, raveling or weathering rated no more than medium, and minor corrugation, shoving or slippage with an average depth under 1½ inches, while bleeding may be present only in isolated areas and low friction numbers are acceptable [1]. The existing asphalt layer must be at least 1½ inches thicker than the milling depth, any paving fabric must lie at least 1½ inches below the HIR cut, and pavements containing rubber or fiber material in the top 2 inches are excluded; raised markers, durable markings, large mastic patches greater than 5 ft² and extensive crack sealant or over‑banded rubberized joint seal must be removed before treatment [1]. Suitable projects include structurally sound, well‑drained composite or full‑depth asphalt pavements, concrete decks with stable joints, and specialty mixes such as stone matrix asphalt or open‑graded friction courses [1].

Pavement Performance is also applied to asphalt‑concrete pavements that are covered by the 60‑month warranty described in Section 496.3(c); under this warranty the pavement must meet the specified minimum thickness, achieve a density greater than 92 % of theoretical maximum density, and have its asphalt content and gradation within the Contractor’s mix‑design tolerances, with remedial action required if the year‑5 survey determines that 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses [2]. The warranty further requires verification of underlying base condition by coring and laboratory testing, and performance criteria are deemed unmet if traffic counts indicate cumulative ESALs have exceeded 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life ESALs or if beyond‑control factors such as base failure, extreme weather, natural disasters, or destructive Department procedures occur [2]. Pavement Performance under this warranty is intended for asphalt concrete pavements constructed as a combination of two or more different asphalt concrete courses; depth testing of binder and wearing courses must be performed within 24 hours after final compaction, and a single core that exceeds the required depth by more than ¼ inch or three consecutive cores each exceeding the requirement by more than ⅛ inch will deem the area defective work [2]. The warranty also mandates informational sampling of loose‑mix box samples at a rate of one per placement and pavement core samples at a rate of one for each 1,000 tons of material placed, to be taken in the presence of the inspector [2].

Section 413 extends Pavement Performance assessment to flexible asphalt pavements such as PG 64E‑22 wearing courses, wearing courses designed for more than 10 million ESALs, 4.75 mm wearing courses, and other asphalt concrete layers placed at compacted depths less than 1½ inches, as well as to all other asphalt courses; the post‑construction evaluation focuses on distresses that include fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling or weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond or delamination [2]. This assessment is only appropriate when placement occurs within the prescribed seasonal windows— for example April 1 to October 15 for the specified wearing courses or April 1 to October 31 for other courses—and when surface conditions are not wet and the air or surface temperature exceeds 40°F [2].

Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) asphalt treatment is considered a relatively short‑term interim wearing surface and therefore must be followed by a permanent surface course before the construction season ends [1]. The HIR process may only be applied when distress is confined to the pavement surface and satisfies the Publication 336 severity limits, namely rutting less than 1 ½ inches, transverse or miscellaneous cracking depth less than 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and raveling or weathering rated no greater than medium [1]. Pavements that exhibit load‑related distress, base problems, rubber in the top two inches, fiber material in the top two inches, extensive mastic patches larger than 5 sq ft, or widespread seal‑coat‑only conditions fall outside these limits, so another or complementary investigation method such as full‑depth reconstruction or alternative surface treatments should be considered [1]. If the asphalt thickness remaining after milling is not at least 1 ½ inches greater than the cut depth, or if paving fabric lies within 1 ½ inches of the HIR cut, the HIR approach is unsuitable and alternative evaluation is required [1]. The reliability of pavement‑performance assessments that rely on laboratory CBR values is limited when those values exceed the thresholds set by the design guidelines; any CBR greater than 10 must receive Chief Geotechnical Engineer approval and no CBR greater than 20 may be used in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation [1]. When the validity of a tested CBR is uncertain or when CBR results show a wide range across the project, additional laboratory or field testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer back‑calculation or Dynamic Cone Penetration should be employed to corroborate the design input [1]. Back‑calculated resilient modulus from FWD data is not an exact science and may produce erroneous values if pavement layer information is inaccurate, the section changes frequently, or existing pavement shows distress; consequently any Mr value above 15 000 psi must be verified by another testing method before it is used [1]. Under the 60‑month warranty, pavement‑performance assessment is limited to periodic automated or manual distress surveys that record only the types and extents defined in Publication 336, and remedial work is triggered only when a segment reaches the threshold of 30 % of segment area with two or more low‑severity distresses [2]. These surveys do not directly identify underlying causes such as base failures, excessive traffic loads, or forces of nature; therefore, when evidence suggests that performance criteria are not being met—or when distress may stem from factors beyond the contractor’s control—the Department may schedule additional or more frequent performance surveys, conduct core drilling and laboratory testing (requiring minimum thickness and density greater than 92 % of theoretical maximum), or permit nondestructive testing with at least seven calendar days notice to complement the standard evaluation [2].

Existing information and records

A Pavement Performance review should begin by obtaining a reliable estimate of future traffic loading on the road because traffic level drives design and evaluation. It must include a recent pavement condition survey—normally following Publication 336 or 343—or an equivalent distress procedure such as MicroPaver™, which records current distresses such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems together with their causes. In‑situ testing results are required, especially Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements taken before rehabilitation together with dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer data to assess subgrade stiffness and support conditions. Appropriate material sampling—covering the number of samples, locations, depth, size, handling and identification of layers—should be collected according to the outlined sampling criteria. The review should also examine all prior performance survey results that identified, measured, and rated distresses in accordance with Publication 336, including any manual surveys for flushing or potholes, and rut‑depth measurements verified with a 12‑foot straight edge. Traffic classification counts must be incorporated to confirm that the Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) estimates remain within the projected values. Core sampling test data on base condition should be considered when fatigue cracking is observed. Records of emergency repair notifications and any remedial work triggered by the threshold that thirty percent of a segment’s area has two or more low‑severity distresses must also be reviewed. The condition survey should verify that surface distresses meet the HIR candidate thresholds, namely rutting severity less than one and a half inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth less than one inch with an average crack width not exceeding a half inch, and raveling or weathering severity not exceeding a medium rating. Additional observations on minor corrugation, shoving and slippage with an average depth less than one and a half inches, low friction numbers, and ensuring that asphalt thickness is at least one and a half inches greater than the milling depth also constitute required performance information. Finally, the assessment should start with a comprehensive evaluation of existing pavement, base, subbase and subgrade conditions, sampling and testing of the pavement, together with a study of construction and maintenance history and traffic data. Under section 4.9 REHABILITATION of the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10), field investigation must supply the missing structural data, verify joint condition, and assess support that are not captured in existing records; a Falling Weight Deflectometer test is needed before any overlay design, core samples of joints must be taken to confirm pavement condition, the presence of slab movement or faulting has to be identified so that stabilization or jacking can be applied if required, and the adequacy of subsurface drainage must be evaluated. Visual inspection and condition assessment

Pavement Performance examinations should record transverse joint faulting, the presence of fines near joints or cracks on traffic lanes or shoulders, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, and corner breaks as indicators of loss of slab support. It should also note any slab movement or faulting, including rocking slabs, and any active pumping of water beneath the concrete that signals poor subgrade conditions. Surface spalling that reflects loss of material integrity is another condition to be documented, together with structural deficiencies or surface deterioration that affect rideability. Visible cracking, including alligator (fatigue) cracking, as well as transverse and miscellaneous cracking, must be observed because they can progress to a point where patching becomes uneconomical. Rutting, bleeding, raveling, potholes, and drainage problems are also listed among the distresses that should be surveyed to understand damage mechanisms. For asphalt‑concrete pavements covered by the 60‑month warranty, flushing and potholes are specifically identified for manual survey, while rut depth is verified with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width to determine severity and extent. The performance review for high‑friction surface treatment projects must detect visible cracks that are one‑quarter inch or greater in width, remove loose material, pre‑treat those cracks, and identify untreated spots where bare asphalt or concrete is exposed; it also must locate areas showing poor aggregate retention, delamination, or color discoloration as signs of deterioration requiring repair before HFST application. Under slab‑stabilization specifications, visual verification must record pumping or any upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch, radial cracks radiating from grout injection holes, transverse cracks developing between adjacent grout injection holes, and panel raise beyond the 0.05‑inch allowable tolerance, each of which may trigger replacement or grinding actions. Joints or cracks that exhibit a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more, together with a joint efficiency at 65 percent or less (or at 65 percent or more if approved), require stabilization. The distress inventory for hot‑in‑place recycled candidate pavements is limited to surface‑level problems; load‑related base distress is excluded. Rutting severity must be less than 1 ½ inches. Transverse and miscellaneous cracking severity should not exceed a depth of < 1.0 inch, and the average crack width should not exceed the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch. Raveling or weathering severity should not surpass the medium rating, described as a rough, pitted surface that may have loose particles. Minor corrugation, shoving, and slippage are acceptable provided their average depth is less than 1 ½ inches. If the final‑year (year 5) performance survey determines that thirty percent of a segment area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment must be applied. Water intrusion into a pavement’s structural section is highlighted as a major cause of loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation, and freeze‑thaw related deterioration, and therefore should be noted during performance evaluation. Field measurements and testing

Pavement Performance uses Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing on existing Portland cement concrete pavements prior to any overlay design; the test records pavement deflections under a 9,000‑lb load and permits back‑calculation of the subgrade resilient modulus (Mr), thereby providing data on current structural condition that guides selection of an appropriate rehabilitation strategy [1]. After construction, FWD testing is again employed to determine the stiffness of the newly placed pavement layer, offering a nondestructive indication of the achieved structural performance [1]. The Evaluation of Concrete Pavement Ride Quality required by Publication 408 Section 507 is applied to long‑life concrete pavements as a nondestructive measure of smoothness and user comfort [1]. When a water‑to‑cement ratio incentive is specified for long‑life concrete projects, an Evaluation of Overall Lot Pay Factor is performed to verify that the lower W/C ratio has been achieved, supporting durability objectives [1]. A recent pavement condition survey conducted in accordance with Publication 336, Publication 343 or a distress procedure such as MicroPaver™ supplies information on the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage deficiencies, and also offers insight into the underlying causes of visible distress [1]. In‑situ subgrade testing may be carried out with a Dynamic Cone Penetrometer (DCP), in which a 17.6‑lb hammer drives a metal cone repeatedly and the penetration per blow is measured; the resulting resistance‑to‑penetration profile can be converted to Mr through the CBR correlation, thereby indicating subgrade strength [1]. The Light Weight Deflectometer (LWD) is also listed as useful for evaluating subgrade conditions, providing additional nondestructive deflection data that complements FWD results [1]. California Bearing Ratio (CBR) testing of subgrade, subbase or base materials assesses potential strength and supplies the values needed to develop Mr and elastic modulus k for design; when laboratory CBR results are uncertain, supplemental field testing with FWD or DCP may be performed for validation [1]. The visual distress survey required for Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) treatments records rutting severity, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth, average crack width, raveling/weathering rating, corrugation/shoving depth, presence of bleeding, friction numbers, and pavement thickness relative to the milling depth; acceptable limits are rutting severity less than 1 ½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth less than 1.0 inch, average crack width not exceeding the Medium Rating of ≤ ½ inch, corrugation/shoving average depth less than 1 ½ inches, and asphalt pavement thickness at least 1 ½ inches greater than the milling depth, each indicating that the existing surface is suitable for HIR treatment by confirming limited rutting, shallow and narrow cracking, moderate surface loss, minor longitudinal distress, acceptable skid resistance, and sufficient structural thickness after milling [1]. Under a 60‑month warranty, Pavement Performance is primarily assessed through annual automated surveys that identify, measure, and report distresses in accordance with Publication 336; manual surveys are used to supplement these results for flushing and potholes, providing the data base for performance evaluation [2]. Rut depth measurements are verified with a 12‑foot straight edge placed across the lane width to determine severity and extent of rutting [2]. Routine traffic classification counts are performed to confirm that equivalent single axle load (ESAL) estimates remain within projected values, linking observed performance to anticipated traffic demand [2]. The contractor may monitor or test warranted pavement courses using nondestructive methods at any time after notice during the warranty period, and must submit the results to the Department for review [2]. Construction verification requires drilling a 4‑inch (or larger) diameter core through all lifts and courses within approximately 2,000 ft intervals and within 24 hours of final compaction to measure binder and wearing‑course thickness; a single core indicating a depth deficiency greater than ¼ inch that cannot be corrected flags defective work, while a uniform deficiency greater than ⅛ inch in three consecutive cores also triggers defect status [2]. Loose‑mix box samples taken directly behind the paver before compaction and six‑inch diameter cores for each 1,000 tons placed are labeled, packaged, and delivered for later laboratory quality checks, ensuring that material properties meet specifications [2]. For High‑Friction Surface Treatment (HFST), a calibrated skid test is performed at least 31 days after placement in accordance with ASTM E274/E274M (AASHTO T 242) using a smooth‑tire lock‑wheel apparatus to quantify friction or skid resistance, thereby indicating the effectiveness of the treatment in providing adequate surface traction [2]. Construction quality for HFST is verified by measuring a uniform binder film thickness of 50–65 mil (25–32 sq ft per gallon) and an aggregate placement rate of 12–15 lb per square yard, which indicate proper bonding and saturation of the surface layer [2]. Slab‑stabilization evaluation includes a field deflection test performed according to ASTM D4695; the loaded slab corner deflection together with the unloaded deflection yields a joint efficiency percentage, and stabilization is required when the deflection is ≥ 0.02 in and the joint efficiency is ≥ 65 % [2]. A vertical‑movement test using equipment capable of measuring lift to within 0.001 in detects upward pavement movement that may not exceed 0.05 in, providing a nondestructive indication of potential heave problems [2]. Pavement‑core sampling is used to verify that in‑place density meets acceptance criteria; cores are required regardless of quantity for mixtures placed at minimum compacted depths, supplying direct evidence of compaction quality [2]. A spring visual/manual performance survey conducted per Publication 336 records surface distresses such as fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond/delamination, furnishing the basis for acceptance or remedial action [2].

Sampling and laboratory investigation

For a full‑depth reclamation (FDR) project, pavement performance sampling should include material from each existing pavement layer that will be reclaimed, as well as the subgrade, following the criteria set out for FDR candidate roadways [1]. The number of samples and their locations, the size of each sample, the specific sampling techniques, the depth of sampling together with clear identification of the layers, and the handling and evaluation procedures are all defined in Appendix J, Section J.2.D (subsections J.2.D.1 through J.2.D.5) [1]. Structural and functional performance

Evaluation of structural capacity for rehabilitation of existing Portland cement concrete pavement requires Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing on the existing PCCP prior to designing an overlay, with deflection measurements obtained at midslab locations that are not cracked using a 9,000‑pound load in accordance with ASTM D 4694/D 4695; these measurements are back‑calculated to a subgrade resilient modulus and when the back‑calculated Mr exceeds 15 000 psi it must be verified by another form of testing [1]. California Bearing Ratio (CBR) testing of the subgrade, subbase or base course is also required, and any CBR value greater than 10 shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer while values greater than 20 are not allowed to be used in the Heukelom‑and‑Klomp correlation [1]. Additional in‑situ testing such as Dynamic Cone Penetrometer (DCP) and Light Weight Deflectometer (LWD) tests may be employed to assess subgrade strength and can be converted to Mr values via CBR conversion, and determination of existing layer thicknesses and verification of adequate drainage complete the required measurements for assessing both structural capacity and functional condition [1]. Functional condition assessment includes measurement of distress such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues; for Hot‑In‑Place Recycled asphalt projects rutting severity shall be less than 1 ½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth must be a depth < 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding the Medium Rating of ≤ ½ inch, raveling or weathering severity should not exceed the Medium Rating, and minor corrugation, shoving or slippage are acceptable provided their average depth is < 1 ½ inches; low friction numbers are tolerated and the existing asphalt pavement thickness must be at least 1 ½ inches more than the milling depth [1]. For Long‑Life Concrete Pavement the performance assessment focuses on evaluating concrete pavement ride quality as required by the acceptance criteria, which underpins the ride‑quality incentive specified in Section 507 of Publication 408 [1]. Under the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation 60‑month warranty for asphalt concrete pavement, structural capacity is evaluated by measuring rut depth with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width and by drilling 6‑inch diameter cores to obtain layer thickness; core density must exceed 92 % of the theoretical maximum and the percent asphalt and gradation of the cores are analyzed against the tolerances indicated in the contractor’s mix design, while traffic classification counts confirm that ESAL estimates remain within projected values [2]. Structural layer thickness is measured by coring each lane at intervals of about 2 000 ft within 24 hours after final compaction, and any single core showing a deficiency of more than ¼ inch or a uniform deficiency of more than ⅛ inch in three consecutive cores is deemed defective work [2]. Data interpretation and diagnosis

Both documents state that visual surveys are the first step in evaluating pavement performance. In the Pavement Policy Manual section 4.9 Rehabilitation, a visual inspection of the existing concrete pavement identifies distress such as rocking, pumping, faulting or surface spalling; this observation establishes the initial condition that determines whether stabilization, full‑depth patching, or a particular overlay type is required [1]. The visual assessment is then quantified by field measurements, specifically an FWD test performed on the PCCP before any overlay design, and it is verified through laboratory results such as cores taken from existing joints to confirm a good condition [1]. When the combined visual and measured conditions indicate a pavement in good to fair structural state, a bonded JPCP overlay may be selected, whereas moderate to severe deterioration leads to an unbonded overlay or another rehabilitation method, and the integrated interpretation guides the preparation steps and overlay specifications outlined in the rehabilitation guidelines [1]. In section 5.15 Full‑Depth Reclamation Techniques, project selection starts with a condition survey that documents visible distress—including rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems—and links those observations to likely damage mechanisms; the survey is summarized in a report that records the level of distress and corresponding observations [1]. The documented distress is combined with in‑situ strength tests such as Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer or lightweight deflectometer to quantify existing stiffness and subgrade support [1]. Representative material samples are collected according to the sampling criteria and tested in the laboratory to determine the appropriate stabilization type and dosage, and the resulting mix design is checked against the observed distress and measured stiffness [1]. After construction a post‑rehabilitation FWD test provides performance data that can be compared with the pre‑rehab measurements and the laboratory‑derived expectations to confirm that the reclaimed layer meets the required structural capacity [1]. In section 5.19 Hot In‑Place Recycled Asphalt, candidate projects are evaluated by combining visual inspection of surface distresses with measured rutting and cracking values and laboratory sampling results; visual checks must confirm that distress is limited to the surface and that raveling or weathering does not exceed a medium rating [1]. Field measurements require Rutting Severity < 1 1/2 inches, Transverse and Miscellaneous Cracking Severity depth < 1.0 inch, the average crack width should not exceed the Medium Rating of ≤ 1/2 inch, and minor corrugation or shoving depths under 1 1/2 inches; sampling and testing of the existing pavement are used to design rejuvenator quantities, and a review of construction‑maintenance history and traffic data confirms that the base and subgrade are sound [1]. Asphalt pavement thickness must be at least 1 1/2 inches more than the milling depth for the HIR treatment to be considered appropriate [1]. Under the 60‑month warranty in the Pennsylvania specifications, performance is first assessed by automated (and when needed manual) surveys that visually locate and rate distresses according to Publication 336; the identified distress limits from Table A are then compared with field measurements such as rut depth verified with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width and traffic classification counts that confirm ESAL estimates [2]. When visual or measured signs of fatigue cracking appear, six‑inch cores are extracted and tested per PTM No. 715, AASHTO T 209, PTM No. 702 and PTM No. 739 to evaluate voids, asphalt content and gradation, providing laboratory confirmation of base condition [2]. All these data streams are integrated in the annual performance analysis; if the year‑5 survey shows that 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, the threshold triggers required remediation such as microsurfacing [2]. Both documents emphasize that the integration of visual observations, quantitative field tests, laboratory verification, and performance data provides the basis for selecting appropriate rehabilitation techniques, confirming that design expectations have been met, and determining whether remedial actions are required under warranty provisions [1][2].

The Pavement Policy Manual identifies that insufficient subsurface drainage, which allows water to enter the pavement structure, is a primary contributor to loss of slab support and subsequent distress (section 4.5) [1]. Rehabilitation guidance in section 4.9 links subsurface drainage deficiencies and weak or unstable support such as slab movement, pumping, or faulting with material deterioration manifested as surface spalling, cracking, pumping and faulted slabs [1]. Section 5.12 requires that surface and subsurface drainage be good or upgraded before thin overlays; it notes that active pumping signals a poor subgrade (weak support) and attributes all‑igator cracking across the entire surface to age oxidation of the pavement, while also citing freeze‑thaw cycles and general aging as additional causes [1]. Full depth reclamation guidance in section 5.15 emphasizes that inadequate drainage and excess moisture that erodes material integrity, together with insufficient subgrade stiffness revealed by in‑situ testing, point to weak support layers and material deterioration such as rutting, cracking, raveling and potholes [1]. The hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt criteria in section 5.19 state that when rutting severity exceeds the < 1½ inch limit, transverse or miscellaneous cracking depth exceeds the < 1.0 inch threshold, or raveling/weathering rises above a medium rating, these exceedances indicate weak support (base/subgrade deficiencies) or material deterioration, and only well‑drained bases are acceptable [1]. Section 6.2 notes that low CBR values less than 5 may require undercutting or stabilization, that frost action can cause differential heaving and freeze/thaw damage, and that isolated low‑CBR areas need improvement, all of which contribute to poor performance [1]. Under the 60‑month warranty for asphalt concrete pavement (Section 496), fatigue cracking or similar distresses that might indicate an unacceptable base condition are deemed a probable cause of weak support, and traffic counts showing cumulative ESALs that have exceeded 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life may be interpreted as evidence of overloading [2]. The same warranty treats floods, earthquakes, tornadoes, brush fires, landslides, sinkholes and other natural disasters, as well as fuel or chemical spills, vehicle fires, and gouging or goring from traffic accidents, as external environmental exposures beyond the contractor’s responsibility; it does not attribute distress to inadequate drainage, material deterioration, construction defects, or aging [2]. Section 496 also classifies any core that reveals a depth deficiency greater than 1/4 inch which cannot be satisfactorily corrected, or a uniform deficiency greater than 1/8 inch over three consecutive cores that likewise cannot be remedied, as defective work and identifies it as the primary cause of the observed condition; other factors such as drainage or loading are not addressed [2]. For high‑friction surface treatment (Section 659), observed deficiencies are most likely tied to construction errors such as overfilling cracks, binder contamination, or failure to properly clean and pre‑treat the pavement, and to adverse environmental conditions when wind speed exceeds 15 mph or gusts reach 20 mph or higher; drainage or support issues are not cited [2]. In the Superpave mixture design and plant‑mixed asphalt construction specifications (Section 413), a spring manual survey evaluates fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond/delamination; any defective material or workmanship identified through this inspection may be considered the likely cause of distress, while inadequate drainage, weak support, overloading, environmental exposure, and aging are not addressed [2].

Findings, confidence, and next actions

Pavement performance assessment provides several specific conclusions regarding suitable rehabilitation and construction practices, while also revealing a range of uncertainties that limit confidence in long‑term outcomes. The Pavement Policy Manual indicates that overlays—rigid or flexible—may be used to correct structural deficiencies, surface deterioration, or rideability problems, and that thin Superpave wearing courses are permissible only when the existing surface is asphalt, structurally sound, less than 2 % of the pavement requires patching, drainage is good, and a minimum thickness of 1 ½ inches is provided on Interstates (or where approved) and the same minimum on non‑Interstates under those conditions [1]. The same manual recommends the Safety Edge as a standard edge treatment for wearing or binder courses with a depth of at least 1 ½ inches and not exceeding 5 inches, adding roughly 1 % to material quantities [1]. Interlayer systems may be incorporated to mitigate reflective cracking and provide moisture barriers, but they are limited to addressing crack‑related distress and do not normally treat rutting, bleeding, raveling, or water intrusion from underground sources, leaving some uncertainty about long‑term performance under severe climate or traffic conditions [1]. Performance data gathered during the initial evaluation—reliable future traffic estimates, a recent condition survey that records distress types such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems, and in‑situ stiffness measurements from Falling Weight Deflectometer testing—allow engineers to conclude whether full‑depth reclamation is an appropriate rehabilitation strategy and to define the required layer thicknesses and stabilization method, yet those conclusions remain limited by uncertainties in traffic forecasts, the snapshot nature of distress surveys, the representativeness of sampled materials, and the need to correctly identify and remediate drainage issues before reclamation; additionally, while increasing chemical stabilizer generally raises strength, excessive amounts may cause brittleness, introducing further performance uncertainty [1]. The investigation shows that hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt mixtures can remediate rutting and other surface distress, recover and improve pavement performance, enhance appearance, and prolong service life, and they are considered suitable for any level of average daily traffic; nevertheless, the treatment is described as a relatively short‑term interim wearing surface that must be followed by a permanent surface course before the construction season ends, and acceptable candidate pavements are limited to those whose rutting severity is less than 1 ½ inches, whose transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth is under 1 inch with an average crack width not exceeding a medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and whose raveling or weathering does not exceed the medium rating, while also requiring at least 1 ½ inches of asphalt below the HIR depth; pavements containing rubber in the top layers, fiber materials within the upper 2 inches, or extensive mastic patches are excluded, and removal of durable markings and certain sealants is required, indicating that project selection and proper sampling/testing remain critical sources of uncertainty for achieving the expected performance gains [1]. The manual further states that pavement design may use the lowest subgrade CBR value to control thickness, but any CBR value greater than 10 must be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and no CBR value greater than 20 may be used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation; when laboratory CBR results are uncertain additional laboratory or field testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer or Dynamic Cone Penetrometer should be performed, yet back‑calculated resilient modulus from Falling Weight Deflectometer data is not an exact science and values above 15 000 psi must be verified by another test before they can be used, leaving residual uncertainty about subgrade strength where CBR variability is wide or field conditions differ from laboratory specimens [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications provide that under the 60‑month warranty a pavement segment complies with the distress limits defined in Table A when the year‑5 survey shows less than thirty percent of the segment area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, and if the threshold is reached the specification requires remedial treatment such as microsurfacing [2]. The warranty program also supports conclusions about durability and ride quality because annual performance analyses are performed and additional surveys may be scheduled when evidence suggests non‑compliance, but those conclusions are limited by the fact that the Contractor is not responsible for distresses caused by base condition deficiencies, traffic loadings that exceed design ESALs, routine Department maintenance, destructive procedures, uncontrolled forces of nature, or accident‑related damage, and any disagreement over survey results must be resolved through a Conflict Resolution Team, which introduces uncertainty about the true cause of observed distress [2]. The investigation can further conclude that a pavement segment complies with the warranty when every core depth measured under the construction requirements is within the allowable limits—no single core shows a deficiency of more than ¼ inch and no set of three consecutive cores shows a uniform deficiency of more than ⅛ inch—and any area that exceeds those thresholds is deemed defective work; this conclusion rests on the prescribed sampling regime—one core per 2,000 ft of lane and occasional loose‑mix or core information samples tied to material placement—so only limited locations are inspected and other performance attributes such as long‑term durability or ride quality are not addressed, leaving uncertainty about conditions that fall between sampled points [2]. For high‑friction surface treatment the investigation can confirm that performance is evaluated by a calibrated skid test performed at least 31 days after installation using ASTM E274/E274M procedures, and any areas showing bare pavement, poor aggregate retention, delamination or discoloration must be identified and retreated before final acceptance; however, the assessment is confined to this early‑age friction test and visual defect repair, leaving uncertainty about performance beyond that initial period [2]. Finally, the Superpave mixture design specifications state that when asphalt mixtures are placed within the prescribed seasonal windows—e.g., April 1 to October 15 for PG 64E‑22 wearing courses or other courses placed at compacted depths less than 1.5 inches—and the air or surface temperature is above 40°F and a pre‑placement QC plan has been approved, the pavement will be eligible for acceptance pending a spring manual performance survey; the survey conducted by May 1 evaluates observed distresses such as fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond/delamination to determine whether remedial action is required, thereby providing a concrete basis for concluding that the pavement meets durability and safety criteria; nevertheless, uncertainties remain because performance cannot be confirmed until after the spring evaluation, acceptance may still be waived at the Representative’s discretion, extended‑season paving introduces additional requirements such as density acceptance by cores, and weather constraints can force halts, all of which limit confidence in early predictions of long‑term pavement behavior [2].

Following a pavement‑performance assessment, loss‑of‑support symptoms such as transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks, small depressions at shoulders, corner breaks, or slab settlement trigger slab stabilization performed in accordance with Publication 72M and Publication 408; when no preliminary testing has been conducted the procedure shall stabilize at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints using cement, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane grout as appropriate to site conditions, and joint and crack sealing must be carried out together with slab stabilization to limit water infiltration while subsurface drains are kept in good condition as part of ongoing preservation [1]. A falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD) test shall be performed on the existing PCCP before any overlay design is prepared [1]. The surface preparation plan shall then include full‑depth patching of the original slab when approved, slab stabilization or jacking wherever slab movement or faulting is observed, and provision of adequate subdrainage to correct subsurface drainage problems [1]. For pavements showing moderate to severe distress, corrective actions such as cracking/breaking and seating or rubblizing may be required to provide a stable base before placing an unbonded concrete overlay, the latter employing a fine‑graded minimum 1‑inch asphalt separation layer or geotextile interlayer, with bonded overlays reserved for fair‑to‑excellent pavements and unbonded overlays used when structural deficiencies are moderate to significant [1]. Long‑Life Concrete Pavement projects shall also trigger an Evaluation of Concrete Pavement Ride Quality and an Evaluation of Overall Lot Pay Factor to verify compliance with Section 507 and to determine eligibility for incentive payments; districts that specify a lower water‑to‑cement ratio must include contract item 0530‑9999 so that the incentive can be paid after those evaluations, and the incentive amount is set at four percent of the estimated total LLCP SY paving cost unless district experience suggests a lower amount [1]. For overlay design, cores shall be taken from the existing structure to verify bond quality before any overlay; a separate leveling course shall be applied when profile correction is required; thin Superpave Wearing Course overlays shall be limited to a minimum thickness of 1½ inches only if the surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than 2 percent patching needed, and both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded, and the overlay must use PG 64E‑22 binder and meet the ADT limits in Table 10.1 [1]. Where edge safety is a concern, a Safety Edge of at least 1½ inches depth shall be incorporated [1]; if reflective cracking, moisture intrusion, or other distresses are identified an appropriate interlayer (geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh, or composite grid) shall be selected based on the listed distress types, climate, traffic, and cost considerations [1]. Full‑depth reclamation shall begin with a detailed distress survey that records the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues, followed by in‑situ testing such as FWD before rehabilitation to gauge existing stiffness and again after construction to verify new stiffness; supplemental Dynamic Cone Penetrometer or Light Weight Deflectometer tests shall be performed for subgrade strength, representative material samples shall be collected according to the prescribed number, size, depth and handling criteria for laboratory analysis, and the resulting data shall be used to determine appropriate layer thicknesses and to correct any identified drainage problems before implementing full‑depth reclamation, while a stabilization mechanism (pulverization, mechanical or chemical) shall be selected based on design checks and lab‑determined stabilizer content [1]. For hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt, additional pavement sampling and laboratory testing including core extraction shall be performed to determine the required rejuvenator dosage; rutting severity shall be less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth shall be under 1.0 inch with average crack width not exceeding a medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and raveling/weathering shall not exceed the medium rating; design checks must verify that asphalt pavement thickness is at least 1½ inches more than the milling depth and, for composite pavements, a minimum of 1‑2 inches of asphalt remains below the HIR layer with any paving fabric at least 1½ inches beneath the cut; corrective actions include milling before the HIR process to treat deeper cracks, removal of raised pavement markers, durable markings, and all mastic patches larger than 5 square feet, pavements containing rubber or fiber in the top 2 inches shall be excluded, with a preservation strategy that treats HIR as a relatively short‑term interim wearing surface and plans a permanent surface course before the end of the construction season [1]. When low or uncertain CBR values are identified in subgrade soils, additional laboratory and/or field testing such as FWD or Dynamic Cone Penetration shall be performed to back‑calculate the resilient modulus, and any Mr value above 15 000 psi must be verified by another test before it is used in design; subgrade strengthening options—including cement or lime stabilization, rock capping, geogrids, or Class 4 geotextiles—shall be evaluated when CBR is below five or frost‑susceptible soils are present, with a cost‑benefit analysis required for projects of at least 25 000 SY on Traffic Pattern Group 1‑4; reconstruction projects shall re‑evaluate the existing subgrade with gradation, in‑place moisture content, density, and CBR testing, incorporate any needed undercutting or stabilization into the design, and all selected values, tests, and procedures shall be documented and signed by the District Geotechnical Engineer [1]. When a year‑5 performance survey shows that at least 30 percent of the segment area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, the warranty requires corrective treatment such as microsurfacing or another Department‑approved method; any repair locations spaced less than 100 feet must be combined into one continuous patch and remedial work shall commence within sixty calendar days after written notice [2]. Prior to remediation the contractor shall verify rut depth with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width, give at least seven calendar days notice before performing any nondestructive testing, and, if base problems are suspected, obtain six‑inch cores for laboratory analysis in accordance with PTM No. 715 and AASHTO T 209 to confirm that density exceeds 92 percent of the theoretical maximum and mix tolerances are met [2]. Ongoing monitoring shall include annual automated surveys together with supplemental manual surveys for flushing and potholes, and routine traffic classification counts shall be performed to ensure that ESAL estimates remain within the projected design values [2]. If a performance evaluation reveals depth deficiencies, the contractor must, within twenty‑four hours after final compaction and in the presence of the Inspector, drill one core of at least four‑inch diameter through all lifts to the top of the existing pavement or base; when any core indicates a depth deficiency greater than ¼ inch that cannot be satisfactorily corrected, the area is deemed defective work and shall be immediately corrected starting at the point of deficiency and proceeding longitudinally and transversely until the entire repair area is within ¼ inch of the required depth [2]. The Inspector may also direct the contractor to obtain one loose‑mixture box sample from directly behind the paver before compaction and one six‑inch diameter pavement core for each 1,000 tons placed, both to be packaged, identified and delivered promptly for laboratory testing; the cores shall be backfilled, compacted and sealed after sampling [2]. Following a performance evaluation of a High‑Friction Surface Treatment, the Department shall schedule a calibrated skid test no earlier than thirty‑one days after HFST placement using ASTM E274/E274M (AASHTO T 242); a second aggregate cleanup shall be performed between fifteen and thirty days after placement, including vacuum sweeping of the shoulder area; any locations where bare asphalt or concrete remain, aggregate retention is poor, delamination is evident, or discoloration appears must be re‑prepared and retreated with HFST at no additional cost [2]. When slab performance is assessed, deflection testing shall be carried out in accordance with ASTM D4695 only if air temperature is between forty °F and seventy °F; each joint or crack shall be measured for loaded slab‑corner deflection and joint efficiency, and any location showing a deflection of 0.02 inch or more together with a joint efficiency of at least sixty‑five percent (if approved) shall be stabilized, or patched if the efficiency is lower but approved [2]. Grout injection pressure shall not exceed two hundred psi, upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 inch shall not be allowed, and work shall stop if radial or transverse cracks develop between injection holes; retesting shall occur twenty‑four hours after grout placement, and any panel that has risen more than the allowable tolerance of 0.05 inch shall be ground to the correct grade [2]. For extended‑season paving, a spring evaluation and manual survey shall be conducted by May 1 in accordance with Publication 336, recording fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond or delamination; any deficiencies identified shall trigger remedial action as directed [2]. Within twenty‑four hours after paving completion the contractor shall submit Form CS‑413EQC with all documentation and measurements associated with the extended‑season plan, and the Department may require removal, replacement or repair at no additional cost, consolidating repairs into a single continuous patch when the distance between repair areas is less than one hundred feet [2].
