Raveling

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Raveling is a topic under Distress.
ravelingraveling-weatheringraveling-weathering-severity
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Raveling is a surface‑level distress in which aggregate particles become detached, leaving the pavement rough, pitted and sometimes littered with loose particles[1]. It is observed on asphalt concrete surfaces—including full‑depth courses, overlay layers, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, and cold‑recycled asphalt base courses—as well as on various concrete pavements where surface preparation or joint cutting can cause aggregate loss[1][2]. The condition is confined to the surface without deeper load‑related damage and is classified under “Raveling/Weathering Severity,” which must not exceed the Medium Rating—a rough, pitted surface that may have loose particles[1]

Description and identification

Raveling is a surface‑level distress in which aggregate particles become detached, leaving the pavement rough, pitted and sometimes littered with loose particles. For projects considered for hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) asphalt, the source specifies that raveling/weathering severity should not exceed the Medium Rating, and it notes that distresses must be confined to the surface of the pavement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133)

In the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course, raveling is recognized as a form of low‑severity cracking; it is accepted only when the overall distress level remains at that low severity and is set apart from infrequent corrugations, settlements, heaves or slippage cracks, which are treated as separate conditions. In hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt mixtures, raveling appears as a rough, pitted wearing surface in which aggregate particles become loose, giving the pavement a weathered appearance; it is classified under “Raveling/Weathering Severity” and must not exceed the Medium Rating, indicating that the distress is limited to the surface without deeper load‑related damage. The raveling condition is distinguished from rutting, whose severity is limited to less than 1 ½ inches, and from transverse or miscellaneous cracking, which must have a depth of less than 1.0 inch and an average crack width no greater than ½ inch. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 107–133)

Where it occurs

The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) notes that in the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course (Section 5.5 B), raveling is accepted only as a low‑severity distress on candidate pavements and therefore may be observed on asphalt concrete surfaces evaluated for this treatment; the manual does not extend this observation to concrete slabs, composite structures, overlays or shoulders [1]. In the same manual, the description of cold‑recycled asphalt base courses (Section 5.13 C) lists raveling among the distresses that affect asphalt pavements and indicates that the cold‑recycling process can rehabilitate asphalt pavements exhibiting fatigue cracks, transverse thermal cracks, reflection cracks, and raveling [1]. The manual further states in the hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt section (Section 5.19 C) that raveling is treated as a surface‑only distress that may be present on any pavement whose surface is being evaluated, explicitly including full‑depth asphalt courses, overlay layers, and the asphalt layer of composite (asphalt‑over‑concrete) structures; for qualification the Raveling/Weathering Severity must not exceed the Medium Rating, which describes a rough, pitted surface that may have loose particles, and when the HIR mix is placed on a composite pavement the underlying concrete must be stable with non‑movement at the joints [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 Specifications addresses raveling in several concrete‑related contexts. For cold‑recycled asphalt base courses (Section 341), the specification calls for applying a fog seal using CSS‑1h or SS‑1h emulsified asphalt material when dust or raveling is a concern, thereby acknowledging that raveling can be present in such bases [2]. In bonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545), the concrete must be strong enough to cut transverse joints without significant raveling or chipping, indicating that raveling is a potential distress in these overlays [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502), saw cuts are required to be made in a manner that avoids raveling, with cutting to begin no later than 18 hours after placement to limit the occurrence of this distress [2]. Likewise, for plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements and their adjacent shoulders (Section 501), joints should be sawed as soon as the concrete has sufficiently hardened to allow sawing without excessive raveling, again recognizing that raveling may develop during construction activities on these surfaces [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 107–133); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 221–222), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–469)

For hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) candidate pavements, distresses—including raveling—should only be on the surface of the pavement. [1] The cold‑recycled asphalt base course specification treats raveling as a surface‑level distress and controls it by applying a fog seal in accordance with Section 472.3(c); no other locations such as wheel paths, joints, cracks or subgrade are mentioned. [2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 221–222)

Severity levels and rating

In the micro‑surfacing project selection (section 5.4), the source requires that candidate pavements exhibit low to medium severity raveling, but it does not provide numeric limits or a distress‑severity table that lists specific raveling categories. In the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course (section 5.5), raveling is permitted only at a low‑severity level for candidate pavements; no numeric count or dimensional limit defines this level and no other severity ratings are mentioned. For hot in‑place recycled asphalt (section 5.19) under the characteristics of HIR mixtures, the source states that raveling/weathering must not exceed the Medium Rating, which is described as a surface that is rough and pitted and may have loose particles; no additional numeric limits or rating categories are given. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 105–133)

In the ASPHALT CONCRETE PAVEMENT GUIDELINES AND POLICIES, Chapter 5 Section 5.5 B (Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course – Conditions for Use) the publication assigns the condition rating for raveling and limits candidate pavements to low‑severity raveling. In the Hot‑In‑Place Recycled asphalt selection guidelines, Chapter 5 Section 5.19 C (Characteristics of Hot‑In‑Place Recycled Mixtures) projects are screened using the Pub. 336 rating criteria, and for raveling or weathering the guideline requires that the severity not exceed the Medium Rating, which is described as a rough, pitted surface that may have loose particles. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 107–133)

Repairs and treatments

According to Section 5.4 POLYMER‑MODIFIED EMULSIFIED ASPHALT PAVING SYSTEM (MICRO SURFACING) > B. Project Selection, Micro Surfacing is recommended for pavements that exhibit low to medium severity raveling (or weathering) together with low cracking, friction loss, or moisture infiltration; under those conditions the treatment may be applied and a double application is advised unless the road surface is in excellent condition [1]. For projects located on an Interstate, the manual recommends a double application of Type A SRL‑E even if the pavement is excellent [1]. The Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course is indicated only when the existing pavement’s distress is confined to low severity raveling (or low severity cracking) as stipulated in Section 5.5 ULTRA‑THIN BONDED WEARING COURSE > B. Conditions for Use [1]. Cold recycled asphalt base courses can rehabilitate raveling by destroying the existing crack pattern and providing a crack‑free layer for a new surface course, and they are applicable when the pavement has structurally sound, well‑drained bases and subgrades, traffic volume does not exceed 200 daily ESALs, the existing road contains at least 2 inches of asphalt material, drainage conditions are adequate, and traffic can be controlled or detoured during base construction and curing, as described in Section 5.13 COLD RECYCLED ASPHALT BASE COURSES > C. Characteristics of Cold Recycled Mixtures [1]. Projects carrying significantly heavy truck traffic (i.e., 200 or more daily ESALs) should not be selected for cold recycling, and if the existing road has deteriorated due to poor drainage conditions, cold recycling should not be attempted [1]. Hot‑In‑Place Recycled asphalt can be applied to pavements exhibiting surface‑only raveling/weathering where the surface is rough, pitted and may have loose particles, provided the raveling severity does not exceed the Medium Rating, and the treatment is appropriate only when all distress—including raveling—is confined to the pavement surface with no underlying load or base problems, as outlined in Section 5.19 HOT IN‑PLACE RECYCLED ASPHALT > C. Characteristics of Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) Mixtures [1]. In the cold‑recycled asphalt base course specification, a fog seal applied with CSS‑1h or SS‑1h diluted in accordance with Section 472 is identified as the treatment that mitigates raveling and dust, and it is required only when the specification directs its use following a surface‑tolerance test in which the average gap measured under a 12‑foot straight edge exceeds ½ inch; if this condition is not met, no other repair for raveling is prescribed in Section 341.3 [2]. For bonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements, the specification requires scarifying the existing pavement to the indicated depth and removing any deteriorated concrete, asphalt or loose reinforcement to eliminate detached aggregate before the overlay, whenever scarification is used to lower the pavement elevation as described in Section 545.3(b)(2), and after scarifying a shot‑blast or water‑blast pass must be performed to produce a roughened surface and clear remaining loose material following the milling operation per Section 545.3(b)(4) [2]. Where only minor surface defects such as limited raveling exist, the specification permits replacing those repairs with the overlay itself [2]. Transverse joints may be saw‑cut only after the fresh concrete has attained sufficient strength so that cutting does not produce notable raveling or chipping, ensuring that joint cutting does not create new raveling as stated in Section 545.3 h [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 105–133); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 221–222), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 466–469)

For polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing, the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242 Change 10) Section 5.4 B states that the treatment may be applied when raveling is of low to medium severity; any higher severity excludes it. For an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course, the same manual in Section 5.5 B limits applicability to raveling of low severity, and the treatment is ruled out if raveling exceeds that level. A hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt overlay may be used provided the raveling or weathering severity does not exceed the Medium Rating, with conditions beyond the medium rating precluding its use, as specified in Section 5.19 C of the Pavement Policy Manual. In the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification Publication 408 (2026) Section 341.3 (f) Surface Tolerance, a fog seal is permitted on a cold‑recycled asphalt base course when dust or raveling is observed, with no severity threshold defined for the raveling condition. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 105–133); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 221–222)

Prevention in design and construction

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10), section 5.4 recommends a double application of micro‑surfacing as a design measure for pavements that exhibit low to medium severity raveling or weathering; this additional layer is intended to mitigate the progressive loss of aggregate and should be used unless the existing surface is in excellent condition [1]. For projects located on an Interstate, the same section specifies that a double application of Type A SRL‑E be employed even if the road surface is in excellent condition, thereby addressing raveling under all Interstate applications [1]. In section 5.13, the manual states that because cold‑recycled mixes have higher void content, providing a wearing surface is imperative; the wearing surface protects the aggregate and therefore helps prevent raveling of the base course [1]. The same subsection also notes that asphalt pavements showing fatigue cracks, transverse thermal cracks, reflection cracks, and raveling can be successfully recycled, because the cold‑recycling process destroys the existing crack pattern and produces a crack‑free layer for a new surface course, which reduces or eliminates raveling when the pavement is selected for recycling [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 Specifications, section 341 (Cold Recycled Asphalt Base Course), permits the application of a fog seal using CSS‑1h or SS‑1h diluted as specified in Section 472.3(c) when surface‑tolerance testing is directed; this treatment is intended to control dust or raveling on the cold‑recycled asphalt base course surface [2]. The same specification, through its maintenance and traffic provision (Section 413.3(n)), calls for a pilot car to provide speed control whenever damage such as raveling is likely to occur, thereby reducing the potential for aggregate loss under traffic loading [2]. In section 545 (Bonded Concrete Overlay), transverse joints shall be saw‑cut only after the concrete has attained sufficient strength to be cut without significant raveling or chipping, a timing requirement that applies when the concrete is strong enough for cutting [2]. Section 502 (Roller Compacted Concrete Pavements) requires that control joints be saw‑cut as indicated on the plans in a manner that does not cause raveling, and this operation must be performed no later than eighteen hours after placement [2]. Likewise, section 501 (Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements) mandates that joints be sawed as soon as the concrete has sufficiently hardened to allow sawing without excessive raveling and before random cracking would occur, applying only when the slab has reached a hardness level permitting sawing yet before random cracking could develop [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 105–123); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 221–222), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–469)

Section 341‑3 requires that when the project documents direct a surface‑tolerance test, a fog seal using CSS‑1h or SS‑1h diluted as specified in Section 472.2 Table A or another approved emulsified asphalt material be applied at no additional cost to control dust or raveling; this practice is intended to prevent the progressive loss of aggregate particles that defines raveling. Section 545.3(h) mandates that transverse joints on bonded‑concrete overlays be saw‑cut only after the fresh concrete has attained enough strength to avoid significant raveling or chipping, and Section 545.3(e) sets a bond‑strength criterion of an average of at least 175 psi (with each individual test not less than 160 psi) that must be met before cutting can occur; these requirements prevent aggregate loss caused by the sawing operation. Section 502.3(d)2 specifies that the paver used for roller‑compacted concrete placement shall be of suitable weight and stability and shall not tear, shove, gouge, or otherwise create permanent surface blemishes, thereby addressing potential raveling on the finished surface; Section 502.3(i)2 requires that when preparing cold joints an approved saw, wheel cutter, or other method that does not tear or ravel the vertical edge be used, directly preventing raveling at joint faces; and Section 502.3(c) prohibits placement of RCC pavement during a hard rain that may damage the pavement surface, avoiding surface conditions that could lead to raveling. Section 501.3(i) requires that transverse joints be sawed only after the concrete has hardened enough to permit sawing without excessive raveling resulting from the sawing operation, and also forbids the use of a vibrating T‑bar or a filler strip in joint work because such equipment can displace coarse aggregate from the joint location, which would create raveling; this prohibition applies whenever joints are being prepared. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 221–222), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–469)

Specification limits and acceptance

In the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course specification (Chapter 5, subsection 5.5 B), raveling must be limited to low severity; the acceptance is based on a qualitative classification of low severity with no numeric threshold or specified measurement method. In the hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt specification (Chapter 5, Section 5.19 C), raveling—or weathering—severity should not exceed the Medium Rating, which is defined as a surface that is rough and pitted and may have loose particles, and evaluation follows the Pub. 336 criteria. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 107–133)

When the specification requires correction for raveling, the remedy is to apply a fog seal using CSS‑1h or SS‑1h diluted as shown in Section 472.2 Table A (or another approved emulsified asphalt material) at no additional cost to the Department; this work is performed by the Contractor under the provisions of Section 341.3 and the source does not state a specific time frame for completing the fog seal. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 221–222)
