Longitudinal Joint

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Longitudinal Joint is a topic under Rigid Pavement.
longitudinal-jointslongitudinal-joint
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-28

The longitudinal joint is a full‑depth contraction joint installed parallel to the pavement centreline that deliberately separates adjacent concrete or asphalt lanes. Its primary function is to confine cracking and limit shear‑crack propagation by providing a controlled plane of movement, while also isolating lateral displacement at unsupported slab edges to preserve lane geometry and smoothness [2]. The joint operates through a precisely cut vertical seam (≤ ¾ in wide) that is cleaned, moistened and sealed with rubberized or hot‑asphalt sealant, often supplemented by dowel or tie bars to restore load transfer across the seam when distress such as spalling, faulting, or water infiltration occurs [1][2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

The longitudinal joint is installed parallel to the centreline to confine concrete cracking to a predetermined location, thereby preventing random or premature panel cracking and limiting shear‑crack propagation along lane edges [2]. By providing a full‑depth contraction joint it also isolates lateral movement at unsupported slab edges, preserving the true lane line and preventing edge distortion or separation that would produce loss of smoothness [2]. When the joint develops distress such as longitudinal cracking, surface spalling, excessive faulting, loss of load transfer, or water infiltration, the condition threatens the smooth transition between adjacent lanes and must be treated [1]. Faulting greater than a quarter‑inch is identified as a threshold for milling the joint, while faulting exceeding a half‑inch requires placement of a thin wearing course or additional grinding to reduce displacement [1]. Spalls confined to the upper half of the slab are repaired with partial‑depth spall patches, whereas deeper spalls that extend beyond mid‑slab depth call for full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs [1]. Delamination that extends through an existing joint is addressed by maintaining the joint and inserting a ¼‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene filler to restore continuity and block further material intrusion [2]. Load‑transfer deficiencies are mitigated by installing dowels or tie bars aligned with saw cuts across the joint, which re‑establish lane‑to‑lane ties and uniform support under the slabs [2]. Periodic crack sealing of approximately 20 % to 25 % of longitudinal joints is prescribed to preserve seal integrity and prevent water infiltration that can accelerate slab movement [1]. Additional rolling with a vibratory roller is recommended to achieve specified density across the joint, eliminating low‑density zones that would otherwise degrade ride quality [2]. In pervious asphalt or ATPBC installations the joint prevents surface irregularities at the seam by overlapping lane edges 1–2 inches and limiting any bump, ridge, or depression to no more than a quarter‑inch measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge, thereby maintaining smoothness [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–75), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 229–231), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 269–334), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–462)

Across the guides, the longitudinal joint is regarded as a key element for preventing pavement deterioration by controlling random cracking through its placement, tie‑bar configuration and saw cutting [2]. The maintenance approach emphasizes preserving and extending the concrete pavement’s service life rather than merely fixing isolated damage, using periodic cleaning and sealing of the joints over a fifty‑year analysis period [1]. Early intervention is provided by sealant applications to roughly one quarter of the joints, which helps keep the joint functional and slows further deterioration [1]. When spalling or lane‑edge distortion occurs, the joint is sealed with hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder and partial‑depth repairs are applied, restoring rideability and the slab’s ability to carry traffic loads, thereby repairing damage and reinstating functionality [2][1]. The combined strategy of regular sealant applications, comprehensive cleaning, and selective partial‑depth rehabilitation is intended to maintain functional integrity while extending the pavement’s useful life for its designed fifty‑year lifespan [1].

Distress characterization and causes

The distress associated with longitudinal joints is located at the seam where two longitudinal pavement passes meet and runs parallel to the centerline for the full length of the joint[1][2]. Across the network roughly one‑quarter of those joints, equivalent to about 615 linear feet per mile or 20 %–25 % of total joint length, are identified for crack sealing[1]. The extent of the problem is expressed both by this longitudinal continuity and by the size of individual damaged segments; spot repairs are prescribed when a cracked portion measures between approximately 15 inches and 6 feet, while any segment longer than 6 feet requires an extended repair, provided that the depth does not exceed one‑half of the slab thickness[2]. Severity is judged moderate when sealant application suffices without resorting to full‑depth patching, yet joints exhibiting vertical faulting greater than a quarter‑inch over a twelve‑foot straightedge are deemed defective and must be milled, and those with faulting exceeding a half‑inch require either a scratch course at the joint or additional milling/grinding to bring movement within acceptable limits[1][2]. The maintenance schedule reflects a progression rate of about five‑year clean‑and‑seal cycles during the first twenty years of service, after which the interval is extended to ten years[1]. Operationally, sealant work introduces temporary delays for traffic users, while uncontrolled faulting in longitudinal joints that intersect wheel paths can degrade ride quality, increase vehicle damage risk, and raise safety and operational concerns[1][2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 71–72); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 332–334), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 416–462)

Distress in longitudinal joints is driven by moisture‑related infiltration and mechanical instability of the supporting slab system, which are emphasized across the guides. [1][2] When drainage pathways within the interlayer or subgrade are deficient, water and chlorides can enter the joint cavity, eroding concrete and weakening the bond between adjacent slabs; this is compounded when the underlying subgrade lacks uniform support, allowing slabs to rock and creating differential settlement that concentrates stresses at the joint. [1][2] The resulting loss of load‑transfer capacity appears as faulting and spalling, with fault levels exceeding one‑quarter inch prompting milling of the joint and fault levels over one‑half inch requiring more extensive rehabilitation such as an unbonded PCC overlay with a geotextile or asphalt interlayer and a Superpave wearing course. [1] Mechanical instability also arises when slabs or slab fragments move under normal traffic, producing non‑uniform support for any overlay and accelerating cracking especially where fault exceeds the ¼ inch limit; visible rocking of slabs signals the need for stabilization before placing an interlayer. [2] Temperature‑induced expansion and contraction generate compressive stresses at the joint face, and if a repair patch is forced against the adjacent slab without adequate clearance, compression can cause partial‑depth repair failure and promote crack propagation toward the patch rather than vertically, leading to spalling. [1] Inadequate preparation such as insufficient roughening, omission of caulking or backer‑rod, and use of low‑strength grout further permits moisture penetration that reduces structural capacity under repeated loads. [2] Heavy traffic in wheel paths concentrates point loads on longitudinal joints, accelerating crack formation; therefore the guides advise avoiding placement of longitudinal joints in wheel paths and controlling panel dimensions (aspect ratio ≤1.5 and maximum dimension ≤18 times overlay thickness) to limit stress concentrations. [2] Effective mitigation includes restoring original joint width with rigid expansion‑joint material or polystyrene board to provide clearance for movement, retrofitting dowel bars, slab stabilization or jacking, cross‑stitching, sealing the joint with rubberized sealant and maintaining a sealant reservoir, and ensuring the interlayer terminates in a drainable layer or daylighted location so that free drainage of geotextile is preserved. [1][2] These combined moisture infiltration, drainage failure, inadequate structural capacity, weak support, material aging, poor construction practices, environmental temperature cycles, and heavy loads constitute the underlying mechanisms accelerating longitudinal joint distress. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 446–462), SECTION 1000 STRUCTURES (pp. 1166–1167)

Applicability and treatment selection

Longitudinal joints are appropriate on rigid concrete overlay pavements when the panel geometry conforms to the prescribed limits—an aspect‑ratio of no more than 1.5 and overall dimensions that do not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness—and when joint spacing follows the project plan while being located away from wheel paths. Installation must occur with pavement or geotextile surface temperatures below 120 °F but not lower than 40 °F, and ambient air temperature must also be at least 40 °F; insulating blankets are required if the air temperature falls to 40 °F or less. For cross‑stitching an existing longitudinal joint or crack, a full‑depth saw cut along the entire length of the joint is required, together with a detailed quality‑control plan and a pre‑work demonstration within project limits before equipment approval; epoxy‑filled tie bars are installed and the joint sealed, and traffic may not return until the anchoring material has fully cured per the manufacturer’s recommendations. On flexible pavements, a notched‑wedge longitudinal joint is suitable for wearing or binder courses that use NMAS mixtures of 19 mm or smaller, provided all loose material has been cleared and the joint is built to the standard drawing. The adjacent lane must be placed within one working day when the joint abuts opposing traffic, or within two days when traffic moves in the same direction, and the newly compacted surface must remain free of vehicular loads for at least 24 hours or until it cools uniformly to 140 °F or lower. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 273–274), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 424–462)

Longitudinal joint cleaning and sealing should be omitted whenever an overlay—whether unbonded Portland cement concrete or asphalt—is planned, because the sealant would interfere with the bond of the new surface [1]. Partial‑depth repair of a longitudinal joint is permissible only when the required removal does not exceed one‑half the slab thickness; if spalling or other damage extends beyond the mid‑slab depth, a full‑depth concrete patch or corner repair must be employed to restore structural integrity [1][2]. When faulting at a joint exceeds half an inch, the specifications call for a scratch course or milling and grinding to reduce the fault rather than installing a new longitudinal joint [2]. Longitudinal joints are also prohibited from being placed directly in wheel paths, as doing so compromises load distribution [2]. If a crack is found within approximately one‑and‑a‑half feet of the intended joint location before sawing, the joint must be omitted and treated according to the prescribed alternative procedures [2]. The cross‑stitch repair method cannot be used when ambient air or pavement surface temperatures are below 40 °F, nor can it be applied to transverse joints or cracks; in those conditions an alternate maintenance or rehabilitation technique is required [2]. When joint performance is compromised by faulting or inadequate load transfer—indicating deficient underlying support or dowel‑bar system—the appropriate response shifts from surface sealing to structural interventions such as slab stabilization, jacketing, or a dowel‑bar retrofit [1]. Hot‑In‑Place Recycled resurfacing is unsuitable on pavements that contain extensive crack sealant coverage or an overbanded rubberized joint seal, necessitating other preservation or rehabilitation methods [1]. Finally, longitudinal‑joint cleaning and sealing are treated as incidental items separate from concrete‑pavement‑patching contracts; if the contract scope is limited to patching, a distinct joint‑sealing agreement must be arranged, otherwise alternative work may need to be pursued [2].

Condition assessment and timing

Determining the need and location for longitudinal joints begins with a review of contract documents and shop drawings to identify any prescribed joint pattern [2]. The engineer then performs a thermal‑expansion analysis using the site’s average daily temperature data so that panel dimensions and expansion‑joint spacing are sized appropriately for ambient conditions [2]. A detailed field survey of existing pavement elevations follows, providing the data needed to calculate theoretical surface elevations and slab sizes in accordance with the proposed joint positions, which are recorded on a layout drawing before any saw‑cutting is undertaken [2]. During fabrication the width of each longitudinal joint is measured and must not exceed three‑quarters of an inch [2]. After placement, inspectors visually examine every seam where adjacent passes meet and use a twelve‑foot straightedge to verify that no bump, ridge or depression exceeds one‑quarter of an inch [2]. The overlap between consecutive longitudinal passes is also measured and shall not be greater than four inches unless a specific exception has been approved [2]. In addition to these dimensional checks, a visual inspection of each joint is required to identify the onset of distress such as spalling, with the inspector determining whether any spalls are confined to the top half of the slab or extend beyond the mid‑slab depth [1]. When spalling is limited to the upper half, the guidelines call for a partial‑depth repair, whereas deeper spalls trigger a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair, informing the decision on whether additional treatment is needed at that location [1]. Cleaning and sealing of the longitudinal joint between traffic lanes and between a lane and a concrete shoulder are specified as routine actions in every concrete pavement rehabilitation project, providing a baseline condition assessment before any further repairs are considered [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 332–334), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Longitudinal joints are addressed when they enter a moderate stage of distress, indicated by cracks appearing in roughly one‑quarter to one‑half of the joints or by surface spalling that does not exceed half the slab thickness [1]. The guides also require treatment as soon as delamination reaches or passes through an existing longitudinal joint, or when a partial‑depth repair zone abuts the joint, because such conditions compromise joint integrity [2]. In both cases the prescribed response begins with resealing or cleaning and sealing of the joints; immediate resealing is recommended at incipient distress, and routine clean‑and‑seal cycles are scheduled based on time and percentage of cracked joints—first at five years when about 25 % show cracking, then at ten years for 20–25 % up to 50 %, continuing at fifteen, twenty, and thirty years with similar or full coverage [1]. When spalling is observed but remains confined to the upper half of the slab, a partial‑depth repair is specified; repairs not deeper than one‑half the slab thickness may be performed as spot repairs for lengths between 15 inches and six feet (Type 1) or as extended joint patches for longer sections (Type 2) [1][2]. The partial‑depth repair must incorporate a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene filler placed in the joint and extending at least half an inch below the repaired surface, after which the joint is sealed according to sealing guidelines [2]. If spalling or delamination extends beyond the mid‑slab depth, a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair is required [1][2]. Additional distress such as vertical faulting also triggers treatment: milling is mandated when fault exceeds a quarter‑inch, and if fault exceeds half an inch a Superpave wearing‑course (scratch) layer or further milling/grinding must be applied before any overlay [1].

Materials and repair details

Longitudinal joints are created by cutting a full‑depth vertical seam through sound compacted pavement at least six inches from any exposed edge using an approved saw or wheel cutter, the cut following the existing joint line, being cleaned of loose material, moistened immediately and sealed according to Section 501.3(n). The width of the cut is limited to three‑quarters inch for most applications, may not exceed one‑half inch when opened to traffic, and is restricted to one‑quarter inch adjacent to concrete median barriers; the saw cut must be at least one‑quarter inch wide and deep enough that its depth is no less than one‑third of slab thickness within twelve inches of an edge and no less than one‑fourth elsewhere, typically about one inch for patch work. Joint locations are normally near the centreline or within twelve inches of lane lines, offset approximately six inches from any joint in the layer below and positioned away from wheel paths, with panel aspect ratios close to one and not exceeding 1.5, and overall dimensions limited to roughly eighteen times the overlay thickness. For concrete pavements the cleaned joint is sealed with a cementitious or polymer‑based sealant, often a rubberized joint‑sealing material as prescribed in Publication 408 Sections 512 and 521, whereas asphalt surfaces receive an asphalt crack‑seal coating; standard crack‑seal coating, seal coat, or micro‑surface may also be placed on the shoulders at about 500 lineal feet per mile. When greater durability is required on flexible pavements, a stress‑absorbing membrane (SAM) or stress‑absorbing membrane interlayer (SAMI) can be installed full‑width directly on the existing surface after any leveling work, and must be covered by at least two overlay courses whose combined thickness satisfies the manufacturer’s recommendation. Joint spacing shall not exceed fifteen feet between thermal cracks, and the underlying slab must be free of excessive or alligator cracking before treatment. In concrete base courses the joint may be filled with a premolded expansion‑joint filler approved by the manufacturer—such as cork, sponge rubber, fiber‑based material or closed‑cell polypropylene foam—that meets AASHTO/ASTM specifications and is supplied in lengths of at least eight feet to provide a continuous smooth surface. For asphalt surface courses a quarter‑inch full‑depth polystyrene board bond breaker is placed in Type A and B patch joints, while Type C patches rely on tie bars without a bond breaker; tie bars, dowel bars and other reinforcement are epoxy‑coated per ASTM A775 and installed by drilling or cross‑stitching, spaced at least thirty inches from transverse joints and twenty‑four inches from any dowelled contraction joint. Tie bars must be tested for pull‑out resistance after the concrete reaches a compressive strength of 3,500 psi, with any bar failing to meet Table B requirements replaced or supplemented, and when cracks wider than one‑eighth inch develop one tie bar is omitted from each adjacent slab, with an additional omission permitted during the October‑to‑April paving season. When unbonded Portland cement concrete overlays are used, the concrete layers may be separated by a geotextile interlayer or an asphalt concrete layer; if a geotextile is specified any joint exhibiting faulting greater than one‑quarter inch must be milled before placement, and a nominal one‑inch Superpave wearing course is then placed over the joint, with a scratch‑type Superpave wearing course applied or the joint milled/grinded when faulting exceeds one‑half inch. Maintenance follows a five‑year clean‑and‑seal cycle in which roughly one‑quarter of the longitudinal joints and their adjoining shoulders are treated; about half of the joint length receives treatment at ten‑ and thirty‑year intervals, and the entire joint length is cleaned and sealed at twenty years, each operation using a sealant such as standard crack‑seal coating, seal coat or micro‑surface material. When spalling is confined to the upper half of the slab, a partial‑depth repair is performed with patch thickness not exceeding one‑half of the slab depth; repairs between fifteen inches and six feet in length are classified as spot repairs and those longer than six feet as extended repairs, using a material that matches the original pavement—Class AA cement concrete modified for normal cure schedules or an accelerated‑setting mix when rapid traffic reopening is required. A rigid polystyrene board equal in width to the existing joint is placed between the new concrete and the adjacent slab to preserve joint function, and if spalling extends beyond mid‑slab depth a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair per Publication 408 Section 519 is executed. For repairs involving precast concrete box beams, the joint faces are roughened, shear‑key areas sealed with caulking, backer rod or closed‑cell neoprene sponge, and the joint filled continuously with nonshrink epoxy grout; curing begins immediately with a double layer of water‑saturated burlap kept wet for at least twenty‑four hours while temperatures remain above fifty degrees Fahrenheit, and traffic may resume only after the grout attains a compressive strength of 3,200 psi, after which the joint is finally sealed with an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant. After placement of adjacent lanes, vibratory rolling is applied to achieve at least eighty‑five percent (or ninety percent) wet density behind the paver and greater than ninety‑six percent after final rolling, followed by a static or rubber‑tired roller to produce a smooth surface; for surface courses joint faces are first painted with a uniform coat of the course’s PG binder or PG 64S‑22 asphalt binder, then hot PG 64S‑22 binder heated between 265 °F and 320 °F is applied in a straight band approximately four inches wide and about one‑sixteenth inch thick, centered within one inch of the joint, with excess material removed. In overlay work the existing longitudinal joint location is referenced before paving; after placement a sealant reservoir one‑quarter inch wide is cut through the full overlay thickness plus an additional half‑inch directly over the joint and sealed per Section 501.3(n), resulting in clean, vertical faces free of honeycomb or segregation. [1][2]

The performance of a longitudinal joint depends on the intrinsic properties of the repair materials, the compatibility of ancillary components, and the existing condition of the pavement structure [1][2]. Stress‑absorbing membranes such as SAM or SAMI may be used only when the underlying pavement is flexible and rests on a flexible base, and the surface must be free of excessive cracking or widespread alligator cracking before their installation [1]. Thermal cracks in concrete slabs must be spaced no more than fifteen feet apart to allow effective joint treatment, and any overlay placed over a membrane must meet the manufacturer’s depth recommendations while providing at least two courses of material for protection [1]. When spalling is present, repairs are governed by the depth of damage; spalls confined to the upper half of the slab require partial‑depth repairs that use a cement concrete matching the original and are isolated from adjacent slabs with a rigid polystyrene board, whereas spalls extending beyond mid‑slab depth demand full‑depth longitudinal‑joint repair and sealing of all joints and cracks with rubberized joint‑sealing material [1]. For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) overlays only limited crack sealant may be applied to the longitudinal and transverse joints, and extensive sealant or an overbanded rubberized seal renders the section unsuitable; additionally any fiber‑reinforced or rubberized material present in the top two inches of the pavement disqualifies it from HIR treatment [1]. In unbonded concrete overlays the condition of the existing joint determines the separation layer: joints showing more than a quarter‑inch of faulting require a geotextile interlayer, and faulting exceeding half an inch necessitates placement of a Superpave wearing course or grinding to reduce the offset before overlay installation [1]. The concrete mix used for longitudinal‑joint repairs must achieve at least 3,500 psi compressive strength before pull‑out testing of tie bars, and it should be placed when material temperature is between 50 °F and 90 °F; panel dimensions should not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the aspect ratio should stay below about 1.5 to avoid premature cracking [2]. Aggregate gradations are monitored by sieve analysis and the coefficient of thermal expansion is taken from the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide, ensuring that the mix reflects site temperature and exposure conditions [2]. The subgrade must be clean, unfrozen, free of ponded water or debris, and remain stable with a functional drainage system to prevent infiltration of water, chlorides, or incompressible materials into the pavement structure; moisture conditions are controlled so that the base stays moist but not water‑logged [1][2]. Tie bars used in longitudinal joints must be epoxy‑coated per ASTM A775, installed at least twenty‑four inches from any dowelled contraction joint, and eliminated wherever adjacent cracks exceed one‑eighth inch; their pull‑out resistance is verified after the concrete reaches the required compressive strength [2]. For Type A and B patches a full‑depth quarter‑inch polystyrene board bond breaker is placed in the joint, while Type C patches omit the bond breaker but require tie bars; all saw cuts are centered over the joint, one inch deep and a quarter‑inch wide, with overall joint width limited to three‑quarters of an inch (or a maximum of a quarter‑inch on each side of a barrier) [2][1]. When a partial‑depth repair abuts an existing longitudinal joint, a preformed cellular polystyrene filler—either ASTM D8139 closed‑cell polypropylene foam or a polyurethane‑bonded recycled‑rubber composite—is installed extending at least half an inch below the bottom of the repair, and if the repair can be sawed without damage the joint is re‑established by cutting through the full depth and then sealing [2]. Sealing materials are temperature sensitive; hot‑asphalt sealants or epoxy must be applied only when ambient air and pavement surface temperatures exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit, with the PG 64S‑22 binder heated to between 265 °F and 320 °F and placed while the air temperature is above forty degrees [2]. Sealant reservoirs are cut a quarter‑inch wide through the full overlay thickness plus an additional half inch, the sealant is placed hot in accordance with Section 705.4(b) or (c), and after placement the joint edges are painted with the same binder used in the course; traffic may not be allowed on newly compacted courses until they have uniformly cooled to 140 °F or less, and a minimum of twenty‑four hours is required before opening to traffic when grout is used [2]. Interlayer materials—whether geotextile or asphalt concrete—must be clean, lightly dampened, and their temperature kept at or below 120 °F before overlay placement; they must also be tied into the mainline and extend through any shoulder reconstruction to ensure uniform support and proper drainage [1][2]. By adhering to these material compatibilities, dimensional thresholds, and condition‑based requirements, longitudinal joints can achieve their intended performance throughout service life [1][2].

Treatment procedures

The longitudinal joint treatment begins after the contractor submits a quality‑control plan for review at least fourteen days before work starts, and work proceeds only when ambient air and pavement temperatures are 40 °F or higher [2]. The joint line is then located and laid out according to the project plans, keeping it at least six inches from any existing joint in the underlying layer and away from wheel paths [2]. All loose or foreign material, spalls, deteriorated concrete, open cracks, heavy paint buildup, raised markers, and any existing sealant are removed by cleaning the surface with oil‑free compressed air, stiff‑bristle brushes, vacuum, or high‑pressure water blast to eliminate defects that could compromise the repair [1][2]. For spalling limited to the upper half of the slab, the damaged concrete is stripped away and a ¼‑inch thick preformed cellular polystyrene board, sized to match the original joint width, is placed as a bond breaker to re‑establish the joint space and prevent new concrete from contacting adjacent slabs [1][2]. If spalling extends beyond mid‑slab depth, a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair is performed using the prescribed full‑depth repair material after the same cleaning and removal procedures [1][2]. Prior to concrete removal, a full‑depth diamond saw cut is made along the entire length of the existing longitudinal joint; the cut width must be at least ¼ inch but not exceed ¾ inch, and its depth shall be no less than D/3 within twelve inches of an edge and no less than D/4 elsewhere (or one‑third slab thickness near edges for precast slabs) [2]. The sawed joint is immediately flushed with water or a high‑pressure blast, and traffic is prohibited on the new cuts for up to seventy‑two hours [2]. Where existing pavement intersects the proposed line, it is removed either by lift‑out or overcutting, and any damaged sub‑base is repaired, fine‑graded, and an approved bedding material not exceeding one‑half inch thick (or two inches when cement‑treated) is placed [2]. After removal, the faces of adjacent slabs or beams are intentionally roughened and cleaned using oil‑free compressed air, stiff‑bristle brushes, or vacuum to provide a bondable surface [1][2]. All reinforcement steel, dowel bars, and longitudinal tie bars are coated with epoxy in accordance with ASTM A775 before installation; tie bars are spaced at least twenty‑four inches from any dowelled contraction joint and omitted where a joint has opened more than one‑eighth inch [2]. For Type A and B patch sections the ¼‑inch polystyrene board bond breaker is installed in the joint, while for Type C patches tie bars are used instead of a bond breaker; epoxy‑coated tie bars may also be cross‑stitched across a cleaned joint when load transfer must be enhanced [1][2]. When dowel bars are required, two parallel saw cuts are made per slot, the concrete between them is removed with a jackhammer not exceeding thirty pounds (fifteen pounds if damage occurs), and the slots are cleaned within twenty‑four hours; a final air‑blast is applied at least thirty minutes before concrete placement [2]. The dowel bars are seated on plastic chairs that provide a minimum half‑inch gap, lubricated with bond‑breaker lubricant for Type B coated bars, and expansion caps maintaining a quarter‑inch clearance are installed [2]. For epoxy‑bonded repairs, holes are drilled on each side of the joint, epoxy bonding compound is injected, the tie bar is rotated three to five times to purge air, one inch of coverage is provided at the surface, and the epoxy is trowelled flush with the pavement [2]. Non‑shrink epoxy grout or dowel grout is pumped into slots and must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 2 500 psi (3 200 psi for beam grout) before traffic may be permitted [2]. The fresh concrete mix, specified as Class AA cement concrete (modified or accelerated as called for), is placed over the bond‑breaker board for partial‑depth repairs or using the full‑depth repair material for deeper repairs; forms are left in place for at least twelve hours before removal [1][2]. After placement the joint faces must be neat, free of honeycomb or segregation, and the seam between adjacent passes may not exceed a quarter‑inch bump, ridge, or depression when measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge; overlap is limited to four inches unless a waiver is granted [2]. Compaction is achieved with a pneumatic‑tire roller at 40–60 psi or a vibratory roller as necessary to obtain the specified density, and additional rolling may be applied along longitudinal joints [2]. Before shrinkage cracking begins, a saw cut of one quarter inch width and depth of one inch for patch joints (or D/3/D/4 for full‑depth work) is made across each longitudinal joint, centered over the joint, flushed with water, and debris cleared [2]. A sealant reservoir one quarter inch wide is cut through the full depth of any overlay plus an additional half inch, then filled with the appropriate sealant; for surface courses hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement heated between 265 °F and 320 °F is applied in a four‑inch‑wide band about one‑sixteenth inch thick, centered within an inch of the joint, and excess sealant is removed [2]. The final step for all treatments is the application of a sealant to restore continuity and protect against water intrusion; sealants are applied only to joints that are clean, dry, and free of loose material [1][2]. Curing follows the applicable specifications: curing compounds are applied within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates, all joints are protected from moisture loss, cold RCC joints receive a moist cure with water mist for at least seventy‑two hours, and slurry seals are kept continuously wet with double‑thickness water‑saturated burlap for at least twenty‑four hours while temperatures remain between 50 °F and 125 °F; the specific curing procedures for Class AA concrete are those outlined in Publication 408 Section 525.3 [1][2]. Traffic control requirements dictate that no vehicular loads are permitted on newly compacted courses for twenty‑four hours or until the surface temperature uniformly drops to 140 °F or lower, and the Representative must authorize opening after the mix has set sufficiently to prevent pick‑up and the grout or sealant has reached its required strength [2]. Prior to reopening, tie bars are tested for pull‑out resistance to meet required values, the joint surface is inspected with a twelve‑foot straightedge and any deviation greater than a quarter inch is ground down, high spots are removed, and all longitudinal joints are resealed [2].

Implementation of a longitudinal joint begins with an accurate survey that establishes the centreline of each joint and identifies any required tie‑bars, which must be installed before concrete placement at least twenty‑four inches from any dowelled contraction joint and may be omitted adjacent to a dominant joint when work occurs between 1 October and 1 April [2]. The first‑day bars are then selected for pull‑out testing once the slab reaches a compressive strength of three‑thousand five hundred psi, and only if they meet resistance criteria is the adjoining lane permitted to be placed [2]. Placement equipment must overlap the previously laid lane by one to two inches and may not exceed four inches unless an exception is granted; the finished seam must not show more than a quarter‑inch irregularity measured on a twelve‑foot straightedge [2]. Full‑depth longitudinal cuts are made before any transverse cuts using a self‑propelled diamond saw equipped with a blade guard, alignment guides, water cooling and cut‑depth control, with each cut limited to one‑quarter inch on either side of a barrier and not exceeding half an inch when the joint will be opened to traffic [2]. Sawing is performed as soon as the concrete has hardened enough to avoid raveling but before shrinkage cracking can develop, after which the joint perimeter is cleaned with a power broom or vacuum truck and all loose material removed within thirty minutes of concrete placement [2]. When unsound concrete must be removed, a chipping hammer not exceeding thirty‑five pounds is operated at a thirty‑ to sixty‑degree angle and a jackhammer not larger than thirty pounds (or fifteen pounds if damage occurs) is used; drilling for epoxy injection employs a hydraulic‑powered drill that minimizes surface damage and creates correctly angled holes [2]. Cleaning and sealing of the longitudinal joint between traffic lanes and the joint abutting a concrete shoulder are required on every concrete pavement rehabilitation project, with cleaning performed prior to any repair work and sealing applied after the patch has cured [1]. If spalling is confined to the upper half of the slab, a partial‑depth repair is executed; when deterioration extends beyond the mid‑slab plane, a full‑depth replacement of the joint segment is required, and in both cases the area is prepared, deteriorated concrete removed, the appropriate patch placed, and curing follows Publication 408, Section 525.3 [1]. Dowel‑bar retrofits are carried out by first cutting two parallel slots per bar with a power‑driven self‑propelled saw that runs parallel to the roadway centreline; epoxy‑coated dowels are then positioned and the voids filled with the selected patch material, while a rigid polystyrene board matching joint width is placed between the new patch and the adjacent slab to restore original spacing and guard against compression failure, especially during periods of pronounced thermal cycling [1]. The selection of repair mix depends on ambient temperature and the time available before reopening to traffic; accelerated mixes such as Class AA cement are used when temperatures are high or schedules tight, whereas conventional mixes are suitable in cooler conditions [1]. Sealing of the joint is performed with hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement applied by a pressure applicator in a four‑inch wide band about one‑sixteenth inch thick, maintaining sealant temperature between 265°F and 320°F, and placement is prohibited when ambient air temperature falls below forty degrees Fahrenheit unless a waiver is obtained; a quarter‑inch‑wide sealant reservoir cut to the full depth of the overlay plus half an inch is prepared over the existing joint before the adjoining lane is placed, and the joint face receives a uniform asphalt coating prior to lane placement [2]. Compaction follows immediately after concrete placement: for ATPBC an eight‑ to ten‑ton steel‑wheel roller operating in static mode makes four passes once the mat has cooled to roughly one hundred degrees Fahrenheit, followed by a pneumatic‑tire roller before any traffic is permitted; for RCC or asphalt courses a vibratory roller provides full‑depth density with the first pass overhanging the unsupported edge by three to six inches and subsequent passes keeping the drum twelve to eighteen inches from the fresh edge until the adjacent lane is placed, while a steel double‑drum roller of at least eight tons makes a minimum of two passes before material temperature drops below one hundred eighty‑five degrees Fahrenheit, after which a pneumatic‑tire roller may be used to avoid bridging [2]. Adjacent lanes must be placed within sixty minutes; otherwise the joint is considered a cold joint and requires special preparation [2]. Environmental limits govern every step: concrete placement is allowed only when concrete temperature is between sixty‑five and ninety degrees Fahrenheit and substrate surface is below one hundred twenty degrees, while ambient air or pavement surface temperatures below forty degrees preclude cutting, drilling, or sealant application; evaporation rates must not exceed 0.2 lb/ft²·hr unless mitigation measures such as windbreaks or fog misting are employed, and work stops during hard rain, when descending air temperature falls to forty degrees, when rising air temperature exceeds eighty‑five degrees causing mix temperature to surpass ninety degrees, or when evaporation rates become excessive [2]. Traffic restrictions are stringent throughout the process: no vehicle may travel on freshly placed ATPBC or RCC except for placement equipment, and the new pavement remains closed until all joints have been sealed, cylinder tests show at least three‑thousand six hundred psi compressive strength, and required bond strengths are achieved; if an approved protection method is used traffic may be allowed earlier, but otherwise joint work must be completed before opening [2]. When a longitudinal joint abuts opposing traffic, the adjoining asphalt mixture must be placed within one working day, whereas when it abuts traffic moving in the same direction placement may extend to two days, any deviation requiring amendment of the traffic‑control plan [2]. Newly compacted courses cannot carry vehicular loads for twenty‑four hours or until the surface uniformly cools to one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit, whichever occurs later, while existing pavement may be used for limited traffic up to seventy‑two hours after sawing provided saw cuts do not exceed half an inch; wider cuts must remain closed [2]. Excavated repair areas must be patched within forty‑eight hours and may not remain unpatched over weekends or holidays, and longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths with one‑lane construction required whenever two‑lane work would interfere with normal traffic flow [2][1].

Quality and workmanship

When a longitudinal joint is inspected the reviewer first confirms that the joint surface has been thoroughly cleaned and all debris removed before any sealing operation, and verifies that the sealant reservoir and the specified rubberized joint‑sealing material are present and in good condition while also checking drainage provisions and subgrade stability for proper function [1]. The materials used in the joint must meet all certification and testing requirements; concrete mix is required to be free of frost, lumps or crusts and placed when ambient temperature is between fifty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit, aggregate gradation is monitored daily by sieve analysis, and reinforcing steel, dowel bars and tie bars must carry an epoxy coating that conforms to ASTM A775 with accompanying material certificates [2]. For patching operations the concrete material must match project specifications, typically Class AA Cement Concrete Modified, and the sealant material identified—usually a hot‑pour or silicone sealant—must be confirmed along with the binder coating PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement before placement [1][2]. Spalling is evaluated against depth criteria: spalls confined to the upper half of the slab call for partial‑depth repair, whereas spalls extending beyond mid‑slab depth require full‑depth longitudinal joint repair as prescribed in the specifications [1]; for any concrete overlay existing joint faulting must be milled when it exceeds one‑quarter inch and after placement the remaining faulting may not exceed one‑half inch, otherwise a Superpave scratch wearing course or additional grinding is required [1].

During preparation the existing joint location is accurately referenced so that it can be located after paving, forms are aligned and shimed to position the joint along the centreline, and sub‑base and fine‑graded bedding are installed to design thicknesses not exceeding one‑half inch with cement‑treated bedding achieving at least five hundred pounds per square inch compressive strength as demonstrated by AASHTO T 106 cubes; any irregularities in the sub‑base greater than one inch are corrected by adding or removing material and compacted accordingly [2]. Full‑depth cuts are made for cold joints, the vertical faces of the cut are moistened immediately before placing the adjacent lane to prevent moisture loss, and saw cutting is performed with a diamond‑tipped blade equipped with water cooling, alignment guides and depth control to produce a one‑quarter‑inch wide, full‑depth slot centred over the joint [2]. After cutting, exposed surfaces are cleaned of all loose material within twenty‑four hours and final cleaning is completed no later than thirty minutes before concrete placement using either an air stream of at least one hundred pounds per square inch or a water blast delivering ten gallons per minute at seven thousand psi; a bond‑breaker lubricant is applied at least one hour prior to concrete placement [2]. Dowel bars are positioned on non‑metallic chairs with tilt and skew not exceeding a quarter inch and longitudinal translation limited to one inch, while joint filler—cork, sponge‑rubber, fiber or polypropylene foam of at least eight feet length with a smooth top surface and pre‑punched holes for dowels—extends at least a half inch below the bottom of any repair and the expansion joint material follows the same depth requirement [2]. The interlayer geotextile is inspected for correct placement, continuous extension through reconstructed shoulders, and proper tie‑in to the mainline, with drainage ensured by daylighting or a vertical subdrain [1].

Installation proceeds with tie bars staked before paving; concrete must reach a compressive strength of three thousand five hundred psi before pull‑out testing, fifteen bars are selected from each day’s placement and tested using a centre‑pull hydraulic jack capable of applying up to twelve thousand pounds or until one‑thirty‑second inch slippage, with results averaged for the spacing [2]. Compaction is achieved with a vibratory roller, requiring a minimum of eighty‑five percent of maximum wet density for lifts less than six inches thick and ninety percent for lifts six inches or greater, and overall density must exceed ninety‑six percent as verified by ASTM C1040 field‑density tests performed within thirty minutes of rolling; the paver overlaps the previously placed lane edge by one to one and a half inches, the first roller pass overhangs the unsupported edge three to six inches with the drum kept about eighteen inches from the joint, while subsequent passes overlap two to six inches and uncompacted mix is maintained at least a quarter inch higher per inch of material [2]. Prior to lane placement a uniform coating of the PG‑binder is painted across the joint face, and after paving a sealant reservoir is saw‑cut one‑quarter inch wide and full depth plus half an inch; hot sealant is then applied in a four‑inch band (±one inch) at a thickness of one‑sixteenth inch (±one‑thirty‑second inch), centred within one inch of the joint, with temperature maintained between two hundred sixty‑five and three hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit, excess material removed and any missed spots resealed, followed by additional rolling as necessary to achieve the specified density and produce a tight, smooth transition across the joint [2]. Installation is accepted only when a rigid polystyrene board or equivalent premolded expansion joint material, sized to the existing joint width, is positioned between the new patch and adjacent slabs so that the patch does not contact the surrounding slabs, preserving an adequate gap for thermal movement, and the completed overlay thickness and material continuity are measured against design specifications before acceptance [1].

When work is completed the joint surface is examined with a straightedge: where cross‑slope is unchanged a twelve‑foot straightedge placed perpendicular to the joint must show overall deviation not exceeding a quarter inch and local irregularities limited to three‑sixteenths inch; if the design cross‑slope changes, measurements are taken on four‑foot sections on each side of the joint with the same tolerance [2]. Joint width may not exceed three‑quarters inch in total and no more than a quarter inch on either side of a barrier, and the finished surface is inspected for honeycomb or segregation defects along both vertical edges; any high spot beyond the allowable limit is ground smooth and rechecked [2]. All loose and foreign material must be removed before opening the lane to traffic, which may resume only after the joint has uniformly cooled or after a minimum twenty‑four‑hour period (one working day for opposing‑traffic joints and two days for same‑direction traffic) [2]; the sealant surface is required to lie between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface [2]. For pervious paving the new layer must overlap the existing lane edge by one to two inches while maintaining a uniform uncompacted depth so that after compaction the joint forms a smooth continuous surface, and where longitudinal passes meet the seam must be neat with any bump, ridge or depression not exceeding a quarter inch as measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge; overlap shall not exceed four inches unless an exception is approved [2]. Finally, the project’s quality‑control plan must specify testing frequencies for material sampling, curing temperature monitoring and post‑construction verification to ensure that all inspected aspects satisfy the opening‑to‑traffic requirements [2].

A satisfactory longitudinal joint must exhibit only early‑stage distress that can be remedied by routine cleaning and resealing with the specified rubberized sealant as part of a concrete pavement restoration (CPR) effort, and the proportion of joints that have required crack sealing may not exceed twenty‑five percent after five years of service. Spalling is acceptable only when it remains confined to less than the mid‑depth of the slab, allowing a partial‑depth repair; spalls extending beyond the midpoint demand a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair before the joint can be deemed satisfactory. When a partial‑depth repair is performed, the new concrete patch must be isolated from the adjacent slab by an adequate gap and a rigid polystyrene board or equivalent premolded expansion material that matches the existing joint width; the presence of this bond breaker and the preserved clearance serve as primary acceptance indicators. In high‑intensity rapid (HIR) asphalt projects the use of standard crack sealant on the longitudinal joint is acceptable, whereas excessive sealant application or an over‑banded rubberized joint seal renders the joint non‑compliant. Vertical misalignment, or faulting, is tolerated up to a quarter‑inch without further action; faulting between one‑quarter and one‑half inch requires milling of the concrete joint, and faulting greater than half an inch mandates placement of a Superpave wearing course (scratch) or grinding to bring the fault within acceptable limits. The guides also require that the finished vertical differential across any longitudinal joint may not exceed a quarter‑inch, and surface deviation measured with a straightedge must remain within a quarter‑inch; high spots exceeding this limit are removed by grinding or cutting, and depressions of three‑sixteenths inch or more between adjacent lanes constitute a defect. The joint cut must be made parallel to the pavement centreline with a full‑depth saw cut centred over the existing joint line; the cut width shall not exceed three‑quarters of an inch and the nominal width for all sawed or milled joints is required to be at least one‑quarter inch, while the depth must be at least one inch. Where faulting exceeds a quarter‑inch, the cut must be milled to a minimum of D/3 within twelve inches of the slab edge and to a minimum of D/4 elsewhere, retaining the one‑quarter‑inch minimum width. Ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature must be at least forty degrees Fahrenheit before work begins, and any hot‑asphalt sealant applied to the joint must be heated to between two hundred sixty‑five and three hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit. For patching operations, Type A and Type B repairs incorporate a full‑depth quarter‑inch polystyrene bond breaker in the longitudinal joint, while Type C repairs omit the breaker; simultaneous two‑lane patching follows the Type L joint detail shown on standard drawings. Dowel bars placed in the joint must rest on non‑metallic chairs with a clearance of at least half an inch from the slot surfaces, and their vertical tilt or horizontal skew may not exceed a quarter‑inch; longitudinal and transverse translations are limited to one inch from the intended location. Tie‑bar holes drilled for epoxy injection shall be no larger than three‑eighths of an inch beyond the bar diameter and must not penetrate the slab bottom, with the epoxy providing at least one inch of surface coverage and being troweled flush with the pavement. The joint area must achieve a minimum in‑place wet density greater than ninety‑six percent of the maximum wet density through the full lift depth, producing a tight, smooth surface free of tearing, cracking or uneven roller marks. A sealant reservoir one‑quarter inch wide is cut to the full overlay thickness plus an additional half‑inch directly over the joint and sealed with hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement applied as a continuous band four inches wide (±1 in) and one‑sixteenth inch thick (±1/32 in), centred within one inch of the joint line; the sealant surface must lie between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface, and sealing must be completed before traffic is permitted. Asphalt mixtures abutting the joint must be placed within one working day when opposite‑direction traffic is present or within two days for same‑direction traffic, and traffic may not resume until the compacted lift meets the density requirement, the sealant has fully cured per manufacturer recommendations, and the asphalt surface temperature has cooled to one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit or less. Final inspection with a twelve‑foot straightedge shall verify that no irregularities exceed three‑sixteenths inch and that depth deficiencies do not exceed a quarter‑inch (or an eighth‑inch in three adjacent cores); any violation renders the joint defective. Performance and service life

Longitudinal joints run parallel to the pavement centreline and serve as primary locations for accommodating thermal movement and for potential cracking and moisture intrusion, so their condition directly governs surface smoothness, lane‑to‑lane continuity, load transfer, and resistance to spalling or faulting, which in turn influences overall pavement condition, functionality, durability and service life. [1][2] Design tolerances typically limit joint width to three‑quarters of an inch and require surface deviation not to exceed a quarter‑inch (or three‑sixteenths of an inch for defect criteria), ensuring that the transition between adjacent lanes remains smooth and free of irregularities that could degrade ride quality. [2] When joints are placed within twelve inches of lane markings, saw cuts are aligned with the centreline and over‑cut to a one‑quarter‑inch face extending through the full overlay thickness plus an additional half‑inch for sealant, after cleaning and moistening the faces; this preparation enables effective sealing against moisture intrusion. [2] Sealing is achieved by applying a hot PG‑64S‑22 binder or equivalent sealant to create a reservoir one quarter inch wide and extending the full depth of the overlay plus half an inch, which restores water‑shedding capacity and sustains load transfer across the joint. [2][1] Tie bars or dowel bars placed according to design drawings, spaced as specified, avoiding placement within twenty‑four inches of contraction joints and removing any bar adjacent to cracks larger than one‑eighth inch, provide continuous load transfer while reducing shear‑related cracking. [2] Additional vibratory rolling over the joint achieves full‑depth density and a tight transition, further protecting the joint from premature surface damage. [2] Avoiding placement of longitudinal joints in wheel paths prevents early wear and spalling, contributing to longer durability. [2][1] Systematic cleaning, crack sealing, and timely resealing are prescribed at intervals; a long‑life maintenance strategy typically reseals about twenty percent of all longitudinal joints at the ten, thirty and forty‑five year marks, while alternative programs may clean and seal roughly twenty‑five percent of joints every five years or treat up to fifty percent—including shoulder joints—at ten‑year intervals, with some designs sealing every joint between fifteen and thirty‑five years. [1] For thin bonded concrete overlays designed for a ten‑year service period, a five‑year clean‑and‑seal treating about twenty‑five percent of the joints (including shoulders) provides comparable protection and maintains rideability throughout the overlay’s design life. [1] Early resealing when distress first appears is emphasized because prompt intervention removes debris, restores water shedding, limits spalling and faulting, and thereby extends the usable life to the planned horizon. [1][2] When a joint exhibits spalling, repairs must accommodate joint movement; inserting a rigid polystyrene board or equivalent premolded expansion‑joint material of the same width as the original joint isolates the patch from adjacent slabs, prevents compression failures and point‑loading issues, and allows unrestricted thermal expansion and contraction, preserving functionality and retaining the remaining service life. [1] Partial‑depth repairs placed while slabs are contracted must provide adequate space between the patch and adjacent slab to avoid compression failure; full‑depth repairs are required for spalls extending beyond mid‑slab depth. [1] By adhering to these joint construction tolerances, sealant applications, bar placements, and maintenance cycles, the pavement retains its intended smoothness, structural integrity, functional performance, and durability, enabling it to achieve the service‑life targets specified in design guidelines. [2][1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 71–76), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 269–303), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–462)

Longitudinal joints in both asphalt and Portland‑cement concrete pavements commonly develop cracking along the joint, which is considered a failure when the opening reaches one‑eighth inch for full‑depth cracks or when it exceeds the routine maintenance threshold of about one‑quarter inch for crack‑sealing cycles [1][2]. The standard preservation practice calls for resealing roughly twenty to twenty‑five percent of the joint length during each clean‑and‑seal cycle, which translates to about 615 linear feet per mile in asphalt analyses and approximately 500 linear feet per mile on shoulders, with intervals scheduled at five, ten, thirty and forty‑five years [1]. When cracks exceed the one‑eighth inch limit they must be removed or the affected slab replaced, while openings greater than a quarter‑inch measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge are classified as defective and trigger corrective actions such as milling or full‑depth reconstruction [2]. Spalling is another recurring distress; spalls confined to the upper half of the slab are treated with partial‑depth repairs, whereas deeper or through‑depth spalling requires full‑depth joint reconstruction [1][2]. The development of spalling is linked to water infiltration, an unstable subgrade, inadequate drainage, heavy traffic loading and construction errors such as omitted tie bars or over‑drilled tie bars on cold slabs, all of which compromise the slab’s support beneath the joint [1][2]. Faulting, defined as vertical displacement across the joint, becomes a treatment trigger when it exceeds one‑quarter inch, at which point milling or grinding is prescribed; if faulting surpasses one‑half inch a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing (scratch) course or additional grinding is required to restore surface smoothness [1][2]. Faulting is primarily driven by insufficient support beneath the joint and poor drainage that allow differential settlement, as well as placement of joints within wheel paths which increases loading on the joint [1][2]. Sealant performance failures occur when the sealant band does not meet the minimum four‑inch width and one‑sixteenth inch thickness, or when it is placed outside the temperature window of 265 °F to 320 °F and ambient temperatures above forty degrees Fahrenheit, leading to water ingress and joint deterioration [2]. Proper sealant application also requires clean, dry joint faces and timely placement; a lapse of more than sixty minutes between lane placements creates a cold joint that reduces bond strength unless the faces are cut, cleaned and moistened before resealing [2]. Construction quality factors such as accurate joint fabrication, correct tie‑bar spacing that does not restrict movement, appropriate shimming or grouting before traffic exposure, and achieving at least ninety‑six percent of maximum wet density through adequate compaction are essential to prevent tearing, cracking and weak transitions across the joint [2]. Inadequate vibratory rolling, honeycomb or segregation on vertical edge faces, and bond‑strength test results below 175 psi average (or any individual result under 160 psi) are identified as defects that can precipitate spalling and sealant failure [2]. Distresses associated with finishing operations include diamond grinding that fractures the surface when equipment settings exceed a two‑inch overlap or when grinding occurs within two feet of curbs, bridges or barriers; any newly sealed joint damaged in this way must be resealed [2][1]. Partial‑depth patch repairs can fail by compression or point‑loading if the new material contacts the adjacent slab rather than remaining within a properly sized joint gap; these failures are eliminated by inserting a rigid polystyrene board or premolded expansion joint material equal in width to the existing joint to recreate the original separation [1]. Overall, the recurring distresses of longitudinal joints—cracking, spalling, faulting, sealant failure, excessive joint opening, and finishing‑related defects—are driven by a combination of environmental influences (temperature fluctuations, humidity, wind), traffic loading, drainage conditions, subgrade stability, and the quality of construction practices including timing, compaction, tie‑bar installation and sealing procedures [1][2].

Monitoring and follow-up

After a longitudinal joint is placed, the completed area is entered into the long‑term maintenance schedule that governs periodic inspections throughout its design life [1]. The first routine inspection occurs at the five‑year clean‑and‑seal cycle, during which all joints are visually examined for cracking and about one‑quarter of them—including their shoulders—are selected for crack‑seal treatment to both protect the joint and provide a monitoring action [1]. Subsequent visual reassessments are scheduled at ten, twenty, thirty, forty and forty‑five years within the fifty‑year service plan, with the same practice of resealing roughly 25 % of the joints each time [1]. In addition to these interval checks, the initial post‑construction inspection includes verification of the longitudinal tie‑bars by selecting a representative sample and testing each for pull‑out resistance once the concrete has reached at least 3,500 psi compressive strength [2]. The joint surface is then examined with a twelve‑foot straightedge placed across and perpendicular to the joint; any bump, ridge or depression greater than one‑quarter inch is deemed unacceptable, and high points or depressions of three‑sixteenths of an inch or more are corrected by grinding [2]. For pervious pavement sections, infiltration testing is performed on three locations per ten thousand square feet in accordance with ASTM C 1701, and any deficient areas identified are repaired before sealing [2]. Prior to sealant placement the joint faces must be cleaned, dried and cleared of loose material, typically with a power broom, and the continuous hot‑asphalt sealant band is applied using a pressure applicator that lays a four‑inch wide (±1 inch) strip at a thickness of 1/16 inch (±1/32 inch), centered within one inch of the joint [2]. After sealing, a concrete‑pavement wipe test confirms seal integrity and the inspector verifies that the sealant band is continuous and free of excess material; any gaps are resealed at no additional cost [2]. The pavement may be opened to traffic only after cylinder testing shows the concrete has attained the minimum required compressive strength of 1,000 psi (or as otherwise specified) and surface temperature has cooled to 140 °F or lower, or after a minimum of twenty‑four hours under those conditions [2]. Where depth checks are required, cores are drilled and any shortfall exceeding one‑quarter inch (half an inch for pervious pavement) is considered defective work that must be corrected [2]. If the joint is filled with non‑shrink epoxy grout, a twelve‑foot straightedge is again used to confirm a neat seam within the same quarter‑inch tolerance and the overlap may not exceed four inches unless an approved exception applies; the grout is covered with double layers of water‑saturated burlap kept continuously wet for at least twenty‑four hours while temperature remains above fifty degrees Fahrenheit, after which compressive strength testing must demonstrate at least 3,200 psi before traffic can be restored [2]. Throughout the service life, any pavement markings damaged during sealing are replaced at no extra cost and any irregularities discovered during periodic visual inspections are addressed in accordance with the same quarter‑inch tolerance criteria used during construction [1][2].

When routine inspections reveal that a noticeable proportion of longitudinal joints have developed visible cracking, the need for additional work is signaled; the guides consider the joint deficient when roughly twenty‑five percent of the joints are cracked after five years or when about twenty percent are cracked at ten‑year intervals such as years 10, 30 and 45. A more specific geometric trigger is the appearance of cracks that have opened to at least one‑eighth inch, which also classifies the joint as deficient and calls for repair actions. Early shoulder deterioration adjacent to the joint serves as an early warning, with loss of approximately five hundred linear feet of pavement shoulder per mile prompting a seal‑coat or micro‑surface treatment to protect the joint area. Minor spalling confined to the upper half of the slab indicates that prompt cleaning and resealing are required and that a partial‑depth spall repair, limited to no more than one‑half the slab thickness, is appropriate. When spalling extends beyond the mid‑slab depth or when corner spalls penetrate past the midpoint, the condition exceeds the limits of partial‑depth work and necessitates a full‑depth concrete patch that incorporates a rigid polystyrene board equal in width to the existing joint to re‑establish proper movement. Measurable faulting across the joint also triggers remedial action; faulting greater than one‑quarter inch requires milling of the concrete joint, while faulting approaching or exceeding one‑half inch calls for placement of a Superpave wearing‑course (scratch) mix approximately 9.5 mm thick at the joint or additional milling and grinding to restore a smooth transition. Poor load transfer manifested as faulting or compression failures may require slab stabilization together with dowel‑bar retrofit to improve distribution of traffic loads across the joint. The guides further specify that surface profiling measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge must not exceed a tolerance of one‑quarter inch, and depressions of three‑sixteenth inch or more between adjacent lanes are considered defective, both conditions prompting corrective maintenance. Cracks intersecting the joint within one and a half feet of its location or extending beyond the sawed joint lines are treated as defects that must be addressed, as are surface blemishes larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch when they would affect an overlay. Sealant applications that are uneven, incomplete, insufficiently thick, or placed on a dirty, wet, or loose surface must be removed and resealed with hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement in a four‑inch band at the prescribed thickness, and any pavement markings damaged by the sealing operation should be replaced. Bond‑strength testing after surface preparation that yields an average below 175 psi or any individual result under 160 psi classifies the joint as defective, especially when accompanied by honeycomb, segregation, rain damage, or an improper finish. Exposed dowel bars showing corrosion, misalignment, or extensive deterioration require full‑depth patching that includes installation of a one‑quarter inch preformed cellular polystyrene extending at least half an inch below the repair bottom and re‑sawing through the full depth before sealing the joint. Tie bars located next to the longitudinal joint that fail to meet the required pull‑out resistance are also considered deficient and must be repaired or replaced as part of the joint rehabilitation effort. Concrete delamination that reaches the joint creates separate repair zones unless the affected areas are less than twenty‑four inches apart, in which case a detailed procedure for reestablishing the joint must be followed. Surface irregularities exceeding three‑eighths of an inch, depth shortfalls of one‑half inch or more, or infiltration rates falling below two hundred inches per hour signal that loosening, adding material, or complete removal and replacement is necessary, with the edge of the lane overlapped by one to two inches as required. Any distortion of the lane edge adjacent to the joint must be corrected to restore its original shape, and overlaps between longitudinal passes greater than four inches—or bumps, ridges, or depressions larger than one‑quarter inch measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge—render the joint defective and demand corrective maintenance before acceptance.
