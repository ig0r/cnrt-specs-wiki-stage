Longitudinal Joint

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Longitudinal Joint is a topic under Rigid Pavement.
longitudinal-jointslongitudinal-joint
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-23

A longitudinal joint is the continuous seam that runs parallel to a pavement’s centreline between adjacent concrete (or asphalt‑over‑concrete) slabs and serves as the primary mechanism for accommodating thermal expansion and contraction while transferring loads across the slab array. Distress in these joints—manifested by cracking, spalling, or vertical faulting exceeding about ¼ in.—is managed through periodic cleaning, sealant application, and, when necessary, partial‑depth or full‑depth repairs that restore joint geometry with materials such as rubberized sealants and rigid polystyrene boards to preserve movement clearance. By maintaining the joint’s integrity, the treatment sustains lane continuity, ride quality, and the intended service life of the pavement [1].

Purpose and treatment objectives

The longitudinal joint treatment is employed whenever the joint exhibits distress that threatens the smooth transition between adjacent lanes. Typical manifestations include longitudinal cracking that degrades ride quality, spalling of the concrete slab surface, excessive faulting that exceeds a quarter‑inch, and loss of load transfer or water infiltration that can lead to further slab movement. To control these conditions the maintenance plan calls for periodic crack sealing—approximately 20 % of the joints in some programs and about one‑quarter (25 %) in others—and for cleaning and resealing the joint surfaces with appropriate sealants, using heavy‑duty membranes when extra protection is required. When spalls are confined to the upper half of the slab, partial‑depth spall patches are applied; deeper spalls that extend beyond mid‑slab depth are repaired with full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs. If faulting exceeds ¼ in., the joints are milled, and when faulting surpasses ½ in., a thin wearing course or additional grinding is placed to reduce displacement. By addressing cracking, spalling, faulting, and seal integrity, the treatment preserves lane continuity, ride quality, and overall pavement service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–75), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 10 (p. 185)

The longitudinal joint is treated primarily to preserve and extend the pavement’s service life rather than merely fixing isolated damage. The maintenance strategy calls for periodic cleaning and sealing of a substantial portion of the joints—initially about half of the longitudinal joints, including shoulders, at the ten‑year mark, expanding to all joints by thirty years—and continues this preventive regimen throughout the fifty‑year analysis period. In addition, roughly one quarter of the joints are targeted for crack‑sealing as an early intervention to keep the joint functional and delay further deterioration. When spalling occurs in the upper half of a slab, partial‑depth repairs are applied to restore rideability and maintain joint movement, thereby avoiding the need for overlay. Collectively, these actions—regular sealant applications, comprehensive cleaning, and selective partial‑depth rehabilitation—are intended to keep the concrete pavement functional for its designed fifty‑year lifespan. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 71–75), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91)

Distress characterization and causes

The distress is manifested as cracking along the longitudinal joints that run parallel to the pavement’s centerline. At each clean‑and‑seal interval roughly one‑quarter of those joints—about 615 linear feet per mile, which corresponds to approximately 20 %–25 % of the total joint length—is targeted for crack sealing. The condition is judged severe enough to merit sealant application but not advanced enough to require full‑depth patching, indicating a moderate severity level. The maintenance schedule reflects a progression rate of about five‑year cycles during the first two decades of service, after which the interval extends to ten years. The primary operational impact is temporary delay to traffic users while the sealing work is performed, although no explicit safety hazard beyond the usual construction‑related exposure is noted. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 71–72)

Distress in longitudinal joints is fundamentally driven by a combination of moisture‑related and structural mechanisms that are amplified when the pavement’s support system or load‑transfer devices are compromised. When drainage pathways in the interlayer or subgrade are deficient, water and chlorides infiltrate the joint cavity, eroding the concrete matrix and weakening the bond between adjacent slabs. This infiltration is especially problematic when the underlying subgrade is unstable, because a lack of uniform support allows slabs to rock and creates differential settlement that concentrates stresses at the joint. The resulting loss of load‑transfer capacity manifests as faulting and spalling; even modest fault levels trigger progressive deterioration—faults exceeding one‑quarter inch are milled, while those over one‑half inch require an unbonded PCC overlay with a geotextile or asphalt interlayer and a Superpave wearing course. In addition to moisture effects, temperature‑induced expansion and contraction generate compressive stresses and point loads at the joint face. If the repaired patch is forced against the adjacent slab without adequate clearance, compression can cause partial‑depth repair failure and promote crack propagation toward the patch rather than vertically, leading to spalling. Restoring the original joint width with a rigid polystyrene board or premolded expansion‑joint material provides the necessary clearance for movement and mitigates these stresses. When load transfer is deficient, retrofitting dowel bars, applying slab stabilization or jacking, and cross‑stitching reestablish interlock and distribute traffic loads more evenly. Sealing the joint with a rubberized sealant and constructing a sealant reservoir further protect against water ingress, assuming that the subgrade remains stable and drainage continues to function. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91), Ch. 10 (p. 185)

Applicability and treatment selection

Longitudinal‑joint cleaning and sealing should be avoided whenever an overlay—whether unbonded Portland cement concrete or asphalt—is to be placed, because sealants interfere with the bond of the new surface. When spalling is confined to the upper half of a slab, a partial‑depth repair can restore the joint, but any spall that extends beyond the mid‑slab depth exceeds the limits for a simple patch and requires a full‑depth concrete pavement repair. The same depth criterion applies at corner locations where longitudinal and transverse joints intersect; if the loss penetrates past the midpoint of the slab, only a full‑depth corner repair will prevent compression or point‑loading failures while still allowing normal expansion‑contraction movement. In addition, when joint performance is compromised by faulting or inadequate load transfer—signs that the underlying support or dowel system is deficient—the appropriate response shifts from surface sealing to structural interventions such as slab stabilization, jacketing, or a dowel‑bar retrofit. Finally, Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) resurfacing cannot be used on pavements that contain extensive crack sealant coverage or an overbanded rubberized joint seal; in those cases the pavement must be addressed with alternative preservation, rehabilitation, or structural repair methods. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91), Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133)

Condition assessment and timing

A visual inspection of each longitudinal joint is required to identify the onset of distress, especially spalling; the inspector must determine whether any spalls are confined to the top half of the slab or extend beyond the mid‑slab depth. If spalling is limited to the upper half, a partial‑depth repair is specified, whereas deeper spalls trigger a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair. This assessment is performed as part of every concrete pavement rehabilitation (CPR) project and guides whether cleaning, sealing, or more extensive repair is needed. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90)

Longitudinal joints are treated when they enter a moderate stage of distress, which is identified by the appearance of cracks in roughly one‑quarter to one‑half of the joints or by surface spalling that does not exceed half the slab thickness. At that point a routine clean‑and‑seal preservation cycle is initiated; the schedule calls for a first operation at five years when about 25 % of the longitudinal joints, including shoulders, show cracking, followed by treatments at ten years (when 20–25 % or up to 50 % are cracked), fifteen and twenty years with similar proportions, then at thirty years all longitudinal joints are sealed, and later intervals treat roughly half of the joints. Immediate resealing is performed as soon as incipient distress appears. When spalling is observed, if it is confined to the upper half of the slab a partial‑depth repair is prescribed; patches up to six feet are executed as spot repairs and longer sections as extended joint patches using the specified concrete mix with polystyrene filler to restore original spacing. Spalls that extend beyond mid‑slab depth require full‑depth longitudinal joint repair. Vertical faulting also triggers treatment: milling is required once fault exceeds a quarter‑inch, after which an unbonded overlay may be placed; if faulting surpasses half an inch a Superpave wearing‑course (scratch) layer or additional milling/grinding must be applied before any overlay. [1]

Materials and repair details

The longitudinal joint is first cleaned and then sealed with a material appropriate to the pavement type. For concrete pavements a cementitious or polymer‑based sealant, often a rubberized joint‑sealing material as prescribed in Publication 408 Sections 512 and 521, is applied; for asphalt surfaces an asphalt crack‑seal coating is used. In addition, a standard crack‑seal coating, seal coat, or micro‑surface may be placed on the shoulders at about 500 lineal feet per mile.

When greater durability is required, a stress‑absorbing membrane (SAM) or stress‑absorbing membrane interlayer (SAMI) can be installed full‑width directly on the existing flexible pavement after any leveling work. The membrane must be covered by at least two overlay courses whose combined thickness satisfies the manufacturer’s recommendation; this configuration is limited to flexible pavements with flexible bases.

Joint spacing shall not exceed fifteen feet between thermal cracks, and the underlying slab must be free of excessive or alligator cracking before treatment.

Maintenance follows a five‑year clean‑and‑seal cycle in which roughly one‑quarter of the longitudinal joints and their adjoining shoulders are treated. The same schedule is often extended so that about half of the joint length receives treatment at ten‑ and thirty‑year intervals, with the entire joint length cleaned and sealed at twenty years. Each clean‑and‑seal operation uses a sealant such as a standard crack‑seal coating, seal coat, or micro‑surface material.

When spalling is confined to the upper half of the slab, a partial‑depth repair is performed. The patch thickness may not exceed one‑half of the slab depth and is classified as a spot repair when its length is between fifteen inches and six feet, or an extended repair when longer than six feet. The repair material matches the original pavement, using Class AA cement concrete modified for normal cure schedules or an accelerated‑setting mix when rapid traffic reopening is required. A rigid polystyrene board equal in width to the existing joint is placed between the new concrete and the adjacent slab to preserve joint function. If spalling extends beyond mid‑slab depth, a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair per Publication 408 Section 519 is executed.

For unbonded Portland cement concrete overlays, the concrete layers may be separated by a geotextile interlayer or by an asphalt concrete layer. When a geotextile is specified, any joint exhibiting faulting greater than one‑quarter inch must be milled before placement. A nominal one‑inch (9.5 mm) Superpave wearing course is then placed over the joint; if faulting exceeds one‑half inch, a scratch‑type Superpave wearing course of the same gradation is applied or the joint is milled/grinded to reduce the faulting. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 62–63), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–76), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91), Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133), Ch. 10 (p. 185)

The effectiveness of a longitudinal‑joint treatment is governed by a combination of material properties, compatibility criteria, and the prevailing condition of the existing pavement. Stress‑absorbing membranes such as SAM or SAMI may be employed only on flexible pavements that rest on flexible bases, and the surface must be free of excessive cracking or widespread alligator cracking. Moreover, thermal cracks in the slab must be spaced no more than fifteen feet apart, and the overlay placed over the membrane must satisfy the manufacturer’s depth recommendations while providing at least two courses of material to protect the membrane. When spalling is present, the repair depth is dictated by the extent of damage: spalls confined to the upper half of the slab call for partial‑depth repairs that use a cement concrete closely matching the original slab and are isolated from adjacent slabs with a rigid polystyrene board; this isolation, together with sufficient joint spacing during temperature‑driven contraction, prevents compression failures. If the spalling penetrates beyond mid‑slab depth, a full‑depth longitudinal‑joint repair is required, and all joints and cracks must be sealed with the specified rubberized joint‑sealing material.

For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) overlays, only limited crack sealant may be applied to the longitudinal and transverse joints; extensive sealant or an overbanded rubberized seal renders the section unsuitable. Composite pavements must exhibit a stable concrete deck with no movement at the joints, and any fiber‑reinforced or rubberized material present in the top two inches disqualifies the pavement from HIR treatment. In unbonded concrete overlays, the condition of the existing joint determines the separation layer: joints showing more than a quarter‑inch of faulting require a geotextile interlayer, while faulting exceeding half an inch necessitates placement of a Superpave wearing course or grinding to reduce the offset. The chosen interlayer—whether geotextile or asphalt concrete—must be compatible with drainage provisions, extending through any shoulder reconstruction and being tied into the mainline to ensure uniform support.

Across all treatment types, a stable subgrade and a functional drainage system are essential to prevent water, chlorides, or incompressible materials from infiltrating the pavement structure. When an overlay is planned, joint sealing should be deferred until after the overlay is placed. By adhering to these material compatibilities, dimensional thresholds, and condition‑based requirements, longitudinal joints can perform as intended throughout their service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 62–63), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91), Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133), Ch. 10 (p. 185)

Treatment procedures

The longitudinal joint treatment begins with a thorough preparation of the joint area. All debris, loose material and any deteriorated concrete are removed by cleaning the surface; this step eliminates spalls and other defects that could compromise the repair. When spalling is limited to the upper half of the slab, the damaged concrete is stripped away and a rigid polystyrene board, sized to match the original joint width, is installed to re‑establish the joint space and keep the new patch from contacting adjacent slabs. The board also provides the required clearance for slab movement during temperature cycles. For these partial‑depth repairs the specified Class AA cement concrete mix—either a modified or an accelerated material as called for in Publication 408, Section 525—is placed over the board, finished so that it does not touch the surrounding slab edges, and then cured according to Publication 408, Section 525.3. If spalling extends beyond mid‑slab depth, a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair is performed in accordance with the specifications of Publication 408, Section 519, following the same cleaning and removal procedures but using the full‑depth repair material prescribed. In cases where the joint itself remains sound yet load transfer must be enhanced, epoxy‑coated tie bars are cross‑stitched across the cleaned joint after the sealant has been applied. The final step for all treatments is the application of a sealant to restore continuity and protect against water intrusion, completing the repair while preserving the joint’s ability to accommodate movement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91)

Implementation of a longitudinal joint begins with thorough cleaning and sealing of both the joint between traffic lanes and the joint that abuts the concrete shoulder, a step required on every concrete pavement rehabilitation project. The repair strategy is dictated by the depth of any spalling: when spalls are confined to the upper half of the slab, a partial‑depth repair is applied; if the deterioration penetrates beyond the mid‑slab plane, a full‑depth replacement of the joint segment is required. Where load transfer must be reestablished, a dowel‑bar retrofit is carried out by first cutting two parallel slots per bar with a power‑driven self‑propelled saw that runs parallel to the roadway centreline; epoxy‑coated dowels are then positioned and the voids filled with the selected patch material. The work sequence follows the standard repair protocol: prepare the area, remove deteriorated concrete, place the partial‑depth (or full‑depth) patch, and cure it in accordance with Publication 408, Section 525.3. A rigid polystyrene board matching the joint width is positioned between the new patch and the adjacent slab to restore the original joint spacing and guard against compression failure, a precaution that becomes especially important during periods of pronounced thermal cycling—hot days followed by cool nights. Selection of the repair mix is governed by ambient temperature and the available window before traffic can be returned. When temperatures are high or the reopening schedule is tight, accelerated mixes such as Class AA cement may be used to achieve the required strength within the limited cure time; cooler conditions allow conventional mixes. Because the pavement cannot be reopened until the patch has attained sufficient strength, the timing of traffic closure and subsequent reopening constitutes a practical traffic restriction that must be coordinated with the curing schedule. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91)

Quality and workmanship

When inspecting a longitudinal joint the reviewer first confirms that the joint surface has been thoroughly cleaned and all debris removed before any sealing operation. The presence and condition of the sealant reservoir and the specified rubberized joint‑sealing material are verified, and the underlying drainage provisions and subgrade stability are checked for proper function. Spalling is evaluated against depth criteria: if spalls are confined to the upper half of the slab a partial‑depth repair is required, whereas spalls that extend beyond the mid‑slab depth mandate a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair in accordance with the cited specifications. For any concrete overlay, existing joint faulting must be milled when it exceeds one‑quarter inch; after placement the remaining faulting must not exceed one‑half inch, otherwise a Superpave scratch wearing course or additional grinding is required. The interlayer geotextile is inspected for correct placement, continuous extension through reconstructed shoulders, and proper tie‑in to the mainline, with drainage ensured by daylighting or a vertical subdrain. When patching, the material used must match project requirements, typically Class AA Cement Concrete Modified, and the area preparation—including concrete removal and curing steps—must follow the prescribed procedures. Installation is accepted only when a rigid polystyrene board or equivalent premolded expansion joint material, sized to the existing joint width, is positioned between the new patch and adjacent slabs so that the patch does not contact the surrounding slabs, preserving an adequate gap for thermal movement. Finally, the completed overlay thickness and material continuity are measured against design specifications before acceptance. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91), Ch. 10 (p. 185)

A longitudinal joint is judged satisfactory when it exhibits only early‑stage distress that can be addressed through routine cleaning and resealing with the specified rubberized sealant as part of a CPR project, and when the proportion of joints requiring crack sealing remains limited; after five years no more than twenty‑five percent of the longitudinal joints may have been sealed for cracking. Any spalling present must be confined to less than the slab’s mid‑depth so that only partial‑depth repairs are needed, while spalls extending beyond the midpoint demand full‑depth repair before the joint can be accepted. Following a partial‑depth repair, the new concrete patch must not contact the adjacent slab and an adequate gap for slab movement must be preserved by installing a rigid polystyrene board or equivalent premolded expansion material that matches the existing joint width; the presence of this board and the maintained clearance serve as primary acceptance indicators. In high‑intensity rapid (HIR) asphalt projects, the joint is acceptable when standard crack sealant is applied, but excessive sealant or an over‑banded rubberized joint seal renders it non‑compliant. Finally, vertical misalignment is tolerated up to a quarter‑inch of faulting without further action; faulting between one‑quarter and one‑half inch requires milling of the concrete joint, and faulting greater than half an inch mandates placement of a Superpave wearing course (scratch) or grinding to bring the fault within acceptable limits. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (p. 75), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91), Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133), Ch. 10 (p. 185)

Performance and service life

Longitudinal joints serve both as the primary means of accommodating thermal movement in concrete and asphalt‑over‑concrete slabs and as the principal locations where cracking and moisture intrusion originate. Because the condition of these joints governs surface smoothness, lane‑to‑lane continuity, load transfer across slabs, and resistance to spalling or faulting, their treatment has a direct impact on pavement condition, functionality, durability and the service life that can be achieved.

In the long‑life asphalt maintenance strategy roughly one‑fifth (20 %) of all longitudinal joints are crack‑sealed at the 10‑, 30‑ and 45‑year intervals. This periodic resealing removes debris, restores the joint’s water‑shedding capacity and sustains load transfer, thereby preserving ride quality and allowing the pavement to reach its intended fifty‑year service life. Alternative programs adopt a five‑year clean‑and‑seal cycle in which about one‑quarter (≈25 %) of the joints are treated each time; some concrete‑pavement programs increase the proportion to 25–50 % at ten‑year intervals and even seal every longitudinal joint—including those adjacent to shoulders—during the 15‑ to 35‑year window. When shoulders are incorporated, the same percentages are applied, with half of the joints (including shoulder joints) routinely treated at years 10, 20, 30 and 40 in certain designs. For thin bonded concrete overlays that are only expected to last ten years, a five‑year clean‑and‑seal that treats roughly one‑quarter of the joints (shoulders included) provides comparable protection, maintaining rideability and durability throughout the overlay’s design period. The guidance emphasizes resealing as soon as early distress is observed; timely intervention prevents progressive deterioration, limits spalling and faulting, and extends the usable life to the planned horizon.

When a joint requires repair because of spalling, the type of repair must respect the joint’s ability to move. Partial‑depth repairs placed while slabs are contracted can fail if the patch is compressed or subjected to point loading. Inserting a rigid polystyrene board—or an equivalent premolded expansion‑joint material—whose width matches the original joint isolates the patch from adjacent slabs, eliminates compression‑related and point‑loading failures, and permits unrestricted thermal expansion and contraction. This treatment preserves the joint’s functionality, maintains ride quality, prevents premature cracking, and enables the repaired portion of the slab to retain the remaining service life that was originally allocated.

Overall, systematic cleaning, crack sealing, and appropriately detailed repairs of longitudinal joints protect the structural integrity of the pavement, sustain its functional performance, and are essential for achieving the expected service‑life targets set out in design specifications. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 71–76), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91)

Longitudinal joints in both asphalt and Portland‑cement concrete pavements exhibit a predictable pattern of distress that must be addressed repeatedly throughout the service life. The most frequent manifestation is cracking along the joint, for which the maintenance plan calls for crack‑sealing about one‑quarter of the joint length during each clean‑and‑seal cycle. In practice this translates to roughly 20 %–25 % of the longitudinal joint being resealed at intervals that have been scheduled at five, ten, thirty and forty‑five years, corresponding to approximately 615 linear feet per mile in the asphalt analysis and about 500 linear feet per mile of seal coat applied on shoulders. The treatment consists of cleaning the surface and applying a flexible crack sealant as part of routine preservation work.

Spalling is another recurring distress. When spalls are confined to the upper half of the slab, partial‑depth repairs are specified; deeper or through‑depth spalling requires full‑depth joint reconstruction. The development of spalling is linked to water infiltration, an unstable subgrade, inadequate drainage, heavy traffic loading and construction errors such as omitted tie bars. Preventive cleaning and sealing each preservation cycle helps limit moisture ingress and preserve the original joint geometry.

Faulting or loss of load transfer appears when vertical displacement exceeds about one‑quarter inch, becoming severe once it surpasses half an inch and is often accompanied by visibly rocking slabs. The primary drivers are insufficient support beneath the joint and poor drainage that permits differential settlement. Treatment options include milling or grinding joints that exceed the ¼‑inch limit, installing a geotextile interlayer, applying a Superpave wearing (scratch) course of 9.5 mm thickness when faulting is greater than ½ inch, slab jacking, dowel bar retrofits and cross‑stitching to tie joints that are beginning to fault.

Partial‑depth patch repairs can fail by compression or point‑loading when temperature‑induced expansion and contraction leave the new material in contact with the adjacent slab rather than within a properly sized joint gap. These failures are eliminated by inserting a rigid polystyrene board or premolded expansion joint material of equal width to the existing joint, thereby recreating the original separation and preventing intrusion of the repair material. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 71–75), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91), Ch. 10 (p. 185)

Monitoring and follow-up

Following completion of a longitudinal joint, the area is entered into the pavement’s long‑term maintenance schedule. At each five‑year clean‑and‑seal cycle the joints are visually examined for cracking; roughly one‑quarter of the joints (including their shoulders) are chosen for crack‑seal treatment, which both protects the joint and serves as a monitoring action. In addition to the five‑year checks, the joint condition is reassessed at the ten‑, twenty‑, thirty‑, forty‑ and forty‑five‑year milestones within the 50‑year life plan, with visual inspections repeated and about 25 % of the joints resealed each time. This recurring inspection‑and‑seal regimen provides the primary mechanism for detecting emerging distress and maintaining performance throughout service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 71–76)

When routine field inspections show that the longitudinal joints are beginning to deteriorate, several measurable signs trigger additional maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation. A crack‑seal program is activated once a substantial portion of the joints develop cracks—typically when about twenty percent of the joints are cracked at ten‑year intervals such as years 10, 30 and 45, and also when roughly twenty‑five percent have cracked after five years. At that point a clean‑and‑seal treatment followed by crack sealing is required to restore joint integrity. Shoulder wear can also be an early warning; if the pavement shoulders lose about 500 linear feet per mile, a seal coat or micro‑surface treatment is indicated to protect the adjacent joint area.

Beyond cracking, the appearance of spalling signals the need for more intensive repair. Early distress, such as minor cracks or spalls confined to the upper half of the slab, calls for prompt cleaning and resealing and may be addressed with a partial‑depth spall repair, provided the repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness. When spalling extends beyond the mid‑slab depth—essentially exceeding half the slab thickness—or when corner spalls penetrate past the midpoint, the joint has surpassed the limits of partial‑depth work and requires a full‑depth concrete patch. In these cases the use of a rigid polystyrene board equal in width to the existing joint is recommended to re‑establish proper movement and prevent repair material from intruding into the adjacent slab.

Joint performance problems such as measurable faulting, compression, or point‑loading failure further dictate remedial action. Faulting greater than one‑quarter inch warrants milling of the concrete joint to remove the unevenness, while faulting exceeding one‑half inch necessitates either placement of a Superpave wearing‑course (scratch) mix of 9.5 mm thickness at the joint or additional milling and grinding to restore a smooth transition. When poor load transfer is identified—often manifested as faulting or compression failures—a slab stabilization procedure together with dowel bar retrofit may be required to improve load distribution across the joint. [1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 71–75), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91), Ch. 10 (p. 185)
