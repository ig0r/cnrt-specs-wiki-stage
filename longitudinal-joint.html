Longitudinal Joint

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Longitudinal Joint is a topic under Rigid Pavement.
longitudinal-jointslongitudinal-joint
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-29

Longitudinal joints are full‑depth saw‑cut planes installed parallel to the pavement centreline in concrete and asphalt‑over‑concrete slabs to provide a controlled location for thermal movement and to relieve restraint stresses from dowels or tie bars [1][2]. They function by creating a uniform gap—typically one‑quarter to three‑quarters inch wide and at least one inch deep—filled with a bond breaker such as a quarter‑inch polystyrene board, allowing adjacent slabs to expand, contract and transfer loads while preventing crack propagation, spalling, faulting and water infiltration [1][2]. The joint’s performance is sustained through periodic cleaning, resealing with hot‑pour or silicone sealants, vibratory rolling for full‑depth density, and strict geometric tolerances (≤¼ in deviation, ≤¼ in overlap, offset ≈6 in in successive layers) that preserve lane continuity, ride quality and service life [1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Longitudinal Joint” also include the following subtopics: Pavement-Shoulder Joint.

The Pavement‑Shoulder Joint serves as a specialized longitudinal interface between concrete pavement slabs and adjacent asphalt shoulders in rigid‑pavement specifications. [1][2] The configuration requires cleaning and the formation of a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir to exclude water, chlorides, and other incompressible materials while maintaining load‑transfer continuity. [1][2] Geometric tolerances limit edge slumping and longitudinal surface deviation to no more than a quarter inch as measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge, ensuring smooth vehicle transitions and structural durability. [2]

Pavement-Shoulder Joint

Purpose and treatment objectives

Longitudinal joints are installed to control and remediate longitudinal cracking and associated distress that develop parallel to the pavement centreline. When cracks exceed one‑eighth inch or when an opening or surface unevenness greater than a quarter‑inch appears, the joint provides a planned full‑depth plane that relieves restraint stresses from dowels or tie bars and confines crack propagation. The treatment also addresses longitudinal cracking that degrades ride quality, spalling of the concrete slab surface, excessive faulting, loss of load transfer, and water infiltration that can lead to further slab movement. Faulting greater than ¼ in. triggers milling of the joint, while faulting exceeding ½ in. calls for placement of a thin wearing course or additional grinding to reduce displacement. Spalls confined to the upper half of the slab are repaired with partial‑depth spall patches; deeper spalls extending beyond mid‑slab depth require full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs. To maintain seal integrity, cleaning and resealing of joint surfaces with approved hot‑poured or silicone sealants is performed, with heavy‑duty membranes used when extra protection is required, and approximately 20 % to 25 % of joints are periodically crack‑sealed in maintenance programs. Joint width is limited to three‑quarters inch and a quarter‑inch polystyrene board bond breaker is installed full depth to provide a uniform finished surface and prevent random cracking. Successive layer joints are offset about six inches, and the top‑layer joint is located at or near the centreline (or within twelve inches of lane lines on multi‑lane roads) to ensure a smooth transition between adjacent lanes. Additional vibratory rolling is applied as necessary to achieve full‑depth density and a tight, smooth transition across the joint. By controlling cracking, spalling, faulting, water infiltration, and preserving load transfer, the longitudinal joint maintains lane continuity, ride quality, structural integrity, and overall pavement service life. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–75), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 234–236), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 276–345), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–474)

The primary purpose of the longitudinal joint is to preserve and extend the pavement’s service life rather than merely fixing isolated damage [1][2]. To achieve this, the guides prescribe periodic cleaning and sealing of a substantial portion of the joints, beginning with roughly half of the longitudinal joints—including shoulders—at ten years and progressing to all joints by thirty years, with continued treatment through fifty years [1]. In addition, about one quarter of the joints are targeted for early crack‑sealing as an intervention to keep the joint functional and delay further deterioration [1]. When spalling occurs in the upper half of a slab, partial‑depth repairs are applied to restore rideability while maintaining joint movement, thereby avoiding the need for overlay and contributing to life extension [1]. The preventive intent is reinforced by design measures such as appropriate joint spacing, prompt saw cutting, and additional vibratory rolling to obtain full‑depth density and a tight, smooth transition across the joint, which together control random cracking and prevent premature deterioration [2]. Although contract specifications often treat longitudinal‑joint work as a repair activity—cleaning, sealing, removal of existing pavement, and patching when damage is present—the underlying objective remains to restore the joint’s condition so that its preventive function can continue [2].

Distress characterization and causes

The distress associated with longitudinal joints appears as cracking that follows the joints running parallel to the pavement centerline and also as surface irregularities at the seam where two adjacent longitudinal passes meet along a lane. Across the network, roughly one‑quarter of the total joint length—about 615 linear feet per mile, representing 20 % to 25 % of all longitudinal joints—is identified for crack sealing during routine clean‑and‑seal operations. The seam must be constructed so that any bump, ridge or depression does not exceed a quarter‑inch when measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge and the overlap between adjacent passes is limited to four inches; violations of these limits are classified as defective work. Cracks that have progressed to the point where sealant application is required but do not yet demand full‑depth patching are considered to be of moderate severity, indicating that the distress is noticeable yet still manageable with surface treatments. The maintenance schedule reflects a progression rate in which crack sealing is typically performed on a five‑year cycle for the first twenty years of service, after which the interval may be extended to ten years. Operationally, the application of sealant introduces temporary delays for traffic users while work crews are present, but no additional safety hazard beyond normal construction exposure is identified. When seam defects exceed the allowable geometric limits or when bleeding occurs at a joint, the guides require correction before the pavement can be opened to traffic, underscoring that such deficiencies could impair safe vehicle operation if left unaddressed. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 71–72); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341)

Distress in longitudinal joints arises from a combination of moisture‑related, structural and construction‑related mechanisms that are intensified when the supporting system or load‑transfer devices are compromised [1][2]. When drainage pathways within the interlayer or beneath the subgrade are inadequate, water and chlorides infiltrate the joint cavity, eroding the concrete matrix and weakening the bond between adjacent slabs [1]. This infiltration is further aggravated when the subgrade lacks uniform stability, allowing slabs to rock and creating differential settlement that concentrates stresses at the joint face [1][2]. Repeated wheel‑path loading on joints that are located within traffic lanes imposes shear forces that can exceed the pull‑out capacity of tie bars or dowel bars, producing cracks larger than one‑eighth inch and accelerating faulting [2]. Inadequate anchorage of these load‑transfer elements—such as tie bars placed too close to a dowelled contraction joint or insufficiently anchored dowels—reduces interlock and promotes slab movement under traffic loads [2][1]. Temperature cycles generate expansion and contraction that impose compressive stresses on the joint face; if the repaired joint does not retain sufficient clearance, these stresses can cause partial‑depth repair failure and direct crack propagation toward the patch rather than vertically [1]. Insufficient vibratory rolling or other compaction deficiencies create low‑strength zones and edge defects such as honeycomb or segregation, which act as initiation points for cracking under traffic and thermal stresses [2]. Moisture loss from the vertical face of a cold joint before placement of the adjacent lane reduces bond strength; areas where average bond test results fall below 175 psi (or any individual test is under 160 psi) are considered defective and prone to spalling [2]. Poor sealing or delayed sealing leaves the joint exposed to water ingress, freeze‑thaw cycles, and corrosion of reinforcement, all of which accelerate deterioration [1][2]. When sealant is applied without meeting temperature and surface cleanliness requirements—air temperature below 40 °F, wet debris, or incomplete coverage—gaps develop that permit further infiltration and stress concentration [2]. Excessive evaporation during concrete placement (rates above 0.20 lb/ft²·hr) can lead to rapid moisture loss, increasing the likelihood of cracking and weakening the joint bond [2]. Load‑transfer deficiencies manifested as missing or undersized dowel bars, lack of cross‑stitching, or inadequate retrofitting exacerbate stress concentrations and result in fault levels that exceed service thresholds (¼ in. fault milled, >½ in. requiring overlay) [1]. The combined effect of these mechanisms—water infiltration, unstable support, thermal movement, heavy wheel‑path loading, insufficient anchorage, inadequate compaction, improper sealing, and uncontrolled moisture conditions—drives progressive distress in longitudinal joints [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 345–346), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 360–474)

Applicability and treatment selection

Longitudinal joints are employed in both rigid‑concrete and flexible‑asphalt pavements, but the applicable conditions differ according to the pavement type. For a rigid concrete slab the joint is used only when an existing longitudinal crack or joint requires repair; it must be executed with a full‑depth saw cut along the length of the crack and cannot be applied to transverse cracks or joints. Both the ambient air temperature and the pavement surface temperature must be at least 40 °F before work begins, and all operations must follow the prescribed quality‑control plan, equipment demonstration, drilling and epoxy procedures outlined in Section 500—Rigid Pavements. Traffic may not be permitted onto the repaired slab until the epoxy anchoring material has fully cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. When the pavement being placed is an asphalt wearing or binder course, a longitudinal joint can be formed with a notched‑wedge configuration provided that the nominal maximum aggregate size of the mix does not exceed 19 mm. The adjacent lane must receive its abutting mixture within one working day if it carries opposing traffic, or within two working days for same‑direction traffic. After compaction the newly placed material must remain free of vehicular loads for at least 24 hours, or until the surface has uniformly cooled to 140 °F (or until sufficient stability and adhesion are achieved when both lanes are placed on the same day). For surface courses of flexible pavements the longitudinal joint is created as a butt‑type joint compacted with rollers and subsequently sealed with hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement. The sealant temperature must be maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F, and sealing may only be performed when the ambient air temperature is at least 40 °F unless an exception is granted by the representative. The joint surface must be clean, dry and free of loose material or debris before sealant application. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 280–346), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 434–435)

Longitudinal joints should not be cleaned and sealed when an overlay—whether unbonded Portland cement concrete or asphalt—is planned because sealants would interfere with the bond of the new surface. Likewise, if a longitudinal joint exhibits extensive crack sealant coverage or an over‑banded rubberized seal, hot‑in‑place recycled resurfacing cannot be applied and alternative preservation or structural repair must be selected. When spalling is confined to the upper half of the slab, a partial‑depth repair may be used, but any loss that extends beyond the mid‑slab depth exceeds the allowable limit for a simple patch and requires a full‑depth concrete pavement repair, including corner repairs where longitudinal and transverse joints intersect. The same half‑thickness rule governs all partial‑depth treatments; depths greater than one‑half of the slab thickness mandate a full‑depth patch or corner repair as specified in Section 516. Joint performance problems such as faulting or inadequate load transfer indicate that the underlying support or dowel system is deficient, prompting structural interventions like slab stabilization, jacketing, or a dowel‑bar retrofit rather than surface sealing. Surface profile tolerances also dictate suitability: if the joint presents a bump, ridge or depression greater than one‑quarter inch measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge, or if depressions of three‑sixteenths inch or more appear between adjacent lanes, the joint is considered defective and must be corrected by grinding or other remedial action. Overlap of longitudinal joints beyond four inches (unless an approved exception) similarly classifies the joint as defective and requires corrective treatment. When a longitudinal crack opens within a lane, the affected slabs must be removed and replaced rather than repaired in place. The specifications also prohibit placing longitudinal joints in wheel paths; if a joint would intersect a wheel path or lie within 1.5 feet of an existing crack, sawing is omitted and the pavement is addressed by the alternative repair procedure described elsewhere. Cross‑stitching as a sealing method is unsuitable when ambient or surface temperatures are below 40 °F, cannot be used on transverse joints or cracks, and must not be applied where slabs have drifted and need repositioning or where drilling would penetrate the slab bottom; in those situations a full‑depth concrete patch or slab replacement is required. When irregularities exceed the one‑quarter inch straight‑edge tolerance, corrective actions such as grinding are applied to bring the joint within acceptable limits rather than leaving it in service. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91), Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–474)

Condition assessment and timing

Determining the need for a longitudinal joint begins with a detailed topographic survey of the existing pavement that records elevations and plan dimensions so that the theoretical slab geometry can be compared with field conditions. [2] The recorded ambient average daily temperature and exposure classification are then applied together with the AASHTO expansion coefficients to calculate expected thermal movement, and those calculations drive the selection of panel size and preliminary joint locations before any saw‑cutting is performed. [2] The dimensions of each unbonded concrete overlay panel are measured from lane geometry and placement width, and an aspect ratio (longer dimension divided by shorter) is computed; the guides require this ratio to be as close to 1.0 as feasible and never to exceed 1.5. [2] Joint spacing indicated on the plans is verified in the field, and when no spacing is shown a layout that satisfies departmental criteria, avoids wheel paths, and meets the aspect‑ratio requirement is prepared. [2] During construction the inspector examines adjacent slabs for signs of shear cracking, and any tie bar located next to a crack that has opened at least one‑eighth inch is removed to prevent joint distress. [2] Mechanically inserted tie bars are inspected at approximately 500‑foot intervals on straight sections and about every 100 feet on horizontal curves, confirming that no tie bar lies within 24 inches of a transverse joint. [2] A representative selects fifteen tie bars for pull‑out resistance testing once the concrete has attained a compressive strength of 3,500 psi, loading each with a center‑pull hydraulic jack to either slippage or the prescribed 12,000‑lb limit. [2] Before the slab hardens, the surface tolerance at every prospective longitudinal joint is measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge (or a four‑foot straightedge where cross‑slope changes), and any deviation greater than one‑quarter inch disqualifies the joint location. [2] After placement the inspector visually confirms a neat seam between adjacent longitudinal passes and re‑measures height variation with the same twelve‑foot straightedge, ensuring that bumps, ridges or depressions do not exceed one‑quarter inch and that overlap does not surpass four inches unless an approved exception applies. [2] Existing longitudinal joints within the overlay area are located, accurately referenced, and a sealant reservoir ¼ inch wide and cut to the full overlay depth plus ½ inch is sawed before sealing; when a partial‑depth repair abuts such a joint a ¼‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene insert extending at least ½ inch below the repair bottom is installed. [2] Finally, a visual inspection of each longitudinal joint for spalling determines whether distress is confined to the top half of the slab—prompting a partial‑depth repair—or extends beyond the mid‑slab depth, which requires a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair; this assessment guides cleaning, sealing, or more extensive rehabilitation as part of every concrete pavement rehabilitation project. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–474)

The guides recommend that longitudinal joints be treated once they enter a moderate stage of distress, which is identified by the appearance of cracks in roughly one‑quarter to one‑half of the joints or by surface spalling that does not exceed half the slab thickness. At that point an immediate resealing is performed as soon as incipient distress appears. A routine clean‑and‑seal preservation cycle is then scheduled, beginning with a first operation at about five years when approximately twenty‑five percent of the longitudinal joints, including shoulders, show cracking, followed by treatments at ten years when twenty to twenty‑five percent or up to fifty percent are cracked, and continuing at fifteen and twenty years with similar proportions before reaching a thirty‑year interval in which all longitudinal joints are sealed; thereafter later intervals treat roughly half of the joints. When spalling is observed but remains confined to the upper half of the slab, a partial‑depth repair is prescribed, using spot repairs from fifteen inches up to six feet and extended joint patches for longer sections, applied with the specified concrete mix containing polystyrene filler to restore original spacing. If spalls extend beyond mid‑slab depth, a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair is required. Vertical faulting also triggers treatment: milling is required once fault exceeds a quarter‑inch, after which an unbonded overlay may be placed, and if faulting surpasses half an inch a Superpave wearing‑course (scratch) layer or additional milling/grinding must be applied before any overlay. The guides further specify that longitudinal joints must be altered during initial placement when an adjacent shear‑crack reaches at least one‑eighth of an inch; in this situation the tie bar is omitted and the joint is sawed to control random cracking. After the slab hardens, any deviation greater than a quarter‑inch measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge is deemed non‑conforming and must be ground or cut before the pavement can be accepted, and the same tolerance governs corrective grinding of existing joints that develop irregularities. Prior to any new paving operation, existing longitudinal joints or cracks that are a quarter‑inch wide or larger are required to be sealed with rubberized sealant at least twenty‑four hours in advance. When deterioration results in loss of material not exceeding half the slab thickness, a partial‑depth concrete patch is prescribed—spot repairs ranging from roughly fifteen inches up to six feet and extended repairs longer than six feet—whereas damage deeper than one‑half the slab thickness calls for full‑depth replacement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 71–76), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 345), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–426)

Materials and repair details

Longitudinal joints are located near the pavement centreline on two‑lane roads and within twelve inches of lane lines on multi‑lane highways, with the requirement that they be kept out of wheel paths; joint spacing is limited to fifteen feet between thermal cracks and panel dimensions are constrained to an aspect ratio of 1.5 and a maximum overall size equal to eighteen times the overlay thickness [2][1]. The joint is formed by a saw cut centred over the intended line, with a nominal width of one‑quarter inch that shall not exceed three‑quarters inch and a depth of one inch; within twelve inches of any pavement edge the cut depth must be at least one‑third of the slab thickness while elsewhere it may not be less than one‑fourth of the slab thickness, and the opened joint opening may not exceed three‑quarters inch [2][1]. After sawing the faces are cleared of all deleterious material before sealing, and for concrete pavements a cementitious or polymer‑based sealant—often a rubberized joint‑sealing material as prescribed in Publication 408 Sections 512 and 521—is applied, whereas asphalt surfaces receive an asphalt crack‑seal coating such as hot PG‑64S‑22 binder heated to 265–320 °F and applied as a straight band approximately four inches wide and one‑sixteenth inch thick; standard crack‑seal coating, seal coat, or micro‑surface may also be placed on shoulders at about five hundred lineal feet per mile [1][2]. When greater durability is required on flexible pavements, a stress‑absorbing membrane (SAM) or stress‑absorbing membrane interlayer (SAMI) can be installed full‑width directly on the existing surface after any leveling work, and the membrane must be covered by at least two overlay courses whose combined thickness satisfies the manufacturer’s recommendation; this configuration is limited to flexible pavements with flexible bases [1]. For precast slabs longer than sixty‑five feet, epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars are required, sized either nine‑sixteenths inch with rolled threads or five‑eighths inch with cut threads and installed with a threaded sleeve nut; the assembled bolt must develop a minimum yielding load of fifteen thousand pounds, be placed no closer than twenty‑four inches to any dowelled contraction joint and at least thirty inches from a transverse joint, and be omitted on either side of a slab where a crack of one‑eighth inch or greater has opened. All installed bars are subjected to a pull‑out test when the concrete reaches 3,500 psi compressive strength and must resist either a twelve‑thousand‑pound load or a slip of one‑thirty‑second inch, with any failing bar replaced in accordance with the specified replacement table [2]. Tie bars are installed by drilling holes no larger than three‑eighths inch beyond the bar diameter, injecting epoxy bonding compound, rotating the bar three to five times during insertion, leaving about one inch of coverage at the surface, trowelling flush and then sealing the joint within one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface; traffic is prohibited until the epoxy cures per the manufacturer’s recommendations [2]. When spalling is confined to the upper half of a concrete slab, a partial‑depth repair is performed with a patch thickness not exceeding one‑half of the slab depth; spot repairs range from fifteen inches to six feet in length and extended repairs exceed six feet, using Class AA cement concrete modified for normal cure schedules or an accelerated‑setting mix when rapid traffic reopening is required, and a rigid polystyrene board equal in width to the existing joint is placed between the new concrete and the adjacent slab to preserve joint function [1]. If spalling extends beyond mid‑slab depth, a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair per Publication 408 Section 519 is executed [1]. For unbonded Portland cement concrete overlays, the concrete layers may be separated by a geotextile interlayer or an asphalt concrete layer; when a geotextile is specified any joint exhibiting faulting greater than one‑quarter inch must be milled before placement, after which a nominal one‑inch (9.5 mm) Superpave wearing course is placed over the joint, and if faulting exceeds one‑half inch a scratch‑type Superpave wearing course of the same gradation is applied or the joint is milled/grinded to reduce the faulting [1]. Patch configurations vary: when both lanes are patched simultaneously a Type L joint is used; for single‑lane or staged repairs (Types A and B) a full‑depth quarter‑inch polystyrene board bond breaker is placed in the longitudinal joint, while Type C patches include tie bars but no bond breaker; all patch types employ the same saw‑cut dimensions of one‑quarter inch width and one inch depth centred over the joint [2]. Maintenance follows a five‑year clean‑and‑seal cycle in which roughly one‑quarter of the longitudinal joints and their adjoining shoulders are treated, with about half of the joint length receiving treatment at ten‑ and thirty‑year intervals and the entire joint length cleaned and sealed at twenty years; each clean‑and‑seal operation uses a sealant such as standard crack‑seal coating, seal coat, or micro‑surface material [1]. Heavy‑duty membranes may be placed over joints where directed, and corrective grinding is performed when the joint profile exceeds a quarter‑inch bump measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge; throughout all operations any thermoplastic or tape markings thicker than two‑tenths inch are removed, contaminated surfaces are thoroughly cleaned, and a tack coat of emulsified asphalt is applied extending at least six inches beyond the joint [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 62–63), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–76), Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91), Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 234–236), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 276–346), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–474), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 684)

The performance of a longitudinal joint is fundamentally linked to the condition of the existing pavement, which must be dry, free of oil, grease, standing water, loose debris, heavy paint buildup, and any other contaminant before any tack coat or sealant is applied, and ambient air and pavement temperatures must be at least forty degrees Fahrenheit and rising. In addition, the surface may not exhibit excessive cracking or widespread alligator cracking, and thermal cracks in a concrete slab must be spaced no more than fifteen feet apart. Any break‑back, spalling, surface irregularities greater than one inch, or cracks wider than one‑eighth inch must be repaired, with open joints and cracks sealed using an acceptable crack sealer or emulsion prior to treatment. For flexible pavements that rest on flexible bases, stress‑absorbing membranes such as SAM or SAMI may be employed provided the overlay placed over the membrane satisfies the manufacturer’s depth recommendations and includes at least two courses of material to protect the membrane. When spalling is confined to the upper half of a concrete slab, a partial‑depth repair using cement concrete that closely matches the original slab is required, and isolation from adjacent slabs is achieved with a rigid quarter‑inch polystyrene board; this spacing during temperature‑driven contraction prevents compression failures. If spalling extends beyond mid‑slab depth, a full‑depth longitudinal‑joint repair is mandated and all joints and cracks must be sealed with the specified rubberized joint‑sealing material. Concrete used in the vicinity of the joint must attain a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a flexural strength of 650 psi before any joint treatment, while bedding layers beneath the joint must develop at least 500 psi compressive strength prior to opening to traffic. Dowel‑slot grout must reach a minimum of 2,500 psi, and pull‑out resistance of tie bars is verified after the concrete reaches 3,500 psi, with the required values compared to Table B. Reinforcement steel, dowel bars and longitudinal tie bars are required to be epoxy‑coated in accordance with ASTM A775; tie bars must be deformed mild‑steel members capable of yielding at least 15,000 lb and provided with either a 9/16‑inch rolled‑thread or 5/8‑inch cut‑thread configuration, and they may not be installed within twenty‑four inches of any dowelled contraction joint. Joint geometry is tightly controlled: the longitudinal joint width must not exceed three‑quarters of an inch and depth at least one inch; saw cuts are centered over the joint, quarter‑inch wide and one inch deep, with a minimum cut depth of one‑third slab thickness within twelve inches of pavement edges and no less than one‑quarter elsewhere, and new cuts must be parallel to existing joints and placed no more than one foot away. Full‑depth sawing is required for cold joints or when re‑establishing a joint after repair, and overcutting the existing joint produces a straight, smooth gap; in successive layers joints are offset by approximately six inches. Bond breakers consist of a full‑depth quarter‑inch polystyrene board for Type A and B patches, while Type C patches omit the bond breaker but incorporate tie bars. For concrete base courses the joint filler must be an approved pre‑formed product—cork, sponge rubber, fiber‑based material or closed‑cell polypropylene foam—minimum eight feet long and meeting Bulletin 15 specifications; a quarter‑inch cellular polystyrene filler may be used in partial‑depth repairs, extending at least half an inch below the repair bottom. When asphalt surfacing is applied, hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement is used as sealant, heated and maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F, applied only when air temperature exceeds forty degrees Fahrenheit, and placed a minimum of six inches on each side of the joint; sealants must conform to the class and type of the wearing course, be rubberized for joints one‑quarter inch or wider, and their over‑band thickness and width must stay within Section 469 tolerances. For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) overlays only limited crack sealant may be applied to longitudinal and transverse joints; extensive sealant or an over‑banded rubberized seal renders the section unsuitable, and any fiber‑reinforced or rubberized material present in the top two inches disqualifies the pavement from HIR treatment. Composite pavements must exhibit a stable concrete deck with no movement at the joints; when existing joint faulting exceeds one‑quarter inch a geotextile interlayer is required, and faulting exceeding one‑half inch necessitates placement of a Superpave wearing course or grinding to reduce the offset. The chosen interlayer—whether geotextile or asphalt concrete—must be compatible with drainage provisions, extending through any shoulder reconstruction and being tied into the mainline to ensure uniform support, and proper drainage of the interlayer is achieved by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain. Across all treatment types a stable subgrade and a functional drainage system are essential to prevent water, chlorides, or incompressible materials from infiltrating the pavement structure; joint sealing should be deferred until after an overlay is placed. Wheel‑path locations are to be avoided for longitudinal joints, and sub‑base surface irregularities exceeding one inch must be corrected by removing or adding material before work proceeds. By adhering to these material compatibilities, dimensional thresholds, and condition‑based requirements, longitudinal joints can perform as intended throughout their service life. [1][2]

Treatment procedures

Before work begins, a quality‑control plan must be prepared and submitted for approval at least fourteen days prior to mobilization, and the joint layout is then marked on the pavement with joints positioned near the centreline of two‑lane roads or within twelve inches of lane lines on wider highways, offset from the joint in the underlying layer by approximately six inches. All surface contaminants—including loose material, foreign debris, heavy paint or thermoplastic markings thicker than 0.2 in, grease, oil, fuel residues and existing sealants—are removed by sweeping and, where required, water‑blasting with a minimum capacity of ten gallons per minute at seven‑thousand psi, after which the joint faces are flushed and dried. The original saw‑cut joints are then cleaned to a depth of at least one‑and‑a‑half inches for saw‑cut joints or three‑quarters of an inch for construction joints, and any open cracks one quarter inch or larger are sealed with a rubberized sealant before further work. For spalling limited to the upper half of the slab, the deteriorated concrete is stripped away and a rigid polystyrene board sized to match the original joint width is installed as a bond breaker to re‑establish the joint space and prevent the new patch from contacting adjacent slabs; for full‑depth repairs where spalling extends beyond mid‑slab depth, the existing concrete is overcut with a diamond or green‑cut saw to produce a full‑depth joint whose depth equals one‑third of slab thickness and width between one‑eighth and three‑quarters of an inch. The vertical face of the cut is extended into sound compacted material at least six inches from the exposed edge, excess concrete is removed, and the faces are cleaned of loose material, flushed of slurry, dried and immediately moistened before placement of fresh lane or repair material. For partial‑depth repairs a Class AA cement concrete mix—either modified or accelerated as specified—is placed over the polystyrene board, finished so that it does not touch surrounding slab edges, and then cured in accordance with the prescribed curing procedures; for full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs the same cleaning and removal steps are followed but the full‑depth repair material prescribed in Publication 408 Section 519 is used. Where tie bars are required, they are installed along the longitudinal joint according to shop drawings, spaced at least twenty‑four inches from any dowelled contraction joint, rotated three to five times while being inserted with epoxy bonding compound so that about one inch of epoxy remains at the surface and is troweled flush; Type C patches receive tie bars without a bond breaker, whereas Types A and B receive a one‑quarter‑inch full‑depth polystyrene board as a bond breaker. After the concrete reaches a compressive strength of three thousand psi, water misting is applied for at least seventy‑two hours to maintain moisture, and when strength has risen to three‑to‑five hundred psi selected tie bars are pull‑out tested to twelve thousand pounds or until one‑thirty‑second inch slip, with any failing bars replaced. The fresh paving mix is spread so that it overlaps the edge of the previously placed lane by one to two inches, forming a butt‑type joint; initial roller passes may overhang the unsupported edge by three to six inches, followed by vibratory rolling as necessary to achieve specified density through the full lift thickness and a final static or rubber‑tired roll to eliminate roller marks and produce a neat vertical edge free of honeycomb or segregation. After paving, a sealant reservoir one quarter inch wide and extending the full depth of the overlay plus an additional half inch is cut over the existing joint, and the joint is sealed with the specified hot‑pour or silicone sealant (heated to between 265 °F and 320 °F) applied as a straight band approximately four inches wide and about one‑sixteenth inch thick; excess material is removed, deficient areas are resealed, and pavement markings disturbed by sealing are replaced. The sealant or epoxy is protected continuously until it has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations before traffic is restored, and a tack coat extending at least six inches beyond the longitudinal joint is applied after cleaning and before placing the new course when ambient temperature is forty degrees Fahrenheit or higher and the surface is dry. Throughout all operations the joint is continuously cured and protected as specified, traffic protection plans are updated when lanes forming a longitudinal joint are not placed on the same day, and vehicular traffic is prohibited until the material has uniformly cooled to 140 °F or lower or for at least twenty‑four hours, whichever occurs later. The final step common to both guides is the application of a sealant to restore continuity and protect against water intrusion, completing the repair while preserving the joint’s ability to accommodate movement. Quality and workmanship

When a longitudinal joint is inspected, the first step is to confirm that every material specified for the joint package possesses current certification and test results that meet the project requirements; this includes concrete mix designs that must achieve at least 3,500 psi compressive strength before any pull‑out testing of tie bars, deformed or deformed‑bent epoxy‑coated tie bars meeting ASTM A775 with a minimum yielding load of 15,000 lb, and the hot PG‑64S‑22 asphalt cement sealant maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F applied in a continuous band four inches wide (±1 inch) and about 1/16 inch thick (±1/32 inch) [2][1]. The guide also requires that any joint filler material used in concrete‑base courses be one of the approved types—cork, sponge rubber, fiber filler or polypropylene foam—each piece at least eight feet long, securely joined, with a smooth top surface and properly punched dowel‑bar holes, while patches must employ Class AA Cement Concrete Modified and, for Type A and B patches, a ¼‑inch full‑depth polystyrene board bond breaker; Type C patches receive tie bars but no bond breaker [2][1]. Preparation begins with an accurate survey layout that references the approved longitudinal‑joint locations and verifies that no joint falls within a wheel path, followed by over‑cutting existing joints to provide a straight, smooth interface before new work starts; saw cuts are centered on the intended line, at least ¼ inch wide (or a minimum of 1/8 inch where specified) and deep enough to meet the greater of one‑third the slab thickness within twelve inches of an edge or one‑quarter thickness elsewhere, extending through the full overlay depth plus an additional ½ inch when an overlay is placed [2][1]. After sawing, all joint faces must be thoroughly cleaned of slurry, debris, curing compound, honeycomb, segregation or any other deleterious material by flushing with a water‑blaster capable of at least 10 gpm at 7,000 psi to a minimum depth of 1½ inch; for cold joints the vertical face is cut full depth (minimum six inches exposed), cleared and immediately moistened [2][1]. The subbase and bedding are required to be fine‑graded, moist and compacted, with trial batches of each bedding or grout mix tested for prescribed compressive strengths—at least 500 psi for cement‑treated bedding and 2,500 psi for dowel‑bar grout—and the interlayer geotextile must be placed continuously through reconstructed shoulders and tied into the mainline with drainage ensured by daylighting or a vertical subdrain [1][2]. During installation the longitudinal‑joint centre line is accurately marked with tacks or approved methods, tie bars are positioned exactly as shown on the shop drawings, spaced no closer than 24 inches to any transverse joint, and their locations are verified every 500 feet on straight sections and every 100 feet in horizontal curves; for two‑lane simultaneous repairs a Type L joint is constructed, while Type A and B patches receive the polystyrene board bond breaker and Type C patches receive tie bars only [2][1]. A rigid polystyrene board or equivalent premolded expansion joint material, sized to the existing joint width, is installed between any new patch and adjacent slabs so that the patch does not contact the surrounding slabs, preserving an adequate gap for thermal movement, and rollers are held back twelve to eighteen inches until both lanes are laid before vibratory rolling is applied to achieve the specified density with field‑density testing confirming compaction across the joint; overlap of adjacent longitudinal passes may not exceed four inches unless a waiver is obtained, and the seam formed must be continuous and free of bumps, ridges or depressions greater than one‑quarter inch when measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge [1][2]. After curing, the inspector verifies that the joint surface passes a wipe test for cleanliness, is dry and free of loose material before sealant application; the sealant is then placed to a level between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface, excess material removed and any gaps or thin spots re‑sealed, with damaged pavement markings restored at no extra cost [2][1]. Pull‑out resistance of each selected tie bar is tested with a center‑pull hydraulic jack capable of applying up to 12,000 lb or until 1/32 inch slip occurs, and the average load per spacing is compared with the required values, with any deficient bars replaced before acceptance [2][1]. The completed work is further inspected for spalling depth—partial‑depth repair is required when spalls are confined to the upper half of the slab, while spalls extending beyond mid‑slab depth mandate a full‑depth longitudinal joint repair—and for concrete overlay faulting, which must be milled if it exceeds one‑quarter inch and must not exceed one‑half inch after repair, otherwise a Superpave scratch wearing course or additional grinding is required [1]. Final flatness is checked with a twelve‑foot straightedge, deviation not exceeding ¼ inch (or 3⁄16 inch after correction of high spots), core depth measurements shall not show a deficiency greater than ¼ inch (or 1⁄8 inch in three adjacent cores), and the final compressive‑strength values for bedding and grout must meet or exceed the required 500 psi and 2,500 psi before opening to traffic; overlay thickness and material continuity are measured against design specifications, and documentation of material certifications, QC plans and compliance with coating, thickness and spacing requirements completes the acceptance criteria for the longitudinal joint [2][1].

Joints and cracks a quarter‑inch or larger must be sealed with the specified rubberized sealant at least twenty‑four hours prior to paving, and in high‑intensity rapid (HIR) asphalt projects the joint is acceptable only when standard crack sealant is applied – excessive sealant thickness or an over‑banded rubberized joint seal renders it non‑compliant [1]. Vertical misalignment is tolerated up to a quarter‑inch of faulting without further action; faulting between one‑quarter and one‑half inch necessitates milling of the concrete joint, while faulting greater than half an inch mandates placement of a Superpave wearing course (scratch) or grinding to bring the fault within acceptable limits, and any surface irregularities or depth deviations exceeding a quarter‑inch must be corrected before the joint can be accepted [1][2]. After repair, compaction requirements call for the roller drum to remain approximately eighteen inches from the joint on the first pass, with subsequent passes overlapping the joint by two to six inches, and a vibratory roller is used as necessary to achieve a minimum density greater than ninety‑six percent of the maximum wet density for all lift thicknesses [2]. Performance and service life

Longitudinal joints serve as the primary mechanism for accommodating thermal movement in concrete and asphalt‑over‑concrete slabs, and they are also the locations where cracking and moisture intrusion most often originate; consequently the condition of these joints directly governs surface smoothness, lane‑to‑lane continuity, load transfer across slabs, and resistance to spalling or faulting, all of which affect pavement condition, functionality, durability and the service life that can be achieved. [1] Guidelines recommend periodic resealing of a portion of the joints—typically twenty percent at the ten‑, thirty‑ and forty‑five‑year marks in long‑life asphalt strategies—to remove debris, restore water‑shedding capacity and sustain load transfer, thereby preserving ride quality and allowing the pavement to reach its intended fifty‑year service life. [1] Other programs call for a five‑year clean‑and‑seal cycle treating roughly twenty‑five percent of the joints each time, while some concrete‑pavement designs increase treatment to twenty‑five to fifty percent at ten‑year intervals and even seal every longitudinal joint—including those adjacent to shoulders—during the fifteen‑ to thirty‑five‑year window; when shoulders are incorporated, half of the joints (including shoulder joints) may be treated at years ten, twenty, thirty and forty. [1] For thin bonded concrete overlays expected to last only ten years, a five‑year clean‑and‑seal that treats about one‑quarter of the joints (shoulders included) provides comparable protection throughout the overlay’s design period. [1] The guidance emphasizes resealing as soon as early distress is observed; timely intervention prevents progressive deterioration, limits spalling and faulting, and extends the usable life to the planned horizon. [1] When a joint exhibits spalling that requires repair, the repair must respect the joint’s ability to move; inserting a rigid polystyrene board or an equivalent premolded expansion‑joint material whose width matches the original joint isolates the patch from adjacent slabs, eliminates compression‑related and point‑loading failures, permits unrestricted thermal expansion and contraction, preserves joint functionality, maintains ride quality, prevents premature cracking and enables the repaired portion of the slab to retain its originally allocated service life. [1] The construction process begins with an accurate survey layout before saw‑cutting, followed by overcutting the existing joint to produce a straight, smooth face whose width does not exceed three‑quarters of an inch; tie bars are installed according to shop drawings for slabs longer than sixty‑five feet, and any tie bar adjacent to a crack that has opened at least one‑eighth inch is removed to limit shear cracking. [2] After paving, a sealant reservoir one‑quarter inch wide is cut to the full overlay depth plus an additional half inch and sealed with the specified hot binder (PG‑Binder or PG 64S‑22) applied in a four‑inch band about one‑sixteenth inch thick while maintaining binder temperature between 265 °F and 320 °F; the joint is also painted with the same binder before adjacent lanes are placed, and the tack coat is extended at least six inches beyond the joint under dry conditions when air temperature is forty degrees Fahrenheit or higher. [2] Joints are offset approximately six inches from the joint in the layer below, positioned near the pavement centreline (or within twelve inches of lane lines on multi‑lane roads), kept out of wheel paths, and limited to a maximum four‑inch overlap unless specially authorized; where two passes meet, the seam must not exceed a quarter‑inch bump, ridge or depression measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge. [2] Compaction follows strict overlap and roller‑pass rules, keeping the first forward drum at least eighteen inches from the joint and providing the joint with as many passes as the rest of the mat; vehicular loads are prohibited for at least twenty‑four hours or until the surface uniformly cools to one‑hundred‑forty degrees Fahrenheit, and any surface irregularities greater than three‑sixteenths inch or depth deficiencies over one‑quarter inch constitute defective work. [2] By adhering to these dimensional tolerances, reinforcement details, sealing practices, timing controls and timely maintenance cycles, the longitudinal joint remains watertight, limits water intrusion and cracking, maintains a smooth transition between lanes, and thereby sustains pavement condition, functional performance, durability and the projected service life of the concrete or asphalt system. Longitudinal joints in both asphalt and Portland‑cement concrete pavements commonly develop cracking that requires periodic resealing of roughly one‑quarter of the joint length during each preservation cycle, which translates to about 20–25 % of the joint being treated at five, ten, thirty and forty‑five year intervals—approximately 615 linear feet per mile for asphalt surfaces and about 500 linear feet per mile on shoulders【1】. When cracks widen beyond one‑eighth inch they are classified as shear or full‑depth longitudinal cracks, triggering the removal of adjacent tie bars and replacement of any slab containing the crack at no additional cost【2】. Cracks that appear outside the intended sawed joint line or within roughly one‑and‑a‑half feet of a planned joint are deemed defective, leading to omission of the sawing operation and remedial repair of the affected area【2】. Spalling is another recurring distress; shallow spalls confined to the upper half of the slab are addressed with partial‑depth repairs, whereas deeper or through‑depth spalling necessitates full‑depth joint reconstruction【1】. The development of spalling and other moisture‑related problems is strongly linked to water infiltration caused by inadequate drainage, an unstable subgrade, construction errors such as omitted tie bars, and heavy traffic loading【1】【2】. Faulting or loss of load transfer becomes evident when vertical displacement exceeds about one‑quarter inch and is considered severe once it surpasses half an inch, often accompanied by visibly rocking slabs; insufficient support beneath the joint and poor drainage are identified as primary drivers【1】. Treatment of faulting greater than a quarter inch may involve milling or grinding the joint, while faulting exceeding a half inch calls for placement of a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing (scratch) course in addition to possible slab jacking, dowel bar retrofits or cross‑stitching【1】. Partial‑depth patch repairs can fail by compression or point‑loading when the new material contacts the adjacent slab instead of remaining within a properly sized joint gap; these failures are eliminated by inserting a rigid polystyrene board or premolded expansion joint material equal in width to the existing joint, thereby recreating the original separation【1】. Edge quality also influences joint performance; the joint edge must present a neat vertical face free of honeycomb and segregation with edge slump no greater than one‑eighth inch within six inches of an adjacent lane, and bond strength measured by three pull‑off tests must average at least 175 psi (no individual result below 160 psi), otherwise debonding, surface cracking and loss of load transfer may occur【2】. Sealant placement is temperature sensitive—both ambient air and pavement surface temperatures must be at least 40 °F and the hot asphalt cement maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F; failure to meet these conditions or to apply sealant on a clean, dry surface leaves the joint exposed to moisture infiltration, delamination through the joint and mortar intrusion unless a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene barrier is installed【2】. Premature traffic loading before the joint material has cooled below 140 °F or achieved adequate adhesion further encourages cracking and delamination, while rapid temperature changes, placement temperatures above specified limits, and disturbance of the sub‑base greater than one inch without proper recompaction can lead to settlement and additional distress【2】. Post‑construction operations such as diamond grinding must avoid causing spalling at the joint, be performed more than two feet from curbs or barriers, limit overlap of longitudinal joints to four inches, and prevent bumps, ridges or depressions greater than one‑quarter inch over a ten‑ to twelve‑foot straightedge; violations result in resealing requirements to restore joint integrity【2】. Monitoring and follow-up

After a longitudinal joint is placed, an immediate post‑completion inspection is required before traffic can be opened, confirming that the joint was sawed at the specified time and sealed in accordance with the contract specifications, and that the concrete‑pavement wipe test has been passed. [2] Surface geometry is then verified using a twelve‑foot straightedge both across and parallel to the joint, with any deviation perpendicular to the joint not exceeding one‑quarter inch and any high spot greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch removed by grinding or cutting. [2] The overlap between adjacent longitudinal passes is limited to four inches unless a written exception has been granted, and cross‑slope is checked at approximately one‑hundred‑foot intervals, requiring correction if the change exceeds one‑quarter inch over a twelve‑foot span. [2] Longitudinal roughness is measured and recorded, with an International Roughness Index not to exceed seventy inches per mile on facilities posted above forty‑five miles per hour and ninety inches per mile on lower‑speed roads. [2] Representative concrete cylinders must demonstrate a minimum compressive strength of one thousand pounds per square inch, and laboratory test results for bedding grout, cementitious grout, and any epoxy‑coated reinforcement are submitted for acceptance. [2] Embedded tie‑bars are inspected at regular intervals—about every five hundred feet on straight sections and every one hundred feet in curves—to verify they remain at least twenty‑four inches from any transverse joint, with any damaged tie‑bar or seal resealed according to contract provisions. [2] After tack coat placement, a visual inspection extending at least six inches beyond each longitudinal joint confirms uniform coverage and the absence of uncoated or over‑coated spots; the tack coat must be allowed to break and set without disturbance before paving proceeds. [2] Once the immediate checks are satisfied, the treated area is entered into the pavement’s long‑term maintenance schedule, where visual examinations for cracking are performed at each five‑year clean‑and‑seal cycle. [1] During each five‑year cycle roughly one‑quarter of the longitudinal joints, including their shoulders, are selected for crack‑seal treatment, which both protects the joint and serves as a monitoring action. [1] Additional reassessments occur at ten‑, twenty‑, thirty‑, forty‑ and forty‑five‑year milestones within the fifty‑year life plan, repeating visual inspections and resealing about twenty‑five percent of the joints each time. [1] This recurring inspection‑and‑seal regimen provides the primary mechanism for detecting emerging distress and maintaining performance throughout the service life of the pavement. [1]

Routine field inspections that reveal the onset of longitudinal‑joint distress trigger a series of remedial actions when specific thresholds are exceeded. When roughly twenty percent of the joints have developed cracks at ten‑year intervals such as years 10, 30 and 45, or when about twenty‑five percent are cracked after five years, a clean‑and‑seal treatment followed by crack sealing is prescribed to restore joint integrity [1]. Shoulder wear that results in the loss of approximately five hundred linear feet per mile also signals the need for a seal coat or micro‑surface treatment to protect the adjacent joint area [1]. Cracks that have opened to one‑eighth inch (0.125 in) or wider, as well as any longitudinal crack occurring within a lane, are considered out‑of‑tolerance and require removal and replacement of the affected slab [2]. Surface profiling that shows high points greater than a quarter inch, depressions of three‑sixteenths inch (0.1875 in) or more between adjacent lanes, irregularities exceeding three‑eighths inch (or one‑half inch after compaction), or any bump, ridge or depression larger than a quarter inch measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge likewise constitute defects that must be corrected by grinding, milling or other surface‑adjustment procedures to bring the joint back within tolerance [2]. Depth deficiencies of half an inch or greater, joint overlaps beyond four inches, and infiltration rates for pervious courses falling below the required two hundred inches per hour (or failing ASTM C1701) also trigger corrective action such as excavation and replacement of deficient sections or replacement of the pervious pavement layer [2]. Early spalling confined to the upper half of the slab calls for prompt cleaning and resealing, with a partial‑depth spall repair permitted provided the repair depth does not exceed one‑half the slab thickness; when spalling extends beyond the mid‑slab depth or corner spalls penetrate past the midpoint, a full‑depth longitudinal‑joint repair is required, employing a rigid polystyrene board equal in width to the existing joint to re‑establish proper movement and prevent intrusion of repair material [1]. Measurable faulting greater than one‑quarter inch necessitates milling of the concrete joint to remove the unevenness, while faulting exceeding one‑half inch requires either placement of a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing‑course (scratch) mix at the joint or additional milling and grinding to restore a smooth transition [1]. Poor load transfer manifested as compression failures or point‑loading problems may require slab stabilization together with dowel‑bar retrofit to improve load distribution across the joint [1]. Distortion of the lane edge caused by traffic must be corrected to its original geometry using acceptable procedures, and any delamination that propagates through a joint, identified by sounding with a steel bar, hammer or drag chains, is repaired by maintaining the joint with two separate minimum repair areas; when a partial‑depth repair abuts an existing longitudinal joint, a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene insert is installed in the joint [2]. Sealant deficiencies—including missing, thin, uneven sealant, or sealant applied to a surface that is not clean, dry and free of loose material—prompt resealing of the affected area, with inconsistent or incomplete coverage also requiring reseal at no additional cost; pavement markings disturbed by sealing operations must be renewed [2]. Finally, longitudinal joints located in wheel paths, cracks occurring within one‑and‑a‑half feet of a sawed joint, and any irregularities that persist after final compaction are all treated as defects that necessitate the prescribed repair or replacement actions to reestablish a smooth, continuous surface conforming to specified tolerances [2].
