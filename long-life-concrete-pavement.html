Long-Life Concrete Pavement

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Long-Life Concrete Pavement is a topic under Rigid Pavement.
long-life-concretelong-life-concrete-pavement
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (LLCP) is a design and construction approach for new or full‑depth replacement concrete pavements that seeks to provide substantially longer service life than conventional concrete pavement by employing high‑quality materials, strict control of every construction step, and specific performance criteria [1][2]. The method requires slabs at least eight inches thick, the use of high‑performance dowel bars for load transfer, a central mix plant and slip‑form paver with minimum clearances, and full control from subgrade preparation through placement, jointing, finishing, curing and sawing [1]. LLCP also mandates evaluation of concrete‑pavement ride quality, adherence to lot acceptance criteria, and may include incentives for lower water‑to‑cement ratios, all aimed at achieving the extended durability over a 50‑year analysis period while excluding thin or bonded pavement applications [1].

Purpose and design objectives

Long‑Life Concrete Pavement is intended to address the engineering problem of insufficient durability and service life in conventional concrete pavements that are subjected to heavy traffic loads and adverse environmental conditions, by providing a substantially longer service life [1][2]. To achieve this extended performance the pavement must be constructed with high‑quality materials and construction processes, as required by both documents [1][2]. The design calls for full‑depth replacement or new pavement construction where control of every aspect of the paving operation—from subgrade preparation through curing and sawing—is possible, which is necessary to meet the longer‑life objective [1]. High‑performance dowel bars are specified in place of standard epoxy‑coated dowels to enhance load transfer and durability [1]. The specification excludes applications that are thin (less than 8 in) or bonded concrete pavements, because such configurations do not satisfy the performance intent [1]. For the 50‑year analysis period used in life‑cycle cost analysis, the pavement is expected to sustain service without excessive traffic‑user delay, provided that a defined maintenance regime is followed [1]. The maintenance regime includes cleaning and sealing all longitudinal joints—including shoulders—and all transverse joints; concrete patching as outlined; diamond grinding applied initially to 2 % of the pavement area at intermediate intervals and later to the full width (100 %) of the pavement, with additional grinding on 25 % of the shoulders to account for tapering [1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 72–73), Ch. 4 (p. 100); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 448)

Long-Life Concrete Pavement must be built with high-quality materials and processes—including high-performance dowel bars—and be installed as a full-depth replacement so that every step from subgrade preparation through concrete placement, jointing, finishing, curing and sawing can be controlled; it is required to have a thickness of at least eight inches because the specification states LLCP is not appropriate for thin (less than 8 inches) applications; the primary durability objective is to provide a longer service life than conventional concrete pavement; and a ride-quality incentive must be applied, meaning that evaluation of concrete pavement ride quality is required under Section 530 as specified in Publication 408. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (p. 100)

Scope and applicability

Long‑Life Concrete Pavement is appropriate for new concrete pavement construction, for reconstruction projects—including those that involve rebuilding fractured concrete pavements—and for unbonded concrete overlays, with the performance evaluated over a 50‑year analysis period. It is also appropriate for new or full‑depth replacement concrete pavements where the contractor can control every step of the paving operation—from subgrade preparation through concrete placement, jointing, finishing, curing and sawing—and where high‑performance dowel bars are used. The approach requires a minimum pavement thickness of 8 inches, so it is not appropriate for thin (less than 8 inches) or bonded concrete pavement applications. It presumes the use of high‑quality materials and processes as defined in Publication 408 Section 530, including a central mix plant and slip‑form paver. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 72–73), Ch. 4 (p. 100)

Long‑Life Concrete Pavement should be avoided when the slab thickness is less than 8 inches or when a bonded concrete pavement is required, because LLCP is not appropriate for thin or bonded applications. It also becomes unattractive if the project cannot provide approximately 4 acres for an on‑site mix plant or cannot meet the minimum clearance of 3 feet on each side needed for a slip‑form paver; under those spatial or equipment constraints another design approach may be preferable. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (p. 100)

Design inputs and requirements

Long‑Life Concrete Pavement is governed by Publication 408, specifically Section 530 for LLCP together with the lot acceptance criteria in Sections 506 and the ride‑quality requirements in Section 507, and all construction methods and materials are to follow the provisions of Section 101.03; the pavement must use high‑performance dowel bars, cannot be thin (less than 8 inches) or bonded, and is limited to full‑depth replacement or new pavement where every step from subgrade preparation through curing can be controlled. Projects may include an incentive for a lower water/cement ratio using contract item 0530‑9999 with the incentive amount estimated at 4 percent of the total LLCP SY paving cost unless district experience suggests a lower amount, and ride‑quality incentives are required per Section 507. Additional constraints require a central mix plant and slip‑form paver, an on‑site plant requiring approximately 4 acres within project limits, and a minimum clearance of 3 feet on each side of the paver. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (p. 100)

Design methods and criteria

Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (LLCP) follows the procedural framework set out in Publication 408 Section 530, requiring full‑depth replacement and control of every step from subgrade preparation through concrete placement, jointing, finishing, curing and sawing, with high‑performance dowel bars instead of epoxy‑coated dowels and a minimum slab thickness greater than 8 inches. The specifications also call for a central mix plant and a slip‑form paver (the latter needing at least 3 feet clearance on each side), an on‑site plant that normally occupies about 4 acres, and the use of lot acceptance criteria in Sections 506 and 530 together with ride‑quality evaluation and incentive payments for lower water‑to‑cement ratios. A decision tree is provided to aid districts in selecting the appropriate item of work under Section 501, 506 or 530. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (p. 100)

Long‑Life Concrete Pavement should be designed and constructed using Publication 408 Section 530, which requires the use of high‑performance dowel bars, a slab thickness that is not less than 8 inches, and full‑depth replacement with control from subgrade preparation through curing and sawing; it also requires a central mix plant and a slip‑form paver that has at least 3 feet of clearance on each side and, if an on‑site plant is used, approximately 4 acres within the project limits. Acceptance of LLCP lots follows the lot acceptance criteria in Publication 408 Sections 506 and 530, and ride‑quality evaluation and incentive payment are required as specified in Section 507. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (p. 100)

Structural section and dimensions

According to the Long-Life Concrete Pavement guidelines, a concrete slab must not be thin; specifically the slab thickness shall be at least 8 inches because LLCP is not appropriate for thin (less than 8 inches) applications. The only other dimensional requirement mentioned relates to construction equipment: a slip‑form paver used for LLCP must have a minimum clearance of 3 feet on each side. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (p. 100)

Materials and mixture design

Long‑Life Concrete Pavement must be placed with high‑performance dowel bars rather than the epoxy‑coated dowels used in conventional concrete, and it is limited to sections at least 8 inches thick. The mix design should employ high‑quality materials and may be required to achieve a lower water‑to‑cement (W/C) ratio when an incentive for reduced W/C is specified. Construction must use a central mixing plant together with a slip‑form paver. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (p. 100)

For Long‑Life Concrete Pavement projects the mixture must be produced with high‑quality materials and processes, using high‑performance dowel bars and a water‑to‑cement ratio that qualifies for the incentive pay (the incentive is calculated as 4 percent of the estimated total LLCP SY paving item cost). The pavement thickness shall not be less than eight inches because LLCP is not appropriate for thin (less than 8 inches) or bonded concrete applications, and it must be placed as full‑depth replacement or new construction so that subgrade preparation through concrete placement, curing and sawing can be fully controlled. A central mix plant and slip‑form paver are required; the on‑site plant needs roughly four acres within the project limits and the slip‑form paver must have at least three feet clearance on each side. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (p. 100)

Structural details and interfaces

Long-Life Concrete Pavement calls for high‑performance dowel bars as its load‑transfer system and is designed to be placed using full‑depth replacement and new pavement so that every aspect of the paving operation can be controlled; it is not appropriate for thin (less than 8 inches) or bonded concrete pavement applications. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (p. 100)

Verification and expected performance

The completed LLCP must be verified against the Lot acceptance criteria set out in Publication 408 Sections 506 and 530, which require that the pavement be built using the construction methods and materials described in Publication 408 Section 101.03. In addition, a formal evaluation of concrete‑pavement ride quality is performed as required by Section 507, and any applicable incentives (such as the lower water‑to‑cement ratio incentive) are paid based on that evaluation. Compliance with these criteria ensures that the pavement meets constructability, performance, and overall project requirements. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (p. 100)

Long‑Life Concrete Pavement is intended to provide a longer service life than conventional concrete pavement by using high‑performance dowel bars and full‑depth replacement with strict control of subgrade preparation, concrete placement, curing and sawing; the specification also requires Evaluation of Concrete Pavement Ride Quality as defined in Publication 408 Section 507 and lot acceptance follows the criteria in Publication 408 Sections 506 (for LLCP) and 530. An incentive for a lower water‑to‑cement ratio—set at four percent of the estimated total LLCP SY paving item cost—further promotes durable concrete. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (p. 100)
