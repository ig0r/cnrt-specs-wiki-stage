Surface Treatment

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Surface Treatment is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
surface-treatmentsurface-treatmentsasphalt-surface-treatment
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Surface Treatment is a preservation technique applied to existing asphalt or concrete pavements that are structurally sound but exhibit minor surface deficiencies such as shallow rutting, low‑severity cracking, and loss of fine aggregate [1]. Its primary function is to restore the pavement’s functional condition by sealing cracks, filling voids or shallow ruts, and applying a fresh binder layer that improves skid resistance and protects against moisture infiltration, without providing additional structural capacity [1][2]. Because it is intended only to retain surface durability rather than raise serviceability, it is used when the underlying structure meets performance thresholds and is typically implemented as polymer‑modified micro‑surfacing, thin overlays, or high‑friction treatments under specified warranty criteria [1][2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Surface Treatment is applied to correct surface deficiencies limited to minor distress—specifically minor rutting, minor cracking, and loss of fine aggregate—and shall not be placed on a pavement in need of structural upgrading. For FB‑1 and FB‑2 wearing courses whose high void content leaves the surface vulnerable, a seal coat or Surface Treatment must be placed to protect against water infiltration and maintain density when the underlying pavement is structurally sound; if the FB surface is in satisfactory condition the application may be postponed for up to 3 years. Polymer‑modified micro‑surfacing, a form of Surface Treatment, is used primarily to address rutting that has developed in the travel lane of a structurally sound pavement when the distress is not caused by base failure, and it also serves to restore skid resistance on such pavements. Micro‑surfacing is selected for pavements that exhibit low‑severity cracking, low to medium severity raveling or weathering, friction loss, moisture infiltration, and minor surface irregularities; the treatment can seal cracks, fill voids, address shallow ruts less than 1/2 inch, while a Type B mix additionally fills moderate ruts from 1/2 inch to 1 ¼ inch and provides a scratch or leveling course. Surface Treatment is applied when an existing pavement’s surface durability has declined, requiring a measure to retain the durability of the wearing course rather than to raise its present serviceability rating, thereby preserving the functional condition by protecting it against further loss of durability. High‑friction Surface Treatment is applied to pavements that exhibit insufficient skid resistance or visible surface defects; the treatment seals the surface, provides a fresh binder layer, and embeds aggregate to create a durable high‑traction film, thereby addressing exposed asphalt or concrete, poor aggregate retention, delamination, color discoloration, and cracks 1/4 inch or greater in width. Polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing is used on existing pavements primarily to remediate rutting and cracking; a Type A mix seals cracks, fills voids, and shallow ruts less than 1/2 inch, while a Type B mix addresses moderate ruts from ½ inch to 1 ¼ inch and can serve as a scratch or leveling course, seal coat, or surface treatment, and the Rut Fill mix is specified for deep 2‑inch ruts in a single pass. Under the 60‑month warranty specification, microsurfacing or another Department‑approved surface‑treatment method is required when a performance survey at year 5 determines that at least 30 % of a pavement segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses such as flushing or potholes. [1][2]

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, surface treatments are intended chiefly to remediate existing surface deficiencies rather than to forestall future decay [1]. For asphalt surface treatments or scratch‑coat/seal‑coat combinations, the goal is to correct minor rutting, minor cracking and loss of fine aggregate so as to restore the pavement’s functional condition, and such treatments shall not be placed on a pavement in need of structural upgrading (section 5.2) [1]. In the case of polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing applied to structurally sound pavements, the primary objective is functional restoration—filling ruts, regaining skid resistance and re‑profiling the surface when an overlay is not required (section 5.4) [1]. Micro‑Surfacing also seals cracks, fills voids and shallow ruts less than ½ inch, providing a scratch course or surface treatment that repairs the damaged surface and restores functionality (section 5.4 B) [1]. Hot‑In‑Place Recycled asphalt is described as an on‑site preservation method whose principal aim is to restore the slab’s functional condition while prolonging the pavement’s useful period (section 5.19) [1]. The HIR mixture specifically seeks to remediate rutting and distress, recover and improve performance, and ultimately prolong the service life of the pavement (section 5.19 C) [1]. Surface treatments may be required chiefly to retain existing surface durability rather than to raise the present serviceability rating, which serves to extend the pavement’s service life (section 6.3) [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania specifications state that the objective of a surface treatment is to repair damage and restore the pavement’s functional condition (Section 483) [2]. The micro‑surfacing system described in Section 483 is applied primarily to fill ruts, seal cracks and voids, and provide a scratch or surface seal that restores the riding surface, addressing shallow to moderate rut depths less than ½ inch up to 1 ¼ inch (Section 483) [2]. Under the 60‑month warranty in Section 496, if a five‑year performance survey finds that 30 % of a segment area exhibits two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action by microsurfacing or another approved treatment is required to bring the pavement back within performance limits, reflecting the repair‑and‑restore objective (Section 496.3) [2].

Distress characterization and causes

The distress that prompts a surface‑treatment such as micro‑surfacing is located on the road surface, often confined to the travel lane of an otherwise structurally sound pavement [1]. In the State Route warranty framework the same type of distress is evaluated within each defined Segment—approximately a half‑mile stretch—and is considered significant when the most recent year‑5 performance survey shows that at least 30 % of the segment area contains two or more distresses classified as low severity [2]. The extent of damage described in the polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt system is limited to the surface layer, with ruts shallow (less than 1/2 inch) and confined to the travel lane, while cracking appears as low severity and raveling or weathering ranges from low to medium severity [1]. For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled mixtures the rutting severity should be less than 1 ½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth should be less than 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and raveling or weathering may not exceed a medium rating that yields a rough, pitted surface possibly containing loose particles; minor corrugation, shoving, slippage with an average depth under 1 ½ inches and isolated bleeding are also acceptable, as are low‑friction numbers [1]. The source does not quantify how quickly the rutting progresses, but the loss of skid resistance associated with the rutted surface represents an operational safety concern because friction loss, moisture infiltration and reduced skid resistance can compromise vehicle control, prompting micro‑surfacing to seal cracks, fill voids and shallow (less than 1/2 inch) ruts in order to restore friction and protect the surface [1]. When the condition is judged potentially harmful or unsafe, the Department may execute emergency repairs and restrict traffic, which can result in road‑user liquidated damages for days the roadway is not open to unrestricted traffic [2].

Surface Treatment is applied to pavements whose distress is driven primarily by moisture‑related mechanisms and material aging [1]. For micro‑surfacing projects the selection criteria include low severity cracking, low to medium severity raveling or weathering, friction loss, and moisture infiltration; these factors are identified as the principal mechanisms that degrade surface performance [1]. The polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt mix is used to seal cracks, fill voids and shallow ruts less than 1/2 inch deep, thereby reducing water infiltration that can accelerate cracking and raveling, restoring skid resistance lost to friction loss, and protecting the pavement from further weathering [1]. A double application of the micro‑surfacing material is recommended unless the road surface is in excellent condition, and on Interstates a double application of Type A SRL‑E is required even if the surface is excellent [1]. Water intrusion into a pavement’s structural section is recognized as a major cause of loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation, and pavement deterioration caused by freeze‑thaw [1]. If alligator cracking exists across the entire surface of the roadway and is not specific to the wheel path, it is most likely caused by age oxidation of the pavement [1]. The overlay specifications permit thin asphalt overlays only when surface drainage is good or will be upgraded to good, subsurface drainage is good or will be upgraded to good, and less than 2 % of the pavement requires patching, reflecting that inadequate drainage and insufficient structural capacity accelerate distress [1]. If active pumping is present, it usually indicates a poor subgrade condition that will require treatment before the use of an interlayer [1]. Distress in pavements that receive a high‑friction surface treatment is accelerated when water infiltrates the fresh binder or when aggregate carries excess moisture; the specification requires that the binder not be placed on a wet surface and mandates moisture‑control methods for the aggregate [2]. Application must also be avoided if pavement temperature falls below the manufacturer’s recommendation, and any sudden rain must be stopped to protect the freshly applied material because low temperatures jeopardize proper curing [2]. Poor construction practices—such as deviating from the approved quality‑control plan or failing to document binder and aggregate quantities—can further degrade performance, which is why a designated QC administrator oversees corrective actions and record‑keeping [2]. The treatment’s effectiveness is verified by requiring HFST projects to achieve a friction reading of at least 65 SN40S within 120 days of installation [2]. Under the Section 496 warranty, fatigue cracking or other signs of an unacceptable base condition indicate weak support or inadequate structural capacity, and when performance surveys detect such distresses the pavement may require surface‑treatment remediation [2]. Excessive traffic loading is identified when cumulative ESALs exceed 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life ESALs, accelerating rutting and base failures [2]. Uncontrolled forces of nature—including floods, earthquakes, tornadoes, landslides, sinkholes, or other natural disasters—can cause distress beyond the contractor’s control [2]. Traffic accident‑related fuel or chemical spills, vehicle fires, and/or gouging or goring of the pavement surface also create damage [2]. When these mechanisms produce distresses that meet the performance‑criteria threshold—specifically when 30 % of a segment area contains two or more low‑severity distresses—remediation such as microsurfacing is required [2].

Applicability and treatment selection

Surface Treatment is appropriate on non‑Interstate roads where the pavement shows only minor surface defects such as slight rutting, small cracks, or loss of fine aggregate and does not require structural upgrading; it must not be used on the Interstate system nor on pavements needing more than cosmetic repair. [1] For low‑volume roadways with an average daily traffic of 1,500 vehicles or less, a friction‑bearing (FB) wearing course may serve as the basis for a surface treatment provided the existing pavement is highly flexible, structurally sound and the FB surface is in satisfactory condition; a minimum of three months of warm‑weather traffic densification of the FB surface is recommended before applying either a seal coat or a surface treatment, although when those conditions are met the application can be postponed for up to three years, and on an FB Modified surface a seal coat or surface treatment may not be required for at least four years. [1] Polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt paving (Micro Surfacing) is appropriate where the existing pavement is structurally sound and does not require significant base repair; it is considered viable on four‑lane roadways where only the travel lane is rutted, in special cases such as over trolley tracks or granite blocks, for rut fill on jointed plain or reinforced concrete pavements, and to restore skid resistance when distress is unrelated to base failure. [1] Thin‑asphalt overlay specifications state that a surface‑treatment overlay is appropriate when the existing pavement surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than 2 % requiring patching, both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded to good as part of the project, no structural upgrade is required, traffic volume falls within the ADT limits shown in Tables 10.1/10.2, overlay thickness complies with Table 9.5, and placement follows the weather restrictions set out in Publication 408 Section 413.3(b). [1] Surface Treatment may also be applied to existing asphalt pavements that have completed a minimum 30‑day cure period, or to concrete pavements that are at least 28 days old, provided the surface is thoroughly cleaned and prepared before placement. [2] Cracks that are one quarter inch wide or greater must be pre‑treated with mixed binder prior to placement, and all pavement markings, rumble strips and adjacent utilities must be covered and protected during surface preparation. [2] When applied over an existing asphalt seal coat placed in accordance with Section 470, the fog seal may be placed between two and thirty days after the seal‑coat’s last day of placement, provided the surface is clean and dry, manholes and other fixtures are covered, and ambient air, surface and aggregate temperatures exceed 60 °F. [2] For high‑friction surface treatment, the pavement must be dry and warm enough to satisfy the binder manufacturer’s temperature recommendation; any wet surface or temperature below that limit makes application inappropriate. [2] Application must be scheduled so that anticipated weather will not interfere, freshly applied material must be protected from sudden rain, and all operations must be stopped if rain begins; wind speeds above 15 mph or gusts of 20 mph or higher, as well as imminent rainfall or expected freezing temperatures within eight hours after placement of emulsified asphalt, prohibit application. [2] Traffic may not occupy the treated lane during the curing period for high‑friction surface treatment, and a safe unobstructed passageway for moving traffic must be maintained throughout work; traffic may also be prohibited on fog‑sealed surfaces until the seal has fully cured, especially in shaded areas. [2] Microsurfacing is deemed appropriate for a warranted asphalt concrete segment when the five‑year performance survey shows that at least 30 % of the segment area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, and the repair must meet the surface tolerance requirements of Section 413.3(l). [2] Repairs may be combined into a single continuous patch when the spacing between individual repair locations is less than 100 feet, provided traffic conditions are acceptable as long as periodic traffic classification counts confirm that ESAL estimates remain within the projected values set out in the contract documents. [2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 103–120); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 327–358), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 580–583)

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, surface treatment shall not be used on any Interstate roadway and may be applied on other roads only after the evaluation prescribed in Table 5.1, being limited to correcting minor distresses such as rutting, cracking, or loss of fine aggregate; when a pavement requires structural upgrading another maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair method must be selected. For friction‑bound (FB) surfaces the manual requires that a seal coat or surface treatment not be placed until at least three months of warm‑weather traffic densification have occurred, and it may be postponed for up to three years only when the underlying pavement is structurally sound and the FB surface is in satisfactory condition; if either the structure or surface condition is unsatisfactory a seal coat or surface treatment is required rather than delayed. An independent FB wearing course is appropriate only on roadways with an average daily traffic (ADT) of 1,500 vehicles or less; for higher traffic volumes a combination of binder and wearing courses must be used, otherwise another maintenance strategy should be employed. The manual also states that a seal coat or surface treatment may not be required on an FB Modified surface for less than four years, indicating that early application would be unsuitable and another preservation approach should be considered. Micro‑surfacing is deemed appropriate only when the existing pavement is structurally sound, does not require significant base repair, and exhibits low‑severity cracking, low‑to‑medium severity raveling or weathering, friction loss, and moisture infiltration; if any of these distresses are more severe—such as moderate to high cracking, severe raveling, or rutting caused by base failure—the treatment is unsuitable and a different maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair approach should be selected. The Pavement Policy Manual further limits surface treatment when rutting severity exceeds 1 ½ inches, transverse or miscellaneous cracking depth exceeds 1.0 inch or average crack width exceeds the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and when raveling or weathering ratings exceed medium; under those conditions a more extensive rehabilitation such as an overlay is required. The presence of rubber in the pavement, fiber materials within the top two inches, any mastic patch larger than five square feet, extensive crack sealant, over‑banded rubberized joint seal, non‑isolated bleeding, a pavement consisting only of a seal coat, or underlying base problems also renders surface treatment unsuitable and calls for an alternative maintenance or repair method. When the goal is to raise serviceability rather than merely preserve existing surface durability—such as when inadequate friction, geometric shortcomings, or insufficient structural capacity are present—the manual directs selection of a more extensive rehabilitation, typically resurfacing or full reconstruction, instead of a thin surface layer. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications for High‑Friction Surface Treatment (HFST) prohibit its application on new asphalt pavements until after a mandatory 30‑day cure period and forbid placement on concrete surfaces or repairs that are less than 28 days old; in those situations another maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair approach should be considered. HFST also must not be placed when wind speed exceeds 15 mph or gusts of 20 mph or higher are anticipated during placement, and the binder shall not be applied if the pavement surface is wet, ambient temperature is below the binder manufacturer’s recommendation, or forecast weather would impede proper placement; under such conditions an alternative method must be used. The fog‑seal surface treatment specified in Section 472 may not be applied when air, surface, and aggregate temperatures are not above 60 °F, when rain is imminent or freezing temperatures are expected within eight hours after placement, or when the road surface cannot be made clean and dry before application; each of these conditions requires selection of a different maintenance or rehabilitation method. Finally, the Department’s warranty provisions limit microsurfacing to segments where the year‑5 performance survey determines that at least thirty percent of the segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses; if this threshold is not met, or when repair spots are spaced less than one hundred feet apart, other actions such as crack sealing, base repairs, or a continuous repair must be employed instead of surface treatment. [1][2]

Condition assessment and timing

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10), the first step in evaluating a pavement for surface treatment is an inspection that confirms the existing surface is asphalt and that it is structurally sound, which is quantified by confirming that less than 2% of the area needs patching. The same manual requires verification that surface drainage is good or scheduled for upgrade, and mandates a coring investigation during the design phase to determine whether an adequate bond exists between any underlying concrete or brick base and the existing asphalt layer. In addition, the manual specifies a visual inspection of surface distresses together with sampling and testing of the pavement material and a review of construction/maintenance history and traffic data; the visual inspection should show rutting severity less than 1 ½ in., transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth less than 1 in. with an average crack width not exceeding ½ in., raveling or weathering not exceeding a medium rating, and any minor corrugation, shoving or slippage with an average depth less than 1 ½ in.; the existing asphalt thickness must be at least 1 ½ in. greater than any milling depth. The Pavement Policy Manual also requires calculation of a Present Serviceability Index (PSI) from measurements of roughness, cracks, patches and rut depth; PSI values range from 0 to 5, and non‑destructive testing provides friction values using a Single Locked Wheel Skid Tester, riding quality with a High Speed Profiler equipped with laser sensors, and structural capacity with a Falling Weight Deflectometer; when these objective values indicate that the present serviceability is approaching the terminal level for its Maintenance Functional Classification or that friction or ride quality are deficient, a surface treatment may be required. Under the STAMPP program described in the same manual, determination for surface treatment begins with the STAMPP pavement condition survey, which annually covers 100 % of NHS roads and 50 % of non‑NHS roads (the full non‑NHS inventory is completed every two years); a contracted video‑log vendor follows Publication 336 to perform semi‑automated field surveys that record all listed asphalt and jointed‑concrete distresses, the resulting distress data are entered into the RMS database where Access tools generate summary reports for high‑risk items such as rutting and fatigue cracking, and segments flagged by a STAMPP treatment recommendation for surface treatment are identified as needing surfacing. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 Specifications states that to decide whether a surface‑treatment such as microsurfacing is needed, the Department first conducts automated performance surveys that identify and measure all distress types according to Publication 336, supplemented by manual surveys for flushing and potholes; if the year‑5 survey shows that 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial treatment must be applied. The same specification adds that additional inspections include rut‑depth checks using a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width, routine traffic classification counts to verify ESAL estimates, and when base condition is in question, core drilling of 6‑inch samples with laboratory tests for voids, asphalt content and gradation. The combined results of these surveys, measurements and tests determine the location and extent of any required surface treatment. [1][2]

Surface Treatment should be applied when the existing pavement shows only minor deficiencies such as minor rutting, minor cracking, or loss of fine aggregate and does not require any structural upgrading; it shall not be placed on a pavement in need of structural upgrading. For FB‑1 or FB‑2 surfaces, a seal coat or surface treatment must be placed after at least three months of warm‑weather traffic densification provided the underlying pavement is structurally sound and the FB surface is in satisfactory condition, although the application may be postponed for up to three years. A FB Modified surface does not require a seal coat or surface treatment for at least four years. Polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing is appropriate when the pavement remains structurally sound, no base repair or structural overlay is needed, and surface distresses such as rutting that are not caused by base failure are present, making it suitable for re‑profiling rutted travel lanes, filling ruts in jointed plain or reinforced concrete pavements, and restoring lost skid resistance. Micro‑surfacing is recommended for pavements exhibiting low severity cracking together with low to medium severity raveling or weathering, friction loss, and moisture infiltration, and it may also be applied to fill minor surface irregularities before they worsen. A thin asphalt overlay as a form of Surface Treatment should be placed when the existing pavement surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than 2 % of the area requiring patching, both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded to good during the project, no structural upgrade is required, and on non‑Interstates this condition must exist before a thin overlay thinner than 1½ inches can be placed. The STAMPP program’s analysis that produces a treatment recommendation for surface treatment normally indicates that the pavement section has reached a condition where applying a surface layer will extend its service life. Surface treatment implemented as a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing system is applied when ruts or surface defects fall within the depth ranges defined for its three mix types: Type A for shallow ruts less than ½ inch, Type B for moderate ruts from ½ inch to 1¼ inch, and a Rut Fill mix for deep ruts of about 2 inches. Under the 60‑month warranty, surface treatment (e.g., microsurfacing) should be applied when the year‑5 performance survey shows that at least 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more distresses classified as low severity, at which point remedial action by microsurfacing or another approved treatment is required. Materials and repair details

Surface treatment may be required on cold‑mix FB‑1 and FB‑2 wearing or binder courses because of their high void content, and a seal coat or surface treatment must be placed on the FB surface [1]. The manual recommends a minimum of three months of warm‑weather traffic densification before applying the seal coat or surface treatment, although if the underlying pavement is structurally sound and the FB surface satisfactory the application may be postponed for up to three years; a seal coat or surface treatment may not be required on an FB Modified surface for at least four years [1]. When the pavement is structurally sound and does not require extensive base repair, a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt paving system—commonly called micro‑surfacing—can be used as an alternative to slurry seals, seal coats, or traditional surface treatments, and it may be placed directly for rut filling, re‑profiling of the asphalt layer, or restoring skid resistance without adding a structural overlay [1]. The micro‑surfacing mix is supplied in three types: Type A, which seals cracks, fills voids and shallow ruts less than ½ inch and can serve as a scratch course or surface treatment; Type B, which fills moderate ruts from ½ inch to 1¼ inch and may be applied as a scratch, leveling, surface‑treatment or seal coat; and Type RF, intended for deep ruts up to 2 inches in a single pass [1]. The procedure normally calls for a double application unless the pavement is in excellent condition, and on an Interstate a double application of Type A SRL‑E is recommended even when conditions are excellent (per Publication 408, Section 483) [1]. Hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt, another form of surface treatment, requires indirect radiant heating of the existing pavement to approximately 300 °F ± 25 °F using propane‑heated firebrick trucks, scarifying the surface to a depth of at least 1½ inches, and adding a predetermined amount of recycling agent that is mixed with the scarified material before it is spread by a heated paving screed; the equipment must be capable of treating lanes between a minimum width of 10 feet and a maximum of 14½ feet [1]. After placement an overlay is required before winter and a four‑week waiting period should be observed before applying chip seals, micro‑surfacing, or slurry seal surface treatments [1]. High‑friction surface treatment requires a mixed binder that must be applied to achieve a uniform thickness of 50 – 65 mils and an aggregate placed at a rate of 12 – 15 lb per square yard [2]. The binder components are required to be uniformly mixed to within ±2 % of the manufacturer’s volume specifications using calibrated containers and a low‑speed, high‑torque drill with a helical stirrer, or continuous mixing equipment with a heated metering pump for mechanized applications [2]. Both binder and aggregate supplied by the manufacturer must be stored in a clean, dry environment protected from rain or moisture, each container labeled with the manufacturer’s name, date of manufacture, batch/lot number, trade name, quantity and mixing ratio, and the contractor’s quality‑control plan must document quantities applied, moisture‑control methods for the aggregate, dust‑control measures during placement, and equipment cleaning and maintenance schedules [2]. Application may proceed only when the pavement surface is dry, the temperature meets the manufacturer’s recommendation, and rain is not expected; unexpected precipitation requires immediate protection or removal of the material [2]. Prior to placement the existing pavement must be cleaned by mechanical sweeping and high‑pressure air washing for asphalt or shot blasting to ICRI CSP 5 for concrete, followed by vacuum‑sweeping at a minimum of 180 cfm and 80 psi; cracks one quarter inch or wider are pre‑treated with binder but not overfilled [2]. After the treatment cures, excess aggregate is removed in two clean‑ups—once immediately before traffic opens and again 15 – 30 days later—and any areas showing poor aggregate retention, delamination, or discoloration must be re‑prepared and retreated at no additional cost [2]. For a roller‑compacted concrete base, all loose debris must be removed from saw‑cut control joints before any overlay, additional emulsified asphalt must be placed along those joints, and a uniform void‑free coat of emulsified asphalt must then be applied over the entire RCC surface using equipment approved by the Representative that will not damage, mar or alter the compacted concrete [2]. Surface treatment using a fog seal requires an underlying asphalt seal coat as specified in Section 470, followed by application of emulsified asphalt delivered by a distributor meeting the requirements of Section 460.3(b) at a residual rate of 0.02 – 0.05 gal per square yard; if directed, fine aggregate (cover sand) is spread uniformly within five minutes of the emulsified asphalt at a rate of 2 – 5 lb per square yard, and the pavement must be clean and dry with debris swept away and openings such as manholes covered [2]. A surface‑treatment design must be submitted at least two weeks before work begins and must follow Appendix E of Bulletin 27; the existing pavement is first conditioned as described in Section 470.3(a), after which an emulsified asphalt binder is applied followed by a layer of coarse aggregate in accordance with Sections 470.3(b) and (c) using the prescribed rates and sequence [2]. The polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt paving system used for micro‑surfacing is supplied in three mix types—Type A, Type B, and Rut‑Fill (RF)—with the same functional descriptions as noted earlier, and a double application is permitted when specified to achieve the total design pounds per square yard for the surface course [2]. Under the 60‑month warranty, if a year‑5 performance survey shows that thirty percent of a segment’s area has two or more low‑severity distresses, remediation may be performed by microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment using plant‑mixed asphalt concrete unless other materials are writtenly accepted; each repair must meet the surface tolerance specified in Section 413.3(l) and be executed as square or rectangular patches, and when individual repair locations are spaced less than one hundred feet they shall be combined into a single continuous repair while providing traffic control per the contract traffic‑control plan [2].

They may correct minor rutting, minor cracking, or loss of fine aggregate but shall not be placed on a pavement that requires structural upgrading [1]. FB‑1 mixes are considered highly flexible because the mix has a high void content and uses asphalt softer than PG 64S‑22; when PG 64S‑22 or PG 58S‑28 binder is specified the material must be mixed hot at a stationary plant and placed hot, whereas FB‑1 cold mixes may be produced with a mobile plant [1]. For thin‑asphalt overlays the interlayer material must be compatible with the asphalt to provide a strong internal adhesive or mechanical bond within the asphalt matrix, remain thermally stable and physically durable during placement; the 6.3‑mm Superpave wearing course requires PG 64E‑22 binder while the 9.5‑mm fine‑grade course shall not use that binder [1]. The underlying pavement must be structurally sound, have an existing asphalt surface, require less than 2 % patching, possess good surface and subsurface drainage, and not need a structural upgrade; these conditions are required before a seal coat or surface treatment can be applied after at least three months of warm‑weather traffic densification, although application may be postponed for up to three years when those conditions are met [1]. Micro‑surfacing performs well on pavements that are structurally sound, do not require significant base repair, and where distress is not related to base failure; a Superpave scratch or leveling course must not be placed before micro‑surfacing [1][2]. Project selection for micro‑surfacing limits applications to surfaces showing low‑severity cracking, low to medium raveling/weathering, friction loss, and moisture infiltration, and a double application is recommended unless the road surface is in excellent condition [1]. Type A mix is used to seal cracks, fill voids, and shallow ruts less than ½ inch deep; Type B mix fills moderate ruts from ½ inch to 1¼ inch and may serve as a scratch or leveling course; Type RF mix fills deeper ruts up to two inches in a single pass [1][2]. For high‑friction surface treatment (HFST) the existing pavement must be at least 30 days cured if asphalt or 28 days old if concrete, and concrete surfaces must be shot‑blasted to an ICRI CSP 5 profile before vacuum sweeping [2]. All oils, greases, dust and loose material must be removed with a detergent wash, high‑pressure air or a vacuum sweep using at least 180 cfm at 80 psi, and any cracks one‑quarter inch wide or greater must be pretreated with the mixed binder without overfilling [2]. The HFST binder must be mixed to within ± 2 % by volume and placed at a thickness of 50‑65 mil, followed by an aggregate spread rate of 12‑15 lb/yd²; placement is prohibited when wind speeds exceed 15 mph or gusts reach 20 mph or higher [2]. Aggregate for HFST must be stored in a clean, dry environment and moisture‑control methods documented to ensure proper compatibility with the binder, and the binder may only be placed when the pavement surface is dry and ambient temperature meets the manufacturer’s recommendation; rain or freezing conditions expected within eight hours require stopping application and protecting the freshly laid layer [2]. For roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) slabs performance of a surface treatment depends on slab condition and compatibility of the emulsified‑asphalt binder; after saw‑cutting control joints loose debris must be removed and additional emulsified asphalt placed in the joints, curing compounds are prohibited, and the emulsified asphalt must be spread uniformly and void‑free using equipment that does not damage or mar the compacted concrete [2]. Diamond grinding is not required for RCC, so the existing as‑built surface may remain provided it is clean, and local traffic may be permitted once the cure period has elapsed before treatment placement [2]. For fog seal portions the pavement must be clean, dry and free of damaged aggregate; emulsified asphalt must be placed with a distributor meeting Section 460.3(b) at a residual rate between 0.02 and 0.05 gallons per square yard, and fine aggregate spread uniformly at 2‑5 lb/yd² within five minutes of the asphalt application; fog seal may only be applied when air, surface and aggregate temperatures exceed 60 °F and must not be followed by rain or freezing conditions expected within eight hours [2]. Under the Section 496 warranty, surface‑treatment performance is tied to existing pavement distress level: if a year‑5 survey shows at least 30 % of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses, microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment must be applied [2]. The underlying base condition also governs success; the pavement must meet the specified minimum thickness, achieve a density greater than 92 % of theoretical maximum, and have asphalt content and gradation within the tolerances of the approved mix design [2]. Traffic loading influences suitability: if cumulative ESALs exceed the projected 20‑year design life, the contractor is relieved of responsibility for rutting or base‑condition failures, implying surface treatments must be compatible with the anticipated load spectrum [2]. Candidate pavements for surface‑treatment must meet distress limits of rutting severity less than 1½ in., transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth less than 1.0 in. with average crack width not exceeding ≤ ½ in., raveling/weathering limited to a medium rating, corrugation, shoving or slippage averaging less than 1½ in.; bleeding is acceptable only in isolated areas [1]. Pavement must be free of incompatible materials: the presence of rubber or fiber material within the top two inches renders it unacceptable for hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) process; paving fabric must lie at least 1½ in. below the cut depth, raised markers and durable markings must be removed, and mastic patches larger than 5 sq ft are not allowed, although limited crack sealant is permissible [1]. The HIR process heats existing asphalt to approximately 300 °F ± 25 °F and scarifies it to a depth of at least 1½ in., improving interlayer bonding for subsequent surface treatments; however, a four‑week waiting period is required before placing chip seals, micro‑surfacing, or slurry seal treatments [1]. When selecting an overlay as the final surface treatment after HIR, engineers must consider existing pavement condition, pavement type, and average daily traffic to achieve compatible and durable results [1].

Treatment procedures

Before any surface‑treatment operation a design must be submitted at least two weeks before the scheduled start for review, and all existing pavement markings, rumble strips and adjacent utilities are to be covered and protected as directed; thermoplastic markings newer than one year are removed, and cracks one‑quarter inch or wider are cleared of loose material and pre‑treated with the mixed binder. For asphalt surfaces the pavement is mechanically swept, high‑pressure air washed, then vacuum‑swept using at least 180 cfm and 80 psi clean dry air, while concrete surfaces are shot‑blasted to meet ICRI CSP 5 requirements and likewise vacuum‑swept; when the treatment will be placed over roller‑compacted concrete, the RCC control joints are first saw‑cut and all loose debris from the cutting operation is removed. Where indicated a separate scratch or leveling course is placed ahead of resurfacing operations to fill wheel ruts and even local depressions, and if profile correction is needed heater planing or milling may be used, but when milling an asphalt layer over a cement concrete or brick base a minimum of 1 ½ inches of existing asphalt must be left in place to retain structural integrity. A non‑tracking asphalt tack coat (NTT/CNTT) is recommended for applications with stopping, turning, or slow moving traffic before the new overlay is placed, and a uniform, void‑free layer of emulsified asphalt is applied as the tack coat in accordance with Section 460.3(b) using equipment that will not damage or mar the compacted RCC surface; no curing compounds are used and the surface does not require diamond grinding before the surface treatment is placed. The overlay itself is then placed using the specified Superpave mix at the minimum and maximum depths prescribed in the relevant tables, while for high‑friction surface treatment the binder components are uniformly mixed to within ±2 % of the manufacturer’s volume specification and immediately after mixing a serrated squeegee is used to distribute a uniform film 50–65 mil thick; emulsified asphalt for fog seal is placed with an approved distributor at a residual rate of 0.02 to 0.05 gal per square yard, and while the binder remains wet coarse aggregate for HFST is mechanically spread at a rate of 12–15 lb per square yard, whereas fine aggregate for fog seal is uniformly distributed within five minutes of the emulsified‑asphalt application at a rate of 2 to 5 lb per square yard followed by another sweep before traffic is allowed. The rates and sequence for applying emulsified asphalt and coarse aggregate for standard asphalt surface treatment follow Sections 470.3(b) and (c). Compaction, finishing, and curing are performed according to standard paving practice; traffic and equipment are barred during curing, HFST may not be placed until after a mandatory 30‑day cure period, concrete surfaces must be at least 28 days old before treatment, and the RCC pavement may be opened to local traffic after the cure period has elapsed. After the binder cures a first aggregate cleanup is performed, a second cleanup occurs between 15 and 30 days later, and final acceptance requires a calibrated skid test at least 31 days after placement; the fog seal must cure for at least two days, with shaded areas checked before opening to traffic, and permanent pavement markings are deferred until three days after the last day of fog‑seal placement. Surface treatments must not be used on the Interstate system and their use on other roads must be determined in accordance with Table 5.1, and wearing courses are limited to roadways with an ADT of 1,500 or less unless combined with a binder course [1]. For FB surface courses, FB‑1 wearing and binder materials are “cold” mixes that are usually produced by a mobile plant, whereas FB‑2 mixes must be mixed at a stationary mixing plant and require hot mixing and placement [1]. A minimum of three months of warm‑weather traffic densification of the FB surface is recommended before the application of either a seal coat or surface treatment, although when the underlying pavement is structurally sound and the FB surface is in satisfactory condition it is possible to postpone these applications for up to three years; a FB Modified surface need not receive a seal coat for at least four years [1]. When polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro surfacing is selected for use, the specifications must be followed exactly and on Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement and Jointed Reinforced Concrete Pavement a Superpave scratch or leveling course must not be placed before applying the micro surfacing; the treatment performs well under traffic when proper controls have been maintained [1]. Implementation of a surface‑treatment overlay follows a defined sequence that begins with any required scratch or leveling course, and only a leveling course can be used to correct the profile or to re‑establish the proper cross‑section of the roadway; the leveling course must be at least 1 inch thick and should not be combined with a binder layer [1]. Thin asphalt overlays will only be placed per the pavement depth limitations stated in Table 9.5 and in accordance with the weather limitations stated in Publication 408, Specifications, Section 413.3(b) when the existing pavement is structurally sound, less than 2 % of the pavement requires patching, surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded, no structural upgrade is needed, and the thickness meets the minimum of 1½ inches [1]. Traffic considerations include observing the ADT limits shown in Tables 10.1/10.2 and, when stopping, turning or slow‑moving traffic is expected, the use of a Non‑Tracking Asphalt Tack Coat is recommended [1]. Implementation of a Hot In‑Place Recycled (HIR) asphalt surface treatment requires a train of either two or three propane‑heated heater trucks that use firebrick to indirectly heat the pavement to approximately 300 °F ± 25 °F via radiant heat, scarify the material to a depth of at least 1½ in., add a predetermined amount of recycling agent, mix the recycling agent and scarified material, and spread the rejuvenated material using a heated paving screed; equipment can treat a minimum width of 10 feet and a maximum of 14½ feet [1]. The HIR process follows the sequence of heating, scarifying, adding agent, mixing, placing, and compacting, and after completion a four‑week waiting period is required before placing chip seals, microsurfacing, or slurry seal surface treatments, with an overlay required to be placed before winter [1]. Any pavement repairs, culvert cuts, milling or other preparatory work shall be finished before the HIR process begins; milling beforehand can address deeper cracks and help retain vertical clearance for overhead structures, curbs, gutters, and guide rails [1]. The HIR layer is typically 1½ inches thick with a range of 1 to 2 inches, and thin layers below the HIR asphalt should be avoided especially if cutting less than 1½ inches; traffic can be opened on the HIR pavement as soon as the pavement is cooled to the required temperature [1]. Implementation of a high‑friction surface treatment (HFST) follows a defined sequence in which existing markings and utilities are covered, the pavement is cleaned—mechanical sweeping for asphalt or shot blasting for concrete—and rinsed; cracks that are at least ¼ in. wide are pre‑treated with binder, and the binder is applied only after the surface has dried [2]. The binder must be mixed to ±2 % by volume using a low‑speed high‑torque drill or other approved equipment and spread to a thickness of 50–65 mil before aggregate is placed at a rate of 12–15 lb/yd² [2]. Equipment such as serrated squeegees, semi‑mechanized mixers, or fully automated continuous applicators may be used, but the latter cannot operate when wind speed exceeds 15 mph or gusts exceed 20 mph and must place material at a minimum of 2,000 yd²/hr [2]. HFST shall not be applied to newly placed asphalt pavement until after a mandatory 30‑day cure period; during the curing period contractor equipment and traffic are not allowed on the treated area and loose aggregate must be removed from active lanes before traffic resumes [2]. When surface treatment follows a roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) base course, the control joints are first saw‑cut; after cutting, loose debris must be removed and additional emulsified asphalt applied along the joints using equipment that does not damage, mar, or alter the compacted RCC surface, and such equipment must be approved by the Representative before use [2]. Once the prescribed cure period has elapsed, the pavement may be opened to local traffic prior to placement of the surface treatment [2]. For fog‑seal surface treatments the underlying seal coat must have cured at least two days and the fog seal must be placed within a window of two to thirty days after the last day of seal‑coat placement; the pavement shall be clean and dry, a motorized broom is used for sweeping and a distributor that meets Section 460.3(b) applies the emulsified asphalt, and fine aggregate, when required, is applied within five minutes of the binder [2]. Application is permitted only when air, surface, and aggregate temperatures are above 60 °F, and it shall be avoided if rain is imminent or freezing temperatures are expected within eight hours after placement; traffic may not enter the treated area until the fog seal has fully cured, shaded sections must be checked for cure, and permanent markings are prohibited until three days after the last day of fog‑seal placement [2]. Binder application is prohibited when the pavement surface is wet, when temperatures are below the manufacturer’s recommendation, or when forecast weather would impede proper placement; all operations shall be halted immediately if rain begins, with the possibility of removal and replacement of material damaged by rain or low temperature [2].

Quality and workmanship

Inspection of a surface‑treatment project should confirm that the existing material meets the HIR candidate criteria, namely that no rubber or fiber is present in the top 2 inches and that any paving fabric lies at least 1½ inches below the proposed cut depth, while all durable markings and mastic patches larger than 5 sq ft have been removed. The preparation stage must be verified against the distress limits – rutting severity less than 1½ inches, transverse or miscellaneous cracking depth under 1 inch with average crack width not exceeding ½ inch, raveling limited to a medium rating, and corrugation or shoving depths below 1½ inches, with only isolated bleeding permitted. During installation the asphalt layer must be placed at least 1½ inches thicker than the milling depth and any crack sealant should be limited to acceptable joint sealing, after which the completed surface is checked for compliance with these material, thickness, and distress thresholds before opening to traffic. For high‑friction surface treatment the contractor must store all binder and aggregate materials in a clean, dry area, protected from rain or moisture, and each batch shall be labeled with the manufacturer’s name, date of manufacture, batch/lot number, trade name, quantity and mixing ratio. The quality‑control plan shall document binder and aggregate quantities so that application rates may be confirmed to be according to the specification, and moisture‑control methods for the aggregate as well as dust‑control measures during placement must be enforced together with a cleaning‑and‑maintenance schedule for equipment. Inspectors should verify that all existing markings and utilities are protected before preparation, that the pavement is washed with detergent, rinsed, dried with hot compressed air, and that cracks ≥¼ inch are pre‑treated with binder without overfill. For asphalt surfaces the preparation shall include mechanical sweeping followed by vacuum or air washing at a minimum of 180 cfm and 80 psi to remove dust; concrete surfaces shall be shot‑blasted to meet ICRI CSP 5 and then similarly air‑washed. After saw cutting the RCC control joints, all loose debris must be removed before any asphalt wearing course or surface treatment is placed. The emulsified‑asphalt material shall be applied in accordance with Section 460.3(b) to produce a uniform, void‑free coat over the RCC surface, and the equipment used shall be approved by the Representative and shall not damage, mar or alter the compacted pavement; curing compounds shall not be used. For HFST installation the binder mix shall be proportioned in calibrated containers and blended to within ±2 % of the manufacturer’s volume specifications, and it shall be applied to a uniform thickness of 50–65 mil. Aggregate shall be mechanically spread onto the wet, uncured binder at a uniform rate of 12–15 lb per square yard without leaving exposed binder or loose aggregate on active lanes, and placement shall not occur if wind speed exceeds 15 mph or if wind gusts of 20 mph or higher are anticipated. For fog‑seal application the underlying pavement must be clean and dry, air, surface and aggregate temperatures must exceed 60°F before placement, and the emulsified asphalt shall be applied at a rate that yields a residual asphalt amount between 0.02 and 0.05 gallons per square yard as confirmed by a test strip; any fine‑aggregate cover sand shall be spread uniformly at 2–5 lb per square yard within five minutes of the emulsion application. After curing, the first aggregate cleanup shall be performed, followed by a second cleanup 15–30 days later, and any untreated or delaminated spots shall be re‑treated. A calibrated skid test shall be performed at least 31 days after installation in accordance with ASTM E274/E274M, and shaded areas shall be checked and cure confirmed before opening to traffic; markings shall follow the prescribed curing periods. Field sampling and testing—including temperature checks, surface‑wetness verification, protection from unexpected rain, and post‑placement skid‑number measurements—must be performed and recorded, and any deviation or unsatisfactory test result shall trigger corrective actions and may require removal or replacement of the affected material. Performance and service life

Because FB‑1 and FB‑2 surfaces have a high void content, a seal coat or surface treatment must be placed on the FB surface to improve its condition and durability by sealing those voids; however, a minimum of 3 months of warm‑weather traffic densification is recommended before the seal coat or surface treatment can be applied. When the underlying pavement is structurally sound and the FB surface is in satisfactory condition, it is possible to postpone these applications for up to 3 years without compromising functionality. A FB Modified surface may not require any seal coat or surface treatment for at least 4 years, effectively extending its service life. Applying a thin asphalt overlay such as the 6.3 mm Superpave surface treatment adds a preservation layer that smooths the riding surface, thereby improving functionality, and because it is placed in thin lifts it provides a longer service life than emulsified alternatives while sealing the pavement against water intrusion. When used on sound pavements the overlay can be combined with a seal coat or other surface‑treatment to further protect the deck, and an interlayer beneath the overlay supplies moisture‑barrier and stress‑absorbing properties that help prevent reflective cracking and thus extend durability and expected service life. The Hot In‑Place Recycled (HIR) asphalt preservation method rejuvenates existing pavement by heating to approximately 300 °F ± 25 °F, scarifying at least 1½ inches deep and mixing in a recycling agent, which removes surface distress and creates hot joints that provide improved joint compaction for longer pavement performance; the heat also penetrates the underlying layer to give better interlayer bonding, thereby enhancing durability and extending expected service life. After HIR treatment a four‑week waiting period is required before placing chip seals, micro surfacing, or slurry seal surface treatments, while no wait is needed for hot mix asphalt, warm mix asphalt, or ultra‑thin bonded overlays. Under the 60‑month asphalt concrete pavement warranty, when a year‑5 performance survey finds that 30 % of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action by microsurfacing (a surface treatment) or another Department‑approved method is required to bring the pavement back within the distress limits and satisfy the surface tolerance, thereby maintaining acceptable condition and functionality for the balance of the warranty period. In surface‑treatment applications for thin asphalt overlays described in section 5.12 Overlays, reflective cracking can occur when the treatment is placed over a cracked existing pavement, and age‑oxidation alligator cracking may propagate through the new wearing course. Additional distresses that may appear include rutting, bleeding, raveling, water‑induced stripping, potholes, localized failures and crack propagation accelerated by freeze‑thaw cycles. The development of these problems is influenced by the condition of the underlying pavement (presence of cracks or alligator cracking), climate factors such as freeze‑thaw and moisture infiltration, traffic loading, adequacy of drainage, and whether an interlayer or appropriate thickness – at least 1 ½ inches for thin overlays – is provided to ensure bonding and moisture protection. Typical failures observed after a surface‑treatment such as hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt covered in section 5.19 Hot In‑Place Recycled Asphalt > C include excessive rutting, transverse or miscellaneous cracking, raveling or weathering that leaves a rough, pitted surface with loose particles, and minor corrugation, shoving or slippage; bleeding and low friction numbers may also appear. These distresses are considered unacceptable when rutting exceeds < 1 ½ inches, crack depth exceeds < 1.0 inch or average crack width is greater than ≤ ½ inch, raveling/weathering surpasses the medium rating, or corrugation/shoving/slippage depth exceeds < 1 ½ inches. Their development is governed by the condition of the existing pavement and underlying layers – distresses must be limited to the surface with no load‑related base problems, the HIR layer must be at least 1 ½ inches thick (or more than the milling depth), and the presence of rubber or fiber in the top two inches disqualifies the treatment; removal of large mastic patches and durable markings is also required. Common distresses observed in High Friction Surface Treatment include poor aggregate retention, delamination of the binder‑aggregate layer, color discoloration that appears as untreated patches where bare asphalt or concrete is exposed, and insufficient friction indicated by skid numbers falling below the required at least 65 SN40S within 120 days. These failures are typically driven by inadequate surface preparation—such as insufficient cleaning or failure to pre‑treat cracks—and by allowing the mixed binder to become exposed, contaminated, or to cure before aggregate placement; wind speeds above 15 mph during application also jeopardize bonding. Compliance with the required curing periods (at least 30 days for new asphalt and 28 days for concrete) and strict control of binder mixing tolerances (± 2 % by volume) are essential, as is protecting freshly applied HFST from sudden or unexpected rain and avoiding application when the pavement surface is wet or the temperature is below the manufacturer’s recommendation. Improper moisture control of aggregate or storage that permits material to become damp further contributes to the recurring distresses. [1][2]

Monitoring and follow-up

After the binder or emulsified‑asphalt component of a surface‑treatment layer has cured, the first step is to perform an aggregate cleanup before the lane is reopened to traffic; for HFST this cleanup is followed by a second vacuum‑sweeper pass performed between 15 and 30 days after placement. Once that second sweep is finished the treated area is inspected for bare spots, delamination, poor aggregate retention or discoloration, and any deficient sections are prepared and retreated. For fog‑seal applications the inspection also includes special checks in shaded areas to confirm full cure, removal of protective coverings over manholes, valve covers and sensors, and sweeping to clear excess emulsified asphalt and aggregate before opening; interim pavement markings may be installed only after the fog seal cures and permanent markings must not be placed until 3 days after the last day of fog‑seal placement. The contractor’s designated QC Plan Administrator then carries out field quality‑control sampling and testing in accordance with the approved QC Plan, retaining complete records of all results; these records shall be made available for review and corrective‑action documentation shall be prepared if any deviation is observed. The Department conducts a calibrated skid test at least 31 days after HFST application in accordance with ASTM E274/E274M (AASHTO T 242), and the friction performance shall be verified by a skid‑number test that meets at least 65 SN40S within 120 days of installation; an unsatisfactory skid number triggers corrective actions, which may require the manufacturer’s technical person to be on site. Thereafter the treated area is inspected and monitored under the same warranty program that applies to asphalt‑concrete pavements. The Department performs annual automated surveys that identify, measure and report distresses in accordance with Publication 336, and conducts manual surveys for flushing and potholes when needed; rut depth may be verified with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width. If the fifth‑year performance survey determines that 30 % of the segment area has two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action such as microsurfacing is required, and traffic classification counts are taken to confirm ESAL estimates.
