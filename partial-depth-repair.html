Partial-Depth Repair

Part of: Concrete Patching is-subtopic-of
Partial-Depth Repair is a topic under Concrete Patching.
partial-depth-repairpartial-depth-repairspall-repairjoint-partial-depth-repairjoint-partial-depth-repairs
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Partial‑Depth Repair is a preservation technique for rigid concrete pavements that targets localized distress—such as surface spalling, interior slab spalls, delamination, or joint cracking—that is confined to the upper half of the slab thickness and does not involve severe loss of load transfer, thereby restoring structural integrity, rideability, and load‑transfer capability without disturbing the surrounding intact pavement[1][2]. The method works by staking a rectangular repair zone that extends roughly three inches beyond the damaged edges, sawing perimeter cuts, removing unsound concrete to a minimum depth of two inches but never deeper than fifty percent of the slab thickness, cleaning and priming the exposed surfaces, and placing a compatible Class AA cement‑concrete mix while installing a rigid polystyrene board or premolded expansion‑joint material to accommodate thermal movement[1][2]. Its defining characteristics include the depth limitation (≤½ slab thickness), minimum patch dimensions of about fifteen inches by ten inches by two inches, application only where joints retain sufficient load transfer and the subgrade is stable and well‑drained, and use on pavements that are not slated for an overlay[1][2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Partial‑Depth Repair is applied to localized distress in rigid concrete pavements that is limited to the upper half of the slab thickness [1][2]. The method addresses surface spalling, interior slab spalls, and delamination where concrete has separated from the underlying material [1][2]. It also targets early‑stage deterioration of transverse or longitudinal joints and minor cracks that have not resulted in severe joint failure such as loss of load transfer [1][2]. By removing only the unsound concrete within the defined repair zone and replacing it with new material, the treatment restores structural integrity, load‑transfer capability, and rideability without disturbing the surrounding intact slab [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–91); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 426–428)

Partial‑depth repair is applied only when spalling is confined to the upper half of a concrete slab and is not caused by severe joint loss, indicating that its purpose is to remediate existing localized damage rather than to preempt future distress. By removing the deteriorated portion and placing new concrete, the technique restores the slab’s structural integrity, load‑transfer capability, and rideability. Because it reinstates support where joints have faulted or lost transfer, the repair preserves pavement performance and can extend the remaining service life when an overlay is not planned. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–91)

Distress characterization and causes

The distress that calls for partial‑depth repair is found at both longitudinal and transverse joints, as well as in isolated cracks and interior slab surface spalling. In longitudinal joints the problem appears between traffic lanes or between a lane and a concrete shoulder, while transverse joints and interior areas exhibit similar spalls. All of these defects are limited to no more than half the slab thickness; the spalling is confined to the upper half of the slab and typically extends from about fifteen inches in length up to sections longer than six feet. When the depth exceeds the mid‑slab level or the failure becomes larger, a full‑depth patch is required, indicating that the severity threshold for partial‑depth repair lies at the halfway point through the slab. The source material does not quantify how rapidly these spalls advance, but their presence degrades rideability and can generate compression or point‑loading failures that jeopardize safety and normal operations. Applying a partial‑depth patch restores the lost structural capacity and extends the service life of the remaining pavement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91)

Partial‑Depth Repair is employed to address distress that arises from a combination of load‑related stresses that exceed the slab’s structural capacity and climate or material durability problems. Inadequate drainage permits water to infiltrate joints, leading to freeze‑thaw damage, chloride ingress, and weakening of an already unstable subgrade, which together reduce support for the concrete. When the subgrade cannot adequately bear the loads, spalling develops in the upper portion of the slab, typically confined to the top half of its depth. Poor joint performance—such as loss of load transfer, faulting, or excessive opening at transverse joints—creates point‑loading and compression stresses that cause surface‑layer failure rather than deep structural damage. Thermal expansion and contraction cycles further aggravate these mechanisms by imposing compressive forces on the patched area if sufficient space is not provided. Aging concrete and other material weaknesses also diminish structural capacity. If these underlying causes are not corrected during the repair, the repaired zone deteriorates rapidly. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–91)

Applicability and treatment selection

Partial‑depth repair is appropriate for existing Portland‑cement concrete pavements that will remain in service without an overlay, provided the distress—such as spalling, cracking, or joint deterioration—is limited to the upper half of the slab so that the required repair depth does not exceed one‑half of the slab thickness. The technique can be applied to transverse and longitudinal joints, cracks, and interior slab spalls meeting this depth criterion. A stable subgrade and a functional drainage system are essential to keep water, chlorides, or incompressible material from entering the pavement structure. Traffic considerations require that the repair be scheduled when sufficient time is available for the chosen material to cure; accelerated‑setting mixes may be used when rapid reopening to traffic is needed. Ambient temperature conditions must be suitable, with particular attention to periods of large day‑night temperature swings, because adequate gap provision between the patch and adjacent slab is necessary to accommodate expansion and contraction and avoid point‑loading failures. Site preparation should include joint spacing devices such as rigid polystyrene boards or premolded expansion‑joint material to maintain proper separation during slab contraction. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–91)

Partial-depth repair is limited to situations where the distress is confined to the upper half of the concrete slab and can be isolated within a rectangular patch that meets minimum size criteria of fifteen inches long, ten inches wide and two inches deep. When the required removal depth would exceed fifty percent of the slab thickness, or when spalling extends beyond the mid‑depth of the slab—whether in longitudinal joints, transverse joints, or corner locations—the technique becomes inappropriate and a full‑depth concrete patch, joint repair, or replacement must be employed. If the cumulative area that needs to be patched exceeds roughly ten percent of the total pavement surface, a cost‑effectiveness comparison with alternative treatments such as overlay, slab jacking, dowel‑bar retrofit, or full‑depth rehabilitation is required and partial‑depth repair is generally deemed unsuitable. Situations in which delamination passes through an existing joint also trigger the need for either two separate minimum‑size repair areas with a detailed joint‑reestablishment procedure or, absent such a plan, a full‑depth joint repair. Exposure of dowel bars during concrete removal that reveals misalignment, extensive corrosion, or other damage that would impair load transfer similarly mandates abandonment of the partial‑depth method in favor of a full‑depth patch or corner repair. When the concrete removal process becomes excessive to the point that the intended partial‑depth area effectively turns into a full‑depth section, the project must proceed with a full‑depth concrete pavement patch at no additional cost. Distresses driven primarily by structural loading, poor drainage, or other climate/material durability issues are not adequately addressed by shallow patches and therefore require more comprehensive rehabilitation such as concrete or asphalt overlay, slab stabilization, or dowel‑bar retrofit. Pavements slated for an overlay should not receive partial‑depth joint rehabilitation because the method is intended only for restoration of existing concrete without subsequent overlay; in these cases other preservation or rehabilitation strategies are preferred. Minor spalling at a transverse joint that originates from inadequate joint performance is better treated with slab stabilization and dowel‑bar retrofit rather than a partial‑depth patch. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–91); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 426–428)

Condition assessment and timing

Determining whether a partial‑depth repair is required begins with a comprehensive visual survey of the concrete pavement to locate transverse and longitudinal joints, cracks, and any interior slab spalling [1][2]. The inspector records each distress, measures its length, and classifies it as a spot repair when the affected length falls between roughly fifteen inches and six feet, or as an extended repair when it exceeds six feet [1][2]. For every identified spall the depth is measured from the surface to the base of the damaged area, and only those whose depth does not exceed one‑half of the slab thickness are eligible for partial‑depth treatment [1][2]. When delamination is suspected, sounding the concrete with a steel bar, drag chains, or a hammer in the presence of the representative confirms the extent of loss of bond [2]. Representative core samples are taken from the affected zones and from random locations near deteriorated joints to evaluate subbase or subgrade conditions and to verify that deterioration does not extend beyond the upper half of the slab [1]. Non‑destructive techniques such as deflection testing, faulting measurements, ground‑penetrating radar, friction testing, and roughness surveys are employed to locate problem zones and assess underlying support [1]. The measured dimensions also guide the layout of repair boundaries; rectangular limits are established extending about three inches beyond each identified edge, with a minimum size of fifteen inches by ten inches and a depth of two inches, and any separate areas spaced less than twenty‑four inches apart are merged into a single repair area [2]. All observations, measured lengths, depths, and test results are tabulated, and if the cumulative area slated for patching exceeds roughly ten percent of the pavement surface, a more detailed cost‑effectiveness analysis is triggered before proceeding with partial‑depth repair [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–91); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 426–428)

Partial‑depth repair is indicated when distress is localized and confined to surface spalling, concrete delamination, or joint cracking that does not exceed the upper half of the slab thickness [1][2]. The damage may appear in transverse or longitudinal joints, isolated cracks, or interior slab spalls, provided it can be bounded within a rectangular repair area extending about three inches beyond the damaged edges [1][2]. The minimum footprint for such a repair is roughly fifteen inches long by ten inches wide and at least two inches deep, while the maximum length for a spot (Type 1) repair is six feet; lengths greater than six feet are treated as extended‑length (Type 2) repairs [2][1]. When spalling or delamination penetrates beyond mid‑depth of the slab, or when joint problems involve loss of load transfer, a full‑depth repair becomes necessary rather than partial‑depth repair [1]. As long as the required repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness and the area can be sound‑tested and isolated, the guides recommend removing the unsound concrete to a depth no less than two inches and then placing the patch material, thereby restoring rideability and extending service life [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–91); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 426–428)

Materials and repair details

Partial‑depth repair is limited to locations where spalling is confined to the upper half of the concrete slab and there are no severe joint losses that would require load‑transfer devices, making it suitable for transverse or longitudinal joints, cracks, and interior slab surface spalls as long as the depth does not exceed one‑half of the existing slab thickness[1][2]. The repair area is defined as a rectangular patch that extends roughly three inches beyond any delaminated edge and must be at least fifteen inches long, ten inches wide and two inches deep; when two such patches would lie within twenty‑four inches of each other they are combined into a single repair[2]. Unsound concrete is removed to a depth not less than two inches and never deeper than one‑half of the slab thickness, after which the perimeter is sawed with a diamond‑tipped blade equipped with a guard, alignment guides, water cooling and cut‑depth control, producing cuts no wider than one‑half inch when opened to traffic[1][2]. The excavation is performed with a chipping hammer weighing no more than thirty‑five pounds or a milling machine fitted with a V, flat or rounded head, and all exposed surfaces are cleaned within thirty minutes and primed with a thin coat of grout or epoxy bonding compound before new material is placed[2]. The patch material follows the standard pavement concrete prescribed in Publication 408; for routine work Class AA Cement Concrete Modified is specified, while an accelerated rapid‑set mix may be selected when early traffic opening is required, provided high‑early‑strength mixes are avoided because of their tendency to shrink and crack. Either a volumetrically mixed concrete conforming to Section 704.3 or a hand‑batched mix prepared in a power‑driven revolving, tilting drum or paddle mixer may be used[1][2]. Adjacent joints or cracks are sealed with rubberized joint‑sealing material forming a sealant pocket approximately three‑quarters of an inch deep and wide; where the patch meets an existing transverse joint a half‑inch expansion‑joint material is installed, and in longitudinal joints a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene form (or similar temporary form) extends to the surface to keep the new concrete out of the joint[1][2]. Fresh concrete is placed when ambient temperature is at least forty degrees Fahrenheit, worked into the saw cuts beyond the corners, consolidated with a spud vibrator or equivalent tamping method, and struck off to match existing wheel ruts before being textured to duplicate the existing cross‑section[2]. Placement must be completed by trained crews within forty‑eight hours of excavation, and any disturbed subbase is re‑compacted or replaced as needed; excavated areas are not permitted to remain un‑patched for more than forty‑eight hours[2]. After placement a white membrane‑forming curing compound is applied, temperature is monitored, and the repair is protected from freezing for at least twenty‑four hours; traffic may be allowed on the repaired area no sooner than seventy‑two hours after cutting, unless an accelerated mix permits earlier opening[1][2]. The guides classify repairs as Type 1 spot repairs ranging from about fifteen inches up to six feet in length and Type 2 extended repairs longer than six feet, both observing the same dimensional and material requirements described above[1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–91); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 416–429)

The effectiveness of a partial‑depth repair depends first on the condition of the existing concrete slab, which must be evaluated for its compressive strength, air content and any visible durability problems such as weak aggregate or frost damage that could impair bond performance [1]. Only distress confined to the upper half of the slab thickness is suitable for this technique; when spalling extends deeper or joint deterioration has eliminated load transfer, a full‑depth patch becomes necessary [1][2]. Adequate load transfer across the repaired area is achieved by dowelling all patch joints and anchoring the dowels with compatible binders such as epoxy, polyester or vinylester, while exposed dowel bars that show little corrosion receive a bond‑breaking coating before the new concrete is placed [1][2]. The pavement surface must retain sufficient friction, ride quality and resistance to polishing so that the interface between the existing surface and the repair concrete remains compatible during service [1]. The repair mix itself must be compatible with the original material; most projects specify a Class AA modified cement concrete for normal schedules or a Class AA accelerated mix when traffic opening time is limited, both of which are more compatible than rapid‑set products and are selected based on ambient temperature, available curing time, cost, and the size and depth of the patch [1][2]. Consistent material properties are obtained by producing the repair concrete with volumetric mixing equipment or an appropriately sized drum‑paddle mixer, ensuring that the specified cement type is used throughout the batch [2]. Prior to placement, all unsound concrete must be removed to a minimum depth of two inches and no deeper than half the slab thickness, and the resulting edges are sawed to create a roughened surface that promotes adhesion [2]. The cleaned area must be addressed within thirty minutes; an oil‑free compressed‑air stream at a pressure of at least one hundred pounds per square inch is required to remove dust before a fresh grout or epoxy bonding compound is applied while still tacky, after which the new concrete is placed immediately [2]. Placement is permitted only when ambient air and surface temperatures are at least forty degrees Fahrenheit and rising, and the cured patch must be protected from freezing for at least twenty‑four hours; during curing temperature fluctuations should not exceed forty degrees per hour and a white membrane forming compound is used to control moisture loss [2]. To accommodate thermal expansion and contraction, a rigid polystyrene board or premolded expansion joint material equal in width to the existing joint is installed between the patch and adjacent slabs, providing space that prevents compression or point‑loading failures during temperature swings [1][2]. Where the repair abuts an existing transverse joint, half‑inch expansion joint material is placed, while a quarter‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene is used at longitudinal joints; temporary forms of these materials extend to the pavement surface to keep fresh concrete out of the original joints and are later re‑established and sealed with rubberized joint sealant as specified [2][1]. The underlying subgrade must be stable and well drained, with all pathways for water, chlorides or incompressible material eliminated; any subbase irregularities greater than one inch are corrected and compacted before the patch is placed, and the excavated area may not remain un‑patched for more than forty‑eight hours to avoid loss of continuity [1][2]. When these conditions—adequate slab integrity, compatible repair mix, proper load‑transfer detailing, appropriate joint treatment, and a sound, well‑drained foundation—are satisfied, partial‑depth repairs can restore structural performance without premature failure [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–91); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 416–429)

Treatment procedures

The contractor first prepares the repair area by developing a project‑specific quality‑control plan that is submitted for review at least two weeks before work begins and approved in accordance with the applicable specifications. In the presence of the Representative, the slab is sounded to locate delamination and a rectangular repair zone is staked extending approximately three inches beyond each edge of the defect, with minimum dimensions of 15 × 10 × 2 inches; zones spaced less than 24 inches apart are combined. The perimeter of the patch is then marked for saw cuts and cut using a diamond‑tipped blade equipped with water cooling, a blade guard, alignment guides, and depth controls to remove unsound concrete to at least two inches but not deeper than one half of the slab thickness. The concrete within the outlined area is broken out in pieces with a chipping hammer no heavier than 35 lb held at a 30–60° angle or by milling, avoiding drop‑hammers or hydro‑hammers to protect adjacent pavement. After removal, corners are squared, any exposed joint reinforcement is cut only where the pavement is jointed and reinforced, dowel bars showing little corrosion receive a bond‑breaking coating, and the area is re‑sounded to verify complete extraction; disturbed subbase irregularities greater than one inch are compacted. The cleaned joint is then fitted with a rigid polystyrene board for longitudinal joints or a premolded expansion‑joint material for transverse joints so that the new patch does not contact adjacent slabs, providing space for slab movement. Within thirty minutes of removal, the exposed surfaces are cleaned with oil‑free compressed air at a minimum pressure of 100 psi, and immediately thereafter grout or epoxy bonding compound is brushed onto all exposed surfaces to keep them tacky. The repair concrete, specified as Class AA cement concrete modified for regular opening times or an accelerated mix when rapid traffic return is required, is prepared by volumetric or hand‑batched equipment and may be placed only when ambient and surface temperatures are at least 40 °F. The fresh concrete is poured into the cleaned zone before the grout dries, consolidated with a spud vibrator (or other acceptable method), worked into the saw cuts beyond the patch edges, and shaped to match the existing cross‑section. Expansion joint material of one‑half inch thickness for transverse joints or one‑quarter inch preformed cellular polystyrene for longitudinal joints is installed extending at least half an inch below the repair, and any required temporary polystyrene forms protect existing joints. The surface is then finished and textured to duplicate the surrounding pavement, all original longitudinal and transverse joints are re‑established and sealed, and any saw cuts that extend beyond the patch perimeter are also sealed. A white membrane‑forming curing compound is applied with an atomizing spray tank whose nozzle is adjustable and the solution agitated before each pass; the repair is protected from freezing for at least twenty‑four hours while curing under the temperature controls specified. Traffic may use the adjacent existing pavement within seventy‑two hours after saw cutting, but the excavated area must be patched no later than forty‑eight hours after removal, including weekends or holidays. After placement, a final resounding of the repaired section is performed as required, and equipment, trained personnel, and hauling capacity are verified before placement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 90–91); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 416–429)

Implementation begins with an approved quality‑control plan that defines each step before field work commences [2]. The first field operation is to saw the perimeter of the damaged area using a diamond‑tipped blade equipped with a guard, alignment guides, water cooling and cut‑depth controls, making all longitudinal cuts prior to any transverse cuts [1][2]. Saw cuts must be less than one‑half inch wide if traffic is to remain on the surrounding pavement, which may stay open for no more than seventy‑two hours after cutting [2]. After the outline is established, the damaged concrete is removed; removal may be performed with a chipping hammer not exceeding thirty‑five pounds and operated at an angle of thirty to sixty degrees, or with a milling machine fitted with appropriate heads, while drop hammers and hydro‑hammers are prohibited to protect adjacent pavement, and excavators are used to extract the spalled portion of the slab [2][1]. Any disturbed subbase is immediately re‑compacted using small vibratory compactors before the next step [2]. The exposed surface must be cleaned within thirty minutes, employing oil‑free compressed air at a minimum of one hundred pounds per square inch, to ensure proper bonding of the fresh patch material [2]. Concrete mixing may be carried out in a volumetric plant or by hand‑batching in a drum or paddle mixer, and placement must occur while any grout or epoxy bonding compound remains tacky [1][2]. Prior to placing the new concrete, dowel‑bar bonding tools are used to fully bond existing dowels, and when the patch length exceeds sixty‑five feet longitudinal tie bars or bolts are installed after removal but before placement to secure the patch to the surrounding pavement [1]. To accommodate slab movement caused by diurnal temperature swings, a rigid polystyrene board for longitudinal joints or a premolded expansion‑joint insert matching the existing joint width is positioned between the fresh patch and adjacent slabs, preventing compression and point‑loading failures [1][2]. The fresh concrete is placed, consolidated with a spud vibrator or equivalent device, worked into the saw cuts beyond the repair edges, then struck off and shaped to match existing wheel ruts [1][2]. Ambient air and surface temperatures must be at least forty degrees Fahrenheit for placement; work must cease below that level and the patch must be protected from freezing for a minimum of twenty‑four hours, with heating or insulation provided if curing temperatures fall under eighty degrees Fahrenheit [2][1]. Expansion‑contraction stresses become especially critical when nights are cool and daytime temperatures high, requiring the provision of appropriate gap material regardless of concrete mix type [1]. Traffic in the adjacent lane is typically limited or rerouted during placement and early curing to avoid damaging partially set material, and the repaired section may not be reopened until the prescribed return‑to‑service time has elapsed [1][2]. On high‑ADT routes any delay beyond the specified opening time can trigger liquidated damages, making accelerated concrete mixes—capable of attaining design strength in four to seven hours—advantageous when an early reopening is mandated [1]. Excavated repair areas cannot remain unpatched for more than forty‑eight hours or over weekends and holidays, ensuring that the work schedule aligns with traffic closure constraints [2]. The choice of repair material—standard Class AA modified concrete versus accelerated mixes—is governed by the available traffic closure time, ambient temperature, size and depth of the patch, and cost considerations [1][2].

Quality and workmanship

Inspectors must first verify that the concrete used for the patch conforms to the project specification, selecting a Class AA cement concrete modified for routine repairs or an accelerated (rapid‑set) mix when early traffic reopening is required. [1][2] The quality‑control plan, which has to be prepared and approved at least two weeks before work begins, must list inspection points covering removal of unsound concrete, mixing, curing, joint sawing, sealing and final sampling. [2] Prior to any cutting, the existing slab is sounded with a steel bar, drag chains or a hammer to establish the extent of delamination, and rectangular repair boundaries are laid out extending beyond the damaged zone. [2] All deteriorated concrete must be removed and the joint cleaned; the cleaning operation must be completed within thirty minutes before placement using oil‑free compressed air at a minimum source pressure of one hundred pounds per square inch. [1][2] A rigid polystyrene board or premolded expansion‑joint filler, equal in width to the original joint, is installed between the new patch and the adjacent slab to re‑establish the joint and prevent compression or point‑loading failures, while an adequate gap is left to accommodate thermal contraction. [1] Saw cuts are inspected for proper width, appropriate blade type and effective water cooling before they are filled with the repair material. [2] The mixing equipment—either a volumetric mixer as specified in Section 704.3 or an approved hand‑batched drum mixer—must be capable of producing batch sizes that meet the prescribed standards, and the mix temperature and consistency are checked against the specifications. [2] Placement of the fresh repair is permitted only when ambient air and surface temperatures are at least forty degrees Fahrenheit and rising, and the material must be protected from freezing for a minimum of twenty‑four hours; placement shall be halted if temperatures fall below this threshold. [2] The concrete is placed uniformly, any grout or epoxy bonding compound is applied without running into low spots, and the fresh mix is fully consolidated by vibration or tamping, worked into saw cuts beyond the corners and struck off to match existing wheel ruts while temporary forms and suitable joint sealants are used. [1][2] Curing compounds are applied and the temperature of the repair area is monitored at least hourly to ensure that no single‑hour change exceeds forty degrees Fahrenheit, in accordance with Publication 408 Section 525.3. [1][2] After finishing, the surface texture and cross‑sectional profile of the patch are examined for match with the existing pavement, disturbed subbase material deeper than one inch is removed and replaced, and any shallower disturbance is re‑compacted with small vibratory compactors. [2] All original joints and any saw cuts extending beyond the patch are resealed, and a final sample of the repair material is tested against established criteria before traffic is permitted. [2] The completed repair is resounded in the presence of the Representative to confirm the absence of delamination, and documentation confirming that every inspection point has been satisfied is required prior to opening the pavement to service. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 90–91); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 416–429)

A satisfactory partial‑depth repair is recognized when the distress being treated does not extend beyond the mid‑slab depth, meaning that spalling is confined to the upper half of the concrete slab, and the repaired joint no longer displays top‑half spalling while restoring adequate load transfer without resorting to a full‑depth patch [1]. The repair zone must be laid out as a rectangle that projects approximately three inches beyond every edge of the delaminated concrete and must meet minimum plan dimensions of fifteen inches in length, ten inches in width, and a depth of at least two inches [2]. All unsound concrete within this envelope is removed to a depth no less than two inches and not greater than one‑half of the slab thickness, with perimeter saw cuts made to a minimum depth of two inches; the width of these cuts may not exceed one‑half inch before traffic is permitted [2]. Full‑depth saw cuts at the repair limits are allowed to extend laterally into adjacent pavement up to the full slab depth plus two inches provided that dowel bars in the neighboring lane remain intact [2]. The exposed surfaces are then roughened by hammer chipping using a tool weighing no more than thirty‑five pounds and applied at an angle between thirty and sixty degrees, after which they are cleaned with oil‑free compressed air at a pressure of one hundred psi or greater within thirty minutes prior to placement [2]. While still wet or tacky, the surfaces receive a thin coat of grout or epoxy bonding compound as a primer before fresh concrete is placed [2]. Placement occurs only when ambient and surface temperatures are forty degrees Fahrenheit or higher and rising, and the fresh concrete must be protected from freezing for at least twenty‑four hours after placement [2]. During placement the mix is consolidated into the saw cuts beyond the corners and shaped to follow existing wheel ruts, and an expansion‑joint material—either a half‑inch strip or a quarter‑inch polystyrene block—extends at least half an inch below the repair [2]. Curing is monitored at least hourly; any temperature swing greater than forty degrees Fahrenheit within one hour renders the work defective, and a white membrane‑forming compound is applied throughout curing [2]. Subbase disturbance shall not exceed one inch; if deeper voids are encountered they must be removed and backfilled with concrete, and surface irregularities greater than one inch are corrected and compacted to match the surrounding pavement cross‑section and original joint geometry [2]. The finished patch surface and joint sealing must align with the adjacent pavement profile, and a representative inspection confirms that no delaminated concrete remains; any remaining delamination requires replacement at no cost to the owner [2]. In addition, the sealant reservoir constructed as part of the repair must satisfy the shape‑factor specifications prescribed in Publication 408 Section 525, completing the set of acceptance criteria for a satisfactory partial‑depth repair [1].

Performance and service life

Partial‑depth repair is appropriate only when spalling is confined to the upper half of a concrete slab and is not the result of severe joint loss of load transfer. In those circumstances the damaged concrete is removed and replaced with a durable patch, which restores surface condition, rideability, and functional performance while leaving the underlying joint system intact. When applied to longitudinal joints that exhibit limited spalling, the treatment also helps maintain load‑transfer capability and curtails further deterioration, thereby enhancing durability. The effectiveness of the repair depends on using suitable cementitious mix designs and incorporating a rigid separation device—such as a polystyrene board or a premolded expansion‑joint element sized to match the existing joint width—to prevent compression and point‑loading failures. As a result, the repaired portion can retain its functionality for the remaining service life of the slab; however, because it does not address severe joint problems, the overall extension of service life is modest compared with full‑depth patching or complete slab replacement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–91)

Partial‑depth repair is effective only when the distress it addresses remains within the method’s intended limits; spalling must be confined to the upper half of the slab thickness and the surrounding joints must retain sufficient load transfer, otherwise the patch is quickly undermined and a full‑depth replacement or thin‑bonded concrete inlay becomes necessary [1][2]. The guides also agree that removal depth should not exceed half the slab thickness, with a minimum of two inches, because deeper cuts convert the repair into a de facto full‑depth patch and increase the risk of failure [2][1]. High early‑strength concrete is discouraged because its tendency to shrink excessively can generate cracking within the new section, compromising durability [1]. Even when the distress is appropriately limited, two principal mechanical failure modes are observed: compression of the fresh concrete against adjacent slabs and point‑loading that occurs when underlying cracks propagate laterally toward the patch rather than vertically, both of which can fracture the repair [1][2]. These mechanisms are amplified by temperature‑driven expansion and contraction cycles, especially when patches are placed while neighboring slabs are in a contracted state during cool nights followed by hot days, creating differential movement that stresses the interface [1][2]. Providing an intentional separation between the repair and the adjoining slabs—by installing a rigid polystyrene board for longitudinal joints or a premolded expansion‑joint material of equal width to the existing joint for transverse joints—eliminates the compression and point‑loading failures by allowing independent movement [1]. Delamination of the patch from the existing slab is reported as the most frequent distress, arising when unsound concrete is not fully removed or when removal exceeds half the slab thickness, thereby reducing bond strength and often necessitating replacement of the repair at no cost to the agency [2][1]. Improper treatment of exposed dowel bars also leads to loss of interface integrity; corroded or misaligned bars that are left without the required bond‑breaking material can cause the patch to separate and may require conversion to a full‑depth repair [2]. Surface preparation is critical because insufficient cleaning or contamination of the repair area immediately before concrete placement diminishes adhesion, accelerating premature separation [2]. Edge spalling or break‑back at saw‑cut boundaries occurs when cuts are too wide, poorly executed, or not sealed promptly; unsealed cuts permit water infiltration that accelerates joint deterioration and can trigger further cracking around the patch [2][1]. Excessive impact from removal tools such as drop hammers can damage adjacent slabs and disturb the subbase; any disturbance greater than one inch creates uneven support, predisposing the repair to settlement and cracking [2]. Curing conditions exert a strong influence: temperature swings exceeding 40 °F within an hour during curing render the repair defective, while placement temperatures must remain above 40 °F and stable throughout the cure period; violations of these thermal limits generate internal stresses that can cause delamination or cracking [2][1]. Consequently, adherence to a quality‑control plan that maintains removal depth between two inches and half the slab thickness, applies bond‑breaking material to acceptable dowels, uses appropriate saw blades with controlled cut width, seals all cuts promptly, avoids drop or hydro‑hammers, recompacts any subbase irregularities exceeding one inch, and monitors ambient and surface temperatures within the specified limits is essential for preventing the recurring distresses associated with partial‑depth repair [2][1].

Monitoring and follow-up

After the partial‑depth repair is placed, the curing process must be closely watched: the temperature of the patch is recorded at least every hour and any change greater than forty degrees Fahrenheit within a one‑hour interval is prohibited, otherwise the work is deemed defective. Once curing is complete, the repaired section is required to be resounded in the presence of the Department’s Representative, and any delaminated area identified during that test must be replaced at no cost to the Department. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–429)

After a partial-depth repair, any indication that distress has progressed beyond the portion of the slab originally treated signals that further action is required. When spalling or corner spalls extend past the midpoint of the slab thickness—i.e., beyond the top half—the limited depth of the original repair is no longer adequate and more extensive measures such as full‑depth patching, slab stabilization, dowel‑bar retrofit, or thin‑bonded concrete inlay should be considered. Likewise, if the repaired joint begins to exhibit new distress such as faulting, loss of load transfer, reduced load‑transfer efficiency, or fresh spalls appearing in the top half of the slab, resealing alone is insufficient and additional rehabilitation becomes necessary. The emergence of compression‑related failures or point‑loading cracks, where a crack beneath the sawed joint propagates toward the patch rather than vertically, also indicates that the partial-depth repair is failing. Deterioration of underlying support or drainage conditions, repeated expansion‑contraction cycles that cause the patched area to contact adjacent slabs, and prolonged neglect of joint distress further increase the likelihood that full‑depth interventions are required. If delamination becomes evident in a partial‑depth patch after placement, this defect must be repaired or replaced. In addition, a change in curing temperature exceeding forty degrees Fahrenheit within any one‑hour period is considered defective work and signals the need for additional maintenance, repair, or replacement.
