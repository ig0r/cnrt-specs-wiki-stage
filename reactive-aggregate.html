Reactive Aggregate

Part of: Mix Design is-subtopic-of
Reactive Aggregate is a topic under Mix Design.
reactive-aggregate
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

Reactive aggregate denotes coarse or fine aggregate whose mineral constituents—such as chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide—can chemically interact with the alkalis supplied by Portland cement (or admixtures) and generate expansive alkali‑silica gel[2]. Because this reaction can produce measurable expansion (typically 0.10 %–0.30 % at 14 days) that leads to cracking and loss of durability, the material must be hard, durable, uncoated particles reasonably free from deleterious substances and its reactivity is verified by ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303 testing before use[2]. When an aggregate is classified as reactive, it may only be placed in concrete mixes that incorporate a compatible cement‑or cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend capable of reducing the expansion to less than 0.1 % (and meeting alkali limits), otherwise it must be rejected[2].

Material purpose and applicability

Reactive Aggregate denotes aggregate material that contains minerals capable of reacting with alkalis in cement, potentially causing deleterious alkali‑silica reaction (ASR) in concrete pavements. In the pavement system it serves as the coarse or fine filler in cement concrete mixes, providing structural bulk while its reactivity can lead to expansion and cracking, so it must be used only with compatible cements or cement‑SCM blends and appropriate mitigation measures must be applied. For Section 704—Cement Concrete > 704.1 General > 1.e.1 Determining Aggregate Reactivity, aggregates are tested according to ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303 to determine the reactivity level of an aggregate; aggregates deemed potentially reactive must be used only with cements or cement‑SCM combinations as specified in Section 704.1(g)3, and if one or both of the aggregates (coarse or fine) used in a mix is reactive, mitigation is required. For Section 545—Bonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfac ed Pavements > 545.2 Material, reactive aggregate refers to coarse aggregate whose mineral constituents can undergo alkali‑aggregate reaction producing measurable expansion; for the pavement mix it must exhibit a 14‑day expansion between 0.10% and 0.30% and be used only together with an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend that reduces that expansion to less than 0.1%, aggregates showing more than 0.30% expansion are rejected, and this requirement applies to all paving concrete on the project. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 465–466), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 672–673)

Composition and characteristics

Reactive aggregate is defined by its mineral composition and physical character that render it capable of chemically interacting with cement alkalis. For both coarse and fine aggregates, the material must consist of hard, tough, durable, uncoated inert particles that are reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation, and other deleterious substances; the presence of specific reactive minerals such as chert (including its chalcedony component), gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica, and hydrated iron oxide causes the aggregate to deviate from this non‑reactive requirement. Additional siliceous constituents that are recognized as potentially reactive include opal; chalcedony occurring in chert within carbonate rock or sand‑and‑gravel particles; high‑temperature silica polymorphs tridymite and cristobalite found in andesite or rhyolite; acid glasses containing more than 65 % silica; intermediate glasses containing between 55 % and 65 % silica; strained quartz present in granite or granite gneiss; and clay minerals occurring in graywackes, argillites, phyllites, and siltstones. When any of these mineral phases are present in quantities large enough to cause inconsistent performance in hardened Portland cement concrete, the aggregate is classified as deleterious and therefore not acceptable as a non‑reactive material. Under the Class AA paving specification, reactive or potentially reactive aggregate is identified by a 14‑day alkali‑aggregate expansion measured between 0.10 % and 0.30 %; such aggregates may be used only with an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend that reduces the expansion to less than 0.1 %, whereas any aggregate showing a 14‑day expansion greater than 0.30 % must be rejected for all paving concrete on the project. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 465–466), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 649–672)

Variations in the mineral composition of both coarse and fine aggregates directly affect durability because any increase in reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide beyond what is considered negligible renders the material deleterious and can lead to inconsistent performance in hardened Portland cement concrete. For coarse aggregate, the source must be listed in Bulletin 14 or approved by the LTS, and petrographic analysis is required to verify that the material remains reasonably free of those reactive constituents (SECTION 703—AGGREGATE > 703.2 COARSE AGGREGATE). Processing such as thorough washing of gravel intended for cement concrete helps remove fines and reduces the likelihood that deleterious minerals remain on particle surfaces, while proportioning limits – no more than 4 % glassy particles in aggregate used for cement concrete – are imposed to keep potentially reactive material below the durability threshold (same section). In fine aggregate, the source also matters; limestone‑derived manufactured sand may not be used in concrete wearing surfaces and steel‑slag fine aggregate must undergo a controlled moist‑curing regime with an acceptable average total volumetric expansion of less than 0.50 % under PTM No. 130, indicating that processing and proportioning that introduce excessive reactive material would exceed performance limits (SECTION 703—AGGREGATE > 703.1 FINE AGGREGATE). Variations in aggregate source or composition are reflected in the reactivity class assigned after petrographic examination and expansion testing according to ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303; when a mix contains aggregates with differing reactivity levels, the highest level governs the mitigation measures, and any presence of a reactive coarse or fine aggregate triggers required mitigation (SECTION 704—CEMENT CONCRETE > 704.1 GENERAL). New Type A sources that lack listed expansions must be tested to determine their class and are treated as potentially reactive (R1) until ASTM C1293 results confirm otherwise, which may also prompt new mix designs. In bonded‑concrete overlays, variations in the source and composition of coarse aggregate are controlled by requiring durable stone or gravel free from slate texture or cleavage planes while prohibiting blast‑furnace slag aggregate; washing during production removes deleterious material that could exacerbate reactivity (SECTION 545—BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF CONCRETE‑SURFACED PAVEMENTS > 545.2 MATERIAL). When aggregates exhibit a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 %, they may be used only with an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend capable of reducing the measured expansion to less than 0.1 %; any aggregate showing expansion greater than 0.30 % must be rejected. The proportioning of supplementary cementitious material, such as slag cement, is limited to no more than 30 % of the total cementitious content, a limit that helps mitigate the potential expansion of reactive aggregates. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 465–466), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 649–673)

Key material properties

The tendency of a reactive aggregate to expand when exposed to cement alkalis is the central property governing its performance, and this expansion is quantified by ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303 testing, with results used to assign a reactivity class that determines required mitigation measures. Physical hardness and durability are also primary; aggregates should consist of hard, durable, uncoated inert particles that are reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation, and deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide. For fine aggregate the average total volumetric expansion must be less than 0.50 % according to PTM No. 130, and petrographic analysis per PTM No. 518 is used to verify that durability requirements can be achieved. Chemical makeup matters; siliceous constituents known to react with alkalis include opal, chalcedony in chert, tridymite, cristobalite, acid glasses containing more than 65 % silica and intermediate glasses containing between 55 % and 65 % silica. The aggregate must be free of frozen lumps and its temperature at the time it is charged into the mixer must not be less than 40 °F nor greater than 100 °F, because expansion caused by alkali‑aggregate reaction is temperature dependent. For Class AA concrete the stone or gravel must be durable and free from slate texture or cleavage planes; aggregates whose 14‑day expansion lies between 0.10 % and 0.30 % may be used only with an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend that reduces the resulting expansion to less than 0.1 %, while any aggregate showing a 14‑day expansion greater than 0.30 % must be rejected. When blended hydraulic cements are used, the allowable mortar expansion is limited to 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at eight weeks. The concrete prism test requires an expansion of less than 0.04 % after two years for a preventive measure to be accepted; this judgment also depends on the mix’s alkali content not exceeding the limits set in Section 704.1(g)3, and the dosage of supplementary cementitious material must stay within the prescribed values because higher levels may cause excessive retardation of setting and early‑age strength development. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 465–466), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 649–673)

The presence of reactive minerals such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica, or hydrated iron oxide is used to classify a fine aggregate as potentially deleterious, and the LTS confirms this classification by petrographic analysis performed in accordance with PTM No. 518. For coarse aggregates the presence and potential reactivity are characterized primarily through petrographic analysis as required by PTM No. 518, which allows the LTS to identify deleterious minerals such as reactive chert. When expansive behavior is a concern the coarse‑aggregate stockpile may be subjected to an expansion test according to PTM No. 130 and accepted only if the total expansion is less than 0.50%. For fine aggregates that may contain expansive constituents a volumetric expansion test conducted per PTM No. 130 after a prescribed curing period is required, and the average total volumetric expansion must be less than 0.50% for the material to be accepted. Reactivity of an aggregate is assessed through three complementary approaches: a field performance history per AASHTO R 80 Section 6.1, petrographic examination to flag potential reactivity, and quantitative expansion testing using either ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303; the test results place the material into a defined reactivity class (e.g., R0, R1) that drives the required mitigation level, with the highest class among any coarse or fine aggregates governing the mix design. For new Type A sources lacking an LTS expansion value AASHTO T 303 is applied first and if the result shows a non‑reactive (R0) condition the aggregate is temporarily recorded with an expansion of 0.11% (R1) and ASR mitigation is required until ASTM C1293 testing by LTS is completed. The reactivity of a potentially reactive aggregate is quantified by the laboratory procedure outlined in Section 704.1(g).1.d.1.c, which assigns the aggregate to a reactivity class shown in Table C; that class is then used with Table D to evaluate ASR risk for a given structure. Aggregates are also classified according to ASTM C295 based on their mineralogical composition, and field verification may be performed by taking cores and conducting petrographic analysis per ASTM C856 to detect alkali‑silica gel and associated microcracking. In Section 704.1(g) aggregates are classified by their measured 14‑day expansion; only those showing an expansion between 0.10% and 0.30% may be used if an approved cement‑supplementary material blend can reduce the expansion to less than 0.1%, while any aggregate with a 14‑day expansion greater than 0.30% must be rejected. The concrete prism test specified in ASTM C1293 is used to quantify the reactivity of aggregates by measuring the dimensional change of prisms that contain the aggregate under controlled conditions; the test tracks expansion over a two‑year period and, for reactive aggregates, an expansion of less than 0.04% after that time indicates that any preventive measure (such as supplementary cementitious materials or lithium nitrate admixture) is effective, and this expansion criterion therefore serves as the primary classification metric for assessing whether an aggregate’s alkali‑silica reaction potential has been sufficiently mitigated. [1]

Behavior and mechanisms

When alkalis from cement, admixtures, de‑icing salts or aggregates come into contact with moisture, reactive aggregate can undergo an alkali‑aggregate reaction; the products of this reaction may cause deleterious expansion within the concrete under certain conditions. The reaction is described as occurring at ordinary temperatures. [1]

[1] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–672)

The behavior of a reactive aggregate is governed primarily by the chemical interaction between alkalis—oxides of sodium and potassium supplied mainly by Portland cement but also possible from admixtures, de‑icing salts or the aggregates themselves—and specific siliceous minerals within the aggregate; this alkali‑aggregate reaction can generate expansive products that, when moisture is present, may produce deleterious expansion in the concrete. The mineralogical thresholds that determine susceptibility are acid glasses containing more than 65 % silica, intermediate glasses containing between 55 % and 65 % silica, as well as opal, chalcedony, tridymite, cristobalite, strained quartz and certain clay minerals. In addition, the inherent silica reactivity of an aggregate—a physical characteristic classified in the aggregate reactivity class—controls how readily the chemical interaction proceeds. The chemical interaction generates expansive alkali‑silica gel, and the resulting mechanical expansion appears as microcracking in the concrete matrix, creating internal stresses that manifest as cracking and other distress in pavement structures. These mechanisms are evaluated through petrographic analysis of cores according to ASTM C856 to detect gel formation and associated microcracking, which informs the risk assessment and preventive measures prescribed for ASR mitigation. For Class AA paving under SECTION 545—BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF CONCRETE‑SURFACED PAVEMENTS, the aggregate’s behavior is controlled primarily by its measured 14‑day expansion, which reflects the chemical reaction between reactive minerals in the aggregate and cement alkalis; only aggregates whose 14‑day expansion falls between 0.10 % and 0.30 % may be used provided an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend can reduce that expansion to less than 0.1 %, while any aggregate showing more than 0.30 % expansion is rejected. The choice of blended hydraulic cement further limits allowable mortar expansion to 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at 8 weeks. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 465–466), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–678)

Durability and deterioration

According to Section 703.2, reactive aggregates contain minerals such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide, and when these substances are present in amounts large enough to cause inconsistent performance in asphalt concrete or hardened Portland cement concrete they are classified as deleterious, making their presence a durability concern that must be evaluated through petrographic analysis and other required tests. Section 703.1 similarly requires fine aggregate used in pavement structures to be free of those minerals because, when present in sufficient quantities, they may cause inconsistent performance in asphalt concrete or hardened Portland cement concrete, representing a durability concern that can lead to deterioration of the pavement material. Section 704.1(e) states that reactive aggregates are a durability concern because they can participate in deleterious alkali‑silica reaction (ASR), a chemical reaction between alkalis from cement or other sources and certain aggregate constituents, whose products may cause expansive gel and deleterious expansion within the concrete, leading to cracking and loss of durability in pavement structures. The same section identifies siliceous substances that are known to react with alkalis, including opal; chalcedony as a constituent of chert in carbonate rock or sand and gravel particles; tridymite and cristobalite; acid glasses containing more than 65 % silica; intermediate glasses containing between 55 % and 65 % silica; strained quartz as a constituent of granite or granite gneiss; and clay minerals as a constituent of graywackes, argillites, phyllites, and siltstones. Section 704.1(e) further requires that when one or both of the aggregates (coarse or fine) used in a mix is reactive, mitigation measures must be applied as specified in Section 704.1(g), after the potential for ASR has been evaluated through field performance history, petrographic examination and expansion testing such as ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303. For pervious concrete pavements, Section 520.2 mandates that if reactive aggregates are used in the mixture, remediation for alkali‑silica reaction shall be performed as specified in Section 704.1(g), identifying ASR as the primary durability concern for reactive aggregate in those systems. In Class AA paving concrete, Section 545.2 permits aggregates that exhibit a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % only when used with an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material combination that reduces the expansion to less than 0.1 %; any aggregate showing more than 0.30 % expansion must be rejected, and when blended hydraulic cements (Type IS or IP) are employed the allowable mortar expansion is limited to 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at eight weeks. Section 704.1(g) limits Portland cement or blended hydraulic cement to a maximum alkali content of 1.25 % Na2Oe, and when silica fume is the sole preventive measure the dosage shall not be less than 7 %. According to Section 704.1(2.a), a preventive measure for reactive aggregates is deemed effective only when concrete prism expansion remains below 0.04 % after two years; substitution of aggregates using the preventive measure is prohibited, and dosage levels of supplementary cementitious materials beyond those prescribed may cause excessive retardation of setting and early‑age strength development, especially in cool or cold weather construction. Section 704.1(2.b) warns that the use of high levels of supplementary cementitious materials may increase the risk of deicer‑salt scaling if the concrete is not properly proportioned, finished, and cured, so structures with potentially reactive aggregates must be designed accordingly. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 418–466), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 649–678)

Reactive aggregate becomes more susceptible to deterioration when either coarse or fine aggregate contains deleterious minerals such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide, because those substances are identified as capable of causing inconsistent performance in hardened Portland cement concrete. Using hard, tough, durable and uncoated inert particles that are reasonably free from those reactive minerals therefore reduces the susceptibility for both coarse and fine aggregates. For coarse aggregate, natural aggregates with an absorption less than 2 % are considered freeze‑thaw resistant, while aggregates whose absorption exceeds 2 % must meet either a sodium sulfate soundness loss of less than 16 % or a freeze‑thaw loss after 25 cycles that does not exceed 7.0 % in order to be regarded as resistant and thus lower the risk of deterioration. For fine aggregate, keeping a steel‑slag stockpile uniformly moist for at least six months and confirming that the average total volumetric expansion is less than 0.50 % further limits expansive behavior that could lead to distress. The susceptibility of a potentially reactive aggregate to alkali‑silica reaction–related deterioration rises with its classified reactivity level, but it can be mitigated by limiting the cement’s alkali content to no more than 1.25 % Na₂Oe and by providing at least 7 % silica fume when that is the sole preventive measure; conversely, using high levels of supplementary cementitious materials without proper proportioning, finishing or curing may increase risk because deicer‑salt scaling can exacerbate damage. Aggregates that show a 14‑day expansion greater than 0.30 % are considered highly susceptible and must be rejected, whereas aggregates with an expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % are classified as reactive but may be used only when paired with an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend that reduces the measured expansion to less than 0.1 %. Employing blended hydraulic cements such as Type IS or IP further limits mortar expansion to a maximum of 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at eight weeks, thereby lowering the potential for deterioration. Using supplementary cementitious materials such as metakaolin or a lithium nitrate admixture also reduces susceptibility, provided that concrete prisms demonstrate an expansion of less than 0.04 % after two years; increasing the SCM dosage beyond the limits prescribed in Section 704.1(g)2.b is not recommended because it may cause excessive retardation of setting and early‑age strength development, a problem that can become critical under cool or cold weather construction. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 465–466), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 649–678)

Material interactions and compatibility

Within Section 703—AGGREGATE > 703.2 COARSE AGGREGATE, the coarse‑aggregate specification requires the material to be hard, durable and inert and reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation and other deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide; therefore the presence of these reactive minerals constitutes a compatibility risk for reactive aggregate. The quality‑requirements portion also calls for determination of clay lumps, indicating that clays are regarded as undesirable in the aggregate mix, and it makes no mention of additional sulfates, organics, contaminants, dissimilar materials, corrosion, shrinkage restraint or chemical attack.

In Section 703—AGGREGATE > 703.1 FINE AGGREGATE, fine aggregate must likewise be reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation and other deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide; when any of these materials are present in amounts sufficient to cause inconsistent performance they are considered deleterious, indicating a compatibility risk for reactive aggregates especially with sulfate‑bearing gypsum, organic vegetation, clays and other reactive minerals. No further compatibility concerns are identified.

Section 704—CEMENT CONCRETE > 704.1 GENERAL (e) Measurement of Material identifies the only compatibility risk as an alkali‑aggregate reaction that can occur when aggregates contain certain siliceous constituents, including clay minerals found in graywackes, argillites, phyllites and siltstones; this section does not discuss sulfates, organic matter, other contaminants, dissimilar materials, corrosion, shrinkage restraint or chemical attack as compatibility issues for reactive aggregate.

Finally, Section 545—BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF CONCRETE‑SURFACED PAVEMENTS > 545.2 MATERIAL limits the use of aggregates that show a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % to situations where an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend reduces the measured expansion to less than 0.1 %; any aggregate whose 14‑day expansion exceeds 0.30 % must be rejected, and when a blended hydraulic cement (Type IS or IP) is employed the allowable mortar expansion is limited to 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at eight weeks. These requirements constitute the only compatibility controls for reactive aggregate in this overlay; the source does not discuss sulfates, organics, clays, contaminants, dissimilar materials, corrosion, shrinkage restraint or chemical attack. [1]

Influence of production and placement

In Section 704, the mix design for concrete that contains potentially reactive aggregate must incorporate a silica‑fume dosage calculated from the cement’s alkali content; when silica fume is the sole ASR preventive measure the mix design must state the maximum assumed alkali limit of the cement and the minimum silica‑fume content may not be less than 7 %. Because high levels of supplementary cementitious materials can increase the risk of deicer‑salt scaling, the concrete must be properly proportioned, finished and cured to achieve the intended performance, and frequent adjustments of the mix during production are impractical, so a consistent, well‑specified mixture is required. In Section 520, mixing pervious concrete shall follow ASTM C1688 and produce a plastic unit weight between 115 lb and 130 lb with a water‑cement ratio of 0.36 ± 0.04; additional mix water may be added only if it yields a wet metallic sheen without causing paste to flow from the aggregate and keeps the plastic unit weight within 5 lb/ft³ of the target design unit weight. When reactive aggregates are used, the mixture must be remedied for Alkali‑Silica Reaction as specified in Section 704.1(g). For hot‑weather placement, a hydration‑stabilizing admixture shall be added in 5°F increments to provide time for placing and finishing before initial set, and cold‑weather curing shall protect the pervious concrete from freezing throughout the seven‑day curing period in accordance with ACI 306.1. In Section 545, mixing practices are governed by field verification of the water‑cement ratio using batch tickets and AASHTO T 318, with a target ratio of 0.40 lb/lb that must not fall below 0.38 lb/lb for all Class AA concrete. Placement shall achieve an air content of 7.0 % ± 1.5 % at the time and point of placement for Class AA Slip‑Form Paving and Accelerated Patching. When potentially reactive aggregate is used, only aggregates that exhibit a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % may be placed provided an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material combination reduces the expansion to less than 0.1 %; any aggregate exceeding 0.30 % expansion shall be rejected. Curing must employ the curing materials specified in Sections 711.1 or 711.2(a) Type 2. [1]

In Section 703, when fine aggregate contains reactive minerals such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide, the durability of concrete pavements can be compromised; moreover, handling of steel‑slag fine aggregate that exceeds the stockpile limit of 30,000 tons, fails to keep the material uniformly moist for at least six months, or permits volumetric expansion that exceeds the less than 0.50 % threshold introduces variability that may accelerate distress in reactive aggregates. In Section 704 2.b, if silica fume is employed as the sole preventive measure, the mix must contain at least 7 % silica fume; any lower proportion weakens protection, and when high levels of supplementary cementitious materials are used, the concrete must be properly proportioned, finished and cured because improper execution can increase the risk of deicer‑salt scaling and associated distress. In Section 545, aggregates whose measured 14‑day expansion exceeds 0.30 % must be rejected; those with expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % may be placed only when an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend reduces the expansion to less than 0.1 %. Additionally, any blended hydraulic cement used with such aggregate must not exceed a mortar expansion of 0.02 % at 14 days or 0.06 % at eight weeks, and the field water‑cement ratio must be verified so that it does not surpass the maximum of 0.42 (target 0.40, never below 0.38). Failure to meet these thresholds during mixing or placement constitutes a construction defect that can allow deleterious alkali–aggregate reactions to develop over time. In Section 704 2.a, using supplementary cementitious materials at levels beyond those prescribed may cause excessive retardation of setting and early‑age strength, jeopardizing durability; lithium nitrate admixtures must be added to the mix water with the required water‑correction, otherwise the preventive chemistry may be compromised; substitution of aggregates while relying on a preventive measure is prohibited, and the effectiveness of any mitigation is judged by whether concrete prism expansion remains less than 0.04 % after two years. Selection, specification, and improvement

For coarse aggregate, Section 703—Aggregate > 703.2 requires that any coarse aggregate used be hard, tough, durable and essentially inert, and it must be reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation and especially deleterious minerals such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide; compliance is verified by petrographic analysis in accordance with PTM No. 518 together with any additional durability tests the LTS deems necessary. For fine aggregate, Section 703—Aggregate > 703.1 mandates that it be hard, durable and uncoated inert particles reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation and any deleterious minerals such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide, must satisfy the physical property limits in Table A, and must come from a source listed in Bulletin 14 or otherwise approved by the LTS; the LTS may require petrographic analysis under PTM No. 518 (and any other tests deemed necessary) to confirm acceptable durability, and when steel‑slag sand is used the cured stockpile must be shown to have an average total volumetric expansion of less than 0.50%. Selection of a reactive aggregate follows Section 704—Cement Concrete > 704.1 General > 1.e.1, which first looks to documented field performance history per AASHTO R 80 Section 6.1, then uses petrographic examination to flag potential reactivity, and finally requires mandatory expansion testing using ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303 to assign a reactivity class; that class determines the required level of alkali‑silica reaction mitigation, with the highest class governing preventive measures when multiple aggregates are used, and aggregates identified as potentially reactive may only be specified with cements or cement‑SCM combinations listed in Section 704.1(g)3, with any mix containing a reactive coarse or fine aggregate required to include the prescribed mitigation. The reactivity class recorded in Table C together with the structure type defined in Table F and the resulting ASR risk level from Table D determines the required prevention level (Table E) as stated in Section 704—Cement Concrete > 704.1 General > 2.b; once that level is known, the cement or cement‑SCM system must meet the specifications for that level, such as using Portland cement that does not exceed a maximum alkali content of 1.25% Na₂Oe when ASR mitigation is required, although a documented service record demonstrating long‑term nonreactivity may allow exemption from these cement requirements. Section 704—Cement Concrete > 704.1 General > 2.a requires verification by the concrete prism test (ASTM C1293), and a mixture is acceptable only when measured expansion of the prisms remains less than 0.04% after two years, provided the alkali limits in Section 704.1(g)3 are not exceeded and substitution of the reactive aggregate itself is prohibited. For Class AA paving, Section 545—Bonded Concrete Overlay > 545.2 permits use of a reactive or potentially reactive coarse aggregate only if its 14‑day expansion falls between 0.10% and 0.30% and it can be paired with an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material blend that reduces the measured expansion to less than 0.1%; any aggregate showing a 14‑day expansion greater than 0.30% must be rejected, and this requirement applies to all paving concrete on the project. Coarse aggregate shall consist of hard, tough, durable and uncoated inert particles reasonably free from deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide; its quality must be verified by petrographic analysis according to PTM No. 518 together with soundness, abrasion and other standard tests, and the LTS will accept the stockpile for use only if the total expansion determined according to PTM No. 130 is less than 0.50%. When a non‑reactive source is not available, alternative durable materials such as stone, properly washed gravel, blast‑furnace slag, steel slag that passes the expansion test (total expansion <0.50% per PTM No. 130), granulated slag with limited impurity content or approved lightweight aggregate may be used provided they meet the applicable absorption and density limits, and a certified aggregate technician on site shall document all acceptance testing as required by Bulletin 14. Fine aggregate shall also be reasonably free from reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide; the LTS must approve any source after petrographic analysis per PTM No. 518 and other durability tests, and when steel‑slag sand is used traceability for material produced after 1 January 2007 shall be established, the stockpile kept uniformly moist for not less than six months, and the average total volumetric expansion measured by PTM No. 130 be less than 0.50% before approval. Alternative non‑reactive options include natural sand or manufactured sand produced from rock or air‑cooled blast furnace slag, while limestone‑manufactured sand is prohibited in concrete wearing surfaces and steel‑slag fine aggregate may be used only in asphalt courses with LTS consent. Any potentially reactive aggregate shall be used only with cement or cement‑SCM blends identified in Section 704.1(g)3 and mitigation measures prescribed in that same section shall be applied whenever a coarse or fine aggregate is classified as reactive; reactivity must be established through expansion testing per ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303, the highest reactivity class among the aggregates governing the level of prevention, new Type A sources lacking LTS data being initially tested by AASHTO T 303 and, if classified non‑reactive (R0), listed with an expansion of 0.11 % (R1) pending ASTM C1293 confirmation. Bulletin 14 aggregates of Type A quality shall be retested on a five‑year cycle using ASTM C1293, and any change in the ASTM C1293 reactivity class obliges the producer to submit revised concrete mix designs within ninety calendar days or to notify the DME/DMM if the class decreases. To mitigate alkali‑silica reaction when reactive aggregates are used the mix design shall first select a prevention level based on aggregate reactivity, structure class and risk and then apply the prescribed cementitious or admixture measures; if silica fume is the sole preventive agent its dosage should not be less than 7 % and may follow the calculated minimum (for example 7.3 % for a concrete containing 500 lb/yd³ with 0.81 % Na₂Oₑ), while alternative prevention can use the minimum SCM levels shown in Table G for cements with 0.70 %‑1.25 % Na₂Oₑ or the approach in Table H for ≤0.70 % Na₂Oₑ, and Level Z permits those SCM levels together with the maximum concrete alkali content listed in Table I; Portland cement should conform to the optional chemical requirement in AASHTO M 85 for a maximum alkali content of 1.25 % Na₂Oₑ if used for ASR prevention, and blended hydraulic cement may be Type IS or IP per AASHTO M 240. Chemical admixtures shall follow Section 711.3, with an exemption possible only after a documented service record and petrographic analysis confirm non‑reactivity. The concrete prism test (ASTM C1293) may be used to qualify mixtures that incorporate supplementary cementitious materials or blended cements as a preventive measure for reactive aggregates provided the prisms expand less than 0.04 % after two years; remediation may also rely on metakaolin or a lithium nitrate admixture, the latter being added to the mix water with its water content accounted for, and any cement‑type or SCM‑type substitutions must stay within the alkali limits of Section 704.1(g)3 while aggregate substitution is prohibited; exceeding the prescribed SCM dosage is discouraged because it may cause excessive early‑age setting retardation. When reactive aggregates are used in pervious concrete, the mix shall be remedied for alkali‑silica reaction as required by Section 704.1(g) and a hydration‑stabilizing admixture (ASTM C494 Type D) shall be incorporated with extra dosage added in 5 °F increments during hot weather to preserve workability before initial set; the mix is designed for a water‑cement ratio of 0.36 ± 0.04 and a plastic unit weight between 115 and 130 lb/ft³, any additional water being allowed only if the resulting plastic unit weight stays within 5 lb/ft³ of the target design unit weight; a quality‑control plan that includes the full mix design with component weights, target void content and 28‑day unit weight shall be submitted at least three weeks before production and approved prior to mixing, and cold‑weather protection is required for the seven‑day curing period. For Class AA concrete overlays reactive aggregates showing a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % may be used only when paired with an approved cement–supplementary cementitious material blend that mitigates the expansion to a value less than 0.1 %, while any aggregate expanding more than 0.30 % is rejected; Portland cement may be replaced with fly ash or slag cement provided at least 400 lb/yd³ of Portland cement remains and total cementitious content stays within Table A limits, the maximum slag cement content not exceeding 30 % of the total cementitious material by weight, and blended hydraulic cements Type IS or IP are permitted but their mortar expansion may not exceed 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at eight weeks. Quality control shall require field verification of the water‑cement ratio using batch tickets and AASHTO T 318 testing, and all stone or gravel must be durable, free from slate texture or cleavage planes and washed thoroughly during production. Performance evaluation and implications

Evaluation of reactive aggregate incorporates field performance records, laboratory petrographic and chemical analyses, and long‑term expansion testing. Field performance history according to AASHTO R 80 Section 6.1 may be used to establish the potential for deleterious alkali‑silica reaction, and visual inspection of each structure for cracking or joint expansion together with documentation of structure type, age, mix proportions, cement alkali content, supplementary cementitious materials and any distress symptoms provides additional field evidence. A service record that qualifies for an exemption must include data from a minimum of 10 structures each over 10 years of age, preferably over 15 years, and petrographic results must confirm mineralogical similarity to the aggregate under consideration. Laboratory evaluation begins with petrographic examination, which may classify an aggregate as potentially reactive but requires expansion testing; fine aggregates are examined by petrographic analysis according to PTM No. 518, and when traceability cannot be shown chemical testing is performed according to DEP General Permit WMGR144. For steel‑slag fines the Representative conducts an expansion test per PTM No. 130 and accepts the stockpile only if the average total volumetric expansion is less than 0.50 %. Aggregates are also classified according to ASTM C295, and cores taken from existing structures are examined petrographically according to ASTM C856 to detect alkali‑silica gel and associated microcracking. Expansion testing is performed using the Concrete Prism Test specified in ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303; aggregates will be tested according to ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T 303 and listed in Bulletin 14, with established Bulletin 14 sources retested on a five‑year cycle using ASTM C1293. For new Type A sources lacking long‑term data LTS initially performs AASHTO T 303 and if the result indicates a non‑reactive (R0) aggregate it is temporarily listed with an expansion of 0.11 % (R1) pending ASTM C1293 testing. Long‑term performance evidence includes monitoring prism expansion over time; a preventive measure is deemed effective when the measured expansion remains less than 0.04 % after two years of curing, which serves as the performance criterion for reactive aggregate in concrete pavements. For Class AA paving concrete laboratory measurement of the aggregate’s 14‑day expansion is required; aggregates showing a 14‑day expansion between 0.10 % and 0.30 % may be used only when an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material combination reduces the resulting concrete expansion to less than 0.1 %, while any aggregate with a 14‑day expansion greater than 0.30 % is rejected. For blended hydraulic cements employed to mitigate reactivity, mortar expansion limits are not more than 0.02 % at 14 days and 0.06 % at eight weeks, providing a performance check on the effectiveness of the cement blend. Field verification of water‑cement ratio is performed using batch tickets and AASHTO T 318 testing to ensure that the designed mitigation remains in place during construction. [1]

Consequently, Section 703.2(a) requires coarse aggregate to be reasonably free of reactive chert and other reactive minerals so that the pavement achieves acceptable durability, and compliance is verified by petrographic analysis performed according to PTM No. 518. The fine‑aggregate specification in Section 703.1 likewise mandates that aggregates be free of reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide because the presence of these substances in amounts sufficient to cause inconsistent performance in asphalt concrete or plastic or hardened Portland cement concrete is considered deleterious and can compromise the durability of concrete pavements. Under Section 704.1(g)3, the presence of potentially reactive aggregates triggers alkali‑silica reaction unless the mix uses only cement or cement‑SCM combinations authorized in Section 704.1(g)3 and applies the mitigation measures required for the aggregate’s reactivity class, with reactivity established through field performance history, petrographic examination, and especially expansion testing performed per ASTM C1293 or AASHTO T303; when a new Type A source shows an expansion of 0.11 % (R1) it is treated as reactive and must be mitigated until definitive ASTM C1293 results are obtained, and all such aggregates are retested on a five‑year cycle to ensure continued compliance. When aggregates contain minerals that can react with alkalis from cement or other sources, an alkali‑aggregate reaction may occur; the reaction products can cause deleterious expansion within the concrete, which compromises pavement durability by inducing cracking and distress, so mix designs that use potentially reactive aggregate must consider this chemical interaction and designers need to identify such aggregates and account for the risk of expansion in their material selections and specifications. Because reactive aggregates can trigger alkali‑silica reaction, pavement designers must first determine the aggregate’s reactivity class and then assess the ASR risk for the intended structure; this classification drives the selection of a prevention level that dictates concrete mix adjustments, and when silica fume is used as the sole preventive method the specification requires a minimum silica fume content of at least 7 %, while Portland cement employed for ASR mitigation must not exceed a maximum alkali content of 1.25 % Na₂Oe; construction practices therefore incorporate appropriate supplementary cementitious materials, blended hydraulic cements, or extra cement as prescribed, and maintenance programs must include visual inspection for cracking and petrographic analysis to verify the presence of ASR gel. In the bonded concrete overlay specification of Section 545.2, reactive or potentially reactive aggregates are acceptable only when their measured 14‑day expansion lies between 0.10 % and 0.30 % and the mix includes an approved cement‑supplementary cementitious material combination that can reduce that expansion to less than 0.1 %; any aggregate showing a 14‑day expansion greater than 0.30 % must be rejected, and this limit requires the design of Class AA paving concrete to incorporate the prescribed cement or blended hydraulic cement, verify water‑cement ratios on site, use thoroughly washed durable stone or gravel, and thereby links aggregate reactivity directly to mix design, placement quality, and long‑term performance. The concrete prism test specified in Section 704.1(a) states that if the expansion of concrete prisms is less than 0.04 % after two years, the preventive measure will be deemed effective with the reactive aggregate(s), and any substitution of aggregates is prohibited; however, dosage levels of supplementary cementitious materials beyond those prescribed in Section 704.1(g)2.b are not recommended because they may cause excessive retardation of setting and early‑age strength development, which can be critical in cool and cold weather construction.
