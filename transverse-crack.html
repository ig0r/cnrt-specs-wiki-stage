Transverse Crack

Part of: Cracking is-subtopic-of
Transverse Crack is a topic under Cracking.
transverse-crackstransverse-cracking
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Transverse cracks are a distress that can develop in concrete pavements when the underlying subgrade becomes saturated, reducing its ability to support traffic loads, and they appear as fractures on the pavement surface that run perpendicular to the roadway centerline [1]. The moisture‑related loss of support, especially during spring thaw when water is trapped beneath a frozen layer, allows traffic loading to produce these cracks across the slab [1]. The resulting surface undulations cause a noticeable loss of ride quality and can compromise safety, while trapped water may freeze and create tenting that further reduces durability; when such a crack forms between adjacent grout‑injection holes the specification requires replacement of the entire slab at no additional cost to the Department [1][2].

Description and identification

Transverse cracks are a distress that can develop in concrete pavements when the underlying subgrade becomes saturated, reducing its ability to support traffic loads. [1] The loss of support under loading may cause transverse cracks to form, appearing as fractures on the pavement surface. [1] Within slab‑stabilization work, a transverse crack is identified as a crack that forms between adjacent grout injection holes in the pavement. [2] When such a crack appears, the specification requires replacement of the entire slab at no additional cost to the Department. [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

In the drainage context of concrete pavements, a transverse crack is recognized as a fracture that runs perpendicular to the roadway centerline and becomes evident when a saturated subgrade can no longer support the slab under traffic loading, particularly during the spring thaw when water is trapped beneath a frozen layer [1]. This moisture‑related loss of support distinguishes the distress from shrinkage or thermal cracks in asphalt, which are described as arising from temperature‑induced brittleness rather than inadequate drainage [1]. For hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) candidate pavements, a transverse crack is defined as a surface‑only distress that must satisfy the transverse and miscellaneous cracking severity limits, namely a depth less than 1.0 inch and an average width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch [1]. Because only surface cracks are permitted, any load‑related distress or base problem excludes the pavement from HIR treatment, thereby separating transverse cracking from other forms such as rutting or shoving [1]. In Pennsylvania’s slab‑stabilization specifications, a transverse crack is identified by visual inspection when it develops between adjacent grout injection holes, which differentiates it from radial cracks that spread outward from the holes [2]. The specification requires replacement of the entire slab at no additional cost to the Department when such transverse cracks are observed, as stipulated in Section 516 [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Where it occurs

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, transverse cracking is identified as a distress that occurs in Portland Cement Concrete (PCC) pavement slabs, where working transverse cracks require load‑transfer treatment such as dowel‑bar retrofit, and the manual does not mention this distress for asphalt concrete surfaces, composite pavements, overlays, or shoulders. The same manual adds that transverse cracking is also observed in concrete pavements and in asphalt overlays that are placed over concrete when subsurface moisture saturates the subgrade, reducing support and allowing cracks to develop under traffic loads, a condition that becomes especially critical during spring thaw; both concrete pavements and their asphalt overlays react similarly to moisture. For surface‑distressed asphalt pavements that are candidates for Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) treatment, the manual requires that transverse‑crack severity be limited to a depth less than 1.0 inch, with an average crack width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and that the distress reside only in the surface layer; when the HIR overlay is placed on a composite pavement, the underlying concrete must be stable with non‑movement at the joints. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that transverse cracking occurs on rigid (concrete) pavement structures, appearing as joints or cracks across slab surfaces and at expansion joints. The dowel‑retrofit provisions require cutting three dowel slots per wheel path and centering them over each crack or transverse joint within the wheel path, and they include a step to saw and seal transverse contraction joints for the full lane width; this retrofit is applied only to existing concrete pavement. The partial‑depth repair sections permit spot repairs (Type 1) of transverse joints, cracks and interior slab surface spalling whose lengths are between 15 inches and 6 feet, and extended repairs (Type 2) for sections longer than 6 feet, with both actions limited to concrete pavement. The unbonded‑concrete overlay provisions require identification and marking of any transverse expansion joints in the existing pavement and their subsequent sawing and sealing as part of overlay panel construction, again referring to concrete‑surfaced pavements. Heavy‑duty membranes are prescribed to be placed over transverse (and longitudinal) joints found on existing concrete pavements. For flexible (asphalt) pavements, the joint and crack sealing specifications describe cleaning and sealing of longitudinal and transverse joints as well as random cracks in existing pavement surfaces. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 313–314), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 416–462)

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), working transverse cracks in Portland‑Cement‑Concrete surfaces are described as occurring within the concrete slab interior rather than at joints, edges or layer interfaces. The same manual notes that a saturated subgrade beneath the slab can provide poor support, allowing traffic loads to generate transverse cracking across the pavement, yet the distress remains situated in the slab itself. For hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt candidate pavements, the Pavement Policy Manual treats transverse cracking as a surface distress that must be confined to the pavement surface with a depth less than 1 inch and therefore does not involve the base or subgrade. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specifications (Publication 408/2020) state in Section 679.3 that transverse cracks develop between adjacent grout injection holes as interior slab cracks, not at joints, edges or the subgrade. In the dowel‑retrofit construction described in Section 527.3, each wheel path is addressed by cutting three dowel slots centered over a transverse crack or joint and aligned parallel to the roadway centerline, thereby treating cracking located within the slab interior along the wheel path. Section 525.1 of the same specifications indicates that transverse cracking may appear either in transverse or longitudinal joints or as cracks within the interior surface of concrete slabs, with partial‑depth repairs applied when the crack is at a joint or inside the slab body and the repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness. The unbonded concrete overlay specification (Section 548.3) treats any transverse cracking that occurs outside the sawed joints as defective, placing such cracks in the slab interior rather than on the joint faces. Section 469.1 specifies that asphalt joint and crack sealing includes transverse cracking among the surfaces cleaned and sealed, targeting both longitudinal and transverse joints as well as random surface‑level cracks, which indicates these cracks are found at joint locations and as surface fissures rather than in deeper layers or the subgrade. The dowel‑retrofit description (Section 527.1) further notes that transverse cracking appears either as cracks within the slab or at transverse joints, with epoxy‑coated smooth dowel bars installed across those cracks or joints. The heavy‑duty membrane specification (Section 467.1) calls for placing membranes directly over transverse joints of existing concrete pavements, confirming that transverse cracking occurs at the pavement’s transverse joint locations and may also be present as random slab cracks. Finally, the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course construction (Section 489.3) requires sealing longitudinal and transverse joints and any cracks ¼‑inch or wider with rubberized sealant, indicating that transverse cracks exist in the slab surface—including at joint locations—and are not associated with the base or subgrade. Causes and mechanisms

According to the drainage subsection, transverse cracks in concrete pavements are linked to poor subsurface drainage; when the subgrade becomes saturated it loses support, and the resulting weak base allows traffic loads to produce cracks that run perpendicular to the pavement centerline. Thus both moisture‑related drainage problems and the applied traffic loading are identified as causes, with no table in this excerpt providing a separate classification. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59)

Transverse cracks develop when saturated subgrades provide poor support for concrete pavements, allowing traffic loads to cause cracking. The condition is particularly critical during the spring thaw when moisture cannot percolate downward into the subsoil because of the frozen layer beneath it, keeping the subgrade wet. Moreover, if a joint deteriorates so that particles impede gravity drainage, trapped water can freeze and produce localized frost heave that further promotes transverse cracking. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59)

Severity levels and rating

Within the candidate‑project guidelines for standalone hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) asphalt, transverse cracking severity is limited to a depth less than 1 inch and the average crack width must stay within the Medium rating of ≤ ½ inch; these limits are applied only when selecting projects in which distresses are confined to the pavement surface and no load‑related base problems exist [1]. In the concrete‑pavement partial‑depth repair specifications, transverse cracks are classified by length for treatment selection: spot repairs (Type 1) are prescribed for cracks whose lengths fall between 15 inches and 6 feet, while extended‑length repairs (Type 2) are required when a crack exceeds 6 feet; in both cases the repair depth may not exceed one half of the slab thickness [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 416)

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), Section 5.19 C for hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt candidate pavements, the transverse‑crack condition is evaluated by applying the criteria from Publication 336 as referenced in Chapter 5; under that system a crack is considered acceptable when its depth is less than 1.0 inch and the average crack width does not exceed the Medium rating of ≤ ½ inch [1]. In the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2020 Specifications, Section 679.3 (Construction) of the slab‑stabilization procedure defines a transverse crack as severe enough to trigger stabilization when a loaded slab‑corner deflection measured in accordance with ASTM D4695 is 0.02 inch or greater and the joint efficiency is at least 65 %, and if such cracks occur between adjacent grout injection holes the specification in Section 516 requires full‑slab replacement [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Measurement, survey and extent

In SECTION 545—BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF CONCRETE‑SURFACED PAVEMENTS, transverse cracks are quantified by measuring their joint width directly immediately before the concrete overlay or inlay is placed; the measurement is taken by measuring the joint width at that time and the saw cut width shall be at least the width of the underlying joint or crack in the existing pavement. In SECTION 679—SLAB STABILIZATION, transverse cracks are evaluated by measuring slab‑corner deflection under load using a deflection test performed in accordance with ASTM D 4695; the test is applied to each transverse joint or crack, the loaded slab corner deflection (in inches) is recorded and compared to the unloaded deflection to compute joint efficiency as (unloaded deflection ÷ loaded deflection) × 100, and cracks are considered for stabilization when the loaded slab‑corner deflection reaches 0.02 inch or more and the joint efficiency is at least 65 %. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 455–457), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Under Section 600 Incidental Construction, Subsection 679—Slab Stabilization §679.3, transverse cracks are surveyed by nondestructive deflection testing in accordance with ASTM D 4695 and supplemented by visual verification when temperature limits preclude testing; the Representative may also direct testing of each joint and crack. The testing is carried out on every transverse joint or crack encountered during the work, effectively making the survey frequency “each crack as it appears” and the sample unit the individual joint/crack segment. Extent of a transverse crack is quantified by measuring the loaded slab corner deflection (minimum 0.02 inch triggers stabilization) and by calculating joint efficiency, expressed as a percentage with the 65 % threshold distinguishing full‑stabilization from patching. These criteria are applied within the field QC plan required by Section 679.3. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Effects on performance

Because saturated subgrades provide poor support, transverse cracks develop under traffic loads, indicating a loss of structural capacity; the resulting surface undulations cause a noticeable loss of ride quality and can compromise safety by creating uneven driving conditions; trapped water that freezes beneath deteriorated joints may cause tenting, further reducing pavement durability and shortening its service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59)

Inadequate subsurface drainage leaves the subgrade saturated, causing concrete pavements to lose support and develop transverse cracks under traffic loads. During spring thaw the frozen layer prevents moisture from percolating downward into the subsoil, worsening the condition. As the cracked joint deteriorates below the pavement surface into fine particles that are small enough to impede gravity drainage, water becomes trapped beneath the joint. In winter this trapped water may freeze and cause tenting, an upward movement of the joints due to localized frost heave. In asphalt pavements a fluid base similarly reduces support, leading to surface roughness and additional cracking adjacent to the original transverse crack. When transverse cracks develop between adjacent grout injection holes they are treated as a failure condition, and the specification requires replacement of the entire slab at no additional cost to the Department. This requirement indicates that if such cracks are not addressed the distress can progress from localized cracking to full‑depth slab replacement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Repairs and treatments

Dowel Bar Retrofit is identified as a repair that provides load transfer on working transverse cracks in concrete slabs and therefore is applied only when such active transverse cracking is present and improved load distribution is required (source [1] section 2.1). The same dowel‑retrofit technique is prescribed for transverse cracks or joints that lack dowel reinforcement, requiring three dowel slots per wheel path centered over the crack, with dowels placed parallel to the centreline and limited to a vertical tilt or horizontal skew of no more than 0.25 inch and a longitudinal or horizontal translation of no more than 1 inch (source [2] section 527). Continuous pavement base drains are required along the outside edge of the pavement, lateral underdrains at transitions from cut to fill, and subgrade drains where water damage is evident; these drainage installations apply whenever subsurface water is identified as a problem by the local maintenance representative or when functional edge drains are absent (source [1] section 2.3). Hot‑In‑Place Recycled Asphalt may be applied when transverse cracking is limited to surface distress with a depth less than 1.0 inch and an average crack width not exceeding ½ inch, provided the pavement otherwise meets the surface‑only criteria of Chapter 5 Section 5.19; for deeper cracks the source indicates that milling before the HIR process can address those deeper transverse cracks while preserving vertical clearance, and the application of crack sealant on longitudinal and transverse joints is also acceptable as a repair for transverse cracking (source [1] section 5.19). Bonded concrete overlay treats existing transverse cracks by first removing any exposed steel plates when an inlay is placed over a joint that contains them, then installing joint backing or another approved filling material to within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface in every existing transverse joint—including those associated with full‑depth repairs—prior to overlay placement; after the concrete attains sufficient strength for cutting without raveling, the overlay must be saw‑cut directly over each transverse crack to a depth equal to the full thickness of the overlay plus ½ inch and to a width at least as wide as the underlying crack, and when the planned panel length would exceed 15 ft intermediate transverse joints must be cut between existing cracks to limit panel length (source [2] section 545). Unbonded concrete overlay addresses existing transverse expansion joints by first identifying and marking them, precutting while the concrete is still plastic, then, once the concrete has hardened enough to avoid excessive raveling, making two full‑depth saw cuts on each side of the precut joint; the saw cut depth must be no less than D/3 within 12 inches of the pavement edges and no less than D/4 elsewhere, and sawing is omitted if a crack occurs at or within 1.5 ft of a joint location (source [2] section 548). The asphalt‑joint and crack sealing procedure cleans the faces of transverse joints and random surface cracks and applies sealant without reconstructing the pavement (source [2] section 469). Heavy‑duty membranes are placed directly over transverse cracks at locations indicated or directed by the Representative (source [2] section 467). Slab stabilization is required for transverse cracks or joints that exhibit a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more; if the joint efficiency is at least 65 percent and the Representative approves, stabilization is performed, whereas if the same deflection is present but the joint efficiency is less than 65 percent and approved, the crack must be patched and stabilized, and when transverse cracks develop between adjacent grout‑injection holes the entire slab must be replaced at no additional cost as directed in Section 516 (source [2] section 679). Concrete‑pavement partial‑depth repairs provide two alternatives for transverse cracks: Type 1 is a spot repair used when the crack length lies between 15 inches and 6 feet and the repair depth does not exceed one half of the slab thickness, while Type 2 is an extended‑length repair employed when the crack length exceeds 6 feet, also limited to a maximum repair depth of one half of the slab thickness (source [2] section 525). The ultra‑thin bonded wearing course requires sealing any transverse cracks or joints that are ¼ inch or wider with the rubberized sealant identified in Section 469; this sealing must be completed at least 24 hours before paving and the resulting over‑band thickness and width may not exceed the tolerances set out in Section 469 (source [2] section 489).

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 313–338), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 416–462), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) asphalt overlays are limited to pavements where the transverse cracking severity is a depth of less than 1.0 inch and the average crack width does not exceed the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch; when both conditions are satisfied the HIR treatment is considered applicable, otherwise it is ruled out [1]. Under the slab‑stabilization provision (Section 679), any transverse crack that develops between adjacent grout injection holes triggers a requirement to replace the entire slab at no additional cost to the Department, and the source provides no further limits on crack severity, width, depth or extent for this action [2]. The dowel‑retrofit procedure described in Section 527 is permitted only when a transverse crack or joint can be located and the dowel slots are cut centered over it; the method may be used for cracks up to ¼ inch wide with preformed cellular polystyrene as the temporary joint material, whereas cracks wider than ¼ inch require expansion‑joint material instead, and each dowel bar must remain within alignment tolerances of a maximum vertical tilt or horizontal skew of 0.25 inch displacement, longitudinal translation not to exceed 1 inch, and horizontal translation not to exceed 1 inch [2]. Concrete pavement cross‑stitching (Section 528) expressly prohibits use on any transverse joint or crack, making the treatment ineligible for transverse cracking regardless of width, depth or extent [2]. For concrete pavement partial‑depth repair (Section 525.1), a transverse crack may be treated when the required repair depth does not exceed one‑half of the slab thickness; cracks that would require a repair depth greater than half the slab thickness are excluded and must be addressed with full‑depth patches, while within the partial‑depth option Type 1 spot repairs apply to transverse cracks ranging from 15 inches up to 6 feet in length and Type 2 extended repairs cover transverse cracks longer than 6 feet [2]. During placement of an unbonded concrete overlay (Section 548.3), if a transverse crack occurs at or within 1.5 feet of a planned joint location—whether identified before sawing or discovered during the sawing operation—the sawing treatment for creating the overlay joint must be omitted and the condition addressed as directed in Section 590, and any panel that shows cracking outside of the sawed joints is considered defective [2]. The preparation clause for the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course (Section 489.3) mandates that at least 24 hours before paving all transverse cracks that are ¼‑inch and wider be sealed with the rubberized sealant specified in Section 469; this sealing constitutes the prescribed treatment for such cracks prior to applying the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course [2].

Prevention in design and construction

The Pavement Policy Manual recommends a dowel‑bar retrofit to supply load transfer across working transverse cracks in Portland‑cement concrete slabs, and it is to be applied whenever such active transverse cracks are identified on PCC surfaces (section 2.1 GENERAL) [1]. It also calls for the installation of adequate subsurface drainage, including lateral underdrains in wet subgrade areas and continuous pavement base drains along outside edges, low‑side superelevations, median sides, and at cut‑to‑fill transitions, a measure that prevents transverse cracks that develop under traffic loads when moisture becomes trapped beneath concrete slabs—particularly during spring thaw or where deteriorated joints impede gravity drainage—and it applies wherever subsurface water problems are identified as directed by the local maintenance representative (section 2.3 DRAINAGE) [1]. For hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt projects, candidate pavements are limited to those whose transverse‑crack depth is less than 1 inch and whose average crack width does not exceed the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, thereby preventing excessive cracking from being overlaid (section 5.19 C) [1]. The same HIR guidelines allow placement of crack sealant on longitudinal and transverse joints before the recycling process to reduce the effect of existing transverse cracks (section 5.19 C) [1]. They further recommend milling prior to the HIR process when repairs, culvert cuts or other work have been completed, as this can address deeper transverse cracks and retain required vertical clearance (section 5.19 C) [1]. The specifications for bonded concrete overlays require that concrete be placed only when its temperature is between 65 °F and 90 °F, a restriction intended to limit thermal contraction that can initiate transverse cracks (Section 545.3(a)1) [2]. They also mandate accurate marking of all existing transverse joint centers before the pour so that saw‑cut joints can be aligned, a step that helps prevent debonding and crack propagation (Section 545.3(d)) [2]. Each transverse joint is to be cut over the underlying joint to a depth equal to the full overlay thickness plus ½ inch with a width at least as wide as the original joint, a practice designed to prevent debonding and subsequent crack propagation (Section 545.3(h)) [2]. Bond strength must be verified by ASTM C1583 testing, requiring an average of at least 175 psi and no individual result below 160 psi, which ensures sufficient adhesion to reduce reflective transverse cracks (Section 545.3(e)) [2]. When the forecast air temperature is 45 °F or lower, insulating blankets must be applied during curing and the surface‑temperature change limited to no more than 40 °F per hour, a measure intended to avoid rapid‑cooling cracks (Section 545.3(g)) [2]. Grout‑injection slab stabilization shall be used on any joint or crack—including transverse cracks—when a loaded slab corner deflection reaches 0.02 inch or more and the joint efficiency is at least 65 % (or, with approval, when efficiency is below 65 %), which is intended to prevent further movement of the crack (Section 679.3) [2]. The specifications expressly prohibit the use of cross‑stitching on transverse joints or cracks, a restriction that applies whenever cross‑stitching would otherwise be considered for a transverse joint or crack (Section 528.3) [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays, any existing transverse expansion joints must be identified, marked and precut in the plastic concrete before full‑depth cuts are made on each side of the joint to permit slab movement; the saw‑cut depth must be no less than D/3 within 12 inches of the pavement edges and no less than D/4 elsewhere with a minimum width of ¼ inch, a procedure that creates proper unbonded overlay joints to reduce cracking (Section 548.3) [2]. Panel geometry should have an aspect ratio as close to 1.0 as feasible and not exceed 1.5, and overall panel dimensions should not exceed 18 times the concrete overlay thickness, a limitation that restricts slab size that can generate transverse stresses (Section 548.3) [2]. Joint spacing follows the project plans or a department‑approved layout, longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths, and all transverse and longitudinal joints are cleaned and sealed after cutting, actions that further reduce cracking (Section 548.3) [2]. Heavy‑duty membranes are placed over transverse joints and random cracks in existing concrete pavements at locations indicated or directed by the Representative, a measure intended to prevent or reduce further development of transverse cracking (Section 467.1) [2]. At least 24 hours before any ultra‑thin bonded wearing course is placed, all longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks one quarter‑inch wide or larger must be sealed with the rubberized sealant prescribed in Section 469 while keeping the sealant over‑band thickness and width within the tolerances of that section, a preparation step that addresses existing transverse cracks by limiting water intrusion and further crack propagation (Section 489.3(b)) [2].

The drainage guidance of Section 2.3 requires that base and subbase layers be constructed with highly permeable materials so that gravity‑drainable water is removed rapidly; this material requirement is intended to prevent the saturated‑subgrade condition that otherwise produces transverse cracks in concrete pavements under traffic loads, especially during spring thaw [1]. Section 2.3 also recommends installing lateral underdrains in wet subgrade areas and at all cut‑to‑fill transitions; this construction practice is meant to keep water from becoming trapped beneath joints where it could freeze and generate transverse cracking, and it applies wherever such moisture‑prone locations exist [1]. Continuous pavement base drains must be provided along the outside edge of the pavement, on the low side of superelevated sections, and on median sides in areas with subsurface water problems for both Interstate and Non‑Interstate projects; this practice eliminates subgrade saturation that leads to transverse cracks, particularly during spring thaw [1]. In Chapter 5 §5.19 concerning Hot‑In‑Place Recycled asphalt, milling before the HIR process can address deeper transverse cracks and preserve vertical clearance; this practice applies when HIR is combined with other repairs performed prior to recycling [1]. The same section permits the use of crack sealant on longitudinal and transverse joints as a material requirement that addresses existing transverse cracking, provided extensive sealant or an over‑banded rubberized joint seal is not present [1]. Candidate HIR projects are limited to transverse‑crack severity depths less than 1 inch with average crack widths not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch; this condition defines when the HIR treatment may be employed [1]. Section 545.3 requires that concrete be placed only when the concrete temperature is between 65°F and 90°F to avoid cold‑induced transverse cracking during curing, mandates adequate insulating blankets if forecast air temperature is 45°F or less, and limits surface temperature change to not exceed 40°F within any one‑hour period to prevent thermal transverse cracks [2]. Section 545.3 also calls for joint backing or other approved joint filling material to be installed to within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface in every existing transverse joint, accurate marking of each transverse joint prior to construction, and saw‑cutting directly over those joints with a width at least equal to the underlying joint or crack; these steps address debonding that can appear as transverse cracking and misaligned joints that could cause transverse cracking [2]. Bond strength testing per ASTM C1583 in Section 545.3 must yield an average of at least 175 psi and each individual test not less than 160 psi, a condition that safeguards against transverse cracks caused by insufficient overlay‑substrate adhesion [2]. Section 679.3 states that slab‑stabilization work shall not be started when daytime temperatures are below 40°F or the subgrade/base course is frozen, a condition intended to avoid transverse cracking caused by cold‑induced movement [2]. Deflection testing of any accessible transverse joint or crack in Section 679.3 may be performed only when air temperature is between 40°F and 70°F and the subgrade is not frozen; joints or cracks showing a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more are then stabilized if joint efficiency is at least 65 % or patched if joint efficiency is less than 65 %, thereby preventing further transverse‑crack propagation [2]. Section 528.3 mandates that cross‑stitching shall not be used on transverse joints or cracks and also prohibits work when either the ambient air temperature or the pavement surface temperature is below 40°F; this prohibition directly prevents any treatment from affecting existing transverse cracking [2]. Section 548.3 requires that transverse expansion joints be identified, precut in the plastic concrete and then sawed with two full‑depth cuts on each side of the joint, using a minimum depth of D/3 within 12 in of edges and at least D/4 elsewhere, to accommodate slab movement and prevent random transverse cracking [2]. Section 548.3 further specifies that panel dimensions shall have an aspect ratio as close to 1.0 as feasible and not exceed 1.5, and overall panel size shall not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness, limiting stress concentrations that could cause transverse cracks [2]. Joint spacing in Section 548.3 must follow the project plans or a department‑approved layout, avoiding longitudinal joints in wheel paths, and saw cutting is prescribed as the sole method for creating overlay joints with a minimum width of ¼ in and depth D/3; sawing shall be omitted if a crack exists within 1.5 ft of the intended joint location, thereby preventing transverse cracking [2]. When a geotextile interlayer is used, Section 548.3 requires that joints with faulting greater than ¼ in are milled before placement and the fabric must be kept clean, damp but not saturated and below 120°F to provide debonding that reduces reflective transverse cracking [2]. Section 469.1 specifies cleaning and sealing of longitudinal and transverse joints and random cracks on existing pavement surfaces; this treatment removes debris and applies a sealant to close the joint, limiting water infiltration and further crack propagation [2]. Section 467.1 prescribes placing heavy‑duty membranes over transverse and longitudinal joints and random cracks in existing concrete pavements; this measure directly addresses transverse cracking by covering those joints, and it is required only at locations indicated or directed by the Representative [2]. Section 489.3 limits placement of asphalt mixtures containing PG 64S‑22 from November 1 through March 31 and PG 64E‑22 from October 15 through March 31, and also forbids placing any mixture when the surface is wet or the air or surface temperature is below 50°F; these weather limits are intended to avoid thermal contraction cracking [2]. Section 489.3 further requires that at least 24 hours before paving operations, longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks ¼‑inch or wider must be sealed using the rubberized sealant of Section 469, a step that prevents water intrusion and propagation of existing transverse cracks [2].

Specification limits and acceptance

For candidate hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) asphalt projects evaluated under Chapter 5 Section 5.19, the Pub 336 criteria require that transverse cracking severity be limited to a depth less than 1.0 inch and that the average crack width not exceed the Medium Rating of ≤ ½ inch; these limits are judged by measuring the maximum crack depth and calculating the mean crack opening across the surface [1]. In the slab‑stabilization specification (Section 679), acceptance of transverse cracks is based on a binary condition—if any transverse crack develops between adjacent grout injection holes, the contractor must replace the entire slab at no additional cost to the Department as referenced in Section 516; compliance is determined by visual inspection for the presence of such cracks [2]. The dowel‑retrofit specification (Section 527.3) stipulates that dowels installed across a transverse crack or joint may not exhibit vertical tilt or horizontal skew greater than 0.25 inch at each end relative to the dowel midpoint, and neither longitudinal nor horizontal translation from the intended crack location may exceed 1 inch; these tolerances are verified by measuring the dowel alignment after placement [2]. Under the concrete‑pavement partial‑depth repair specification (Section 525), transverse cracks are classified for repair based on length, with spot repairs applicable to cracks whose length falls between 15 inches and 6 feet and extended‑length repairs required for cracks longer than 6 feet; the appropriate repair method is selected by measuring the crack length [2]. The ultra‑thin bonded wearing course specification (Section 489) requires that all longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks that are ¼‑inch or wider be sealed with the prescribed rubberized sealant at least 24 hours before paving, and the sealant over‑band thickness and width must not exceed the tolerances set in Section 469; compliance is assessed by measuring crack width and verifying sealant dimensions [2].
