Transverse Crack

Part of: Cracking is-subtopic-of
Transverse Crack is a topic under Cracking.
transverse-crackstransverse-cracking
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Transverse cracks are a distress that can develop in concrete pavements when the underlying subgrade becomes saturated, reducing its ability to support traffic loads and producing fractures on the pavement surface[1]. They appear as fractures running perpendicular to the roadway centerline and are especially common during spring thaw when water is trapped beneath a frozen layer, a moisture‑related loss of support that distinguishes them from shrinkage or thermal cracks in asphalt which arise from temperature‑induced brittleness rather than inadequate drainage[1][2]. This loss of support allows traffic loads to generate surface‑only cracks that can diminish structural capacity, degrade ride quality and create safety concerns such as tenting from frost heave[1].

Description and identification

Transverse cracks are a distress that can develop in concrete pavements when the underlying subgrade becomes saturated, reducing its ability to support traffic loads. The loss of support under loading may cause transverse cracks to form, appearing as fractures on the pavement surface. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59)

In the drainage context of concrete pavements, transverse cracks are identified as fractures that run perpendicular to the roadway centerline and appear when a saturated subgrade can no longer support the slab under traffic loading, especially during the spring thaw when water is trapped beneath a frozen layer; this moisture‑related loss of support distinguishes them from shrinkage or thermal cracks in asphalt, which arise from temperature‑induced brittleness rather than inadequate drainage. Within the hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) candidate‑pavement guidelines, a transverse crack is recognized as a surface‑only distress that must satisfy severity limits of depth less than 1.0 inch and an average width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch; because only surface cracks are permitted, any load‑related distress or base problem excludes the pavement from HIR treatment, thereby separating transverse cracking from other forms such as rutting or shoving. During slab stabilization, a transverse crack is recognized when a crack appears between adjacent grout injection holes, a pattern that signals a transverse distress distinct from radial cracks, which spread outward from the injection holes. When such transverse cracks are observed in slab stabilization, the specification requires replacement of the entire slab at no additional cost to the Department as directed in Section 516. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Where it occurs

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, transverse cracking is identified as a distress that occurs in Portland Cement Concrete (PCC) pavement slabs, where working transverse cracks require load‑transfer treatment such as dowel‑bar retrofit; the source does not mention transverse cracking for asphalt concrete surfaces, composite pavements, overlays, or shoulders. The manual further notes that transverse cracking is observed in concrete pavements and in asphalt overlays that are placed over concrete when subsurface moisture saturates the subgrade, a condition that becomes especially critical during spring thaw, and that both concrete pavements and their asphalt overlays react similarly to moisture. In addition, transverse cracking is addressed for surface‑distressed asphalt pavements that are candidates for Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) treatment and also for composite structures where an asphalt overlay rests on a stable concrete base; for HIR candidate pavements the transverse‑crack severity must be limited to a depth less than 1.0 inch, with an average crack width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and the distress must reside only in the surface layer, while when the HIR overlay is placed on a composite pavement the underlying concrete must be stable with non‑movement at the joints. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications likewise observe transverse cracking on both rigid concrete pavements and flexible asphalt pavements as well as on structures that receive overlays or protective membranes. For rigid concrete pavements the distress may appear between adjacent grout injection holes, and the slab‑stabilization specification calls for replacement of the entire slab at no additional cost to the Department (Section 679). Dowel retrofit work on rigid pavements locates a crack or transverse joint in a concrete slab and aligns saw cuts parallel to the roadway centreline before installing dowels across it; this retrofit is specified for concrete slabs that are not being overlaid (Section 527). The cross‑stitching specification expressly prohibits its use on transverse joints or cracks, indicating that such cracks exist in concrete slabs (Section 528). Partial‑depth repair provisions apply to rigid pavements where a transverse crack or joint is located within an existing slab; the repair depth must not exceed half the slab thickness, spot repairs are used for cracks up to 6 feet long, and extended‑length repairs are required for lengths greater than 6 feet (Section 525). For bonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements all existing transverse joints—including any underlying cracks—must be identified, marked, and saw‑cut through the overlay; the saw‑cut depth must equal the full thickness of the overlay plus 1/2 inch and the cut width shall be at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack (Section 545). Unbonded concrete overlays over existing concrete‑surfaced roads also require identification and marking of transverse expansion joints; controlled transverse joints are created by saw cuts and panel dimensions are limited so that the longer side does not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness to avoid premature cracking (Section 548). In asphalt pavement maintenance, the Asphalt Joint and Crack Sealing work calls for cleaning and sealing of longitudinal and transverse joints as well as random cracks in existing pavement surfaces, thereby recognizing transverse cracking on asphalt pavements (Section 469). The preparation of flexible pavements that are to receive an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course requires sealing any transverse cracks 1/4‑inch and wider before paving operations (Section 489). Heavy‑duty membranes are specified for placement over transverse and longitudinal joints and any random cracks in existing concrete pavements, providing a remedial action for transverse cracking on those structures (Section 467). [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 321–345), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 426–474), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Both the Pavement Policy Manual and the Specification indicate that working transverse cracks in Portland‑Cement‑Concrete slabs are located within the interior of the concrete slab rather than at joints, edges or layer interfaces. The Policy Manual notes that a saturated subgrade beneath a concrete slab can cause such interior transverse cracking because poor support allows traffic loads to generate cracks across the pavement. For hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt, the Policy Manual treats transverse cracking as a surface distress limited to a depth of less than 1 inch and explicitly states it does not involve the base or subgrade. The Specification’s slab‑stabilization section (679‑3) observes transverse cracks developing between adjacent grout injection holes, reinforcing that the distress occurs within the interior of the concrete slab rather than at joints, edges, or subgrade layers. The dowel‑retrofit provisions (527‑3) require cutting retrofit slots directly over each transverse crack along the wheel path, further confirming that the cracks are situated in the slab interior where they intersect the pavement. Section 525‑1 describes transverse cracks as being present at the transverse joints of a concrete slab and also on the interior surface of the slab (interior slab spalling), with spot or extended repairs applied depending on length, indicating that the distress can occur both at joint locations and on the interior face of the slab. The joint‑and‑crack sealing section (469‑1) states that transverse cracking appears either as a transverse joint or as a random surface crack, meaning that in those cases the distress is located at joints or at the pavement surface rather than within deeper layers such as interfaces, base or subgrade. Heavy‑duty membrane specifications (467‑1) call for membranes to be placed over transverse joints of existing concrete pavements and note that random cracks may also be present within the slab, reinforcing the occurrence at joint locations and occasional interior random cracking. The ultra‑thin bonded wearing‑course preparation (489‑3) requires sealing of longitudinal and transverse joints and any crack at least one‑quarter inch wide before overlay work, treating those cracks as surface‑level features. Causes and mechanisms

According to the drainage subsection, transverse cracks in concrete pavements are linked to poor subsurface drainage; when the subgrade becomes saturated it loses support, and the resulting weak base allows traffic loads to produce cracks that run perpendicular to the pavement centerline. Thus both moisture‑related drainage problems and the applied traffic loading are identified as causes, with no table in this excerpt providing a separate classification. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59)

Transverse cracks develop when saturated subgrades provide poor support for concrete pavements, allowing traffic loads to cause cracking. This condition is particularly critical during the spring thaw because moisture cannot percolate downward into the subsoil due to a frozen layer beneath it, keeping the subgrade wet. When a joint deteriorates below the pavement surface into particles small enough to impede gravity drainage, water becomes trapped beneath the joint and can freeze, producing localized frost heave that further promotes transverse cracking. In rigid concrete overlays, transverse cracks also develop when slab movement such as thermal contraction or shrinkage is not accommodated by properly placed transverse expansion joints, generating tensile stresses that are released as cracks. The specification requires identification and precutting of existing transverse joints to allow slab movement before sawing, and the joint layout must follow the spacing shown in the project plans to avoid random cracking. The risk of premature cracking is accelerated when panel dimensions exceed a limit of 18 times the overlay thickness or when the aspect‑ratio (longer dimension divided by shorter dimension) exceeds 1.5, because such oversized panels are subject to premature cracking. Delaying saw cuts or failing to saw at the recommended window also promotes random cracking, since sawing is the only acceptable technique for creating concrete overlay joints and must be done before excessive raveling occurs. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 472–474)

Severity levels and rating

The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) specifies that for projects considered for hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt, transverse cracking is evaluated by depth and average width; the severity must be less than 1 inch in depth and the average crack width may not exceed the Medium rating of ½ inch, and these thresholds are applied only when the distress is confined to the surface and no load‑related base problems exist [1]. In contrast, the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications for concrete pavement partial‑depth repair define transverse‑crack severity by length: cracks measuring from 15 inches up to 6 feet are classified as Type 1 spot repairs, while cracks longer than 6 feet fall into a Type 2 extended‑length category, and both categories are limited to repairs that do not exceed half the slab thickness, with deeper issues requiring full‑depth patches [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 426)

For HIR asphalt candidate pavements the transverse‑crack condition is rated using the Pub. 336 criteria referenced in Chapter 5. The source specifies that Transverse and Miscellaneous Cracking Severity should be a depth < 1.0 inch, and that the average crack width should not exceed the Medium Rating of ≤ ½ inch. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133)

Measurement, survey and extent

Transverse cracks are evaluated by performing deflection testing in accordance with ASTM D4695, using vertical‑movement equipment that can detect slab lift to within 0.001 inch; the test is applied at each transverse joint or crack (subject to air temperatures between 40°F and 70°F) and the loaded slab corner deflection is recorded. If the measured loaded slab corner deflection equals or exceeds 0.02 inch, the joint or crack is classified for stabilization under the slab‑stabilization specification. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Under Section 679—SLAB STABILIZATION, Subsection 679.3 Construction, transverse cracks are surveyed by performing deflection testing in accordance with ASTM D4695 on each joint and crack when directed by the Representative; if air temperature is below 40 °F or exceeds 70 °F a visual verification is used instead. The survey is executed for every transverse joint and crack as part of the field QC plan, using each individual joint or crack as the sample unit rather than a periodic schedule. Extent of the distress is quantified by measuring the loaded slab corner deflection and calculating Joint Efficiency, which is expressed as a percentage; cracks with a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more and a Joint Efficiency at 65 % or higher are candidates for stabilization or replacement per Section 516. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Effects on performance

Because saturated subgrades provide poor support, transverse cracks develop under traffic loads, indicating a loss of structural capacity; the resulting surface undulations cause a noticeable loss of ride quality and can compromise safety by creating uneven driving conditions; trapped water that freezes beneath deteriorated joints may cause tenting, further reducing pavement durability and shortening its service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59)

Inadequate subsurface drainage leaves the subgrade saturated, which reduces support for concrete pavements and allows transverse cracks to develop under traffic loads [1]. During the spring thaw the frozen layer beneath the pavement prevents moisture from percolating downward into the subsoil, thereby worsening the condition of the newly formed crack [1]. As the cracked joint deteriorates below the surface into fine particles, these particles become small enough to impede gravity drainage, causing water to accumulate beneath the joint [1]. When winter arrives this trapped water may freeze and generate tenting, an upward movement of the joints produced by localized frost heave [1]. In asphalt pavements a fluid base similarly reduces support, producing surface roughness and spawning additional cracking adjacent to the original transverse crack [1]. If a transverse crack forms between adjacent grout injection holes and is not stabilized, the specification requires full‑depth slab replacement at no additional cost to the Department, as stipulated in Section 516, indicating that an untreated transverse crack can progress from a joint defect to a condition that mandates complete slab removal and replacement [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Repairs and treatments

Dowel Bar Retrofit is listed as a repair that provides load transfer on working transverse cracks in concrete slabs and therefore is applied only when such active transverse cracking is present and improved load distribution is required [1]. Continuous pavement base drains are required along the outside edge of the pavement, lateral underdrains must be installed at transitions from cut to fill and at other needed locations as identified by the local maintenance representative, and subgrade drains should also be considered where the existing roadway shows evidence of water damage and in sag areas; these drainage installations apply whenever subsurface water is identified as a problem by the local maintenance representative or when functional edge drains are absent [1]. In Section 5.19 Hot‑In‑Place Recycled Asphalt may be applied when transverse cracking is limited to surface distress with a depth < 1.0 inch and an average crack width not exceeding ≤ ½ inch, provided the pavement otherwise meets the surface‑only criteria of Chapter 5 Section 5.19; for deeper cracks the source indicates that milling before the HIR process can address those deeper transverse cracks while preserving vertical clearance, and the application of crack sealant on longitudinal and transverse joints is also acceptable as a repair for transverse cracking [1]. For bonded concrete overlay, joint backing or approved joint‑filling material must be placed within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface in all existing transverse joints before the overlay, thereby treating those transverse cracks and preventing displacement; when the fresh concrete attains sufficient strength, transverse joints are to be saw‑cut directly over each existing transverse joint to a depth equal to the full overlay thickness plus ½ inch, with the cut width at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack, and when an inlay is used the cuts must extend to the full depth of the inlay with a minimum width of ½ inch [2]. Slab stabilization stabilizes any joint or crack—including a transverse crack—when a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more is present and the joint efficiency is at least 65 % (or, if less than 65 %, it is patched and then stabilized) provided the representative approves; if during stabilization transverse cracks develop between adjacent grout injection holes, full‑depth slab replacement is required at no additional cost to the Department as specified in Section 516 [2]. Dowel retrofit is prescribed for transverse cracks or joints in rigid pavements by cutting three parallel slots per wheel path centered over the crack, placing coated dowels aligned with the existing joint, and this treatment is applied only when the saw cuts are made parallel to the roadway centerline and placed at the mid‑depth of the slab (or the thinner slab in thickness transitions), with each dowel end limited to a maximum vertical or horizontal tilt of 0.25 inch and a longitudinal or horizontal translation not exceeding 1 inch [2]. Concrete pavement partial‑depth repair may treat transverse cracks with a Type 1 spot repair when the crack length is between 15 inches and 6 feet, or with a Type 2 extended‑length repair when the length exceeds 6 feet, provided that the required repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness [2]. For unbonded concrete overlay, controlled transverse joints are created by first identifying and marking existing transverse expansion joints, precutting them in the plastic concrete, then sawing two full‑depth cuts on each side of the precut joint and removing the intervening concrete; the saw cuts must be at least D/3 within 12 inches of the pavement edges and at least D/4 elsewhere, and sawing shall be omitted if a crack is found at or within 1.5 feet of a joint location, in which case Section 590 applies [2]. Asphalt joint and crack sealing consists of cleaning and sealing longitudinal and transverse joints as well as random surface cracks, and the work is limited to cleaning and sealing those cracks on the current pavement surface [2]. Heavy‑duty membranes are placed over transverse (and longitudinal) joints and random cracks in existing concrete pavements, applied only at locations identified or directed by the Representative [2]. For an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course, transverse cracks that are ¼‑inch and wider must be sealed with rubberized sealant prior to placement; this sealing treatment addresses the transverse cracking by closing those joints before the new surface is applied, and it must be done at least 24 hours before paving operations [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 321–345), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 426–474), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Under the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) Section 5.19 C, a Hot‑In‑Place Recycled asphalt overlay is considered applicable only when the transverse cracking severity is less than one inch deep and the average crack width does not exceed the medium rating of half an inch; if either threshold is exceeded the treatment is ruled out [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification 408/2026, Section 545 for a bonded concrete overlay may be applied when the contractor first marks joint locations and then saw‑cuts each transverse crack to a depth equal to the full thickness of the overlay plus one‑half inch without damaging dowel devices, and cuts a width that is at least as wide as the underlying crack, provided that joint backing or an approved filling material is placed within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface in every transverse joint before the overlay is placed; the section contains no exclusion based on crack severity [2]. Full‑slab replacement described in Section 679 is applicable only when a transverse crack develops between adjacent grout injection holes, and no other width or depth criteria are specified [2]. The dowel‑retrofit repair of Section 527 may be used for transverse cracks or joints that are centered under the saw cuts provided the crack or joint width does not exceed ¼ inch; when the width exceeds ¼ inch the procedure calls for using expansion joint material instead of preformed cellular polystyrene, and the installed dowels must remain within placement tolerances such that vertical tilt or horizontal skew may not exceed a 0.25‑inch displacement and longitudinal translation relative to the intended crack location may not exceed 1 inch [2]. Cross‑stitching in Section 528 shall not be used on transverse joints or cracks, thereby excluding any transverse crack regardless of its width, depth, severity, or extent from this treatment [2]. The partial‑depth repair outlined in Section 525 is suitable for a transverse crack when the required repair depth does not exceed one‑half of the slab thickness; cracks whose length is between fifteen inches and six feet are classified as Type 1 spot repairs, while cracks longer than six feet are treated as Type 2 extended‑length repairs, and any repair that would require removal deeper than half the slab thickness is excluded and must be addressed with full‑depth patches [2]. For an unbonded concrete overlay in Section 548 the only acceptable technique for creating overlay joints is sawing, but if a transverse crack is present at or within 1.5 feet of a joint location—whether before or during sawing—the specification requires omitting the sawing and addressing the condition according to Section 590 [2]. The ultra‑thin bonded wearing course described in Section 489 requires that transverse cracks that are at least ¼‑inch wide be sealed with a rubberized sealant as specified in Section 469 before placement, and the treatment does not apply to narrower transverse cracking [2].

Prevention in design and construction

In the General section of the Pavement Policy Manual, a dowel‑bar retrofit is prescribed to supply load transfer across working transverse cracks in Portland‑cement concrete slabs, and it is to be applied whenever such active transverse cracks are identified on PCC surfaces.[1] The Drainage subsection directs the installation of adequate subsurface drainage—including lateral underdrains in wet subgrade areas and continuous pavement base drains along outside edges, low‑side superelevations, median sides, and at cut‑to‑fill transitions—to prevent transverse cracks that develop under traffic loads when moisture becomes trapped beneath concrete slabs—particularly during spring thaw or where deteriorated joints impede gravity drainage—and it applies wherever subsurface water problems are identified, as directed by the local maintenance representative and in accordance with the design principles of that subsection.[1] Within the Hot‑In‑Place Recycled Asphalt guidelines, candidate HIR projects are limited to pavements whose transverse‑crack depth is less than 1 inch and whose average crack width does not exceed the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, thereby preventing excessive cracking from being overlaid.[1] The same HIR section allows crack sealant to be placed on longitudinal and transverse joints before HIR placement in order to reduce the effect of existing transverse cracks.[1] Milling prior to the HIR process is recommended when repairs, culvert cuts or other work have been completed, because it can address deeper transverse cracks and retain required vertical clearance.[1] The bonded concrete overlay specification requires that all existing transverse joints be accurately marked before placement and that the marking method receive approval at least twenty days prior, so that saw‑cutting can be performed directly over those joints once the fresh concrete has attained sufficient strength to be cut without raveling; this preparation is intended to prevent debonding and reduce shrinkage‑induced transverse cracking.[2] The same specification also mandates intermediate transverse cuts such that no overlay panel exceeds fifteen feet in length, a condition that limits the development of thermal‑contraction cracks across long panels.[2] When forecast air temperature is 45 °F or less, curing controls are prescribed requiring insulating blankets to be used and removed only after the air rises above 45 °F or concrete strength passes 3,600 psi, with surface temperature change limited to no more than 40 °F per hour; these measures are aimed at avoiding thermal‑contraction transverse cracks.[2] For unbonded concrete overlays, existing transverse expansion joints must be identified and precut in the plastic concrete before full‑depth sawing on each side of the joint, allowing slab movement prior to cutting to reduce random transverse cracking.[2] Overlay panels are required to have an aspect ratio as close to 1.0 as feasible and not exceed 1.5, and no panel may be larger than eighteen times the overlay thickness; these geometric limits keep stresses from promoting premature transverse cracks.[2] Saw cuts must achieve a depth of at least one‑third of slab depth within twelve inches of the pavement edges and at least one‑quarter elsewhere, and they must be cleaned and sealed, providing sufficient joint depth to control crack propagation.[2] Joint spacing must follow the plan or an approved layout, with longitudinal joints avoided in wheel paths, to ensure proper interruption of transverse cracking.[2] Sawing shall occur as soon as the concrete is hardened enough to avoid random cracking, using early‑entry saws and a timing window defined by FHWA HIPERPAV software when applicable, thereby addressing the timing of joint creation to prevent transverse cracks from developing.[2] The heavy‑duty membrane provision specifies placing membranes over transverse (and longitudinal) joints and random cracks in existing concrete pavements; this treatment covers the crack or joint and thereby reduces further transverse cracking, and it is applied only at locations identified or directed by the Representative.[2] In the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course specification, sealing of longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks that are one quarter inch or wider with the prescribed rubberized sealant is required to prevent propagation of existing transverse cracks, and this sealing must be performed at least twenty‑four hours before paving operations.[2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 321–345), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 466–474)

The Pavement Policy Manual requires base and subbase layers be constructed with highly permeable materials so that gravity‑drainable water is removed rapidly, a material requirement intended to prevent the saturated‑subgrade condition that otherwise produces transverse cracks in concrete pavements under traffic loads, especially during spring thaw (Section 2.3) [1]. The same manual recommends installing lateral underdrains in wet subgrade areas and at all cut‑to‑fill transitions, a construction practice meant to keep water from becoming trapped beneath joints where it could freeze and generate transverse cracking; this recommendation applies wherever such moisture‑prone locations exist (Section 2.3) [1]. Continuous pavement base drains are required along the outside edge of the pavement, on the low side of superelevated sections, and on median sides in areas with subsurface water problems for both Interstate and Non‑Interstate projects, a practice that eliminates subgrade saturation that leads to transverse cracks, particularly during spring thaw (Section 2.3) [1]. For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled asphalt, the manual specifies milling before the HIR process can address deeper transverse cracks and help retain vertical clearance for overhead structures, curbs, gutters, and guide rails; this practice applies when HIR is combined with other repairs performed prior to recycling (Section 5.19) [1]. The manual also permits the use of crack sealant on longitudinal and transverse joints as a material requirement that addresses existing transverse cracking, provided extensive sealant or an over‑banded rubberized joint seal is not present (Section 5.19) [1]. Candidate HIR projects are limited to transverse‑crack severity depths less than 1 inch with average crack widths not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, defining when the HIR treatment may be employed (Section 5.19) [1]. The specifications require insulating blankets to be applied during curing whenever the forecast air temperature is 45 °F or less, a measure intended to prevent rapid heat loss that could induce transverse cracking (Section 545.3(g)) [2]. Saw‑cutting each existing transverse joint after the concrete has attained sufficient strength using a cut width at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack and a depth equal to the full overlay thickness plus ½ inch is required to prevent debonding that would otherwise appear as transverse cracks (Section 545.3(h)) [2]. Intermediate transverse joints must be placed so that no overlay panel exceeds 15 feet in length, thereby limiting joint spacing and reducing the tensile stresses that generate transverse cracking (Section 545.3(i)) [2]. The dowel‑retrofit procedure calls for a power‑driven self‑propelled saw capable of making two parallel cuts per dowel bar slot without causing pavement spall or fracturing surface aggregates, and limits jackhammer weight to 30 lb (or to 15 lb if damage occurs), practices that keep slab damage around the transverse crack from worsening (Section 527.3) [2]. Parallel cuts are required to be aligned with the roadway centreline and three dowel slots must be centered over each transverse joint, controls placement relative to the crack (Section 527.3) [2]. Slots must be cleaned of all loose material within 24 hours and receive a final air‑blast cleaning at least 100 psi no more than 30 minutes before concrete placement, preventing debris or contaminant intrusion into the repaired crack (Section 527.3) [2]. Dowel bars coated with Type B must receive bond‑breaker lubricant applied by daubing, mopping or gloved hand at least one hour before concrete placement, a step that helps prevent moisture and concrete from entering the existing transverse crack (Section 527.3) [2]. Dowel bars must be positioned with no more than 0.25 inch vertical tilt or horizontal skew and not more than 1 inch longitudinal or horizontal translation, ensuring the dowel does not misalign the transverse joint (Section 527.3) [2]. Caulking compound must seal the temporary joint material on the sides and bottom of the transverse crack before backfilling, and a sealant reservoir is to be sawed and sealed across the lane width after placement, actions that keep moisture and concrete from entering the existing crack (Section 527.3) [2]. Partial‑depth repair may be used for transverse cracks when the required repair depth does not exceed one‑half of the slab thickness; spot repairs are prescribed for cracks between 15 inches and 6 feet in length and extended repairs for cracks longer than 6 feet, both addressing existing transverse cracking (Section 525.1) [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays, transverse expansion joints must be identified, precut in the plastic concrete and then saw full‑depth on each side of the underlying joint to control slab movement and prevent random transverse cracking; panel geometry is limited by an aspect ratio not greater than 1.5 and a maximum dimension not exceeding eighteen times the overlay thickness (Sections 548.3(i)5‑7) [2]. Saw cuts must be at least D/3 within twelve inches of edges and at least D/4 elsewhere, with joints sealed; cutting is permitted only after the concrete has hardened enough to avoid excessive raveling, using a green‑cut saw to a depth of D/3 and width of ¼ inch, and cuts are omitted if an existing crack lies within 1.5 feet of the planned joint, thereby preventing new transverse cracks (Sections 548.3(i)6‑9) [2]. Placement of heavy‑duty membranes over transverse joints and random cracks is prescribed where the Representative has indicated or directed such placement, a practice used to address transverse cracking (Section 467.1) [2]. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, any longitudinal or transverse joints and cracks that are at least ¼‑inch wide must be sealed at least twenty‑four hours before paving operations using the rubberized sealant specified in Section 469, a step intended to prevent distress associated with existing transverse cracking (Section 489.3(g)) [2].

Specification limits and acceptance

For candidate hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) asphalt projects evaluated under Chapter 5 Section 5.19 Hot‑In‑Place Recycled Asphalt, the Pub 336 criteria require that transverse cracking severity be limited to a depth less than 1.0 inch and that the average crack width not exceed the Medium Rating of ≤ ½ inch; these limits are part of the selection guidelines for standalone HIR asphalt surfaces [1]. In the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, Section 679—Slab Stabilization item 7 of 679.3 Construction states that any transverse crack that develops between adjacent grout injection holes triggers replacement of the entire slab at no additional cost to the Department, and this condition is judged by visual detection of such cracks [2]. Section 527—Dowel Retrofit, 527.3 Construction governs dowel placement near a transverse crack or joint, specifying that vertical tilt or horizontal skew of each dowel end relative to its midpoint shall not exceed 0.25‑inch displacement and that both longitudinal translation along the travel direction and horizontal translation across the crack shall not exceed 1 inch; compliance is verified by measuring the dowel ends against the intended alignment [2]. Section 528—Concrete Pavement Cross‑Stitching, 528.3 Construction imposes a categorical prohibition on applying cross‑stitching to transverse joints or cracks, so any such distress must be left untreated by this method and is identified simply by recognizing a transverse joint or crack [2]. Section 525—Concrete Pavement Partial‑Depth Repair, 525.1 Description limits repairs of transverse cracks by requiring that the repair depth not exceed half the slab thickness; for cracks whose length is between 15 inches and 6 feet a Type 1 spot repair is prescribed, while cracks longer than 6 feet require a Type 2 extended‑length repair, with compliance judged by measuring crack length and the required repair depth [2]. Section 489—Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course, subsection g of 489.3 Construction requires that any transverse crack ¼‑inch or wider be sealed with the rubberized sealant specified in Section 469 at least 24 hours before paving, and that the over‑band thickness and width of the sealant not exceed the tolerances set out in Section 469; this is judged by measuring the crack width and the applied sealant dimensions [2].
