Cement Grout

Part of: Slab Stabilization is-subtopic-of
Cement Grout is a topic under Slab Stabilization.
cement-groutfluid-cement-grout
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Cement grout is a fluid mixture of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and, when required, admixtures that is pumped beneath a concrete slab or into voids to provide support[1][2]. By filling the slab‑subbase interface it restores full bearing, reduces deflection, pumping, joint faulting and cracking, thereby returning structural integrity even though it does not increase the pavement’s design capacity[1][2]. The mix is typically proportioned as one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material with water adjusted for an efflux time of 10–15 seconds in slab‑stabilization or 16–26 seconds in slabjacking, limited expansion of 0‑10 %, an initial set within 1–6 hours and a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi (higher strengths such as 2,500 psi are required for dowel or bedding applications)[2]. After placement the grout must cure—often about twelve hours depending on the mix design—before traffic can be reopened[1][2].

Material purpose and applicability

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), cement grout is a fluid mixture of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and, when required, admixtures that is pumped beneath a concrete slab; it is used for slab stabilization by filling voids at the slab‑subbase interface, which restores full support to the slab and reduces deflections that cause pumping, joint faulting and cracking, thereby returning the pavement’s structural integrity even though it does not increase the design structural capacity. The same manual further describes cement grout in slabjacking as a fluid mixture composed of cement, water, supplementary cementitious materials and possibly admixtures that can be pumped beneath a concrete slab; when injected into drilled holes it fills voids under the pavement and lifts the slab to the desired profile, thereby restoring the structural integrity and rideability of the pavement. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications for full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529) state that cement grout is a manufacturer‑specified cement‑based mix pumped into transverse dowel slots, longitudinal tie‑bar slots, lifting insert ports and the voids beneath the slabs; the grout must achieve at least 2,500 psi compressive strength before the slabs are opened to traffic for dowel and tie‑bar work and at least 1,000 psi before traffic for bedding grout, rising to 2,500 psi within 12 hours, thereby providing structural support and load transfer. In slab stabilization (Section 679) the specification defines cement grout as a stabilization material composed of one part cement by volume, three parts supplementary cementitious material, water adjusted for an efflux time of 10 to 15 seconds and optional admixtures; it is mixed to give 0 %–10 % expansion, an initial setting time of 1 hour to 6 hours, bleed loss not more than 2.5 % of the volume and a minimum 7‑day compressive strength of 700 psi, and it is injected under pressure not exceeding 200 psi into drilled holes no larger than 1½ inches to fill voids through the base layer, bond the concrete and limit upward slab movement to 0.05 inch, with traffic reopening after at least 12 hours. For partial‑depth repairs (Section 525) cement grout is the mixing‑type bonding material specified for Class AA repairs; after cleaning it is mechanically mixed and brushed or scrubbed on as a thin tacky coating over all exposed surfaces to bond the new concrete patch to the existing pavement, and the fresh concrete must be placed before the grout dries. In slabjacking (Section 681) cement grout—formulated with one part cement by volume, three parts supplementary cementitious material, optional admixtures and water giving an efflux time of 16 seconds to 26 seconds (or 10 seconds to 15 seconds for initial injection)—is injected into holes beneath a concrete slab to raise and stabilize the slab; continuous pressures up to 200 psi are allowed, lifts are limited to within 0.02 foot of the string‑line grade for continuously reinforced slabs or within 0.03 foot for jointed slabs, over‑jacking is corrected by grinding while excessive lift (>0.10 foot) requires pavement removal. For bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 540) a non‑shrink cement grout is placed into cracks larger than ¾ inch to fill the voids and create a bondable surface for the new concrete overlay, ensuring proper adhesion before cleaning and overlaying. [1][2]

Composition and characteristics

In slab stabilization as described in the Pavement Policy Manual (section 4.5), cement grout is defined as a fluid mixture composed of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and, when required, admixtures; it is pumped beneath the concrete slab to fill voids and remains flowable enough for injection but hardens after curing, which can be 12 hours depending on the mix design, thereby restoring support to the slab. The same manual’s slabjacking guidance (section 4.6) characterizes the grout similarly as a fluid mixture that typically contains cement, water, supplementary cementitious materials and possibly admixtures, creating a pumpable slurry placed beneath the pavement; after placement the grout must cure before traffic can return, which can be 12 hours depending on the specific mix design. For estimating purposes in slab stabilization the manual suggests about 1 cubic foot of grout per hole, equivalent to 0.25 bag of cement per hole, while for slabjacking it calls for approximately 0.40 bags of cement per hole. In the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications for slab‑stabilization work (section 679), grout is defined as a volumetric blend of one part cement by volume combined with three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, with water added to achieve an efflux time between 10 seconds and 15 seconds measured according to ASTM C939; any required admixtures such as a multiphase wetting agent, expansive agent or accelerator may be incorporated. The resulting mixture is expected to expand no more than 10 percent (range 0 % to 10 %), to set initially within a period of 1 hour to 6 hours as specified by AASHTO T 131, to limit bleeding to not more than 2.5 % of its volume, and to develop a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi based on the average of five test cylinders. For cementitious bedding grout used beneath slabs, the specification requires a compressive strength of at least 1,000 psi prior to slab opening and an increase to 2,500 psi within twelve hours; for dowel‑slot, tie‑bar slot and lifting‑insert applications the grout must attain a compressive strength of at least 2,500 psi before any traffic loading. In slabjacking applications (section 681) the grout again follows the volumetric ratio of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material and may include accepted admixtures; water content is controlled so that the efflux time falls between 16 seconds and 26 seconds per ASTM C939, although a more fluid mix achieving an efflux time of 10 seconds to 15 seconds may be used for the initial injection in each hole. If the standard mix does not achieve the desired lift, a stiffer mix may be employed. For payment purposes in the slab‑stabilization pay item the specification describes cement grout simply as bags of cement, with the unit price covering an accelerator when it is required and no additional components are enumerated. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 436–438), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 600–611)

In slab stabilization, cement grout is defined as a mixture of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material, and possibly admixtures, and the presence of these additional constituents modifies workability and strength potential. The cure time may be up to twelve hours and varies with the mix design, indicating that changes in proportioning directly affect setting time. For slabjacking, adding supplementary cementitious materials or admixtures produces a more fluid mixture that can be pumped under the slab, and the proportioning of these ingredients influences the cure time, which may be up to twelve hours depending on the mix design. Accurate proportioning of dry cement grout components by mass, or their supply in uniform‑volume sacks, together with mixing on equipment capable of 800 rpm to 2 000 rpm and use within one hour, is required to retain the consistency that limits upward pavement movement to no more than 0.05 inch and injection pressure to not exceed 200 pounds per square inch; flowability must be measured at least twice each shift. The baseline chemistry of the grout is defined by a cement‑to‑supplementary cementitious material ratio of one part cement to three parts SCM by volume, and this ratio governs expansion that shall fall between 0% and 10%, an initial setting time that may range from 1 hour to 6 hours, bleed that must not exceed 2.5% of the volume, and a 7‑day compressive strength that shall be at least 700 psi. Water is adjusted so that the efflux time lies within 10 seconds to 15 seconds, which influences workability and shrinkage/expansion behavior; admissible wetting or expansive admixtures can be used to fine‑tune setting time and expansion within those limits, and a new mix design must be submitted whenever any material source is changed. For slabjacking applications the water quantity is instead set to obtain an efflux time of 16 seconds to 26 seconds for normal injection, while a more fluid mix with an efflux time of 10 seconds to 15 seconds may be employed for the initial hole; because the water‑to‑solid ratio directly controls that efflux time, changing it alters grout fluidity and its ability to fill voids, and if lift is insufficient a stiffer mix (lower water content) is recommended. The type of cement grout selected for partial‑depth repairs—Class AA Cement Concrete Modified, Accelerated, or Rapid‑Set patching material—determines handling and curing requirements; all types must be placed only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40°F or greater and placement must stop if the temperature falls below that level. Mixing water must conform to ASTM C1602 and be reasonably clean, being free from vegetable matter, oil, acid, alkali, sugar or other substances that are injurious to the finished product, because contaminants may reduce strength or durability. In slabjacking, selecting high‑density polyurethane instead of cement changes the grout’s viscosity and penetration ability; the two‑component material has low viscosity that results in better penetration into voids beneath the slab, expands to create a lightweight, rigid, closed‑cell foam, and is resistant to water and chemical attack while offering long‑term durability. Key material properties

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), both slab‑stabilization and slabjacking require that the cement grout remain sufficiently fluid to be pumped beneath the concrete, a condition achieved by a mixture of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material, and possibly admixtures that produces a fluid cement grout mixture. After placement, the same manual advises that the grout must develop adequate cure strength before traffic can be reopened, which may require about 12 hours depending on the mix design. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications for full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529) stipulate that the grout must be pumpable into dowel slots or voids beneath slabs and must attain a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before the slabs are opened to traffic. For bedding grout in the same treatment, the specifications require at least 1,000 psi prior to opening and 2,500 psi within 12 hours. When setting bolts are used, the specifications call for a compressive strength of 50 psi. Slab‑stabilization work (Section 679—Construction) may only begin when daytime temperatures are at least 40 °F, and any deflection testing must be performed with air temperature between 40 °F and 70 °F. The same section requires that flowability of the grout be measured at least twice each work shift, that injection pressure shall not exceed a maximum of 200 psi unless otherwise directed, and that upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch will not be allowed. Material requirements for slab‑stabilization (Section 679—Material) specify an efflux time between 10 and 15 seconds, bleeding no more than 2.5 % of its volume, expansion ranging from 0 % to 10 %, an initial setting time of 1 hour to 6 hours, and a 7‑day compressive strength of at least 700 psi; laboratory testing shall also report shrinkage, expansion and flowability results. For partial‑depth repairs (Section 525—Construction), the grout must remain fluid enough to be brushed onto a freshly cleaned surface yet viscous enough that it does not run or puddle in low spots, and it must be placed before it dries or while the epoxy bonding compound is still tacky to achieve proper chemical adhesion; mixing and application may only occur when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F or greater, and if temperatures fall below this value the grout must be protected from freezing for at least 24 hours. The curing regime for these repairs requires a white membrane‑forming curing compound and demands that the temperature of the repair area remain stable so that any change does not exceed 40 °F within a one‑hour period, with insulation or heating required if ambient air drops below 80 °F. Slabjacking material specifications (Section 681—Materials) prescribe mix proportions of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, optional admixtures, and water adjusted so that the efflux time falls between 16 seconds and 26 seconds according to ASTM C939; a more fluid mix with an efflux time of 10 seconds to 15 seconds may be used for the initial injection, but if this flow range does not generate sufficient lift a stiffer mix must be employed. For bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavement (Section 540—Construction), grout that fills cracks larger than 3/4 inch must enable the overlay to attain a minimum compressive strength of 3,000 psi before opening to traffic, must be non‑shrink to maintain bond integrity, and the rate of temperature change at the concrete surface must not exceed 40 °F within any one‑hour period; insulation may be removed only when this thermal condition is satisfied. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–461), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 600–611)

In Section 529 the verification of cement grout begins with a trial batch that is mixed under the same temperature and time conditions expected on site at least 28 days before installation and independent laboratory test results are submitted; compressive strength is taken as the primary measured property, with cubes for cement‑treated bedding material tested according to AASHTO T 106 to achieve a minimum of 500 psi and cylinders for dowel grout and cementitious bedding grout tested according to PTM No. 521 to achieve minima of 2,500 psi before traffic (and an additional 1,000 psi before traffic and 2,500 psi in 12 hours for the latter), while the mix must also be pumpable into voids or slots so flowability is confirmed during the trial batch and, when setting bolts are used, cylinders are tested until they reach at least 50 psi. In Section 679.3 work‑shift monitoring requires flowability measurements to be made at least two times during each shift, injection pressure is kept below a maximum of 200 psi, any upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited, vertical‑movement testing equipment must be capable of detecting lift to within 0.001 inch, and slab performance is evaluated by deflection testing in accordance with ASTM D4695 only when air temperature is between 40°F and 70°F together with a joint‑efficiency calculation expressed as the unloaded corner deflection divided by the loaded corner deflection times 100. In Section 679.2 the cement grout mix must be characterized through independent laboratory testing that records the 1‑day, 3‑day and 7‑day compressive strengths together with flowability, shrinkage/expansion and the initial set time; water content is adjusted so that the efflux time falls within 10 seconds to 15 seconds (ASTM C939), the mix is required to exhibit an expansion between 0 % and 10 %, an initial setting time from 1 hour to 6 hours (AASHTO T 131), bleeding not exceeding 2.5 % of its volume (ASTM C940), and a minimum 7‑day compressive strength of 700 psi, based on the average of five test cylinders, is stipulated as the performance threshold for acceptance. In Section 679.4 the relevant properties of cement‑grout stabilization are quantified by a deflection test carried out for each unit, by counting holes individually as they are drilled, and by measuring the grout material in number of bags of cement supplied (including any accelerator if required). In Section 681.2 the cement grout used for slabjacking is characterized by a prescribed mix design of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume with optional admixtures, and its flowability is measured by the time of efflux according to ASTM C939; water content is adjusted so that the efflux time falls within 16 seconds to 26 seconds for normal placement, while a more fluid mix may be used initially if its efflux time is between 10 seconds to 15 seconds. In Section 681.3 key properties are monitored by measuring injection pressure and the resulting pavement lift; continuous pressures to 200 psi are permitted, pressures up to 300 psi may be used only for short periods as directed, brief spikes to 600 psi are allowed when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade, and the achieved elevation must be controlled so that continuously reinforced concrete slabs are raised to within 0.02 foot, plus or minus, of a string‑line grade and jointed slabs or bridge‑end slabs to within 0.03 foot, plus or minus, of the specified grade. [2]

Behavior and mechanisms

Cement grout used for slab stabilization must develop a minimum 7‑day compressive strength of 700 psi, indicating its capacity to resist loading after curing. The mix water is limited so that the efflux time falls between 10 and 15 seconds and bleeding does not exceed 2.5 % of the volume, which controls moisture movement within the grout. Setting is governed by temperature‑sensitive requirements: the initial set must occur within 1 to 6 hours (an accelerator may be added if required) and rapid‑set patching materials must be applied within the manufacturer’s shelf‑life and temperature limits. No specific provisions are given for chemical exposure or other service conditions. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 600–601)

In slab‑stabilization work the behavior of cement grout is first governed by its physical ability to flow as a fluid mixture of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and admixtures so that it can be pumped beneath the concrete slab [1]. Chemically, the grout must cure before traffic may be opened; the curing period can be 12 hours, depending on the mix design [1]. Mechanically, once cured the grout restores full support at the slab‑subbase interface, reducing deflections and thereby limiting further pumping, faulting and cracking [1]. The physical condition of the pavement also influences grout performance because high temperatures cause slab expansion that places joints in compression, making slabjacking impractical under such conditions [1]. Mechanically, lifting should be done in increments to minimize slab stresses and avoid cracking; extrusion from joints or cracks indicates the need for additional holes [1].

For slab‑stabilization as specified by the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation, the chemical makeup controls performance: one part cement combined with three parts supplementary cementitious material, together with any selected admixtures such as wetting or expansive agents, must be mixed with water so that the efflux time measured by ASTM C939 falls between 10 and 15 seconds, which governs flowability and bleed [2]. The grout is required to expand between 0 % and 10 %, set within 1 to 6 hours, bleed no more than 2.5 % of its volume, and achieve a minimum 7‑day compressive strength of 700 psi measured on five cylinders [2]. Physically, slab‑stabilization work may not be performed when daytime temperatures are below 40 °F or when the subgrade is frozen, upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited, and injection pressure must not exceed 200 psi unless otherwise directed; mixed material shall not be held in the mixer or pump sump for more than one hour after mixing, and flowability is verified at least twice each shift [2].

In full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs the grout’s behavior is governed by its ability to flow under pressure and to develop prescribed compressive strengths as cement hydration proceeds; the mix must be pumpable into dowel slots or voids beneath the slab and must attain a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic for dowel grout, while bedding grout must reach at least 1,000 psi before traffic and 2,500 psi within 12 hours, and an early‑age strength of 50 psi is required when setting bolts are used [2].

For slabjacking the physical flowability is controlled by water content adjusted so that the efflux time lies between 16 and 26 seconds, although a more fluid mix with an efflux time of 10 to 15 seconds may be used for the initial injection in each hole; chemically the binder system mirrors that of slab stabilization (one part cement plus three parts supplementary cementitious material with optional admixtures) [2]. Mechanically, slabjacking performance is governed primarily by hydraulic pressure and injected volume: continuous pressures are limited to 200 psi, pressures up to 300 psi may be applied only for short periods as directed, brief rises to 600 psi are permitted when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade, and pumping must raise continuously reinforced concrete pavement to within ±0.02 foot of the specified grade line [2]. The physical condition of the pavement also affects slabjacking because high temperatures cause slab expansion that places joints in compression, rendering the procedure impractical under such conditions [1]; lifting should be performed in increments to minimize stresses and avoid cracking, and extrusion from joints or cracks signals the need for additional holes [1].

In partial‑depth repairs the grout may be placed only when ambient air and surface temperatures are at least 40 °F and rising; placement must cease if temperature falls below 40 °F, and the repaired area must be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours [2]. Curing temperature changes shall not exceed 40 °F within any one‑hour period, a white membrane‑forming curing compound is applied to regulate moisture loss, and after mechanical mixing and brushing onto clean surfaces the grout is consolidated with a spud vibrator or an acceptable vibration/tamping method [2]. Chemically, the quality of mixing water is a controlling factor; water shall conform to ASTM C1602, be reasonably clean, and be free from vegetable matter, oil, acid, alkali, sugar or other substances injurious to the grout, thereby ensuring uniform set and intended performance [2].

Durability and deterioration

In SECTION 679—SLAB STABILIZATION, work must not start if daytime temperatures are below 40 °F and deflection testing is prohibited when air temperature is below 40 °F or exceeds 70 °F. Also in that section, the grout may not be held in the mixer or pump sump for more than one hour after mixing, and pumping pressures must not exceed 200 psi. In the same context, any upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 inch will not be allowed, and the cured grout cannot be opened to traffic for less than 12 hours. The appearance of radial cracks radiating from injection holes or transverse cracks between adjacent holes signals poor quality or improper methods and may require re‑grouting or slab replacement. In SECTION 525—CONCRETE PAVEMENT PARTIAL‑DEPTH REPAIR, cement grout must be applied to freshly cleaned surfaces and prevented from running or puddling. The repair must be protected from freezing for at least 24 hours after placement, and any curing temperature swing exceeding 40°F within a one‑hour period renders the work defective. Delamination of the repaired area is recognized as a failure that requires replacement at no cost to the Department. In SECTION 720—WATER, mixing or curing water must conform to ASTM C1602 and be reasonably clean, free from vegetable matter, oil, acid, alkali, sugar, or other substances injurious to the finished product; such impurities can interfere with cement hydration, leading to reduced strength, increased porosity, and long‑term durability loss in the grout matrix. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–429), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 709)

Within the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), Section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION, water that enters the pavement contributes to pumping and void formation, which can accelerate deterioration of cement grout used for slab stabilization. Joint and crack sealing must be performed in conjunction with slab stabilization to reduce the amount of water that enters the pavement and contributes to pumping. Subsurface drains should be kept in good condition, thereby reducing water ingress and lowering susceptibility to degradation. A high‑density polyurethane grout is described as resistant to water and chemical attack and has long‑term durability, indicating that resistance to moisture and chemicals reduces the risk of deterioration. In SECTION 525—CONCRETE PAVEMENT PARTIAL‑DEPTH REPAIR, the susceptibility of cement grout increases when the material is placed or cured under low temperatures; placement must not occur if ambient air and surface temperatures fall below 40 °F, and any repair must be protected from freezing for at least 24 hours. Placement should be made only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F and rising, which helps reduce deterioration risk. The curing temperature must be controlled so that the repair area does not experience a curing temperature change in excess of 40 °F within any one‑hour period during the curing operation. If ambient air drops below 80 °F, insulation or heating must be provided to maintain stable conditions. In SECTION 540—BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF ASPHALT‑SURFACED PAVEMENT, moisture and contaminants on the base increase the risk that cement grout will deteriorate, so no standing water should remain on the surface after the milling operation; any standing water must be drained immediately. The cleaned and prepared surface must be protected from oil or grease drippings from compressors, concrete trucks, spreaders, pavers, etc., which reduces susceptibility to degradation. Immediately before placing the concrete overlay, the asphalt surface must be less than 120 °F, a condition that helps preserve grout integrity. The rate of temperature change at the concrete surface must not exceed 40 °F within any one‑hour period, limiting rapid temperature swings that could damage the grout. Cracks greater than 3/4 inch should be cleaned and filled with non‑shrink grout or other approved material before pavement surface cleaning, which enhances bond quality and mitigates future degradation. In SECTION 720—WATER FOR MIXING OR CURING CEMENT CONCRETE, MORTAR, OR GROUT, the susceptibility of cement grout to deterioration is strongly linked to the quality of the mixing and curing water; water conforming to ASTM C1602 that is reasonably clean, free from vegetable matter, oil, acid, alkali, sugar, or other injurious substances helps limit chemical attack and reduces cracking or loss of strength. Using water containing any of those contaminants introduces deleterious agents that can accelerate degradation. [1][2]

Material interactions and compatibility

In slab stabilization, cement grout is a fluid mixture of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and possibly admixtures that is pumped beneath a concrete slab to fill the voids at the slab/subbase interface; by occupying those spaces it restores full support, reduces slab deflection and limits further pumping, faulting or cracking, and the interaction is essentially mechanical as the grout bonds the slab to the underlying base, re‑establishing structural continuity. The grout must be allowed to cure before traffic can resume, which can be 12 hours depending on the mix design. In a partial‑depth repair, after the repair area is thoroughly cleaned a thin layer of cement grout is brushed onto all exposed concrete surfaces to create an adhesive interface; the grout must remain wet or tacky so that new concrete can be placed before it begins to dry, otherwise the hardened grout must be removed and fresh grout reapplied, and the grout must not run or puddle in low spots, ensuring uniform contact with the existing pavement substrate. For slab stabilization under Section 679.2, cement grout is formulated by combining one part cement with three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, then adding water in an amount that yields a flow (efflux) of ten to fifteen seconds; this water content governs the paste demand and workability while allowing the mix to expand up to ten percent and bleed no more than 2.5 % of its volume, which controls how the grout bonds to adjacent slab material, and a multiphase wetting agent and an expansive agent may be used to improve the grout’s interaction with surrounding substrates, with an accelerator permissible if required to shorten the initial setting time. In slabjacking under Section 681.2, cement grout is also formulated with one part cement combined with three parts supplementary cementitious material, but water is added only to achieve an efflux time of sixteen seconds to twenty‑six seconds, although a more fluid mix with an efflux of ten seconds to fifteen seconds may be used for the initial injection; the water‑to‑binder proportion controls workability and the grout’s ability to fill voids, optional admixtures can be incorporated if accepted, further modifying binder‑aggregate bonding or setting characteristics, and if the prescribed mix does not generate sufficient lift a stiffer mixture is recommended, reflecting the need to adjust the interaction between the grout and the surrounding slab. For bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavement under Section 540.3, cement grout is applied to cracks larger than three‑quarters inch where the specified non‑shrink grout fills the void and creates a bond between the existing pavement surface and the new concrete overlay; it may also be placed as a premixed, non‑shrink grout to fill core holes, consolidating with the surrounding course material. [1][2]

The specification requires that any deleterious material be removed before placing the cement grout overlay because oil, grease, moisture or standing water can prevent proper bonding of the non‑shrink grout to the prepared surface. Accordingly, the milled asphalt must be completely cleaned, protected from oil or grease drippings, and kept free of standing water prior to grout placement. The source does not discuss compatibility issues related to sulfates, organics, clays, reactive aggregates, dissimilar materials, corrosion, shrinkage restraint, or chemical attack. [2]

Influence of production and placement

Proper mixing of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and any admixtures produces a fluid grout that can be pumped into slab‑subbase voids, thereby restoring full structural support and reducing deflection and the risk of faulting or cracking. The Pennsylvania specification requires that the dry ingredients be measured accurately and batched immediately with water, adjusting the water so that the efflux time falls within 10 seconds to 15 seconds as measured by ASTM C939; this control of workability is mandatory for slab‑stabilization grout. For slab‑jacking the mix is defined by a volume ratio of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material, and water is added until the efflux time is between 16 seconds and 26 seconds, although a more fluid mix with an efflux time of 10 seconds to 15 seconds may be used for the initial injection; if that mix fails to raise the slab a stiffer mix may be adopted. The mixed grout may not be held in the mixer or injection‑pump sump for more than one hour, and flowability measurements must be performed at least twice each work shift. A trial batch prepared at least 28 days before slab installation under the same time and temperature conditions is required so that cylinders tested per PTM No. 521 demonstrate a minimum early‑age compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic for full‑depth precast slabs, or at least 1,000 psi before traffic and 2,500 psi after 12 hours for bedding grout. The stabilization mix must also meet dimensional‑stability limits of 0 % to 10 % expansion, an initial setting time of 1 hour to 6 hours, bleed no more than 2.5 % of the volume, and a laboratory‑verified 7‑day compressive strength of at least 700 psi. During placement the grout is pumped first at the lowest point and the slab is lifted in increments; this incremental lifting helps keep stresses low and prevents cracking, and the rate of pumping is reduced as gauge blocks approach the reference string line, stopping when they touch. The grout must be capable of being pumped into dowel slots, tie‑bar slots, lifting ports, or voids beneath slabs, and upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed. Injection pressure shall not exceed 200 psi unless otherwise directed; continuous pressures may be limited to 200 psi, brief pressures up to 300 psi are permitted as directed, and pressures up to 600 psi for short periods are allowed when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade. The pumping pattern must raise continuously reinforced slabs to within ±0.02 ft of the string‑line grade or jointed slabs to within ±0.03 ft, and overjacking beyond 0.10 ft triggers grinding or replacement. After placement the grout is consolidated with a spud vibrator or other acceptable vibration/tamping method to eliminate voids and enhance strength. For bonded concrete overlays the cement‑based overlay is applied after the milled asphalt surface has been cleaned and is below 120 °F; finishing and texturing are completed before curing materials are applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation at a rate of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 square feet. A minimum curing period of 12 hours before reopening to traffic is required for slab stabilization, and the curing operation must maintain temperature control so that the repair area does not experience a temperature change exceeding 40 °F within any one‑hour period; insulation blankets are provided when air temperature is 45 °F or less and may be removed only when the surface temperature change does not exceed 40 °F per hour or the average compressive strength exceeds 3,000 psi. Water used for mixing or curing must conform to ASTM C1602 and be reasonably clean, free from vegetable matter, oil, acid, alkali, sugar, or other injurious substances; using contaminated water can impair strength development and durability. Selection, specification, and improvement

In slab‑stabilization projects the primary driver for choosing cement grout is the prevailing site conditions, which may make grout more advantageous than alternatives such as hot asphalt or high‑density polyurethane, and the required traffic reopening time because the grout must cure before vehicles can be allowed back on the pavement; this curing period can be up to 12 hours depending on the mix design [1]. The same considerations apply to slabjacking, where the subgrade characteristics, drainage, size and depth of the depression, amount of correction required, and proximity to joints or cracks determine the suitability of grout, while high ambient temperatures may render grout‑based lifting impractical [1]. Both the policy manual and the state specifications require a minimum 12‑hour cure before the slab can be reopened to traffic after grout placement [1][2]. For slab stabilization the specification mandates a mix proportion of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, with water adjusted so that the efflux time measured by ASTM C939 falls between ten and fifteen seconds [2]. The grout must exhibit an expansion ranging from 0 % to 10 %, an initial setting time of one to six hours, bleed no more than 2.5 % of its volume, and achieve a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi based on the average of five test cylinders [2]. The mix design, including independent laboratory results for 1‑, 3‑ and 7‑day compressive strengths, flowability, shrinkage/expansion and initial set time, must be submitted to the district for review and approval prior to installation [2]. When cement grout is used as dowel grout in full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slabs it must be pumpable into the required slots and attain a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before the slabs are opened to traffic [2]. If the material serves as bedding grout the mix must reach at least 1,000 psi before traffic loading and 2,500 psi within twelve hours [2]. A trial batch mixed at least twenty‑eight days before installation under the same time and temperature conditions expected on site must be tested independently, and the same mix used for the demonstration must be employed for the actual work [2]. Work may commence only after it is satisfactorily shown that qualified personnel possessing a minimum of five years’ experience are available [2]. Daytime temperatures must not be below 40 °F for slab‑stabilization operations, and ambient air and surface temperatures must be 40 °F or higher and rising for partial‑depth repairs; placement must be stopped if temperatures fall below this limit [2]. Joints or cracks are selected for stabilization when the loaded slab corner deflection is 0.02 inch or greater together with a joint efficiency of 65 % or more (or less than 65 % when approved by the representative) [2]. Grout injection holes may not exceed a diameter of 1½ inches, mixed material may not be held for more than one hour, pump speed must be between 800 and 2000 rpm, and continuous injection pressure is limited to 200 psi, with short‑term pressures allowed up to 300 psi only as directed and brief spikes up to 600 psi permissible when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade [2]. Upward movement of the pavement during injection may not exceed 0.05 inch, and the slab cannot be opened to traffic for less than twelve hours after grouting operations have been completed [2][1]. The amount of grout must be controlled so that the lifted slab ends within a tolerance of ±0.02 ft for continuously reinforced concrete pavements or ±0.03 ft for jointed slabs, and any over‑jacking greater than 0.10 ft triggers removal and replacement of the pavement [2]. For partial‑depth repairs the grout must be mechanically mixed and applied as a thin brushed or scrubbed coating on all freshly cleaned exposed surfaces, taking care that it does not run or puddle in low spots; concrete may be placed only while the grout remains wet or an epoxy bonding compound stays tacky [2]. When existing cracks exceed a width greater than ¾ inch in a bonded concrete overlay, the selected cement grout must be capable of providing a bond to the overlay and only non‑shrink grout or other material approved by the Department may be used [2]. For estimating purposes the policy manual recommends using 0.40 bags of cement per hole when performing slabjacking [1].

In the slab‑stabilization treatment (section 4.5) described in the Pavement Policy Manual, performance of cement grout may be improved by incorporating supplementary cementitious material and, where appropriate, admixtures into the mix to obtain a fluid grout that can be pumped beneath the slab; quality control calls for prohibiting traffic on the slab until the grout has cured, which can be 12 hours depending on the mix design, and it should be accompanied by joint and crack sealing together with good subsurface drainage to limit water entry[1]. When site conditions favor other treatments, hot‑asphalt undersealing may be used, but traffic must not be opened until the asphalt cools, which can be a minimum of 30 minutes after pumping[1], or a two‑component high‑density polyurethane may be applied, which expands and hardens after a minimum of 30 minutes[1]. In the slab‑jacking procedure (section 4.6) the same approach of adding supplementary cementitious materials and possibly admixtures enhances fluidity and overall performance; the grout still requires a cure period of up to 12 hours before traffic is permitted, whereas the high‑density polyurethane alternative—provided as a two‑component low‑viscosity system—penetrates voids more readily, resists water and chemical attack, and reaches sufficient hardness after a minimum of 30 minutes[1].

Improving cement‑grout performance in Pennsylvania specifications begins with strict adherence to the manufacturer‑specified mix design and the use of prepackaged material that can be pumped into the required slots; for dowel grout the final mix must attain a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic loading, while bedding grout must reach at least 1,000 psi before opening to traffic and 2,500 psi within twelve hours[2]. A trial batch is required to be mixed at least twenty‑eight days before slab installation under the same time and temperature conditions expected on site, with independent laboratory testing of cylinders (or cubes for cement‑treated bedding) to verify that flowability and compressive strength meet the prescribed thresholds, after which the proven mix is used for actual placement[2]. Where appropriate, alternative materials such as epoxy grout, polyurethane bedding grout, asphalt grout or high‑density polyurethane grout may be specified in lieu of conventional cement grout provided they satisfy the same performance criteria[2].

For slab stabilization under the Pennsylvania specifications, dry ingredients shall be weighed rather than loosely measured by volume and the mixed grout must be placed within one hour after mixing; flowability measurements are required at least twice each shift, lift monitoring must ensure that upward pavement movement does not exceed 0.05 inch and pressure is kept below 200 psi during injection, holes shall be drilled no larger than 1½ inches using vertical smooth‑walled cores, and a minimum of twelve hours curing before traffic reopens further reduces the risk of cracking or displacement[2]. The mix may be modified by adding a multiphase wetting agent and an expansive agent, and an accelerator may be used if required; the proportion shall be one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, with water adjusted so that the efflux time is within ten seconds to fifteen seconds (ASTM C939) for many applications, while other sections permit a water‑efflux range of sixteen seconds to twenty‑six seconds and allow a more fluid mix with an efflux time of ten seconds to fifteen seconds for the initial injection in each hole, or a stiffer mix if the standard blend fails to raise the slab[2]. Quality controls require submission of the mix design for review, independent laboratory testing of one‑, three‑ and seven‑day compressive strengths, flowability, shrinkage and expansion, and initial set time; water shall be controlled to meet the specified efflux times, expansion limited to 0 %–10 %, bleed not to exceed 2.5 % of the volume, and a seven‑day compressive strength of at least 700 psi based on the average of five test cylinders[2].

In partial‑depth repairs, performance is enhanced by first applying a thin mechanically mixed layer of grout or an epoxy bonding compound to all exposed surfaces, which improves adhesion before concrete placement; the concrete must be placed while the grout is still wet or the epoxy tacky and only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F or greater and rising, with repairs protected from freezing for at least twenty‑four hours[2]. Curing is improved by applying a white membrane‑forming curing compound with an atomizing spray tank, providing insulation or heating if the ambient temperature drops below 80 °F, and monitoring the repair area at least hourly to ensure that the curing temperature does not change more than forty degrees Fahrenheit within any one‑hour period; when faster strength gain is required, a rapid‑set concrete patching material may be used as an alternative[2].

For slabjacking, a prescribed field quality‑control plan requires submission of existing roadway profiles and a post‑stabilization profile report so that the Representative can direct additional injection if the achieved grade deviates from the target; during mixing and jacking pressures shall be limited to continuous 200 psi, with short‑term increases to 300 psi only as directed and brief spikes to 600 psi permitted only when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade[2]. Overjacking must be kept within a tolerance of 0.02–0.03 ft of the string‑line grade, and any lift exceeding 0.10 ft triggers removal and replacement of the pavement; high‑density polyurethane grout may be substituted where cement grout’s limitations are a concern[2].

In bonded concrete overlays, performance is enhanced by a comprehensive quality‑control plan that documents surface preparation, mixing, hauling, placement timing, temperature monitoring, joint sawing, systematic sampling and strength testing before opening to traffic; cracks greater than three‑quarters of an inch shall be filled with non‑shrink grout approved by the Department to promote reliable bonding, and where transitions rest on treated base or existing concrete a geotextile debonding layer may be placed as an alternative interface[2]. Curing is improved by applying a white‑opaque curing compound at a minimum rate of one gallon (± 0.1 gallon) per 125 ft² in a single coat or split before and after sawing, while insulating blankets are used when air temperature is 45 °F or less; removal of the blankets shall be controlled so that the rate of temperature change at the concrete surface does not exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit within any one‑hour period, and concrete strength must exceed 3,000 psi[2].

The contract also incorporates quality‑control measures such as a mandatory deflection test, counting each drilled hole, and a payment provision that reduces the number of cement bags by one for every five linear feet of radial cracking observed; the unit price includes an accelerator if required[2].

Performance evaluation and implications

For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529) the contractor must prepare at least one trial batch of grout at least 28 days before slab placement under the same time and temperature expected during actual work, submit independent laboratory results showing that grout cylinders meet the required compressive strengths—2,500 psi for dowel grout before traffic opening and, for cementitious bedding grout, at least 1,000 psi before traffic and 2,500 psi within 12 hours—and demonstrate pumpability into slots or voids; when setting bolts are employed an early strength of 50 psi must be verified. For slab‑stabilization grout (Section 679.2) independent laboratory testing submitted with the mix design must include 1‑day, 3‑day and 7‑day compressive strengths, flowability, shrinkage and expansion results, and the time of initial set; the grout must have an expansion between 0 % and 10 %, an initial setting time between 1 hour and 6 hours, bleed no more than 2.5 % of its volume, and a minimum 7‑day compressive strength of 700 psi based on the average of five test cylinders. Field verification for slab stabilization (Section 679.3) requires a detailed QC plan and deflection testing in accordance with ASTM D4695 when air temperature is between 40 °F and 70 °F, using a loaded‑slab corner deflection criterion of at least 0.02 inch and a joint‑efficiency threshold of 65 %; vertical movement must be monitored with lift‑measurement equipment capable of resolving changes to within 0.001 inch and any upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited during injection; flowability of the grout mix must be measured at least twice each work shift, pressure is limited to 200 psi, a retest is required 24 hours after grouting, and slabs that still deflect ≥ 0.02 inch are re‑grouted before acceptance. The department evaluates each slab‑stabilization job by conducting a deflection test, counting every hole drilled, and recording the quantity of bags of cement used as part of the measurement. For partial‑depth repairs (Section 525.3 f) performance is confirmed by curing test cylinders in the laboratory under the same conditions applied to the field repair while hourly temperature monitoring ensures that the cure temperature does not change more than 40 °F within any one‑hour period; after placement the repaired sections are resounded in the presence of the Representative and any delaminated areas must be replaced at no cost to the Department. In slabjacking (Section 681.2) evaluation relies on a laboratory test of flowability measured by the efflux time specified in ASTM C939, with the mix required to achieve an efflux time between 16 seconds and 26 seconds, although a more fluid mix with an efflux time of 10 seconds to 15 seconds may be used for the initial injection in each hole. Field performance documentation for slabjacking (Section 681.3) requires that existing roadway profiles showing depressions and the proposed grade line be provided, and that a Roadway Profile Report with surveyed profile drawings of the existing grade, intended grade, and grade actually achieved after grout injection be submitted before work acceptance; no laboratory testing or long‑term performance criteria are specified in this section. According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10) in slab stabilization (section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION), a fluid mixture of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and possibly admixtures is pumped beneath the concrete to fill voids at the slab/subbase interface; when the voids are filled sufficiently full support is restored, which reduces slab deflections and lessens the likelihood of pumping, joint faulting, corner breaks and diagonal cracking, thereby preserving structural integrity even though it does not increase the pavement’s design structural capacity. The manual estimates one cubic foot of grout per hole (0.25 bag of cement per hole) and requires that the slab remain closed to traffic until the cement grout cures, which can be twelve hours depending on the mix design. Because restored support mitigates future settlement, joint and crack sealing together with good subsurface drainage are required to keep water out and maintain the benefit of stabilization. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications (Publication 408/2026) require that cement grout used for slab stabilization achieve a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi, with a prescribed mix of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material plus any approved admixtures and water adjusted so that the efflux time is between ten and fifteen seconds, limiting bleed to no more than 2.5 % of the volume and allowing an expansion of 0 % to 10 %. The same specifications limit the initial setting time to one to six hours and permit placement only when daytime temperatures are above 40 °F and the subgrade is not frozen; mixed grout must be placed within one hour of mixing, flowability checked at least twice each shift, and injection pressure may not exceed two hundred pounds per square inch. During slab stabilization any upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited, especially for joints showing a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more, and after grouting the pavement must remain closed to traffic for a minimum of twelve hours before service loads resume. In slabjacking (section 4.6 SLABJACKING) the same type of fluid cement grout is pumped beneath the pavement to fill voids and raise slabs; its setting time directly influences construction scheduling and maintenance downtime because traffic should not be opened until the cement grout cures, which can be twelve hours depending on the mix design. Properly executed grout placement restores structural integrity and rideability, while improper execution may induce cracking, tilting or faulting that degrade pavement performance. The manual advises designing hole patterns and estimates use of 0.40 bags of cement per hole to guide material quantity and ensure adequate support during the lift; if done correctly slabjacking can be more economical than slab replacement and is usually completed in less time with minimal interference to traffic. The Pennsylvania specifications prescribe that cement grout used for slabjacking be proportioned as one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material, with water adjusted so that the efflux time falls between sixteen and twenty‑six seconds, although a more fluid mix with an efflux of ten to fifteen seconds may be employed for the first injection in each hole. Slabjacking grout may be injected under continuous pressures up to two hundred pounds per square inch; higher pressures of three hundred pounds per square inch are allowed only briefly and up to six hundred pounds per square inch in short bursts when the slab is bonded to the subgrade. By regulating the injection pattern and volume, the grout can raise reinforced concrete slabs to within 0.02 foot (or 0.03 foot for jointed slabs) of the specified string‑line grade, and any lift exceeding the overjacking limit of 0.10 foot requires removal and replacement of the pavement. For transverse dowels and longitudinal tie‑bar slots the specifications require a grout that can be pumped into the slots and attain a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before the slabs are opened to traffic, thereby securing load‑transfer elements and helping prevent premature cracking under service loads. Cementitious bedding grout placed beneath slabs must be pumpable into voids and reach at least 1,000 psi before opening to traffic and increase to 2,500 psi in twelve hours, providing rapid early strength that supports slab elevation and limits settlement; a trial batch must be mixed at least twenty‑eight days before installation and independent laboratory test results of grout cylinders are required. The specifications also require that cracks exceeding three‑quarters of an inch be cleaned and filled with non‑shrink cement grout or another approved material before a concrete overlay is placed, creating a reliable bond that helps the pavement resist premature cracking and maintain load transfer. After final cleaning, a thin layer of grout or epoxy bonding compound must be applied to all exposed surfaces within the repair area while it remains tacky, and the repair concrete must be placed before the grout dries; placement is limited to ambient air and surface temperatures of at least forty degrees Fahrenheit, with the repaired area protected from freezing for a minimum of twenty‑four hours, and curing temperature changes may not exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit within any one‑hour period.
