Cement Grout

Part of: Slab Stabilization is-subtopic-of
Cement Grout is a topic under Slab Stabilization.
cement-groutfluid-cement-grout
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Cement grout is a pumpable, fluid mixture of Portland cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and, when required, admixtures that is prepared on‑site to a prescribed efflux time so it remains flowable for injection beneath concrete slabs [1][2]. It is placed in voids at the slab‑subbase interface or in drilled holes to fill gaps, bond the slab to its supporting base and, in slab‑jacking operations, lift the pavement to the desired grade, thereby restoring full structural support and reducing deflection, joint faulting and cracking [1]. After placement the grout cures to develop early compressive strength—typically at least 1,000 psi before traffic and 2,500 psi within 12 hours for bedding applications, or 2,500 psi for dowel‑slot grout—providing the load‑bearing capacity needed for service [2].

Material purpose and applicability

Cement grout is a fluid mixture of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and, when required, admixtures that is pumped beneath the concrete slab. In slab‑stabilization it is injected to fill voids at the slab/subbase interface, thereby restoring full support to the slab and reducing deflections that cause pumping, joint faulting and cracking, which returns the pavement’s structural integrity although it does not increase the design structural capacity. In slabjacking the same fluid grout is pumped into drilled holes beneath a concrete slab; by filling the voids and lifting the slab to the desired profile it restores the structural integrity and rideability of the pavement. Cement grout is a cementitious material that may be supplied as a pumpable bedding grout, a specialized dowel‑slot grout, a stabilization slurry, or a non‑shrink grout depending on the pavement application. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs the grout is mixed to the manufacturer’s recommendations and placed in voids beneath the slabs or in transverse dowel and longitudinal tie‑bar slots; it must fill those spaces and develop sufficient strength to support traffic loads, with the bedding grout required to attain a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi before the slab is opened to traffic and 2,500 psi within 12 hours, while the dowel‑slot grout must achieve at least 2,500 psi before traffic. In slab stabilization the grout is prepared on site by accurately measuring dry cementitious materials and batching them with water, then injected as a slurry into vertically drilled holes; it must fill voids beneath the slab, bond the concrete to the underlying base, and lock the slab in place, with placement required to be completed within one hour of mixing, upward movement of the pavement may not exceed 0.05 inch, injection pressure may not exceed 200 psi, and the mix is defined as one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume with enough water to give an efflux time of 10–15 seconds; it must attain a 7‑day compressive strength of at least 700 psi, set within 1 to 6 hours, and limit bleed to no more than 2.5 % of its volume. For partial‑depth repairs of rigid concrete pavements the grout is a cement‑based bonding material mixed according to prescribed initial weight proportions, mechanically mixed and applied as a thin layer by brushing or scrubbing onto all cleaned, exposed surfaces within the repair area, must be placed before it begins to dry (or while an epoxy bonding compound remains tacky), and must be placed within 60 minutes of mix completion, serving as a fill or patching material that restores the repaired portion. In slabjacking applications the grout is a volume‑based blend of one part cement and three parts supplementary cementitious material with optional admixtures; water is adjusted so that the efflux time falls within 16 to 26 seconds, although a more fluid mix achieving an efflux time of 10 to 15 seconds may be used for the initial injection, and it is pumped through an expanding rubber packer inserted into drilled holes beneath the pavement; its primary function is to stabilize the base and raise the pavement to the required grade by jacking the slab until the surface is within 0.02 ft (or 0.03 ft for jointed slabs) of a string‑line grade, with continuous pressures allowed up to 200 psi, short‑term pressures to 300 psi, and brief spikes to 600 psi when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade, and any jacking that exceeds 0.10 ft must be removed and replaced. When a bonded concrete overlay is placed over an asphalt‑surfaced pavement, non‑shrink cement grout is used as a filling material to clean and fill cracks wider than 3/4 inch so that a reliable bond can be achieved between the existing pavement and the new concrete overlay. [1][2]

Composition and characteristics

In the Pavement Policy Manual’s slab‑stabilization section 4.5, cement grout is described as a fluid mixture composed of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and, when required, admixtures; it is pumped beneath the concrete slab to fill voids while remaining flowable enough for injection and then hardens after curing, which can be 12 hours depending on the mix design [1]. The same manual’s slabjacking section 4.6 defines the grout similarly as a fluid mixture that typically contains cement, water, supplementary cementitious materials and possibly admixtures, forming a pumpable slurry placed beneath the pavement; after placement the grout must cure before traffic can return, which can be 12 hours depending on the specific mix design [1]. For estimating purposes the slab‑stabilization guidance suggests about 1 cubic foot of grout per hole, equivalent to roughly 0.25 bag of cement per hole, whereas the slabjacking guidance calls for about 0.40 bags of cement per hole [1]. In the Pennsylvania DOT specifications for slab‑stabilization (Section 679), grout is defined as a freshly mixed slurry produced by accurately measuring the dry constituents and water, which must be used within one hour of mixing [2]. The material is placed through vertical round holes that are not larger than 1½ inches in diameter; flowability is checked each shift, injection pressure may not exceed 200 pounds per square inch, and upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch will not be allowed [2]. The grout composition for slab stabilization consists of Portland cement, water, optional admixtures such as a multiphase wetting agent, expansive agent or accelerator, supplementary cementitious material, and a rapid‑set concrete patching product from an approved manufacturer [2]. It is proportioned at one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, with enough water to give a flow efflux time of 10 seconds to 15 seconds as measured by ASTM C 939 [2]. The mix must be designed to expand between 0 % and 10 % (ASTM C 940), to set initially within 1 hour to 6 hours (AASHTO T 131), to bleed no more than 2.5 % of its volume, and to achieve a minimum 7‑day compressive strength of 700 pounds per square inch as measured on five test cylinders [2]. In slabjacking applications the same volumetric blend of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material is used; admixtures are added only if required and accepted, and water is mixed in an amount that yields an efflux time of 16 seconds to 26 seconds (ASTM C 939), although a more fluid mix achieving an efflux time of 10 seconds to 15 seconds may be used for the initial injection, with a stiffer mix employed if the standard mixture fails to raise the pavement slab [2].

Within the Pavement Policy Manual, cement grout is described as a mixture of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material, and possibly admixtures; the inclusion of these additional constituents modifies both workability and strength potential. In slab stabilization (section 4.5) the setting time can be up to twelve hours and varies with the mix design, indicating that changes in proportioning directly affect cure time and consequently site suitability for using this cement‑based grout. Similarly, in slabjacking (section 4.6) adding supplementary cementitious materials or admixtures produces a more fluid mixture that can be pumped under the slab, and the proportioning of these ingredients influences the cure time, which may also be up to twelve hours depending on the mix design. If high‑density polyurethane is selected instead of cement, the grout’s viscosity is reduced, allowing better penetration into voids; the two‑component material expands to form a lightweight, rigid, closed‑cell foam that resists water and chemical attack while providing long‑term durability. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania specifications require that cement grout be mixed exactly as the manufacturer recommends and that a trial batch be produced under the same time and temperature conditions expected in the field; this trial must demonstrate that the mix can be pumped and achieve the prescribed strengths—2,500 psi for transverse dowel grout before traffic and 1,000 psi (rising to 2,500 psi in twelve hours) for cementitious bedding grout—so any alteration in composition, source material, or proportioning must be validated. Section 679.3 specifies that consistent grout performance depends on precise proportioning of the dry constituents and water, with dry materials measured by mass or supplied in uniform‑volume sacks before batching, and water measured through a meter or scale; mixed material may not remain in the mixer or pump sump for more than one hour after mixing because longer residence can impair workability, and flowability measurements are required at least twice each work shift to keep pavement lift below 0.05 inch and pressure under 200 psi during injection. Section 679.2 mandates a grout mix of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, with water adjusted so that efflux occurs in ten to fifteen seconds; the resulting grout must expand no more than 0 % to 10 %, set within one hour to six hours, bleed less than 2.5 % of its volume, and achieve a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi based on five cylinders, and when the source of any material is changed a new mix design must be submitted for district review to verify that these thresholds are still met. In slabjacking (section 681.2) variations in the cement‑to‑supplementary material ratio, water content and optional admixtures directly control the grout’s flow and lifting ability; the prescribed blend uses one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume with water adjusted so that the efflux time falls between 16 seconds and 26 seconds per ASTM C 939, but a more fluid grout may be used for the first injection if water is increased to achieve an efflux of ten to fifteen seconds, while a stiffer mix may be required when the standard blend does not raise the slab, and admixtures are permitted only when they are accepted, allowing further tailoring of strength or workability. [1][2]

Key material properties

Cement grout must remain sufficiently fluid to be pumped beneath concrete slabs, which is achieved by a mixture of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material, and possibly admixtures that produce a fluid grout mixture. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs the grout’s mechanical performance is governed primarily by its compressive strength, requiring a minimum of 2,500 psi before traffic loading and at least 1,000 psi before opening with an increase to 2,500 psi within 12 hours. Early strength development is also required for setting‑bolt applications, where the grout must attain at least 50 psi before the slab is set to elevation. A seven‑day compressive strength of not less than 700 psi, measured as the average of five cylinders, is mandated as a minimum mechanical property. Physical workability is expressed through flowability criteria; trial batches prepared under the same time and temperature conditions expected during installation must demonstrate the required flow, and flowability measurements are to be taken at least twice each work shift. The mixed grout may not be retained in the mixer or pump sump for more than one hour after mixing in order to preserve its workability. For stabilization mixes the efflux time must fall between 10 seconds and 15 seconds (ASTM C‑939), while slabjacking mixes require an efflux time of 16 seconds to 26 seconds, with a more fluid mix of 10 seconds to 15 seconds permissible for the initial injection in each hole. Expansion of the grout is limited to a maximum of 10 percent and may be as low as 0 percent; the initial set must occur within a window of 1 hour to 6 hours, and bleeding shall not exceed 2.5 percent of the volume. Chemical composition for slabjacking grout is specified as one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, with water content adjusted to achieve the prescribed efflux times. Thermal considerations require that placement occur only when ambient air and surface temperatures are at least 40 °F and rising; placement must be stopped if temperatures fall below this threshold, and the repaired area must be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours. After placement, the grout must develop sufficient cure strength before traffic can be reopened, which may require about 12 hours depending on the mix design. The grout must be capable of sustaining continuous injection pressures of 200 psi, with pressures of 300 psi allowed only for short periods and brief spikes to 600 psi permissible when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–93); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 418–428), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 587–598)

Laboratory verification of cement grout performance in Section 529 requires that specimens be prepared under the same time‑and‑temperature conditions as field placement; dowel and tie‑bar grout cylinders are cast and tested according to PTM No. 521 to confirm that the mix can be pumped into the slots and attains a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before the slabs are opened to traffic, while bedding grout must also demonstrate at least 1,000 psi before opening and 2,500 psi after 12 hours, and cement‑treated bedding material is evaluated with standard concrete cubes tested per AASHTO T 106. In the slab‑stabilization work of Section 679, vertical‑movement devices that resolve changes to within 0.001 inch are used to monitor slab lift and any upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited; the crew records flowability of the fresh grout at least twice each shift, pressure in the grout line is continuously observed and must not exceed a maximum pressure of 200 psi unless otherwise directed, and dry ingredients are weighed by mass while water is metered or scaled to control mix proportions. The mix design for cement grout stabilization must be supported by independent laboratory testing that quantifies 1‑day, 3‑day and 7‑day compressive strengths, flowability, shrinkage/expansion and the time of initial set; flowability is evaluated by efflux time and shall fall within 10 seconds to 15 seconds (ASTM C 939), expansion is limited to 0 % to 10 %, bleed may not exceed 2.5 % of the volume (both ASTM C 940), the initial setting time must be between 1 hour and 6 hours (AASHTO T 131), and a minimum 7‑day compressive strength of 700 psi is required based on the average of five test cylinders. The specification in Section 525 classifies cement grout used for partial‑depth repairs into three categories—Class AA Cement Concrete, Modified; Class AA Cement Concrete, Accelerated; and Rapid‑Set Concrete Patching Material. Performance of the cement grout in Section 679 is quantified through a deflection test performed for each installation, an individual count of holes drilled, and recording the quantity of grout material by the number of bags of cement used; when radial cracking exceeds five linear feet, the department reduces payment by one bag of cement per that length. In slabjacking provisions of Section 681, consistency of cement grout is quantified by its flow or efflux time measured according to ASTM C 939, and the mix must be adjusted so that the efflux time falls between 16 seconds and 26 seconds for normal placement, while a more fluid blend may target an efflux time of 10 seconds to 15 seconds when initially injecting into each hole. The cement grout stabilization process is evaluated by measuring injection pressure and the resulting pavement lift; continuous pressures may be up to 200 psi, pressures of 300 psi are allowed only for short periods as directed, brief rises to 600 psi are permitted when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade, and the pavement must be raised within 0.02 foot plus or minus from a string line grade (or within 0.03 foot for jointed slabs), with any overjacking greater than 0.10 foot triggering grinding or replacement. [2]

Behavior and mechanisms

In SECTION 529—FULL DEPTH PRECAST/PRESTRESSED CONCRETE PAVEMENT SLABS the specification requires cement grout placed in transverse dowel slots, longitudinal tie‑bar slots and lifting insert ports to be pumpable and to attain a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before the slabs are opened to traffic, indicating it must resist service loading only after reaching that strength. For cementitious bedding grout the same section requires at least 1,000 psi before opening to traffic and an increase to 2,500 psi within 12 hours, providing a defined load‑bearing capacity as curing progresses. The source does not describe any performance changes due to moisture, temperature variations, chemical exposure, or other service conditions. In SECTION 681—SLABJACKING, during cement grout stabilization for slabjacking the grout can sustain continuous pressures to 200 psi; pressures to 300 psi may be applied only for short periods as directed, and brief pressure rises to 600 psi are permitted when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade, defining its load‑bearing behavior under jacking operations. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 426–428), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 597–598)

In slab stabilization the behavior of cement grout is first governed by its physical ability to flow as a fluid mixture of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and admixtures so that it can be pumped beneath the concrete slab. In slabjacking the physical condition of the pavement influences grout performance because high temperatures cause slab expansion that places joints in compression, making the procedure impractical. Chemically, the grout must cure before traffic may be opened; the curing period can be 12 hours, depending on the mix design. Mechanically, once cured the grout restores full support at the slab‑subbase interface, reducing deflections and thereby limiting further pumping, faulting and cracking. Mechanically, the fluid mixture is pumped under the slab and lifting should be done in increments to minimize slab stresses and avoid cracking; extrusion from joints or cracks indicates the need for additional holes. In Section 529 the behavior of cement grout is governed by its hydraulic chemistry, which must develop sufficient compressive strength as it cures, and by its physical flowability that permits pumping into dowel slots or voids. The final mix must be pumpable and attain a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before the slabs are opened to traffic for transverse dowels and tie‑bar slots, while bedding grout must be pumpable, reach at least 1,000 psi before opening and increase to 2,500 psi within 12 hours. During grout injection in slab stabilization the upward movement of the pavement must not exceed 0.05 inch and the pressure in the grout line is limited to a maximum of 200 psi, which together control how the grout expands into voids. The mixed material may not be retained in the mixer or pump sump for more than one hour after mixing and flowability measurements are required at least twice each shift to keep the rheology within acceptable bounds. Holes are drilled vertical, round and not larger than 1½ inches in diameter, providing a physical conduit that defines placement while the injection pump operates between 800 and 2000 rpm to deliver grout at the prescribed rate. Slab lift is monitored with equipment capable of detecting movement to within 0.001 inch. The behavior of cement grout in slab stabilization is also controlled by mix proportions of one part cement combined with three parts supplementary cementitious material and by adding enough water so that the efflux time falls within 10 seconds to 15 seconds, which together set flowability, limit bleed to no more than 2.5 % of the volume and restrict expansion to between 0 % and 10 %. Chemical admixtures such as a multiphase wetting agent, an expansive agent, or an accelerator may be used to adjust the initial setting time, which must lie between 1 hour and 6 hours. The resulting mechanical performance is required to achieve a minimum 7‑day compressive strength of 700 psi as verified by independent laboratory testing of five cylinders. In partial‑depth repair the performance of cement grout is governed by temperature conditions, the timing of concrete placement relative to grout setting, and mechanical consolidation; grout or epoxy bonding compound must be applied immediately after final cleaning while ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F and rising, placement must be discontinued if the temperatures fall below 40 °F, and repairs must be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours. The mixture is consolidated with a spud vibrator, tamping, or other acceptable vibration method to achieve proper density. In slabjacking the behavior of cement grout is controlled by its mix proportions and resulting fluidity; a blend of one part cement with three parts supplementary cementitious material provides the chemical basis for hydration and strength, and water is added so that the efflux time falls within 16 seconds to 26 seconds, giving a predictable viscosity for injection. A more fluid mix may be used initially when the efflux time is limited to 10 seconds to 15 seconds to improve penetration. If this blend does not raise the slab, a stiffer mix—adjusted by water or admixtures—is employed to increase the mechanical pressure on the pavement. The performance of cement grout during stabilization is governed primarily by mechanical hydraulic pressures and placement constraints; continuous pressures are limited to 200 psi, while higher pressures up to 300 psi may be used only for short periods and brief spikes to 600 psi are permitted when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade. The grout must be injected without extending the packer below the lower surface of the concrete pavement. The volume pumped is controlled so that the raised pavement stays within a tolerance of 0.02 foot (or 0.03 foot for jointed slabs) of the specified grade line. [1][2]

Durability and deterioration

Cement grout used in partial‑depth repairs is vulnerable to freeze damage; the procedure requires protecting the repaired area from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours. Placement occurs only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F or greater, and placement is discontinued if the ambient air and surface temperatures fall below 40 °F. If grout begins to dry before concrete is placed, it is removed, the surface re‑cleaned, and fresh grout reapplied, indicating that premature drying can compromise bond quality. Additionally, grout or epoxy bonding compound must not run or puddle in low spots, as pooling could create weak zones. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 418)

Water that enters the pavement contributes to pumping and void formation, which can accelerate deterioration of cement grout used for slab stabilization; therefore joint and crack sealing must be performed in conjunction with slab stabilization and subsurface drains should be kept in good condition to reduce water ingress and lower susceptibility to degradation [1]. A high‑density polyurethane grout is described as resistant to water and chemical attack and has long‑term durability, indicating that resistance to moisture and chemicals reduces the risk of deterioration [1]. In partial‑depth repair, exposure of the grout to ambient air or surface temperatures below 40 °F or allowing it to freeze before it has cured increases its susceptibility to deterioration [2]. Maintaining ambient air and surface temperature at 40 °F or higher while the grout remains tacky and protecting the repaired area from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours reduces that susceptibility [2]. The specification also requires that grout must be placed within 60 minutes of mix completion, implying that delayed placement would increase its vulnerability [2].

Material interactions and compatibility

Cement grout is defined as a fluid mixture of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and possibly admixtures that is pumped beneath a concrete slab to fill the voids at the slab/subbase interface, thereby restoring full support, reducing slab deflection and limiting further pumping, faulting or cracking. In this application the interaction is essentially mechanical as the grout bonds the slab to the underlying base, re‑establishing structural continuity. For slab stabilization the specification requires a volumetric ratio of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material, with water added only until the mix attains an efflux time between 10 and 15 seconds, which controls workability and paste demand. Admixtures such as a multiphase wetting agent or expansive agent may be incorporated to modify wetting characteristics and permit expansion from 0 % up to 10 %, while the grout must limit bleeding to no more than 2.5 % of its volume, set within an initial setting time of 1 hour to 6 hours, and develop at least 700 psi compressive strength after seven days. When used for partial‑depth repair the grout is applied as a thin bonding layer on cleaned existing concrete surfaces, adhering to the old cement matrix and providing a fresh interface for the new repair concrete; placement of the new concrete must occur while the grout remains wet or the epoxy bonding compound stays tacky, otherwise the hardened grout must be removed and the surface re‑cleaned before recoating. In slabjacking the same one‑part‑cement to three‑parts‑SCM ratio is used, but water may be adjusted so that the efflux time falls within 16 to 26 seconds, although a more fluid mix with an efflux time of 10 to 15 seconds may be employed for the initial injection in each hole; if this mix does not raise the pavement slab, a stiffer mix is then used. For bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements, non‑shrink cement grout is injected into cracks greater than 3/4 inch to create a continuous bonding surface before the existing asphalt is cleaned and the new concrete overlay is placed; by filling these voids the grout links the existing pavement substrate to the new concrete, allowing shear transfer and preventing loss of adhesion. The cured grout must be allowed to set before traffic can resume, which may require up to 12 hours depending on the mix design. [1][2]

Influence of production and placement

Proper mixing of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and any admixtures creates a fluid grout that can be pumped into slab‑subbase voids, thereby filling the gaps and restoring full structural support [1]. The mix must be designed according to the manufacturer’s recommendations and a trial batch mixed at least 28 days before installation under the same time and temperature conditions to ensure the mixture will achieve the required compressive strength when placed [2]. Accurate measurement of dry ingredients and batching with water, where the amount of water is controlled so that the efflux time is within 16 seconds to 26 seconds (ASTM C 939), governs flowability, and mixed material must not be held in the mixer or injection‑pump sump for more than one hour [2]. The specified proportions are one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, with admixtures optional [2]. For initial injection a more fluid mix having an efflux time of 10 seconds to 15 seconds may be used, and if that mix does not raise the pavement slab a stiffer mix is required [2]. Placement is performed by pumping the grout first at the lowest point and lifting the slab in increments, reducing the rate of pumping as the gauge blocks approach the reference string line and stopping when they touch, which helps keep stresses low and prevents cracking [1]. Continuous injection pressures may be up to 200 psi, pressures of 300 psi are allowed only for short periods as directed, and brief spikes to 600 psi may be permitted when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade; upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch will not be allowed [2]. The grout must be capable of being pumped into dowel slots or voids beneath the slabs because adequate flowability is needed to fill the spaces completely, and limiting upward movement to no more than 0.05 inch and keeping injection pressure at or below 200 psi prevents over‑lifting and loss of grout, directly affecting slab stabilization [2]. Placement shall be discontinued if ambient air and surface temperatures fall below 40 °F, and the repair area must be kept at or above 40 °F during placement and protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours after finishing [2]. For concrete overlay work the milled asphalt surface must be cooler than 120 °F before placement [2]. Consolidation of the grout is achieved with a spud vibrator or other acceptable method that works the material into saw cuts and eliminates voids, thereby controlling bond quality and early‑age strength development [2]. After the grout has been placed, striking off and shaping the concrete to match the surrounding pavement provides the final surface finish [2]. Curing materials are applied after finishing and texturing and within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation, and insulating blankets are provided when air temperature is 45 °F or less to prevent rapid heat loss; insulation may be removed when the air temperature exceeds 45 °F or when the average concrete compressive strength surpasses 3,000 psi, with removal performed so that surface‑temperature change does not exceed 40 °F in any one‑hour period [2]. The grout must attain a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before slabs are opened to traffic for transverse dowel applications, and for bedding grout it must reach at least 1,000 psi before traffic and 2,500 psi within 12 hours, with testing of cylinders per PTM No. 521 to verify compliance [2]. Traffic must not be permitted for a minimum of 12 hours after grouting so that the grout can set before loading, and the cure time may be 12 hours depending on the mix design [1][2].

During slab stabilization, pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement may create small voids beneath the concrete slab, especially near transverse joints and cracks at outside corners; these loss‑of‑support defects remove bearing and cause excessive slab deflection and stress that manifest as joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately slab breakup, so unfilled or inadequately filled voids allow continued pumping, faulting and cracking of the grout [1]. Improper execution of slabjacking can introduce defects such as cracking, tilting and faulting of the slab that jeopardize the long‑term behavior of the cement grout used for lifting; if grout starts to extrude from joints, cracks or the pavement edge before the target elevation is reached, additional drilling and grouting are required, indicating premature extrusion also undermines durability [1]. Performing slabjacking when the pavement is hot and expanded is usually impractical because the joints will be in compression, further increasing damage risk [1].

In full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs any deviation from the specified grout mix design or failure to achieve the required early compressive strengths and flowability is identified as a critical defect; the dowel grout must be pumpable into the slots and reach a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic loading, while bedding grout must attain at least 1,000 psi before opening and 2,500 psi within 12 hours, and when setting bolts or strongback beams are used the grout cylinders must be tested for a compressive strength of 50 psi, with use of a mix other than that demonstrated in the trial batch or not conducting the trial under identical temperature and time conditions considered a defect that can compromise durability [2]. For slab stabilization, holes larger than 1 ½ inches in diameter, holes that are not vertical or round, or breakouts through the slab bottom prevent proper grout placement; holding the mixed grout more than one hour after mixing reduces its flowability; an upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 inch and injection pressures exceeding 200 psi create voids and cracking; and opening the slab to traffic in less than the required 12‑hour cure period permits premature loading, each of which can lead to loss of grout and accelerated deterioration [2]. The grout mix must follow the prescribed cement‑to‑supplementary material ratio of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material, a water content that yields an efflux time between 10 seconds and 15 seconds, an expansion limited to 0 %–10 %, an initial setting time between 1 hour and 6 hours, bleed rates not exceeding 2.5 % of the volume, and a 7‑day compressive strength of at least 700 psi based on the average of five cylinders; any change in material source mandates submission of a new mix design because unapproved variations can alter these parameters and compromise long‑term behavior [2]. In partial‑depth repairs allowing the grout or epoxy bonding compound to run or puddle in low spots, placing concrete after the grout has dried instead of while it is still tacky, performing the work when ambient air and surface temperatures are below 40 °F, failing to protect the repair from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours, or providing insufficient consolidation of the repair material each constitute defects that can compromise durability, and the grout must be placed within 60 minutes of mix completion; any delay beyond this period is regarded as a defect that may affect durability and strength development [2]. The specification also treats the presence of radial cracking as a construction defect, where each occurrence of five linear feet triggers a reduction of one bag of cement grout and requires that any drilled holes be patched, which can significantly affect the long‑term integrity of the stabilized base [2]. For slabjacking, variations in the grout mix such as the cement‑to‑supplementary material ratio of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material, a water quantity that produces an efflux time between 16 seconds and 26 seconds (or a more fluid mix with an efflux time of 10 seconds to 15 seconds for the initial injection), optional use of admixtures, and the decision to employ a stiffer mix when the initial blend does not raise the slab are identified as factors that most strongly dictate long‑term performance because they control grout fluidity, lift capability, and eventual strength development [2]. Construction tolerances for slabjacking specify that the pavement must be raised to within 0.02 foot, plus or minus, of the string line grade for continuously reinforced slabs and within 0.03 foot for jointed slabs; overjacking greater than 0.10 foot requires removal and replacement of the pavement; continuous pressures are limited to 200 psi, short‑term pressures exceeding 300 psi (or brief spikes to 600 psi only under special bonding conditions) can compromise grout integrity, and extending the discharge end of the expanding rubber packer below the lower surface of the concrete pavement is prohibited because it may introduce voids or uneven grout placement [2].

Selection, specification, and improvement

In section 4.5 on slab stabilization, the selection of cement grout is primarily driven by site conditions, which may make it more advantageous than alternative materials such as hot asphalt or high‑density polyurethane, and by the required traffic reopening time because the cement grout must cure before traffic can be allowed back on the pavement; this curing period can be up to 12 hours depending on the mix design [1]. In section 4.6 on slabjacking, selection is likewise guided primarily by site conditions; the material that best suits the subgrade, drainage, size and depth of the depression, amount of correction required, and the location of joints or cracks will be chosen, and the speed at which traffic must be reopened may also influence the choice because cement grout typically requires a 12‑hour cure while high‑density polyurethane expands and hardens in a minimum of 30 minutes; temperature conditions further affect whether grout‑based lifting can be performed at all, and for estimating purposes 0.40 bags of cement per hole should be used [1]. Selection of cement grout is first guided by the manufacturer’s recommendations together with performance criteria that assure installability and strength (Section 529); the mix must be pumpable into the intended slots or voids and achieve a compressive strength of at least 2,500 psi before traffic for dowel‑grout applications, while cementitious bedding grout must reach a minimum of 1,000 psi before traffic and rise to 2,500 psi within 12 hours [2]. A trial batch shall be mixed at least 28 days prior under the same time and temperature conditions expected on site, tested in cylinders according to PTM No. 521, and independent laboratory results submitted to the Representative; the mix design and pump equipment also require approval before use, and the production mix for installation must match the demonstrated trial batch [2]. For slab‑stabilization applications the selection follows a detailed mix‑design specification that must be submitted to the District Executive for review and acceptance (Section 679.2); the prescribed composition calls for one part cement by volume combined with three parts supplementary cementitious material, water adjusted so that the efflux time falls between 10 seconds and 15 seconds, and any required admixtures such as a multiphase wetting agent, expansive agent or accelerator [2]. The grout must meet performance limits of 0 % to 10 % expansion, an initial setting time of 1 hour to 6 hours, bleed loss not exceeding 2.5 % of the volume, and a minimum 7‑day compressive strength of 700 psi based on five cylinders; any change in material source requires a new mix design submission [2]. Construction further requires equipment that includes a positive‑displacement injection pump and mixing machine capable of operating at a minimum speed of 800 rpm and a maximum speed of 2000 rpm, a water tanker with adequate capacity and pressure, and drilling tools that produce smooth vertical holes no larger than 1½ inches in diameter (Section 679.3); mixed material shall not be held in the mixer or injection‑pump sump for more than one hour after mixing, flowability measurements shall be made at least two times during each work shift, upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch will not be allowed, pressure shall not exceed a maximum of 200 psi unless otherwise directed, and equipment must be capable of making these measurements to within 0.001 inch [2]. The repaired area shall not be opened to traffic for a minimum of 12 hours after grouting, or as required by the contractor’s approved field QC plan and the manufacturer’s recommendations [2]. When cement grout is specified for partial‑depth repair (Section 525), the mix shall follow the prescribed initial weight proportions and be placed no later than sixty minutes after the mix is completed; the grout shall be mechanically mixed and applied as a thin brush or scrub coat to all cleaned surfaces, may not be allowed to run or puddle in low spots, and concrete placement shall occur before the grout dries or while an epoxy bonding compound is still tacky [2]. Work shall proceed only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F and rising; placement must be discontinued if temperatures fall below 40 °F, the repair shall be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours, and the return‑to‑service time shall be considered before opening the pavement to traffic [2]. For slabjacking (Section 681.2) the cement grout mix design is defined as one part cement and three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, with water controlled so that the efflux time measured according to ASTM C939 falls within 16 seconds to 26 seconds, although a more fluid mix achieving an efflux time of 10 seconds to 15 seconds may be used for the initial injection in each hole and a stiffer mix may be employed if the prescribed mix does not raise the pavement slab [2]. Selection is further driven by pressure and elevation tolerances: continuous pressures shall be limited to 200 psi, higher pressures up to 300 psi may be used only for short periods as directed, and brief spikes to 600 psi are permitted when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade (Section 681.3); the grout must be pumped so that the lifted surface falls within ±0.02 ft of the target grade for continuously reinforced concrete or within ±0.03 ft for jointed slabs, and any overjacking exceeding 0.10 ft requires grinding or replacement of the pavement [2]. Finally, for bonded‑concrete overlays any longitudinal crack greater than ¾ inch shall be filled with non‑shrink grout or another Department‑approved filler to provide a reliable bond before the surface is cleaned for overlay placement (Section 540.3) [2].

In the slab‑stabilization treatment (section 4.5 of the Pavement Policy Manual) performance of cement grout may be improved by incorporating supplementary cementitious material and, where appropriate, admixtures to obtain a fluid grout that can be pumped beneath the slab [1]. The Pennsylvania DOT specification for full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs likewise requires a manufacturer‑approved prepackaged mix that includes one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material and may include a multiphase wetting agent, an expansive agent, or an accelerator when required [2]. For slab‑jacking (section 4.6 of the Pavement Policy Manual) adding supplementary cementitious materials and possibly admixtures enhances grout fluidity and overall performance, with the same cure period of up to 12 hours before traffic is permitted [1]. The DOT specification for slab‑jacking also calls for a mix proportioned as one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material, with water adjusted so that efflux time falls within 16 seconds to 26 seconds (or 10 seconds to 15 seconds for the initial injection) and permits the use of accepted admixtures to achieve the desired fluidity [2]. Both documents allow high‑density polyurethane grout as an alternative stabilizing material; the Pavement Policy Manual describes it as a two‑component low‑viscosity system that expands, hardens after a minimum of 30 minutes, and resists water and chemical attack, while the DOT specification lists high‑density polyurethane grout among permitted alternatives for faster set or higher durability [1][2]. The Pavement Policy Manual further notes that hot‑asphalt undersealing may be used when site conditions favor other treatments, provided traffic is not opened until the asphalt has cooled for at least 30 minutes after pumping [1]. In partial‑depth repairs the DOT specification improves performance by using Class AA Cement Concrete, Modified mix and applying a thin layer of grout or epoxy bonding compound immediately after final cleaning, with placement required only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F and rising and with protection from freezing for at least 24 hours [2]. Quality control in slab‑stabilization under the Pavement Policy Manual requires prohibiting traffic on the slab until the grout has cured, which can be 12 hours depending on the mix design, and calls for joint and crack sealing together with good subsurface drainage to limit water entry [1]. The DOT specification mandates a documented quality‑control loop that includes mixing a trial batch at least 28 days prior under expected temperature conditions, independent laboratory testing of cylinders according to PTM No. 521 to verify flowability and compressive strength, submission of the mix design, pump equipment details, and test results for approval before field use [2]. Additional quality‑control measures in the DOT specification require dry ingredients be weighed by mass, water measured on a meter or scale, mixed grout not remain in the mixer or injection pump sump for more than one hour, flowability checks at least twice each work shift, lift monitoring equipment with 0.001 inch resolution, and prohibition of any upward movement greater than 0.05 inch during injection [2]. Grout line pressure shall not exceed 200 psi unless otherwise directed; continuous pressures up to 200 psi are allowed, short‑duration pressures up to 300 psi may be used as directed, and brief spikes to 600 psi are permitted only when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade [2]. The DOT specification also sets performance thresholds for cement grout strength: at least 2,500 psi compressive strength before traffic for transverse dowel grout, at least 1,000 psi before traffic and 2,500 psi within 12 hours for cementitious bedding grout, a minimum 7‑day compressive strength of 700 psi based on five cylinders, expansion limited to 0 %–10 %, initial setting time between 1 hour and 6 hours, bleed not exceeding 2.5 % of the volume, and efflux time as previously noted [2]. For bonded concrete overlays the DOT specification requires cracks wider than three‑quarters inch to be cleaned and filled with non‑shrink grout or another approved material before surface cleaning, and a comprehensive quality‑control plan must detail all operations from surface preparation through mixing, hauling, placing, curing, temperature monitoring, joint sawing, and sampling and strength testing prior to traffic opening [2]. Curing for the overlay should begin within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates; when ambient temperatures are 45 °F or lower insulating blankets must be used so that concrete surface temperature does not change more than forty degrees Fahrenheit in any one‑hour period [2].

Performance evaluation and implications

For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529), evaluation of cement grout is performed by laboratory testing of trial batches prepared at least 28 days before slab placement under the same time and temperature conditions expected during actual installation. Cylinders are cast and tested in accordance with PTM No. 521 to verify that the mix can be pumped into slots or voids and attains the required compressive strengths—2,500 psi for dowel grout before traffic, and for bedding grout at least 1,000 psi before traffic and 2,500 psi within 12 hours—and meets flowability criteria. Independent laboratory test results demonstrating these properties must be submitted to the Representative, and the same mix used in the trial batch is employed for the actual installation. For cement‑grout stabilization under Section 679.2, independent laboratory testing must document the 1‑day, 3‑day, and 7‑day compressive strengths together with flowability, shrinkage/expansion results and initial set time; the mix also has to meet specified limits of expansion (0 %–10 %), initial setting time (1 hour to 6 hours), bleed loss (no more than 2.5 % of volume) and water efflux (10 seconds to 15 seconds). A minimum 7‑day compressive strength of 700 psi is required based on the average of five test cylinders as stipulated in PTM No. 521. For slab‑stabilization construction (Section 679.3), evaluation relies on field measurements. Vertical movement testing equipment capable of detecting lift to within 0.001 inch is used to confirm that upward pavement movement does not exceed 0.05 inch during injection; flowability of the mixed grout is measured at least two times each work shift, and pressure in the grout line is monitored so it never exceeds 200 psi. The contractor also must wait a minimum of 12 hours after grouting before opening to traffic, providing an additional field performance check. For slab‑stabilization payment (Section 679.4), evaluation includes a deflection test performed for each unit and a count of holes drilled, which serve as field evidence of installation; the quantity of grout material is tracked by bags of cement, and the Department applies a reduction of one bag for every 5 linear feet of radial cracking identified in the work. For slabjacking construction (Section 681.3), evaluation relies on field performance documentation. A Field QC Plan requires existing roadway profiles that display depressions and the proposed grade line; before acceptance, a Roadway Profile Report with surveyed drawings showing the existing, proposed, and achieved grades for the stabilized sections must be submitted to the Representative, who may request additional injection based on those profile results. In slab stabilization a fluid mixture of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and possibly admixtures is pumped beneath the concrete to fill voids at the slab‑subbase interface; when the voids are filled sufficiently full support is restored, which reduces slab deflections and lessens the likelihood of pumping, joint faulting, corner breaks and diagonal cracking, thereby preserving structural integrity even though it does not increase the pavement’s design structural capacity [1]. The procedure estimates one cubic foot of grout per hole (0.25 bag of cement per hole) and requires that the slab remain closed to traffic until the cement grout cures, which can be twelve hours depending on the mix design [1]. Because restored support mitigates future settlement, joint and crack sealing together with good subsurface drainage are required to keep water out and maintain the benefit of stabilization [1]. In slabjacking the same type of fluid cement grout is pumped beneath the pavement to fill voids and raise slabs; its setting time directly influences construction scheduling and maintenance downtime because traffic should not be opened until the cement grout cures, which can be twelve hours depending on the mix design [1]. Properly executed grout placement restores structural integrity and rideability, while improper execution may induce cracking, tilting or faulting that degrade pavement performance [1]. Design of hole patterns and the estimated use of 0.40 bags of cement per hole guide material quantity and ensure adequate support during the lift, and if done correctly slabjacking can be more economical than slab replacement and is usually completed in less time with minimal interference to traffic [1].

For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs the cementitious grouts must be capable of being pumped into dowel slots or slab voids and must attain defined compressive strengths before traffic is allowed; transverse dowel grout must reach a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before the slabs are opened to traffic, while cementitious bedding grout must attain at least 1,000 psi before opening and 2,500 psi in twelve hours [2]. The mix design, flowability and strength are verified by trial batches at least twenty‑eight days before installation and by independent laboratory testing of cylinders so that the required performance can be confirmed prior to construction [2]. During slab stabilization the grout must be mixed and placed promptly—material held longer than one hour after mixing is to be discarded—to preserve its flowability, which is verified at least two times each shift [2]. The injection pressure may not exceed 200 psi and any upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited, preventing over‑lift or damage to the slab [2]. After grouting the pavement cannot be opened to traffic for a minimum of twelve hours, allowing the grout to set and contribute to long‑term durability [2].

In partial‑depth concrete pavement repairs the grout must be placed within sixty minutes of mix completion; this limited working time governs construction sequencing and helps ensure that the grout achieves proper bonding before setting, which is essential for the repair’s durability [2]. The grout is mechanically mixed and applied by brushing or scrubbing in a thin layer that must not run or puddle, because a uniform, tacky coating is required for effective bonding before the concrete is placed; concrete may be placed only while the grout (or epoxy bonding compound) is still tacky and certainly before it dries, and placement is permitted only when ambient air and surface temperatures are forty °F and rising, with repairs protected from freezing for a minimum of twenty‑four hours, underscoring the grout’s sensitivity to temperature and its impact on durability and serviceability [2].

For slabjacking the cement grout must have an efflux time within sixteen to twenty‑six seconds per ASTM C‑939; a more fluid mix with an efflux of ten to fifteen seconds may be used for the initial injection in each hole, and a stiffer mix is called for if the above mix fails to raise the pavement slab [2]. The injected grout must be pressurized within defined limits: continuous pressures up to 200 psi are allowed; pressure to 300 psi is permitted only for short periods as directed, and brief spikes to 600 psi may be used when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade [2]. The lift must be controlled so that reinforced concrete pavements are raised to within 0.02 foot (plus or minus) of the string‑line grade, jointed slabs within 0.03 foot, and if overjacking exceeds 0.10 foot the section must be satisfactorily removed and replaced, linking grout behavior directly to maintenance actions and overall durability [2].

When a bonded concrete overlay is placed on an asphalt‑surfaced pavement any cracks larger than three‑quarters of an inch must be cleaned and filled with non‑shrink cement grout; the grout’s resistance to shrinkage creates a stable, bonded interface that helps maintain structural continuity and reduces the likelihood of delamination under traffic loads, while the milled asphalt surface must be kept below 120 °F and any standing water removed after cleaning, further protecting the grout‑to‑overlay bond and supporting long‑term pavement performance [2].
