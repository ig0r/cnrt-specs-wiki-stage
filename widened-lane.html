Widened Lane

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Widened Lane is a topic under Rigid Pavement.
widened-laneswidened-lane
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

A widened lane is a concrete or flexible‑pavement extension, typically one to two feet wide and incorporated as an integral part of the existing carriageway, that serves as a supported edge alongside asphalt or roller‑compacted‑concrete shoulders. By adding this extra width it preserves required lateral clearance after overlay rehabilitation, reduces slab deflection on low‑volume rural highways, and—because the lane is treated as a supported edge—permits a one‑inch reduction in the minimum slab thickness while still meeting serviceability (SI 2.5 for collectors, 2.0 for locals) and reliability targets [1]. The geometry is limited to a maximum overall slab width of fourteen feet (striped at twelve feet with the remainder acting as an effective shoulder), is confined to two‑way traffic volumes under ten thousand vehicles per day where an incident‑management lane is unnecessary, and relies on the prescribed load‑transfer coefficient J for asphalt or RCC shoulders [1].

Purpose and design objectives

The widened‑lane element is introduced to meet several interrelated performance needs in pavement design. By adding a lane one to two feet wide alongside asphalt or RCC shoulders, it preserves the required lateral clearance and shoulder width after concrete overlay rehabilitation, ensuring that the rebuilt section retains adequate support. Extending the concrete slab up to fourteen feet and coupling it with this widened edge also reduces excessive slab deflection on low‑volume rural highways, providing a functional outside lane that serves as an effective shoulder where a dedicated incident‑management lane is unnecessary for traffic volumes under ten thousand ADT. In addition, treating the widened lane as a supported edge supplies extra lateral support to the concrete slab, allowing designers to deduct one inch from the minimum slab thickness prescribed in the tables, thereby satisfying serviceability and reliability objectives while limiting material use. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–167)

When a widened lane is used as the supported edge of a roller‑compacted concrete pavement, the design must achieve structural adequacy by permitting a one‑inch reduction in the minimum slab thickness; it also has to satisfy durability criteria that assume a twenty‑year service life and tolerate up to fifteen percent of slabs cracking at the end of that period. Serviceability objectives are expressed through terminal serviceability indices—2.5 for collector roads and 2.0 for local roads—and reliability targets of ninety percent and eighty‑five percent, respectively, ensuring the lane can handle the projected traffic while maintaining acceptable ride quality. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 166–167)

Scope and applicability

Widened lane provisions are appropriate for new‑construction or reconstruction projects that employ flexible pavements, where the travel lane is extended two feet into the shoulder area, and also for rigid‑pavement projects that incorporate concrete shoulders, with specific guidance found in Chapter 8.14 of the construction standards. In concrete‑pavement rehabilitation using bonded overlays, a lane may be widened one to two feet and paired with asphalt shoulders; additional fill widening is provided as needed to retain proper shoulder widths in accordance with Chapter 10 requirements. For rigid (concrete) pavements the design permits expanding a slab up to fourteen feet wide, striped at twelve feet, leaving the remainder as a right‑hand shoulder constructed of Type 1 asphalt or roller‑compacted concrete. This configuration is limited to low‑volume rural Interstates and Expressways carrying two‑way traffic volumes under ten thousand vehicles per day where an incident‑management lane is not required; when a tied concrete shoulder exists, a thirteen‑foot outside lane striped at twelve feet may be used to reduce slab deflection while preserving the conventional twelve‑foot passing lane. In roller‑compacted concrete designs for low‑volume local and collector highways (ADT ≤ 2000), a widened lane can serve as a supported edge—either a curb and gutter or a widened lane—allowing the RCC slab thickness to be reduced by one inch relative to the minimum depth for an unsupported edge, assuming a standard six‑inch unbound granular subbase, a twenty‑year design life, and typical PennDOT material inputs for PCC pavements. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 60–61), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–167)

When the required lane width would exceed fourteen feet, or the roadway carries more than about ten thousand daily vehicles in each direction, a widened‑lane solution is no longer appropriate; similarly, if an incident‑management lane is needed, designers revert to conventional 12‑foot lanes with tied concrete shoulders. In those cases the standard design—12‑ft passing lanes and a concrete shoulder—or alternative shoulder treatments are preferred over a widened slab. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (p. 165)

Design inputs and requirements

In new construction or reconstruction projects that employ flexible pavement, the lane shall be extended two feet into the shoulder area, except on inside shoulders of Interstate and other limited‑access freeways, as required by the design manual and reinforced by construction standards [1]. The widened lane is classified as a type of shoulder and therefore must satisfy the same chapter‑level geometric and structural criteria that govern the associated pavement work, including Chapter 10 for asphalt overlays and Chapter 9 for full reconstruction [1]. For concrete pavements, guidance appears in the sections dealing with widened lanes and shoulders, and any concrete shoulder or widened lane is limited to one to two feet in width and must be paired with an asphalt shoulder, all of which must meet the applicable Type 6 or Type 7 paved‑shoulder specifications [1]. When a widened concrete lane is provided as a concrete shoulder, the total slab width may not exceed fourteen feet and the lane shall be striped at twelve feet, with the remaining width functioning as an effective shoulder when combined with a Type 1 asphalt or roller‑compacted concrete shoulder; such configurations are restricted to low‑volume rural Interstates or expressways carrying less than ten thousand two‑way average daily traffic and where an incident‑management lane is not required [1]. In cases where tied concrete shoulders are used, a thirteen‑foot outside lane striped at twelve feet may be employed to limit slab deflection while the regular passing lane retains the conventional twelve‑foot dimension, and the appropriate load‑transfer coefficient (J) for widened lanes with asphalt or RCC shoulders must be applied as specified in the relevant table [1]. Design of widened lanes follows the department’s reinforced concrete pavement criteria, employing ACPA StreetPave12 software and standard Portland cement concrete values, and because a widened lane is considered a supported edge, the required concrete thickness may be reduced by one inch relative to an unsupported edge provided that the design adheres to a twenty‑year service life, 1 % annual traffic growth, a six‑inch unbound granular subbase, a fifteen‑percent allowable slab‑crack rate, reliability levels of eighty‑five percent for local roads and ninety percent for collector roads, and terminal serviceability values of two point zero for local roads and two point five for collectors; no other depth‑reduction methods are permitted on department roadways [1]. Construction of a widened lane that utilizes slip‑form concrete paving must be performed with a fully energized, self‑propelled slip‑form paver equipped with side forms and automatic alignment controls as stipulated in the specification section governing such work [2]. Longitudinal joints shall be placed parallel to and within twelve inches of the final pavement markings, and edge slumping between adjacent lanes must not exceed one‑eighth inch, with tighter tolerances required at shoulders [2]. When an existing concrete pavement is widened, any new transverse or contraction joint created shall be aligned with the original joints where feasible; if alignment is not possible, the new joint shall be located at least ten feet away from any existing joint [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 60–61), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–167); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

Design methods and criteria

The widened‑lane design on concrete pavements is governed by a sequence of dimensional limits and material selections that are tied to traffic volume and the type of shoulder provided. The slab width is limited to a maximum of fourteen feet, and the lane marking is placed at twelve feet from the edge; this geometry applies to low‑volume rural interstates and expressways where the two‑way average daily traffic does not exceed ten thousand vehicles and an incident‑management lane is unnecessary. When a tied concrete shoulder is incorporated, the outer lane may be widened to thirteen feet while still being striped at twelve feet in order to restrain slab deflection; the adjacent passing lane retains the conventional twelve‑foot width. The principal design parameter for these configurations is the load‑transfer coefficient J, which is taken from the table that lists values for widened lanes supported by either Type 1 asphalt or roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) shoulders. For projects that employ an RCC shoulder, the widened lane is treated as a supported edge and the required concrete slab thickness is reduced by one inch in accordance with the tabulated guidance. Thickness calculations are performed with ACPA’s StreetPave12 software; the program evaluates structural depth using the subgrade resilient modulus expressed as MRSG = 1 500 × CBR, adopts a design life of twenty years, assumes an annual traffic growth rate of 1.0 %, and applies terminal serviceability indices of 2.5 for collector roads and 2.0 for local roads together with reliability targets of 90 % (or 85 %). These empirical relationships and model inputs constitute the adjustment that a widened lane introduces into the overall pavement design. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 165–167)

In flexible‑pavement projects that involve new construction or reconstruction, the lane is required to extend two feet into the adjacent shoulder, a provision that is omitted for inside shoulders on Interstate and other limited‑access freeways. When the widened portion functions as a concrete shoulder, its width is constrained to between one and two feet and must be paired with an asphalt shoulder; any necessary fill widening is provided so that the intended shoulder dimensions are preserved. For widened concrete slabs, the slab may not exceed fourteen feet in overall width and is striped at twelve feet, creating an effective shoulder when combined with a Type 1 asphalt or roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) shoulder. This configuration is limited to low‑volume rural Interstates and expressways where the two‑way average daily traffic is under ten thousand vehicles, and design calculations must employ the load‑transfer coefficient specified for widened lanes with asphalt or RCC shoulders. When a widened lane serves as a supported edge in RCC pavement design, the required RCC thickness can be reduced by one inch. The overall design assumes a twenty‑year service life, a 1 % annual traffic growth rate, and permits up to fifteen percent of slabs to be cracked at the end of that period. For collector roads the terminal serviceability index is set to 2.5 with a reliability target of ninety percent. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 60–61), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–167)

Structural section and dimensions

The design guidance for a widened lane varies with pavement type but does not prescribe additional layer thicknesses beyond the existing structure. For flexible‑pavement projects the widening is achieved simply by extending the surface course two feet into the adjacent shoulder; no separate base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab, edge‑support or reinforcement dimensions are called for. In rigid‑pavement applications a widened lane may be provided as a concrete shoulder whose width ranges from one to two feet and is typically paired with asphalt shoulders, again without any specified changes to the underlying layers. When the widened lane is classified as a supported edge in reinforced cement concrete design tables, the slab thickness can be reduced by one inch relative to an unsupported edge, but no other dimensional requirements for base, subbase, treated‑soil, edge support structures or reinforcement are given. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 60–61), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 8 (pp. 166–167)

Materials and mixture design

The guidance specifies that a widened lane is built as a concrete element, generally 1 to 2 feet wide, and is provided with asphalt shoulders; however, it does not prescribe particular material properties or mixture proportions for the concrete or asphalt, merely indicating the type of material to be used. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69)

Structural details and interfaces

When a widened lane is added to an existing concrete pavement the design provides a concrete extension typically one to two feet wide that is tied directly to an adjacent asphalt or reinforced‑cement‑concrete shoulder, and if the original cross‑section would lose its intended shoulder width additional fill widening is placed to retain the proper dimensions [1]. The widened slab may be as wide as fourteen feet but is striped for traffic use at twelve feet, with the remaining width serving as an effective shoulder when combined with the Type 1 asphalt or RCC shoulder [1]. Design follows the standard passing‑lane geometry of a twelve‑foot lane plus a tied concrete shoulder and requires the application of the appropriate load‑transfer coefficient J for widened lanes with asphalt or RCC shoulders as listed in Table 8.2, without any extra reinforcement, special transition details, or additional connections beyond these provisions [1]. Where the underlying base consists of composite layers, the transverse joints at the edge of the widened portion must be rehabilitated to ensure proper performance [1]. Joints in the widened lane should align with existing concrete construction or contraction joints whenever feasible; if alignment is not possible new joints are required to be located at least ten feet away from any existing joint [2]. Load‑transfer units such as dowel bars or LTU assemblies are installed before paving, secured in their designated positions, and must be set so that vertical tilt does not exceed a quarter‑inch and longitudinal or horizontal translation does not exceed one inch [2]. Reinforcement placed on the slab prior to concrete placement must be clean of rust and extend the full length of the slab, while tie bars may be used provided they are not installed within twenty‑four inches of a dowelled contraction joint [2]. These joint alignment, reinforcement, and load‑transfer requirements together provide edge control and structural continuity for the widened lane [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 8 (p. 165); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

When a lane is expanded its new width is treated as an integral part of the existing pavement system. The added portion may be provided with concrete shoulders that extend one to two feet beyond the original edge, and these are matched by asphalt shoulders on the adjacent surface. If the existing shoulder dimensions would otherwise be compromised, fill widening is employed so that the final shoulder widths remain consistent across the transition. At the junction between the widened segment and the pre‑existing pavement, continuous base drainage must be maintained, joints are to be cleaned and sealed, and heavy‑duty membranes are installed where required; guide rails and any associated drainage structures are relocated or replaced to follow the new geometry. The widened lane itself is typically constructed as a concrete slab whose overall width does not exceed fourteen feet. Twelve‑foot striping defines the travel portion, leaving the remaining width to function effectively as a shoulder when it abuts a Type 1 asphalt or roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) shoulder. The structural interaction between the slab and the adjoining material is governed by the load‑transfer coefficient J specified for widened lanes with asphalt or RCC shoulders, ensuring appropriate stress distribution across the joint. Where tied concrete shoulders are used, an outside lane of thirteen feet, also striped at twelve feet, may be provided to limit slab deflection. In RCC designs for local and collector roads carrying up to two thousand vehicles per day, the widened lane is classified as a supported edge—analogous to a curb and gutter. Recognizing this support allows the design tables to reduce the minimum concrete thickness by one inch while still applying the standard PennDOT inputs of a twenty‑year service life, one percent annual traffic growth, and a six‑inch granular subbase. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–167)

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

A widened lane shall be added with an extra width of roughly one to two feet beyond the standard travel lane and shall be bounded on each side by asphalt shoulders that meet the same pavement standards as the main carriageway. Those shoulders must conform to the paved‑shoulder classification employed elsewhere in the rehabilitation plan, typically a Type 6 or Type 7 shoulder. Where the existing surface is concrete, the total slab width of the widened lane may not exceed fourteen feet; the lane shall be striped at twelve feet, with the remaining width serving as an effective shoulder when paired with a Type 1 asphalt or RCC shoulder. In cases where tied‑concrete shoulders are used, an outside lane up to thirteen feet wide may be provided and also striped at twelve feet in order to limit slab deflection, while the regular passing lane remains a conventional twelve‑foot lane with its own tied concrete shoulder. Such configurations are limited to low‑volume rural Interstates or Expressways carrying fewer than ten thousand average daily traffic where incident‑management lanes are not required. Fill widening shall be performed as necessary to retain proper shoulder widths, and the load‑transfer coefficient J for these widened lanes shall be taken from Table 8.2. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 8 (p. 165)

Verification and expected performance

When a widened lane is used as a supported edge, the RCC design assumes a 20‑year service life with a 1 % annual traffic growth, and performance is evaluated against a terminal serviceability of 2.5 for collector roads (or 2.0 for local roads) together with a reliability target of 90 % (85 %). The design also limits overall distress by permitting no more than 15 % of the concrete slabs to be cracked at the end of the design period, and the required pavement thickness is reduced by one inch relative to an unsupported edge. These outcomes are measured through the ACPA StreetPave12 software calculations that incorporate subgrade modulus, subbase thickness, and the specified serviceability and reliability criteria. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 166–167)
