Pavement Management

Part of: PennDOT Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242) is-subtopic-of
Pavement Management is a topic under PennDOT Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242).
pavement-managementnetwork-levelpavement-management-system
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Pavement Management is a systematic, data‑driven framework that enables agencies to decide which maintenance or rehabilitation treatment should be applied to each pavement segment so that the required service level is achieved at the lowest total cost over the analysis period [1]. It integrates condition surveys, traffic and history information stored in the Roadway Management System, applies defined treatment cycles and cost‑effectiveness analyses to prioritize both network‑wide planning, budgeting and project‑level design decisions [1]. By tracking mileage, expenditures, and performance metrics such as International Roughness Index, the process provides objective evidence of how funding choices affect the network, identifies out‑of‑cycle backlogs, and supports performance‑based investment selections [1].

Purpose and decision context

Pavement Management supplies a systematic framework that enables agencies to decide which maintenance or rehabilitation treatment should be applied to a particular pavement segment, ensuring the chosen option delivers the required service level at the lowest total cost over the analysis period; at the network scale it also informs general planning, programming and policy choices tied to budgeting and agency priorities. It is used to decide which treatment or project type should be applied to a given roadway segment by using defined treatment cycles as the basis for selecting appropriate treatments and by providing a methodology to assess the backlog of “out‑of‑cycle” pavements, requiring that both maintenance cycles and reconstruction cycles be evaluated when selecting the project type. Pavement Management is used to decide how money for surface‑improvement projects should be spent, documenting funds to be expended and showing how many miles of network are treated each year, which feeds directly into business‑plan guidelines and the district’s budget request to the Governor. It supports the decision of whether a newly built or rehabilitated pavement meets ride‑quality objectives and how it will perform over time by providing the data needed to evaluate construction effectiveness and predict future pavement life, relying on Pavement History information entered within four weeks for Department force projects or six weeks for contractor projects and IRI testing performed within three months after that entry. Pavement Management is used to decide whether a proposed maintenance or rehabilitation treatment can be classified as Pavement Preservation and therefore exempt from a full pavement design, drawing on cost‑effectiveness information and requiring that overlay depths not exceed 2 inches for flexible pavements, patching be less than 10 % of concrete area, and milling depth be determined by core inspection to be eligible. Finally, it is intended to support the decision of which rehabilitation or replacement projects should be undertaken on a roadway network, using pavement condition data to identify projects that meet identified needs with the minimum required treatment, employing pavement history and traffic information to evaluate structural capacity, and applying Maintenance Functional Codes to determine appropriate design criteria for the chosen alternatives. [1]

[1] — Ch. 11 (pp. 190–197), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 14 (p. 224)

Pavement Management addresses the need for a systematic, data‑driven approach that enables agencies to set goals, allocate limited budgets, and plan long‑range maintenance so that pavement performance can be preserved at the lowest total cost. By regularly collecting condition data, storing it in a database, and applying analysis programs, managers can forecast future conditions, evaluate the impact of delayed treatments, and compare alternative funding or treatment scenarios to meet performance‑based investment objectives. The process also exists to identify pavements that have failed or become functionally obsolete so that reconstruction can be undertaken, while preservation strategies are applied to enhance pavement performance by extending service life, improving safety, and meeting motorist expectations. In the context of polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing, Pavement Management seeks to identify roadways that exhibit low severity cracking, low‑to‑medium severity raveling or weathering, friction loss, moisture infiltration, or minor surface irregularities so that appropriate treatment types can be matched; Type A is used to seal cracks, fill voids, and shallow (less than 1/2 inch) ruts, whereas Type B is used for moderate (1/2 inch to 1 ¼ inch) ruts. It further addresses the need to keep pavements on the maintenance and reconstruction cycles prescribed by the MECE initiative, classifying each route into treatment networks—Concrete, Unpaved, High Level Asphalt or Low Level Asphalt—based on RMS‑derived surface type codes and Functional Class Codes, thereby providing a methodology to assess the backlog of roadways that are “out of cycle” and ensuring cost‑effective allocation of resources; districts may manually edit the treatment network indicators from “L” to “H” or vice versa when usage changes. Pavement Management also fulfills the requirement for systematic documentation of surface‑improvement fund expenditures, demanding accurate mileage capture so that the annual amount of roadway treated can be assessed and progress reports run throughout the year to measure whether prescribed cycles such as 3‑to‑5‑year crack sealing or 5‑year joint sealing are being met. Accurate, up‑to‑date roughness information is another need; Pavement History data must be entered within four weeks of a Department project’s reopening to traffic and within six weeks for contractor projects, after which International Roughness Index testing is performed on the newly recorded pavements within three months, providing condition data essential for asset‑management decisions and ride‑quality improvement. The system is used to select preservation treatments that have been shown to be cost‑effective, allowing early interventions such as micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing course to improve skid resistance, ride quality, and rut filling before advanced distresses appear; overlay projects on existing flexible pavements that exceed 2 inches are not eligible for Pavement Preservation except as noted, and concrete patching exceeding 10 % of the total pavement area is likewise ineligible unless cost‑effectiveness can be demonstrated. Finally, Pavement Management addresses the need for reliable, up‑to‑date information on pavement condition, history, traffic and related characteristics so that agencies can decide which rehabilitation or replacement projects are required and what minimum treatment is appropriate; because RMS data may be outdated or incomplete, field inspections and testing must supplement database information to ensure sufficient data quality and timeliness for effective decision‑making. Scope and applicability

Pavement Management is appropriate both for network‑wide planning projects and for individual pavement treatment projects; at the network level it supports general planning, programming, policy decisions and budget prioritization, while at the project level it guides the selection of the best maintenance or rehabilitation treatment for a specific pavement section, defining a cost‑effective strategy that meets desired service levels. Under Penn DOT’s framework the approach may be applied to any roadway project whose treatment network can be identified by surface‑type and functional‑class codes, including Concrete with surface type codes 71‑76, Unpaved with surface type code <50, High‑Level Asphalt with surface type codes 51, 52, 61, 62 combined with functional class codes 01,02,06,07,11,12,14,16,17,19,99, and Low‑Level Asphalt with the same asphalt codes combined with functional class codes 08 (Rural Minor Collector) or 09 (Rural Local). A subset of the Low‑Level Asphalt network called the Recycling Network is further limited to routes with an average daily traffic below 1 000 vehicles and a truck percentage below 10 %. The maintenance cycles and the reconstruction cycles must both be evaluated when selecting the project type, ensuring that pavements are kept within their prescribed cycles rather than being “out of cycle.” Pavement Management is applied to all surface‑improvement projects funded through Highway Maintenance (10582) that are tracked in the annual mileage and cost reports, and it begins when a project is marked open to traffic for the fiscal year. The practice covers resurfacing, which requires pavement design and follows 3R criteria; widening where capacity or roadway width is increased 10 feet or greater; intersection improvements such as turning lanes; reconstruction; and concrete rehabilitation that uses CPR and an overlay of 4 inches or greater. It also includes preservation activities, notably crack sealing on the resurfacing network on a 3‑to‑5‑year cycle and joint sealing on the concrete network on a 5‑year cycle, with reporting required in the prescribed mileage categories and templates for each fiscal year. Pavement Management is applied to preservation‑type projects that are deemed cost‑effective by PennDOT’s Pavement Management System, allowing them to proceed without a full pavement design; for flexible pavements it includes mill‑and‑overlay generally limited to 2 inches unless deeper work is needed to correct rutting, micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses applied early in the maintenance cycle before advanced distresses appear, cold in‑place recycling of base up to a maximum depth of 4 inches with a thin asphalt overlay not exceeding 1½ inches, and ultra‑thin bonded concrete overlays limited to 2 inches. For rigid pavements it covers diamond grinding, micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded courses, thin asphalt overlays, and full‑depth concrete patching provided the total patch area is less than 10 % (or can be shown cost‑effective if higher) and overlay thicknesses do not exceed 4½ inches with a Superpave 9.5 mm mix or 5 inches with a Superpave 12.5 mm mix, and bridge preservation activities may also be bundled with roadway items when feasible. Pavement Management, as implemented through Pennsylvania’s Roadway Management System, is appropriate when a rehabilitation or replacement project is being planned for state‑owned highway segments; it relies on the RMS’s pavement condition snapshot, history and traffic data to evaluate the existing structural capacity and to decide which projects are needed and what minimum treatment is required, with Maintenance Functional Codes further guiding the selection of design criteria during this early design stage, although field inspection and testing remain necessary because the database may contain outdated or incomplete records. [1]

[1] — Ch. 11 (pp. 190–194), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 14 (p. 224)

Under the design guidelines of Section 12.3, Pavement Management (as Pavement Preservation) is not appropriate when the required treatment exceeds the thickness or patching limits set out in that section; for flexible pavements any overlay that exceeds 2 inches cannot be treated as preservation, and for rigid pavements any scratch overlay that exceeds 4½ inches when a Superpave 9.5 mm Wearing Course mix is used or exceeds 5 inches when a Superpave 12.5 mm Wearing Course mix is used must follow the 3R criteria; likewise, projects that would require concrete patching of more than 10 % of the total pavement area are not eligible for preservation unless cost‑effectiveness can be demonstrated, in which case a full design or other documented design exception procedure is required. In addition, Section 14.1 indicates that Pavement Management that relies on the Roadway Management System (RMS) is unsuitable when its data are outdated or incomplete—for example, when the condition snapshot has not been updated after recent maintenance, when traffic counts may be several years old, or when pavement‑history records have missed layers or contain recording lapses—because the RMS cannot accurately represent the current pavement state and a direct field viewing and testing of the existing pavement is the preferred alternative. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 14 (p. 224)

Information needs

Pavement Management must gather detailed condition data on each pavement segment, including adequacy of drainage, need for base repair or removal of obvious failures, requirements for surface leveling or milling, structural thickness of binder courses, surface friction levels, shoulder condition, and the presence of cracks, joints or other distress. For micro‑surfacing projects it must also collect information on low severity cracking, low to medium severity raveling or weathering, any friction loss, signs of moisture infiltration, and the depth of surface irregularities such as ruts; mix type selection then follows rut‑depth thresholds (Type A for shallow ruts less than ½ inch, Type B for moderate ruts from ½ inch to 1¼ inch, and Type RF for deep ruts up to 2 inches) and a double application is recommended unless the road surface is in excellent condition, with Interstate projects requiring a double application of Type A SRL‑E regardless of condition. Site information must indicate whether surface or subsurface water removal is needed, any required maintenance on detour routes before traffic can be shifted, overlay thickness (1 inch to 1.5 inches with or without leveling, or 2 inches or greater when the 3R criterion is not followed), concrete patching percentages (2 % or greater of total area earns full mileage credit, while less than 2 % uses a conversion of 280 square yards per mile), existing pavement thickness, and structural limits such as ensuring that widening depth does not exceed the existing pavement structure and that shoulder upgrades require a minimum depth of 4 inches of Asphalt Base Course. Cost considerations include evaluating the typically high expense of detours, applying a minimum 15 % retainage for milled material (with the entire quantity provided to the contractor when the estimated total milled material is less than 200 tons), and conducting cost‑effectiveness analysis from PennDOT’s Pavement Management System when concrete patching exceeds 10 %; patching may be performed up to, but not exceeding, 10 % unless the cost effectiveness of exceeding 10 % can be shown. Traffic data encompass temporary traffic control needs, practicality of using detours which must be coordinated with the District Traffic Unit, and network classification based on RMS surface type codes (71, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76) and Functional Class Codes (01, 02, 06, 07, 11, 12, 14, 16, 17, 19, 99); for the Recycling Network a route must have an ADT less than 1000 and a Truck Percentage less than 10 %. Environmental information requires assessing recycling opportunities such as reclaimed asphalt pavement use and complying with waste‑management guidance for recycled materials. Pavement Management also depends on a comprehensive set of roadway data stored in Pennsylvania’s Roadway Management System, which must include pavement‑history records, current condition ratings, surface roughness measurements, traffic counts and vehicle classifications, friction characteristics, divider type, lane count, and urban‑rural classification. The accuracy and completeness of the pavement‑history information is considered essential for a successful management process. The condition snapshot from the STAMPP survey is used to prioritize projects and define minimum treatments, and together with traffic data it also supports structural capacity analysis. Maintenance Functional Codes are incorporated to guide design criteria, although users must recognize that condition and traffic information may be outdated until a new survey is performed. Reporting must provide precise segment‑mile counts of all surface improvements together with associated dollar amounts by the fiscal year when a project is opened to traffic, list the specific Plant Maintenance M‑213 activity codes, capture condition cycles (crack sealing on the resurfacing network every 3 to 5 years and joint sealing on concrete every 5 years), and categorize mileage under betterment types such as resurfacing, widening, intersection improvements, reconstruction and concrete rehabilitation. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 5 (p. 105), Ch. 11 (pp. 190–197), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 14 (p. 224)

The information used for pavement management must be accurate, current, and complete, but the required degree varies with the type of data and its purpose. For surface‑improvement program reporting, mileage figures must be captured accurately because they determine how much of the network is treated each year; mileages and costs must be reported according to the fiscal year when projects open to traffic and progress reports are generated throughout the year, and has to be complete by recording every activity listed in Plant Maintenance (such as mixer paver, seal coat, crack sealing, joint sealing, etc.) together with a table of segment miles for each county and a district summary submitted annually. Regarding Pavement History data that support International Roughness Index testing, districts should enter data for Department‑force projects within 4 weeks of a road reopening to traffic and for contractor projects within 6 weeks; after entry the Pavement Testing and Asset Management staff runs weekly searches and is committed to perform IRI testing on the new pavement within 3 months of that entry, making timely and complete history essential because without it IRI values cannot be reliably analyzed for trends, construction effectiveness, or life‑prediction. The pavement management system also relies on information that is accurate and complete; however, the currency of condition values is limited to the snapshot taken on the survey day and traffic counts may be several years old, so users must still perform field viewing and testing to verify current conditions. [1]

[1] — Ch. 11 (pp. 193–197), Ch. 14 (p. 224)

Alternatives

When a pavement manager evaluates treatment alternatives, the viable options must be compared according to the surface type. For existing asphalt pavements the feasible treatments are an asphalt structural overlay, a bonded‑concrete structural overlay, or total reconstruction; for Portland cement concrete pavements the alternatives are an asphalt overlay, an unbonded concrete overlay, a crack‑and‑seat with a structural asphalt overlay, or total reconstruction. The comparison is based on condition items such as drainage, base repair, surface friction, shoulder performance and required structural thickness determined by nondestructive testing or other acceptable design procedures while also ensuring compliance with Federal‑aid Highway Program policies and any traffic control constraints. In the selection of a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt paving system, the feasible alternatives are the three mix classifications: Type A for sealing cracks, filling voids and shallow ruts less than ½ inch and providing a scratch or surface course; Type B for moderate ruts from ½ inch to 1 ¼ inch and for leveling, surface or seal‑coat applications; and Type RF for deep ruts up to 2 inches applied in a single pass. The manager must also decide between a single or double application, with a double coat generally recommended unless the pavement is in excellent condition, and an Interstate project specifically calls for a double coat of Type A SRL‑E regardless of condition. At the strategic level the feasible alternatives to evaluate are reconstruction, rehabilitation, and preservation. Reconstruction involves replacing the entire existing pavement structure; rehabilitation adds structural enhancements such as overlays or partial recycling to extend service life; and preservation employs a network‑level, long‑term set of cost‑effective treatments that improve performance without full replacement. Under Pavement Management the feasible options to compare are defined for each network category: Concrete pavements identified by Surface Type Codes 71‑76; Unpaved surfaces with Surface Type Code less than 50; High Level Asphalt pavements with Surface Type Codes 51, 52, 61, 62 combined with Functional Class Codes 01, 02, 06, 07, 11, 12, 14, 16, 17, 19, 99; and Low Level Asphalt pavements using the same Surface Type Codes but with Functional Class Codes 08 or 09. For routes that belong to the Recycling Network—a subset of the Low Level Asphalt network—traffic criteria of ADT less than 1000 and Truck Percentage less than 10 % must also be considered when comparing treatment types. In surface‑improvement program reporting the feasible options that must be compared are the Betterment categories: resurfacing, which requires pavement design and follows 3R criteria; widening, where capacity or roadway width is increased ten feet or greater; intersection improvements such as turning lanes; reconstruction; and concrete rehabilitation consisting of CPR and an overlay of four inches or greater. Pavement Preservation activities constitute a separate set of reporting categories. The feasible alternatives to be compared also include thin‑overlay resurfacing of 1 inch to 1.5 inches (or 2 inches or greater when the 3R criterion is not followed), concrete patching and repair (CPR) that receives full mileage credit when at least 2 % of the total area is patched, otherwise using a conversion of 280 square yards of patching to one mile of improvement; recycling or seal‑coat treatments applied by cold in‑place or pug mill methods; widening projects evaluated against Publication 23 criteria and limited to a depth not exceeding the existing pavement structure; leveling and sealing options such as micro‑surfacing, combined leveling with seal coat (counted once when done in the same fiscal year), seal‑coat only, or leveling only; surface repair choices like mechanized patching at 900 tons per mile or skin patching at 3,696 gallons per mile; and shoulder improvements that may involve upgrades, resurfacing or seal coating for widths of two feet or greater. For flexible pavements the feasible preservation alternatives include a mill‑and‑overlay to eliminate rutting or problematic materials normally 1½–2 inches thick but allowed to exceed that depth when core testing shows deeper distress; micro‑surfacing or an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course applied early in the maintenance cycle; cold in‑place recycling of the base limited to a maximum depth of 4 inches combined with up to a 1½‑inch asphalt overlay containing 60 lb/yd² of scratch course; and an ultra‑thin bonded concrete overlay of 2 inches used as a non‑structural wearing layer. For rigid pavements the comparable options are full‑depth concrete patching and joint rehabilitation generally not exceeding 10 % of the area unless cost effectiveness is demonstrated, diamond grinding or micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or a 1‑inch thin asphalt overlay with slab stabilization, various thickness Superpave overlays such as 1½ inches of 9.5 mm mix on 2½ inches of 19.0 mm mix, and dowel‑bar retrofit or other concrete pavement restoration techniques. [1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 5 (p. 105), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 11 (pp. 191–196), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Decision criteria

In pavement management, decisions should be guided chiefly by performance metrics of the pavement system and the cost‑effectiveness of treatments while respecting any user‑defined budget or resource limits; this approach emphasizes objective condition measures to select the most economical strategies that meet desired service levels (section 11.1 INTRODUCTION). For PennDOT, the Maintenance Efficiency and Cost Effectiveness (MECE) initiative defines treatment cycles that serve as the basis for selecting appropriate maintenance actions, so maintenance efficiency and cost‑effectiveness are identified as the primary criteria controlling pavement management choices rather than safety or public impact (section 11.2 PAVEMENT TREATMENT CYCLES). [1]

[1] — Ch. 11 (pp. 190–193)

Analysis methods and tools

Pavement Management evaluation employs several analytical tools. For projects that require a life‑cycle cost analysis (LCCA), the LCCA must be submitted for review and approval to BODD, and for Federal Oversight designs the pavement design must have FHWA approval unless the FHWA determines otherwise based on risk. The process relies on systematic condition data collection and a computer database that stores pavement condition, traffic, inventory and maintenance information; these data feed an analysis program that identifies the most cost‑effective maintenance and rehabilitation strategies. The approach incorporates cost/benefit analysis at both the project level and the network level, life‑cycle analysis of selected treatments, and risk‑based asset management planning as required by Section 119(e) of Title 23 USC. In addition, the Pennsylvania Roadway Management System uses stored pavement condition data as a rating of segment health to help decide which projects are needed and the minimum treatment required; it combines pavement history with traffic counts to assess structural capacity, and applies Maintenance Functional Codes (MFC) to select appropriate design criteria for rehabilitation or replacement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 11 (pp. 190–191), Ch. 14 (p. 224)

When a roadway is evaluated for micro‑surfacing within pavement management it is assumed that the existing surface exhibits low severity cracking, low to medium severity raveling or weathering, friction loss and moisture infiltration; these condition thresholds define which projects are eligible. It is recommended that a double application be used unless the road surface is in excellent condition, and for Interstate routes it is recommended to use a double application of Type A SRL‑E even if the road surface is in excellent condition, introducing uncertainty about the benefit of a single coat under those circumstances. The appropriate mix type is selected on the basis of assumed rut depths—less than ½ inch for Type A, between ½ inch and 1¼ inches for Type B, and up to 2 inches for Type RF—and any mis‑estimation of rut depth creates uncertainty in the chosen treatment. The required Skid Resistance Level is taken from Table 5.4, implying that the SRL value used is assumed appropriate for the specific pavement condition.

In the Surface Improvement Program reporting it is assumed that resurfacing counts an overlay of 1 inch to 1.5 inches with or without leveling, while overlays of 2 inches or greater are only included when the 3R criterion is not followed. Concrete patching is assumed to receive full mileage credit when it covers 2% or more of the total area, whereas less than 2% uses a conversion of 280 square yards per mile. The depth of widening is assumed to be based on the existing pavement structure and should not exceed that structure. Shoulder upgrades assume a minimum base‑course depth of 4 inches. Mileage counting assumes that seal or resurfacing performed in the same fiscal year are counted only once, with separate years requiring reporting in both categories.

The Pavement Management results rely on the assumption that pavement history data are entered promptly—within four weeks for Department force projects and within six weeks for contractor projects—and that a weekly search will trigger International Roughness Index testing within three months of that entry; this timing constraint is intended to ensure IRI measurements reflect the actual condition after reopening to traffic. Uncertainties arise when history data are delayed or incomplete, when testing occurs before construction is finished, or when non‑NHS routes fall outside the regular testing cycle, all of which can obscure trends and deterioration rates.

Finally, Pavement Management results depend on the assumption that Roadway Management System data are accurate and complete, especially the recorded pavement history. Uncertainty arises because condition surveys capture only a single‑day snapshot and are not updated after maintenance until a later resurvey, traffic counts may be several years old, and some surface treatment layers or other work may have been omitted from the history records, creating gaps that must be addressed through field verification. [1] — Ch. 5 (p. 105), Ch. 11 (pp. 194–197), Ch. 14 (p. 224)

Stakeholders and constraints

Input on pavement management decisions is provided by certified PME/PMs, who evaluate designs and recommend approval to the District Engineer (DE). The DE makes the final decision for PennDOT Oversight pavement designs. When a design deviates from the Manual, a waiver must be submitted to BODD for its review and approval, and for Federal‑Oversight projects FHWA reviews and approves the design package after BODD’s quality‑control review. The overarching Pavement Management plan is established through the Maintenance Efficiency and Cost Effectiveness (MECE) initiative; within that framework the MECE Pavement Management Subgroup defines the pavement cycle charts that guide treatment selection, thereby holding the decision authority. District engineers may manually edit the treatment network indicators from “L” to “H” or vice versa for asphalt classifications when facility usage or other factors affect maintenance, providing operational input that is incorporated under the MECE authority. The districts are required to provide mileage and cost data in a county‑level table and a district‑wide summary each year; this information supports the budget request that is submitted to the Governor, who ultimately approves the funding for pavement management activities. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 11 (pp. 191–194)

Within the General provisions (section 2.1), pavement‑management decisions must conform to the requirement for temporary traffic‑control permits as described in Publication 213. They also must follow FHWA’s federal‑aid highway program policy on pavement management and design. PennDOT specifications such as Publication 408 impose additional constraints; for projects that use the Superpave Restricted Performance Specification, the existing pavement must be stable, properly leveled, sealed and patched, and surface‑course thicknesses must satisfy the minimums shown in Table 9.5, while concrete RPS projects require a uniform nominal depth of not less than 5,600 square yards and continuous placement operations. When recycling milled asphalt, at least fifteen percent of the estimated total milled material must be retained by the contractor, and if the estimate is under two hundred tons the entire quantity must be provided to the contractor; the Percent Within Limits quality measure further constrains density‑acceptance methods and contractor pay factors, so districts must follow Publication 408 Section 413.3(j) when setting acceptance criteria. Selection of a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt paving system (micro‑surfacing) is governed by Chapter 5 guidelines (section 5.4); candidate roadways must exhibit low‑severity cracking, low‑to‑medium severity raveling or weathering, friction loss and moisture infiltration, and a double application is recommended unless the surface condition is excellent. For Interstate projects a double application of Type A SRL‑E must be used in accordance with Publication 408 Specification Section 483 even when conditions are excellent, and the skid‑resistance level must be selected from Table 5.4; the mix type (A, B or RF) is limited by the depth of ruts it is intended to fill. Under the Pavement Design Analysis requirements (section 6.1), any design that requires a life‑cycle cost analysis must be submitted to the Bureau of Design Development for review and approval, and the responsible project manager or engineer must hold certification obtained by completing five DARWin designs covering full‑depth asphalt, full‑depth concrete, full‑depth reclamation, structural asphalt overlay and a concrete overlay on either concrete or asphalt. All designs must conform to Publication 242, Pavement Policy Manual, and any deviation requires a waiver approved by BODD; Federal‑Oversight projects additionally require FHWA approval and coordination with BODD. For PennDOT‑Oversight projects whose mainline pavement surface work is less than thirty thousand square yards, the type‑determination procedure described in Chapter 3 is not required provided that sufficient justification for the selected pavement type is documented in the project file. Pavement Preservation projects must be let within fifteen months of the Pavement Type Selection approval date. Section 119(e) of Title 23 USC obliges each State to develop a risk‑based asset management plan for the National Highway System, and agency policies tie pavement‑management programs to the budgeting process; the Maintenance Efficiency and Cost Effectiveness (MECE) initiative defines treatment cycles based on RMS surface type codes and functional class codes, assigns indicators automatically, and restricts changes to “C” or “U”. The Recycling Network, a subset of the Low‑Level Asphalt Network, is limited to routes with an average daily traffic less than one thousand vehicles and a truck percentage less than ten percent. Annual surface‑improvement mileage and cost must be reported in the business‑plan guidelines, and only work funded with Highway Maintenance (account 10582) dollars may be counted; program codes 381 and 383 dictate reporting categories, while Plant Maintenance M‑213 defines the surface‑improvement programs. Resurfacing work must satisfy the 3R design criteria specified in Publication 13M, and thin overlays are limited to a thickness of one inch to one‑point‑five inches identified by codes 7123, 7125 or 7135; an overlay of two inches or greater may be used only when the 3R criterion is not followed. Concrete patching receives full mileage credit only when it equals or exceeds two percent of the total area; otherwise a conversion factor of two hundred eighty square yards per mile applies. Widening projects must meet the width requirements of Publication 23 Maintenance Manual and their depth may not exceed the existing pavement structure, while shoulder upgrades (codes 7216, 7233) require a minimum base depth of four inches of asphalt or recycled equivalent. Data for Department‑force projects must be entered within four weeks after reopening to traffic and contractor projects within six weeks; Asset Management staff must complete International Roughness Index testing on newly entered pavements within three months, with IRI collected annually for NHS routes and on a two‑year cycle for non‑NHS routes. Design guidelines in Chapter 12, Section 12.3 further constrain preservation alternatives: flexible overlays greater than two inches are excluded from pavement‑preservation consideration; rigid scratch overlays may not exceed four‑and‑a‑half inches when a Superpave 9.5 mm mix is used and may not exceed five inches with a Superpave 12.5 mm mix; cold‑in‑place recycling of the base is limited to a maximum depth of four inches together with a maximum asphalt overlay thickness of one‑and‑a‑half inches, and coring must be performed to verify milling depths. Concrete patching may not exceed ten percent of the pavement area unless cost effectiveness can be demonstrated. Any geometric or bridge‑related exceptions must follow Publication 10C, while pavement‑design‑specific exceptions are directed to HDTS. Maintenance Functional Codes (MFC) can be used to determine the appropriate design criteria, and the accuracy and completeness of pavement‑history data recorded in the Systematic Technique to Analyze and Manage Pennsylvania Pavements (STAMPP) is essential for a successful pavement‑management system. Timing and implementation

The decision for Pavement Management should be made during long‑range planning and budgeting so that maintenance and rehabilitation can be programmed at the optimum time. At the network level the Pavement Asset Management System supports general planning, programming, and policy decisions tied to the budget process and agency priorities, while at the project level it guides the choice of the best treatment for a specific pavement section. [1]

[1] — Ch. 11 (pp. 190–191)

Action is triggered when a pavement condition meets a defined threshold such as wheel rut depth less than 0.5 inches, which calls for diamond grinding of Portland Cement Concrete surfaces provided the pavement is structurally sound; similarly, contractors must achieve consistent density results near the middle of the target field density range (for example 92.0% to 98.5%) for Standard and RPS construction of Wearing Courses in order to avoid reduced pay factors. For asphalt overlays, the existing pavement must be stable (non‑movement under the roller) and properly leveled, sealed and patched before the resurfacing course is placed, and if the estimated total quantity of milled material is less than 200 tons the entire quantity should be provided to the Contractor. A micro‑surfacing project is selected when the road surface exhibits low severity cracking, low to medium severity raveling or weathering, friction loss, or moisture infiltration; a double application is recommended unless the surface is in excellent condition, and on Interstate projects a double application of Type A SRL‑E is recommended even if conditions are excellent. Reconstruction is required when a pavement has either failed or become functionally obsolete, while rehabilitation is employed to restore structural and functional adequacy through resurfacing or overlays. Pavement Preservation projects must be let within 15 months of the Pavement Type Selection approval date, and any project that exceeds this limit will be required to undergo a review by the District PME/PM to re‑verify the scope of work. Action is also triggered when a pavement segment falls out of its prescribed treatment cycle – that is, when it has not been treated within the defined timeframes and therefore appears on the backlog of ‘out‑of‑cycle’ roadways – and a change in the RMS‑derived surface type code (concrete, unpaved, or asphalt) or a change in Functional Class Code updates the treatment‑network indicator at that moment, prompting reassignment to the appropriate network. Routes whose average daily traffic drops below 1000 and truck percentage is under 10 % move into the Recycling Network, which follows its own cycle and thus initiates the corresponding maintenance schedule; districts may also manually edit a segment’s indicator from “L” to “H” or vice versa based on facility usage, thereby initiating the treatment plan for the newly assigned network. Action is triggered when a surface‑improvement project reaches the fiscal year it is open to traffic, at which point mileage and cost must be reported; an annual surface‑improvement report is also required each fiscal year for the business plan and Governor’s budget request. Crack sealing on the resurfacing network must be completed on a 3 to 5‑year cycle, and any resurfacing segment that is five years or greater in age with no crack sealing completed or planned prompts a close review; the concrete network must be joint sealed on a five‑year cycle. An overlay of 2 inches or greater applied without following the 3R criterion triggers full mileage credit, while concrete patching without diamond grinding that reaches a threshold of 2 % or more of the total area also yields full mileage credit and, when below that level, the conversion of 280 square yards to one mile of surface improvement applies. Widening projects generate mileage credit once the pavement width satisfies the Publication 23 Maintenance Manual criteria provided the widening depth does not exceed the existing structure; leveling or seal‑coat treatments performed in the same fiscal year are counted only once, whereas if they occur in different fiscal years they are reported separately, and shoulder upgrades are recorded only when the improvement is at least 2 feet wide and includes a minimum depth of 4 inches of Asphalt Base Course or recycled equivalent. Under the International Roughness Index program, data for Department‑force projects should be entered within four weeks of reopening to traffic and data for contractor projects within six weeks; once entered, Pavement Testing and Asset Management runs weekly searches that commit IRI testing on the new pavements within three months of data entry, and IRI measurements are collected each year for all NHS routes and on a two‑year cycle for non‑NHS routes. When a flexible pavement exhibits rutting or other identifiable material problems, milling and overlay depths of 1 ½ inches or 2 inches may be exceeded to remove and replace the existing pavement, which triggers a preservation‑type action despite exceeding normal limits; micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing course, cold in‑place recycling (maximum depth 4 inches with up to a 1 ½‑inch asphalt overlay), and ultra‑thin concrete overlays (2‑inch asphalt depth) are applied early in the maintenance cycle before advanced distresses emerge, serving as triggers for intervention. For rigid pavements, any scratch overlay that would exceed 4 ½ inches when a Superpave 9.5 mm wearing course is used or 5 inches when a Superpave 12.5 mm mix is used is not eligible for consideration as Pavement Preservation and must follow the 3R criteria, while concrete patching exceeding 10 % of the total pavement area disqualifies a project from preservation unless cost‑effectiveness can be shown. Outcomes and follow-up

Success for Pavement Management is realized when the process consistently provides objective evidence of how funding choices affect the network, delivers cost‑effective maintenance and rehabilitation recommendations, accurately forecasts future pavement condition under alternative budget scenarios, and reliably tracks treatment performance through life‑cycle analysis; these outcomes are measured by establishing quantifiable performance measures, performing statistical analyses, and generating reports that document system condition, treatment results, and the impact of funding decisions, thereby supporting performance‑based investment decisions and demonstrating credibility and accountability. Success is achieved when each pavement segment is treated in accordance with the treatment cycles defined by the MECE initiative for its assigned network—Concrete, Unpaved, High Level Asphalt, or Low Level Asphalt—as identified by RMS surface type and Functional Class Code, and any manual edits of the network indicator from “L” to “H” or vice versa reflect actual usage while indicators cannot be changed to or from “C” or “U”; success is measured by maintaining roadways according to these cycles, which will extend pavement life and increase the time before major rehabilitation or reconstruction is necessary, and by quantifying the backlog of “out of cycle” roadways that have not been treated within the defined timeframes. Success for Pavement Management is demonstrated when each fiscal year the district can provide accurate segment‑mile totals and dollar amounts for all surface improvements that were opened to traffic, with those figures captured in the required county tables and a district summary; this accuracy is verified by progress reports run throughout the year, which track performance against the Plant Maintenance M‑213 activity list and ensure that preservation tasks such as crack sealing on the resurfacing network are completed on a 3 to 5 year cycle and joint sealing on the concrete network on a 5‑year cycle, thereby showing that funds are being allocated correctly and the pavement network is being treated as planned. Success is achieved when every surface‑improvement activity follows the specified treatment criteria and the resulting mileage is reported each year to the Governor’s Office of Budget as the program performance measure; compliance is demonstrated by meeting the thickness, patching percentage, conversion, depth, and counting rules for each category. Thin overlays must be 1 in to 1.5 in (or 2 in or greater when the 3R criterion is not followed). Concrete patching that reaches 2 % or more of the total area receives full mileage credit while less than 2 % uses a conversion of 280 square yards per mile. Shoulder upgrades require at least a 4‑in depth, and widening depths should not exceed the existing pavement structure. Mileage is counted once when treatments occur in the same fiscal year and separately when they span different years. Success for Pavement Management is achieved when the Pavement History database contains timely, complete entries for every resurfacing, reconstruction or other maintenance project and the International Roughness Index of those new surfaces is measured promptly; districts must record history data within four weeks for Department force projects and within six weeks for contractor projects, and Pavement Testing and Asset Management staff must complete IRI testing on the newly opened pavement within three months of that entry, providing measurable evidence that the system is delivering current, reliable smoothness data for analysis and decision‑making. [1]

[1] — Ch. 11 (pp. 190–197)

Results for pavement management are monitored through a combination of scheduled reviews, data tracking, and performance analyses. An annual PennDOT Oversight QA visit is carried out by a PDAU‑staff QA review team for each District regardless of certification status; the outcomes are recorded in a detailed report issued by the Director BODD to the DE and copied to FHWA, the Deputy Secretary for Highway Administration, the Bureau of Maintenance and Operations, the Pavement Testing and Asset Management Section Chief, and the PME/PM. Certified PME/PMs then use these findings to recommend approval of future Federal or PennDOT Oversight pavement designs based on sound engineering principles and conformance to Publication 242. Continuous monitoring also requires tracking the performance of selected treatments through life‑cycle analysis and establishing statistically analyzable performance measures, with all condition, traffic, construction and maintenance data entered into a central database so that objective documentation is maintained. The stored results feed an analysis program that forecasts future pavement conditions, evaluates the impact of delayed treatments or alternative funding scenarios, and selects the most cost‑effective maintenance or rehabilitation strategy for both network‑level planning and project‑level decisions. Monitoring of treatment cycles is achieved by applying the defined cycles to identify any pavement segments that are “out of cycle,” a methodology used to assess backlog; documentation occurs through the RMS Network Identification Screen where surface‑type codes and Functional Class Codes assign each segment a network indicator (C, U, H, L), with automatic updates when the RMS surface type or functional class changes and manual edits permitted only for the L/H designation. The recorded cycle status and backlog information are then fed into project selection because both maintenance cycles and reconstruction cycles must be evaluated when choosing the project type. Annual surface‑improvement results are monitored by running progress reports throughout the year and by tracking each project in MPMS, marking mileage as completed when it is “open to traffic.” Documentation requires a county‑by‑county table and a district summary that list segment miles and dollars for every activity listed in Plant Maintenance M‑213, entered into the fiscal‑year‑specific “Surface Improvement Miles” spreadsheet and the accompanying “Betterment Projects” template for each of the three Highway Maintenance funding scenarios. Reported mileages must be captured accurately and assigned to the fiscal year in which the work opens to traffic so that the amount of network treated annually can be assessed; these data are then used to justify budget requests, evaluate whether older resurfacing sections have received required 3‑to‑5‑year crack sealing or 5‑year joint sealing, and guide future maintenance planning. For resurfacing and concrete patch repair, results are monitored by recording the annual mileage for each surface‑improvement activity and comparing it against program thresholds: when concrete patching without diamond grinding reaches 2 % or more of the total area, full mileage credit is provided; when less than 2 %, 280 square yards of concrete patching equals one mile of surface improvement. Mileage is counted only once when two treatments—such as leveling with seal coat and resurfacing with CPR—occur in the same fiscal year, but it is reported separately if they occur in different years, and the associated costs for both operations must be included in the same year’s report; these yearly mileage and cost totals are submitted to the Governor’s Office of Budget as performance measures that allow planners to evaluate which treatments meet the defined criteria and adjust future resource allocation. The Bureau of Maintenance and Operations Asset Management Division monitors pavement performance system‑wide by collecting International Roughness Index (IRI) data annually for the National Highway System and on a two‑year cycle for non‑NHS routes, and by running weekly searches of Pavement History entries to trigger testing of newly resurfaced or reconstructed sections. Districts must document each maintenance or construction project in the Roadway Management System within four weeks for Department force projects and within six weeks for contractor projects; the Pavement Testing and Asset Management staff then perform IRI testing on the new pavement within three months of data entry, storing the resulting IRI values for analysis. Results of pavement evaluations are entered into the RMS, which stores detailed elements such as pavement history, condition, roughness, traffic counts and classifications, and Maintenance Functional Codes; monitoring relies on periodic STAMPP surveys that capture a snapshot of each segment and only change when the segment is resurveyed after maintenance or rehabilitation. Accuracy and completeness of these data are essential because they support selecting projects, determining the minimum treatment required, analyzing structural capacity, and applying appropriate design criteria, while field viewing and testing remain necessary to validate recorded results before they are used in later decisions.
