Saw and Seal

Part of: Asphalt Overlay is-subtopic-of
Saw and Seal is a topic under Asphalt Overlay.

Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Saw and Seal is a pavement maintenance technique used to control cracking and associated deterioration at transverse and longitudinal joints in both flexible (asphalt) and rigid (concrete) surfaces[1][2]. The method creates a uniform channel by sawing a groove typically three‑quarters of an inch wide and about one inch deep—widened to one inch and deeper for wider fissures—immediately cleaning the cut with water or high‑pressure air, then filling it with an asphalt‑based sealant whose surface is set slightly below the pavement grade[1][2]. By sealing out water infiltration, Saw and Seal preserves joint continuity, protects the underlying structure, and extends the service life of both the existing pavement and any subsequent asphalt overlay, especially when applied as a mid‑life preventive measure around twenty to thirty‑five years of service or whenever an overlay is placed[1][2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Saw and Seal is a pavement maintenance technique used to control cracking and associated deterioration at pavement joints and cracks in both flexible (asphalt) and rigid (concrete) surfaces [1][2]. The treatment targets transverse joints, which are the most common location of distress, as well as longitudinal joints that may also be affected [1][2]. By sawing a groove along an existing crack or joint to a typical width of three‑quarters inch and depth of one inch, the method creates a uniform channel that can receive sealant [2]. The freshly cut groove is then thoroughly cleaned immediately before applying the sealant, ensuring proper adhesion and sealing performance [2]. Sealing the groove limits water infiltration, which otherwise would accelerate crack propagation, joint deterioration, and loss of structural integrity at the overlay‑over‑concrete interface when an asphalt overlay is placed over a concrete base [1][2]. Consequently, Saw and Seal helps preserve the continuity of the pavement surface, protect the underlying joint or crack from further widening, and extend the service life of both the existing pavement and any subsequent asphalt overlay [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–75), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 464)

Applicability and treatment selection

Saw and seal is recommended for sealing transverse joints where an asphalt overlay is placed over a concrete pavement, but it should not be used when the chosen overlay type is micro‑surfacing; in that situation the micro‑surfacing treatment itself replaces saw‑and‑seal as the appropriate preservation method. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Condition assessment and timing

Saw and Seal is employed as a mid‑life preventive measure on concrete pavements, typically when the structure reaches about twenty years of service and again around the thirty‑five‑year mark. At these points—after earlier actions such as concrete patching, joint cleaning and sealing—the transverse joints are sawed and sealed to arrest water infiltration before more extensive cracking or rehabilitation becomes necessary. In addition, whenever an asphalt overlay is placed on an existing concrete pavement, the transverse joints of the new asphalt layer that coincide with the underlying concrete joints must be cut and sealed, except when the overlay consists of a micro‑surfacing treatment. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 73–74), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Materials and repair details

The Saw and Seal technique begins with a thorough removal of debris, old sealant, and any foreign material from the crack or joint before any cutting is performed [1][2]. A saw or grinder then creates a groove whose width is at least three‑quarters of an inch; when the existing fissure measures between three‑quarters and one inch the cut is widened to one inch, and the depth is targeted to be one and a quarter inches for such cases, while the minimum final depth required for all joints is one and a half inches, with many applications extending the groove to one‑and‑a‑half or two inches deep [2][1]. Immediately after sawing the groove is flushed with water, and if water‑blasting is employed the equipment must deliver no less than ten gallons per minute at 7 000 psi; alternatively, a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi measured at the source may be used for cleaning [2]. The sealant applied is an asphalt‑based material, and when additional protection is desired a heavy‑duty membrane backing can be placed beneath the sealant within the groove [1]. Placement of the sealant is permitted only when both ambient air temperature and pavement temperature are 40 °F or higher, and it is installed so that its surface lies between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the finished pavement grade as defined in the applicable specifications [2]. After the sealant has cured, the joint is commonly finished with a seal coat or a micro‑surface treatment applied to the shoulders to provide a smooth riding surface, especially on transverse joints that have been sawed and sealed across concrete or asphalt overlays [1]. The process is used on both concrete pavements and existing asphalt surfaces, including any overlay that will be sawed and sealed over transverse or patch joints, and it is normally performed on all transverse joints of the pavement structure [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–75); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 393–464)

The performance of a Saw‑and‑Seal treatment is governed by the interaction of equipment characteristics, dowel bar specifications, crack geometry, environmental conditions, and the condition of the existing pavement surface. A power‑driven, self‑propelled saw that can make two parallel cuts per dowel slot must be used; any equipment that could cause spalling of the pavement or fracture of surface aggregates is prohibited. Slots are cut at the mid‑depth of the slab, or at the thinner slab when thickness varies, with the bottom of each slot flush with the roadway surface, and all debris must be removed within the prescribed time window before concrete placement.

When dowel bars are installed, Type B coated bars require a bond‑breaker lubricant unless one has already been applied in the shop; Type A coated bars do not need lubricant. Chairs are placed on the bottom of each slot and expansion caps are provided with clearances that meet the specified limits. The dowel bar must be positioned parallel to the centreline, with vertical tilt or horizontal skew limited to 0.25 inch, longitudinal translation not exceeding one inch, and horizontal translation also limited to one inch.

The crack itself must be prepared to the required dimensions: a width of three‑quarters of an inch and a depth of one inch; if the existing opening is between three‑quarters and one inch wide, it should be widened to one inch with a depth of 1¼ inches. All existing sealant, asphalt, or foreign material must be removed, and the saw‑cut joint cleaned to at least 1½ inches deep (¾ inch for formed construction joints). Immediately after cutting, the joint is flushed with water to eliminate residual particles.

Sealant placement is permitted only when both ambient air temperature and pavement temperature are equal to or exceed 40 °F. The sealant reservoir is then filled so that its surface lies between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface, providing proper adhesion and durability. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 393–464)

Treatment procedures

The saw‑and‑seal operation starts with a thorough preparation of the crack or longitudinal joint. All existing sealant, loose asphalt, and any foreign material must be removed so that the joint surface is clean. The original saw‑cut joint is then cut back to the required depth—at least 1½ inches for an existing cut, or at least ¾ inch for a formed construction joint; where individual cracks are being treated the groove is typically sized to three‑quarters of an inch wide and one inch deep, with larger dimensions permitted for wider cracks. Immediately after the sawing or grinding step the groove is flushed with water; if water blasting is employed the equipment must be capable of delivering at least 10 gpm at 7 000 psi. The cleaned faces are then dried, often using a compressed‑air stream of no less than 100 psi measured at the source, until they satisfy the Representative’s acceptance criteria. Sealant may only be placed when both ambient air temperature and pavement temperature are 40 °F or higher. The sealant is introduced into the groove so that its surface finishes between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the existing pavement surface, in accordance with Section 501.3(n). No additional curing step is specified beyond allowing the sealant to set under the prevailing temperature conditions. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 393–464)

The operation begins with a power‑driven, self‑propelled saw capable of cutting two parallel grooves to the required dimensions of roughly three‑quarters of an inch in width and one inch in depth. Immediately after the cuts are made, the groove must be flushed with water; a water‑blaster delivering at least ten gallons per minute at seven thousand pounds per square inch satisfies this requirement. All loose or foreign material must be removed no later than twenty‑four hours before sealant placement, and a final cleaning using compressed air of at least one hundred pounds per square inch—free of oil, moisture, and other contaminants—must be completed within thirty minutes of sealant application. The sealant is then placed to a depth between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface. Placement may only occur when both ambient air temperature and pavement temperature are at least forty degrees Fahrenheit, ensuring proper curing. Traffic cannot be reopened until the cure time specified in Section 516.3(q) has been satisfied, and equipment that could cause spalling of the pavement must be avoided throughout the process. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 393–464)

Quality and workmanship

The inspector must first verify that the cutting equipment conforms to the specification: a power‑driven, self‑propelled saw capable of producing two parallel cuts for each dowel slot. The cuts are required to be centered in the slab at approximately mid‑depth and aligned with the roadway centreline. For typical cracks the groove should be cut to three‑quarters of an inch wide by one inch deep; where the existing crack is wider, the groove may be enlarged to one inch wide by 1¼ inches deep. All longitudinal joints must be cleared of any residual sealant, asphalt or foreign material and the original saw‑cut joints cleaned to at least one‑and‑half inches depth (construction joints to three‑quarters of an inch). Immediately after cutting each joint is to be flushed with water; if a water‑blaster is used it must deliver the prescribed flow and pressure. The exposed faces are then required to be cleaned again within 24 hours, with all loose material removed, and a final air‑blast cleaning completed no later than thirty minutes before concrete placement, using compressed air of at least one hundred pounds per square inch measured at the source.

The dowel bars and their temporary joint material must be prepared and lubricated as indicated in the contract documents. Placement tolerances are strict: each end of a dowel may not exceed a vertical tilt or horizontal skew of a quarter‑inch relative to its centreline, and overall translation may not exceed one inch in any direction. Once the concrete is placed the slot should be vibrated to achieve consolidation and then finished so that the surface profile matches the surrounding pavement.

Sealant application is permitted only when both ambient air temperature and pavement temperature are forty degrees Fahrenheit or higher. After the sealant is placed it must be packed into the joint leaving a surface recess of roughly one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the existing pavement surface, and the transverse joint sealant reservoirs must be sealed across the full lane width.

Finally, the completed work is inspected for conformity with the specified surface finish, proper sealant level, and that all reservoirs are fully sealed. Any deviation from the dimensional tolerances, cleaning intervals, temperature limits, or sealant recession criteria constitutes a non‑conforming condition. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 393–464)

A satisfactory Saw and Seal treatment is judged by a series of dimensional, cleanliness, environmental and placement tolerances that must all be met before the work can be accepted. For ordinary transverse cracks the groove is cut or ground to a width of three‑quarters of an inch and a depth of one inch; if the existing crack already measures between three‑quarters and one inch wide the cutter is set to produce a one‑inch wide, 1¼‑inch deep slot. Existing longitudinal saw‑cut joints require a deeper groove, at least one‑and‑a‑half inches, while formed construction joints are only required to be three‑quarters of an inch deep. After sawing, every face must be thoroughly cleared of all prior sealant, asphalt and debris and then flushed with water; when water blasting is used the equipment must supply a flow of ten gallons per minute at 7,000 psi. The cleaned surfaces are dried, typically with compressed air that reaches a minimum of one hundred pounds per square inch at the source, and no further work proceeds until the supervising Representative confirms the cleanliness. Sealing may only commence when both ambient air temperature and pavement temperature are forty degrees Fahrenheit or higher. The sealant is placed so that its final surface finishes between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the existing pavement level, providing the required protection against water intrusion. [2]
