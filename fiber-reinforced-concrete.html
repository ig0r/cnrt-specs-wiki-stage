Fiber-Reinforced Concrete

Part of: Portland Cement Concrete is-subtopic-of
Fiber-Reinforced Concrete is a topic under Portland Cement Concrete.
synthetic-macrofibersresidual-strengthfiber-contentfiber-contents
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

Fiber‑Reinforced Concrete (FRC) is a Portland cement concrete that incorporates synthetic, steel, or virgin polypropylene macrofibers as reinforcement in the mix [1][2]. The fibers function as crack‑bridging reinforcements that develop a measurable residual tensile strength (f150)—typically 100 to 225 psi—after cracking; this residual strength is added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength, enabling thinner overlays with higher flexural and ultimate load capacities for heavy traffic, variable support conditions, or extended design life [1][2]. Specified fibers are monofilament or bundled macrofibers 1 to 2½ in long (aspect ratio ≤150) dosed at roughly 0.2–0.5 % by volume (maximum 5 lb per cubic yard), which can reduce fresh‑mix slump by up to 4 in but improve post‑cracking toughness, limit crack widths to less than about 1 mm, and raise load‑transfer efficiency by approximately 30 % [1][2].

Material purpose and applicability

Fiber‑Reinforced Concrete (FRC) is a Portland cement concrete that incorporates synthetic, steel, or virgin polypropylene monofilament macrofibers as reinforcement in the mix. [1][2] The inclusion of these macrofibers raises the flexural and ultimate load capacity of the slab, giving the overlay additional structural capacity. [1] For overlays where thinner slabs, higher traffic loads, variable support conditions, or longer design lives are desired, FRC is employed. [1] In unbonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements with thicknesses less than 7 inches (Section 548, 548.2), the material must contain 100 % virgin polypropylene monofilament or bundled fibers whose length is at least 1.0 inch and not more than 2½ inches, an aspect ratio that does not exceed 150, and it is limited to a maximum dosage rate of 5.0 pounds per cubic yard unless workability can be demonstrated; the fibers are added to achieve a residual strength ratio (R150,3) of 20.0 percent, thereby reinforcing the overlay. [2] For thin bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 541, 541.2), synthetic or steel macrofibers required by Section 708.1(a) are added at a rate sufficient to meet the design residual strength value (f150) specified in Table B, and this contribution is verified by conducting ASTM C1609‑12 testing at 14 days to calculate residual strength versus fiber volume fraction, providing the overlay with the required tensile capacity for thin applications. [2] By limiting crack widths and preserving joint or crack load‑transfer efficiency, FRC reduces slab movement, misalignment, plastic shrinkage cracking, and overall crack deterioration, which extends the pavement’s serviceability. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 463–470)

Fiber‑Reinforced Concrete is appropriate for thin bonded concrete overlays (typically 3 to 6 in. thick) placed over asphalt or composite pavements when a thinner slab, higher traffic with heavy repeated loads, variable support conditions, or an extended design/service life are desired, and it also performs well in freeze‑thaw climates; however, if the existing pavement is poor or deteriorated such that an unbonded overlay is required, or where joints need dowel bars for faulting control, another material should be selected instead of relying on fibers. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

Composition and characteristics

Fiber‑Reinforced Concrete (FRC) is a concrete material in which a conventional Portland cement matrix incorporates synthetic or steel macrofibers as reinforcement [1][2]. For pavement overlay applications the fibers are typically dosed at 0.2 % to 0.5 % of the mix volume, are 1 to 2.5 in long with an aspect ratio of 30 to 100, and may be round, rectangular or otherwise textured; this geometry together with material properties governs the residual strength (f150) which usually falls between 100 and 225 psi and is added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength [1]. In unbonded concrete overlays the specification requires Class AA cement concrete reinforced with 100 % virgin polypropylene (PE) fibers supplied as a monofilament or bundled monofilament having a length between 1.0 in and 2½ in and an aspect ratio not exceeding 150 [2]. The fiber dosage in such unbonded overlays must not exceed 5.0 lb per cubic yard unless a field demonstration shows the mix remains workable, and enough fibers shall be added to obtain a residual strength ratio (R150,3) of 20.0 percent [2]. For thin bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements with an overlay thickness less than three inches, No. 67 coarse aggregate is used in place of No. 57, and fibers are added at a rate sufficient to achieve the design residual flexural strength (f150) as determined by ASTM C1609‑12 testing at 14 days [2]. The mix for these overlays must also satisfy a permeability requirement of low, very low or negligible chloride ion penetration after a curing period of fifty‑six days or less; if a moderate rating is accepted the chloride charge may not exceed 2,800 coulombs (AASHTO T 277) or the resistivity may not be lower than 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4×8 in cylinders or 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6×12 in cylinders (AASHTO T 358) [2]. The inclusion of fibers reduces fresh‑mix workability, lowering slump by up to 4 in and can alter air content requiring admixture adjustments [1]. In the hardened state the fibers retain residual strength after many freeze/thaw cycles, improve crack‑width control, increase load‑transfer efficiency by about 30 % higher, and enhance flexural fatigue performance without increasing compressive or basic flexural strength [1].

Variations in the type, geometry, stiffness and surface characteristics of synthetic macrofibers alter the residual strength (f150) of concrete overlays, so different fibers require distinct dosage levels to achieve a target f150; consequently fiber content may be set within the typical range of 0.2 % to 0.5 % by volume [1]. Increasing the amount of fiber or selecting stiffer fibers can raise the effective flexural strength and load‑transfer efficiency, yet the addition of macrofibers reduces workability, with slump potentially decreasing by up to 4 in (100 mm) unless water‑reducing admixtures are employed and air‑entraining admixture may need adjustment; these compositional changes improve post‑cracking toughness, flexural fatigue performance and can increase the load‑transfer efficiency of fiber‑reinforced concrete by about 30 % when crack widths exceed 1.0 mm, while they do not affect compressive or basic flexural strengths [1]. For unbonded concrete overlays thinner than 7 in, the specification mandates that the synthetic reinforcement consist of 100 % virgin polypropylene fibers manufactured to an optimum gradation and supplied as monofilament or bundled‑monofilament with lengths no shorter than 1.0 inch and no longer than 2½ inches and an aspect ratio not exceeding 150; this geometry directly controls the residual strength ratio, which must reach 20 % (R150,3) [2]. The fiber dosage in such overlays may not exceed 5.0 lb per cubic yard, and any increase beyond this limit is permitted only when a field demonstration shows that the mix remains workable and that fiber clumping does not occur because higher proportions can impair workability [2]. In thin‑bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements whose thickness is less than 3 inches, the aggregate gradation is altered by using No. 67 coarse aggregate instead of No. 57, a change that influences strength development [2]. The amount of synthetic or steel macrofiber must be proportioned so that the design residual strength value (f150) is achieved; a 14‑day ASTM C1609‑12 test relates residual strength to fiber volume fraction, and higher fiber fractions raise the measured residual strength [2]. Additionally, the concrete mixture must be designed to meet a target chloride‑ion penetration classified as Low, Very Low, or Negligible after curing for 56 days or less, indicating that variations in cementitious content, admixtures, or curing affect durability thresholds [2].

Key material properties

The performance of fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays is governed by an integrated set of mechanical, physical, chemical and thermal attributes. Mechanically, the residual tensile strength designated f150 typically falls between 100 psi and 225 psi, and together with the resulting effective flexural strength (feff) it controls flexural capacity and load‑transfer efficiency, which may be increased by 30 % relative to plain concrete when crack widths exceed 1.0 mm. In unbonded overlays thinner than 7 in., the mechanical contribution of the fibers is quantified by a residual strength ratio R150,3 of 20.0 percent, which governs tensile performance under service loads. The fiber dosage shall not exceed 5.0 lb per cubic yard unless field testing demonstrates that the mixture remains workable without fiber clumping. Physically, the macrofibers are specified with lengths ranging from 1 in. to 2.5 in., and their shape and surface texture influence the bond with the cement matrix that develops the residual strength. For Pennsylvania specifications, synthetic fibers must be monofilament or bundled monofilament having a minimum length of 1.0 in., a maximum length of 2½ in., and may have an aspect ratio up to 150. The Pavement Policy Manual further recommends aspect ratios between 30 and 100 to optimize interaction with the matrix. Chemically, the fibers are required to be 100 % virgin polypropylene (PE) to provide the prescribed composition of the reinforcement material. The inclusion of these fibers can reduce fresh‑mix slump by as much as 4 in., necessitating adjustments to water‑reducing or air‑entraining admixtures in order to satisfy workability and air‑content specifications. In addition, the concrete mix must be designed to achieve a Low, Very Low, or Negligible chloride ion penetration rating after a curing period of 56 days or less, as measured by AASHTO T 277 or T 358. If a Moderate chloride‑penetration rating is accepted, the mixture may not exceed 2,800 coulombs per AASHTO T 277 and must meet resistivity limits of 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4‑in × 8‑in cylinders and 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6‑in × 12‑in cylinders. Thermally, the macrofibers help retain significant residual strength after repeated freeze/thaw cycles, thereby improving fatigue performance, post‑cracking strength and overall toughness of the overlay. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 463–470)

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10) the performance of fiber‑reinforced concrete used in pavement overlays is quantified primarily by measuring its residual strength (f150) in a flexural test prescribed by ASTM C1609, and this residual strength is added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength (feff) for structural design; for overlay applications the residual strength typically ranges between 100 and 225 psi [1]. Fibers in that context are classified according to the residual strength they can deliver rather than by a fixed volume or weight fraction, with typical fiber dosages for overlays ranging from 0.2% to 0.5% by volume depending on design objectives [1]. In the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfaced Pavements quantifies tensile durability of polypropylene‑fiber reinforced concrete by requiring a residual strength ratio (R150,3) of 20.0 percent as determined with the Illinois Modified ASTM C1609 test method [2]. The same specification characterizes the fibers themselves by imposing a minimum length of 1.0 inch, a maximum length of 2½ inches, and an aspect‑ratio limit of 150, and limits the mixture to no more than 5.0 lb of fiber per cubic yard unless field testing demonstrates acceptable workability and absence of clumping [2]. Section 541—Thin Bonded Concrete Overlay of Asphalt‑Surfaced Pavement characterizes residual tensile strength by the design residual strength value f150, which is obtained by running ASTM C1609‑12 on a 14‑day specimen and relating the result to the fiber volume fraction [2]. Permeability or chloride ion penetration for these overlays is classified using AASHTO T 277 or T 358 after up to 56 days of curing; acceptable categories are designated as Low, Very Low, Negligible, or, at discretion, Moderate, and a Moderate rating requires that test results not exceed 2,800 coulombs on T 277 or remain below 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑inch by 8‑inch cylinders and 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑inch by 12‑inch cylinders on T 358 [2].

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 463–470)

Behavior and mechanisms

Under repeated vehicular loading, macro‑fiber reinforced slabs exhibit higher flexural and ultimate load capacities than plain concrete, which translates into an effective flexural strength that includes the residual strength (f150) measured by ASTM C1609; this also raises the load transfer efficiency at joints by as much as 30% when crack widths exceed 1.0 mm. The fibers do not raise the basic compressive or uncracked flexural strength but they do enhance post‑cracking toughness and fatigue performance, allowing the overlay to retain significant residual strength after many freeze/thaw cycles. By limiting crack opening to less than about 0.5–1.0 mm, the macrofibers improve durability and maintain joint LTE under repeated loading. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

In the pavement overlay context described in the Pavement Policy Manual (section 10.10), the primary mechanical mechanism governing fiber‑reinforced concrete is the bridging action of synthetic macrofibers, which supplies a quantifiable residual tensile strength (f150) after cracking and thereby increases effective flexural capacity and slab load‑transfer efficiency[1]. Physically, the fibers’ material composition, geometry, stiffness and surface texture control their bond to the cement matrix and their ability to span cracks; this bond reduction in crack number and width helps maintain joint longitudinal tensile effort (LTE) comparable to that provided by tie bars[1]. Chemically, inclusion of macrofibers may necessitate adjustments to water‑reducing or air‑entraining admixtures to retain workability and target air content, although such admixture modifications do not change the underlying mechanical bridging mechanism[1]. The thin bonded concrete overlay specification (section 541.2) states that the mechanical response is also governed by the quantity of synthetic macrofiber added so that the design residual tensile strength (f150) prescribed in Table B is achieved, with verification performed by ASTM C1609‑12 at 14 days and expressed as a function of fiber volume fraction[2]. Early‑age strength development in this overlay is linked to the maturity method, whereby degree‑hours of cure are correlated to compressive strength according to PTM No. 640, providing a time‑dependent mechanical property evolution[2]. Durability mechanisms are controlled by concrete permeability; the specification requires that chloride ion penetration be classified as Low, Very Low, or Negligible after a curing period not exceeding 56 days and that measured values do not exceed 2,800 coulombs (AASHTO T 277) or the prescribed kΩ‑cm limits from AASHTO T 358[2].

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 463)

Durability and deterioration

Fiber‑Reinforced Concrete overlays in pavement are vulnerable to freeze–thaw cycling; although the material can retain significant residual strength after many freeze/thaw cycles, fibers do not increase compressive or flexural strength and therefore cannot fully prevent damage [1]. Shrinkage‑induced cracking and uncontrolled crack growth also affect durability, but macrofibers help by reducing the number of cracks and limiting average crack widths often to less than 0.5–1.0 mm, which improves load‑transfer efficiency at contraction joints under repeated loading [1]. Proper timing and depth of saw‑cut joints are required to activate the joint early enough to avoid premature cracking and dominant joints, and because fibers do not substitute for dowel bars, joints that require fault‑control still need conventional reinforcement [1]. The durability of thin‑bonded concrete overlays that contain synthetic macrofibers is controlled primarily through limits on permeability to chloride ions; the mix must achieve a target classification of Low, Very Low or Negligible chloride ion penetration within 56 days according to AASHTO T 277 or AASHTO T 358 [2]. If a Moderate level of penetration is permitted, it may not exceed 2,800 coulombs per AASHTO T 277, and the resistance values must be at least 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑inch by 8‑inch cylinders and 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑inch by 12‑inch cylinders as specified in AASHTO T 358 [2].

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 463)

Material characteristics that lower the susceptibility of fiber‑reinforced concrete (FRC) to deterioration include achieving a higher residual strength—typically between 100 and 225 psi for overlay applications—and providing an appropriate macrofiber content of 0.2 % to 0.5 % by volume that yields those strengths; synthetic fibers with suitable geometry, stiffness and surface texture also help maintain performance [1]. Exposure conditions that are less damaging are those in which the concrete experiences many freeze‑thaw cycles, because FRC has been shown to retain significant residual strength even after a large number of such cycles [1]. Larger crack openings increase vulnerability, whereas tighter joint and crack openings—less than 0.5 to 1.0 mm—result in improved durability [1]. A concrete mix that attains the specified low‑permeability targets—designing for a chloride ion penetration classified as Low, Very Low, or Negligible after a curing period of fifty‑six days or less—reduces its susceptibility to deterioration [2]. If only Moderate penetration is permitted, the mix must not exceed 2,800 coulombs on AASHTO T 277 or resistivity limits of 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4‑in × 8‑in cylinders (or 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6‑in × 12‑in cylinders) on AASHTO T 358, thereby constraining the exposure‑related risk [2].

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 463)

Material interactions and compatibility

In fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays the macrofibers develop a bond with the cement matrix that provides the residual strength (f150) used to raise the effective flexural strength, with residual strength values for overlay applications typically ranging between 100 and 225 psi. This bond also affects fresh properties; the addition of macrofibers will result in a decrease in workability and can reduce slump by up to 4 in, so water‑reducing admixtures or other mix modifications are normally used to restore slump and finishability. Because fibers may alter entrapped air, the air‑entraining admixture is typically adjusted during trial batches to meet air content specifications. The fiber‑matrix interaction improves flexural fatigue performance, post‑cracking strength and toughness of the hardened concrete, but it does not improve compressive or flexural strengths. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

Influence of production and placement

Adding macrofibers to the concrete mix reduces its workability, with slump potentially dropping up to 4 in (100 mm) depending on fiber type and dosage; this loss can generally be offset by water‑reducing admixtures or other mixture tweaks, which also help maintain finishability. Because fibers may alter air content, the air‑entraining admixture should be adjusted during trial batches so that the fresh‑property specifications are met, ensuring consistent placement and compaction performance. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

The construction variations that most affect the long‑term behavior of fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays are the timing and depth of sawcut contraction joints, the workability loss caused by fiber addition, and the need to adjust air‑entraining admixture. When sawcuts are not placed at the proper time and depth, premature cracking can develop and become a dominant joint, reducing durability. The addition of macrofibers may reduce slump by up to 4 in (100 mm), so without water‑reducing admixtures or other mix modifications the workability loss could impair placement and finishability, leading to defects such as slab movement or plastic shrinkage cracking. Because fibers also affect air content, failure to adjust the air‑entraining admixture during trial batches may compromise fresh‑property specifications and later performance. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

Selection, specification, and improvement

For a concrete overlay, the primary performance parameter that guides material selection is the required residual strength (f150) needed to satisfy design objectives such as slab flexural capacity, service life, crack‑width criteria and joint load‑transfer efficiency. The pavement engineer specifies this residual strength while the contractor selects the fiber type and dosage that will achieve it, with the macrofiber—synthetic or steel—chosen on the basis of its ability to deliver the specified residual strength rather than its geometry, shape, or surface texture. For unbonded concrete overlays less than 7 inches thick, the specification mandates a mix reinforced with 100 % virgin polypropylene monofilament or bundled‑monofilament fibers whose length is at least 1.0 inch and not more than 2½ inches and whose aspect ratio does not exceed 150. That mix must attain a residual strength ratio (R150,3) of 20.0 percent, and the fiber dosage may not exceed 5.0 pounds per cubic yard unless a field demonstration proves that the mixture remains workable without clumping. For thin bonded concrete overlays placed over asphalt‑surfaced pavements, synthetic or steel macrofibers are required in accordance with Section 708.1(a), and the fiber content is calibrated to achieve the design residual strength value (f150) using Table B together with ASTM C1609‑12 performed at 14 days to relate residual strength to fiber volume fraction. Across all overlay applications, typical fiber contents range from 0.2 % to 0.5 % by volume, and the mix must accommodate the associated loss of workability because slump can be reduced by up to 4 in (100 mm), which may require adjustments to air‑entraining admixture verified through trial batches. In addition, the concrete mixture must meet a permeability target of Low, Very Low, or Negligible chloride ion penetration after a curing period of 56 days or less as measured by AASHTO T 277 or T 358, although Moderate penetration may be accepted provided it does not exceed 2,800 coulombs on T 277 or the specified kΩ‑cm limits on T 358. [1][2]

For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, engineers are advised to specify the required residual strength instead of a fixed fiber volume and to confirm compliance through trial batches that are always recommended to verify that the mixture meets all fresh property specifications. Because macrofibers can reduce slump by up to 4 in. (100 mm), the addition of water‑reducing admixtures or other mixture modifications may be used to compensate for the slump loss so that workability remains acceptable, and air‑entraining admixture can be adjusted as needed. Typical fiber contents for concrete overlays range from 0.2 % to 0.5 % by volume, with target residual strengths between 100 and 225 psi, which may be estimated with the Residual Strength Estimator spreadsheet or measured in accordance with ASTM C1609. Selecting synthetic macrofibers—easier to handle and less prone to balling—or steel macrofibers provides alternative reinforcement options, the choice being based on residual‑strength performance in the particular concrete matrix. For unbonded concrete overlays described in SECTION 548.2, performance may be enhanced by incorporating 100 % virgin polypropylene fibers that are monofilament or bundled monofilament with a minimum length of 1.0 inches and a maximum length of 2 ½ inches and a maximum aspect ratio of 150. The mix should be dosed so that the residual strength ratio (R150,3) reaches 20.0 percent as measured by the Illinois Modified ASTM C1609 procedure. Fiber dosage must not exceed 5.0 lb per cubic yard unless a field demonstration proves that the concrete mixture remains workable and free of clumping, and mixing shall follow the manufacturer’s recommendation. For thin bonded concrete overlays described in SECTION 541.2, the mix may be adjusted to meet a design residual strength (f150) by adding fibers at the rate specified in Table B and verifying the resulting strength with an ASTM C1609‑12 test conducted at 14 days, which also yields the relationship between residual strength and fiber volume fraction. When the overlay thickness is less than 3 inches, substituting No. 67 coarse aggregate for No. 57 helps maintain strength. The mixture must be designed to achieve a chloride‑ion penetration rating of Low, Very Low, or Negligible after a curing period of 56 days or less as measured by AASHTO T 277 or T 358; if a Moderate rating is accepted, the limits shall not exceed 2,800 coulombs (AASHTO T 277) or 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑by‑8‑in cylinders and 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑by‑12‑in cylinders (AASHTO T 358). [1][2]

Performance evaluation and implications

In Section 10.10 of the Pavement Policy Manual, the evaluation of fiber‑reinforced concrete relies on laboratory slab tests that quantify flexural and ultimate load capacity and the resulting load transfer efficiency compared with plain concrete, with the residual‑strength parameter f150 obtained per ASTM C1609. The same section reports field investigations of bonded or unbonded overlays in overlay projects that have shown consistently better performance than comparable plain‑concrete slabs. Long‑term durability in that section is demonstrated by tests indicating that FRC retains significant residual strength after a large number of freeze/thaw cycles, supporting its ability to limit crack growth and maintain joint load transfer efficiency over service life. In Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements, the performance of polypropylene‑fiber reinforced concrete overlays is evaluated by applying the residual strength test defined in the Illinois Modified ASTM C1609 and confirming that the residual strength ratio R150,3 reaches at least 20.0 percent. The mix design in that section limits fiber addition to no more than 5.0 pounds per cubic yard unless the manufacturer can demonstrate through a field demonstration that the concrete mixture will be workable and that fiber clumping is not a problem. Additionally, the overlay must meet Performance Level 1 as referenced in Section 21 Note 17. In Section 541—Thin Bonded Concrete Overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavement, laboratory evaluation includes running ASTM C1609‑12 at 14 days and relating the residual flexural strength to the fiber volume fraction for the selected fiber type. Compressive‑strength development in that section is tracked with the maturity method using degree‑hours correlated to strengths measured at 8 hours, 1 day, 7 days, 28 days and 56 days. Durability assessment in that section is performed by chloride‑ion penetration tests in accordance with AASHTO T 277 or T 358, requiring a target classification of Low, Very Low, or Negligible after a curing period of 56 days or less; moderate penetration levels are permitted only if the total charge does not exceed 2,800 coulombs or the electrical resistance does not exceed 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑inch × 8‑inch cylinders and 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑inch × 12‑inch cylinders. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 463–470)

Fiber‑reinforced concrete used in pavement overlays increases flexural and ultimate load capacity and raises the load transfer efficiency between slabs, which permits designers to reduce overlay thickness or slab size when the residual strength (f150)—typically 100 to 225 psi for overlay applications—is added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength used in mechanistic‑empirical design programs; achieving this residual strength requires adding enough synthetic macrofibers and verifying it by ASTM C1609‑12 testing at 14 days, thereby linking fiber volume fraction to tensile performance of the pavement. [1][2] The macrofibers also limit crack development, keeping joint and crack openings generally less than 0.5 to 1.0 mm, which improves durability by reducing crack widening and maintaining load transfer efficiency that can be up to 30% higher than plain concrete; this resistance to crack growth persists after many freeze‑thaw cycles. [1] In construction, adding synthetic macrofibers at 0.2%–0.5% volume may lower workability, with slump reductions of up to 4 in., but water‑reducing or air‑entraining admixtures can be adjusted in trial mixes to meet fresh‑property specifications. [1] The mix must also be designed to meet a target chloride ion penetration classified as Low, Very Low, or Negligible after a curing period of 56 day or less, with an optional Moderate level limited to not exceed 2,800 coulombs (AASHTO T 277) or the resistance values given in AASHTO T 358; these permeability limits directly influence durability against chloride‑induced deterioration. [2] When the overlay thickness is less than 3 inches, the specification calls for No. 67 coarse aggregate instead of No. 57, a change that interacts with the fiber reinforcement to maintain performance. [2] The combined structural and durability benefits translate into longer service life and less frequent maintenance such as crack sealing or joint repair. [1]
