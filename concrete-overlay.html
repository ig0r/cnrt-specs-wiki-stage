Concrete Overlay

Part of: Overlay is-subtopic-of
Concrete Overlay is a topic under Overlay.
concrete-overlayconcrete-overlaysconcrete-overlay-s
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-29

Concrete overlay is a structural rehabilitation technique applied to existing pavements or bridge decks that have lost adequate load‑bearing capacity or functional performance because of distresses such as spalling, cracking, rutting, loss of friction and poor drainage. By placing an additional layer—typically one to eight inches of fiber‑reinforced Portland‑cement concrete—directly on a prepared substrate (bonded when the underlying slab is sound, or unbonded with a separation interlayer when it is not), the new slab forms a monolithic system that restores uniform flexural strength, improves ride quality and extends service life for eight to twenty years [1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Concrete Overlay” also include the following subtopics: Bond Breaker, Bonded Concrete Overlay, and Unbonded Concrete Overlay.

Concrete Overlay employs Bond Breaker as a separation interlayer when the substrate cannot support monolithic action, resulting in an Unbonded Concrete Overlay that treats the existing pavement as a subbase. [1][2] When the underlying slab remains sound, the technique utilizes Bonded Concrete Overlay to create a single structural unit with improved load transfer and flexural strength. [1][2]

Purpose and design objectives

Concrete overlay is applied when an existing pavement or bridge deck no longer provides adequate structural support or functional performance due to a range of distresses such as spalling, cracking, rutting, shoving, slippage, thermal cracking, loss of load‑transfer at transverse joints, low‑friction surfaces, inadequate drainage, insufficient shoulder width, deteriorated slab material, loose or moving slabs, surface voids and corrosion. The primary engineering need addressed by the overlay is to restore uniform load‑bearing capacity and ride quality while extending service life for a design period of at least eight years up to twenty years. To meet this need the overlay provides an additional concrete layer—typically three to six inches of fiber‑reinforced Portland‑cement concrete when placed over asphalt bases, or a thickness sufficient to raise the load‑carrying capacity of existing concrete decks—creating a monolithic slab that increases flexural strength and overall bearing capacity. When the underlying pavement is in good‑to‑fair condition, a bonded overlay is placed directly on the prepared surface so that the new concrete acts as a single slab with the existing material; when moderate to severe distress exists, an unbonded system may be used after stabilizing or repairing the substrate. Prior to placement, unstable slabs and moving fragments must be stabilized or repaired, surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch are filled with patching material, cracks wider than three‑quarters of an inch are cleaned and sealed with approved non‑shrink grout, standing water is removed, and the existing surface is scarified or hydro‑demolished within seven days and fully cleaned within twenty‑four hours to provide a clean, rust‑free base. The overlay thickness must be at least one inch, with panel dimensions limited to eighteen times the thickness and length‑to‑width ratios not exceeding 1.5, and when the thickness is less than three inches No. 67 coarse aggregate together with macrofibers is required to achieve the specified residual strength (f150). The concrete mix is designed to attain a compressive strength of at least 3,600 psi before traffic is allowed and an early strength of 3,000 psi for bond testing, while the vertical pull‑bond test performed 24–72 hours after curing must demonstrate a minimum bond strength of 250 psi (average ≥175 psi and no individual result below 160 psi). Surface finish requirements include applying abrasive sand at a rate of at least 2.2 lb per square yard immediately after placement to provide adequate friction and checking surface tolerances to prevent delamination, cracking or tearing under traffic loads. By providing a uniform wearing layer that restores required strength, improves friction, enhances drainage, allows for joint rehabilitation, slab stabilization, and the addition of concrete shoulders or widened lanes of one to two feet, the overlay satisfies both structural deficiencies and functional performance needs. [2] The existing pavement surface shall be cleaned, dried and free of oil, with final preparation completed no more than thirty minutes prior to concrete placement to assure uniform bearing and reliable bond. [1][2] The overlay thickness is selected by acceptable procedures such as non‑destructive testing, rounded up to the nearest half‑inch, and must fall within the minimum and maximum limits prescribed—typically between 2.5 in and 4 in for thin‑bonded applications or 4 in to 8 in for conventional bonded designs, with an absolute lower bound of one inch. [1][2] When a composite base is present, slab stabilization and uniform support are required together with a continuous pavement‑base drain, and any areas of obvious failure in the base must be removed and replaced with material that supplies adequate structural capacity and facilitates drainage. [1] All failed transverse joints are to be rehabilitated, cleaned and sealed for unbonded overlays, and load‑transfer capability is provided through dowel‑bar retrofits or fully bonded dowels spaced at the standard thirty feet for reinforced concrete (or fifteen feet for plain concrete) with coated bars and appropriate joint seals; tie bars are installed in patches longer than sixty‑five feet. [1] Panel dimensions shall not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the ratio of the longer to shorter side shall be limited to one point five, while longitudinal joints are prohibited in wheel paths and joint spacing follows the design plans. [2] Saw cuts must be made to a depth of at least one‑third of slab thickness with a minimum width of one‑eighth inch, placed at least one‑third of slab depth from edges and at one‑quarter depth elsewhere, and bond strength requirements call for a pull‑off value of no less than 250 psi (average at least 175 psi and no individual test below 160 psi) after the concrete reaches three thousand psi compressive strength. [2] Traffic may be opened only when compressive strength reaches 3,600 psi and the required bond strength is achieved, and the surface must be textured with abrasive sand applied at a minimum rate of 2.2 lb per square yard to provide adequate friction for safe vehicle operation. [1][2] Concrete shoulders or a widened lane one to two feet wide with asphalt shoulders are incorporated, and guide‑rail and drainage structures are adjusted or replaced as needed to retain shoulder widths and verify bridge clearances. [1] Durability is promoted by avoiding high early‑strength mixes that are prone to shrinkage cracking, using fiber‑reinforced concrete where macro‑fibers reduce both the number of cracks and their average width under restrained shrinkage conditions and can increase longitudinal transfer efficiency by roughly thirty percent when crack widths exceed one millimetre. [1] Temperature control requires fresh concrete temperature between 65 °F and 90 °F, existing pavement surface temperature below 120 °F, and geotextile surface temperatures not to exceed 120 °F; if air temperature falls to 45 °F or lower, insulating blankets are applied and removed only after the concrete surface temperature change is less than forty degrees per hour or when an average compressive strength of three thousand psi is achieved. [2] Curing compound shall be applied at a rate of one gallon per 125 ft², edge slump is limited to one‑quarter inch near shoulders and one‑eighth inch adjacent to lanes, and for overlays thinner than three inches No. 67 coarse aggregate is mandated together with macro‑fibers dosed to meet the design residual strength (f150) verified by a fourteen‑day ASTM C1609‑12 test; chloride ion penetration must fall within low, very low or negligible limits as defined by AASHTO methods. [2] Safety objectives are satisfied by enhancing surface friction, providing proper shoulder support to reduce edge stresses, limiting slab movement, misalignment and faulting through the reduction in both the number of cracks and their average width, and correcting low‑friction conditions. [1][2] Construction traffic is restricted on geotextile layers, dowel baskets are anchored to limit movement to a quarter inch, live loads are prohibited until the specified strength thresholds are met, and any surface cracks deeper than one‑quarter inch or wider than 0.007 in to 0.016 in must be repaired at no additional cost. [2][1] Serviceability goals require that drainage, shoulders and a properly shaped overlay surface be maintained so the pavement can sustain a minimum design life of eight years and a maximum of twenty years under projected traffic loads, with load‑transfer efficiency preserved throughout the service period. [1][2] The overlay must retain adequate load‑transfer efficiency at joints and existing cracks throughout its service period, thereby supporting continued ride quality and structural performance. Scope and applicability

Concrete overlay is applicable to a wide range of existing pavement structures, including Portland‑cement concrete slabs, asphalt decks, composite asphalt‑concrete sections, and bridge decks, provided that the underlying base possesses adequate load‑bearing capacity [1][2]. When the existing concrete slab or deck is in good to fair condition and exhibits only limited cracking or faulting, a bonded overlay is recommended; the new concrete is placed directly on the sound substrate forming a monolithic slab, with thickness as thin as 2½ inches for surface‑only distress correction and ranging from four to eight inches when additional structural capacity or an extended service life of eight to twenty years is sought [1]. For asphalt decks that are in fair to good condition, bonded overlays require at least three inches of sound milled material to remain after milling, and the nominal concrete thickness typically falls between 2½ inches and four inches; when the required thickness is less than three inches, No. 67 coarse aggregate replaces No. 57 and synthetic or steel macrofibers are added to achieve the design residual flexural strength (f150) [1][2]. Fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays that incorporate such macrofibers can be specified with thicknesses of three to six inches to provide residual strengths of roughly one hundred to two hundred twenty‑five psi, which is advantageous in higher traffic or variable‑support situations [1]. If the pavement exhibits moderate to severe deterioration—such as fractured concrete slabs, heavily cracked composite sections, or extensive faulting—an unbonded overlay is indicated; this system rests on a stabilized platform that includes a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt separation layer or a non‑woven geotextile and incorporates a bond breaker when leveling is required, thereby restoring structural capacity without transmitting underlying distress to the new slab [1]. The same bonded‑overlay concept can be employed on existing concrete pavements when proper surface preparation, joint sawing and sealing are performed, and it may replace full reconstruction of an asphalt pavement if the asphalt can be milled to the specified depth, unsound areas removed, and cracks larger than three‑quarters of an inch filled with approved non‑shrink grout [2]. For concrete bridge decks, overlay placement is permissible only after a minimum curing period of twenty‑eight days, when the surface has been shot‑blasted, is dry, free of moisture, and the substrate temperature lies between forty and one hundred degrees Fahrenheit; a high‑performance primer must be applied within five minutes of mixing and polymer‑modified concrete placed promptly to achieve at least a one‑inch thickness, followed by an abrasive sand finish to provide required friction [2]. All overlay mixes follow standard Portland cement concrete specifications such as those in Publication 408 or use polymer‑modified concrete for bridge decks, with design targets that may include low, very low, or negligible chloride ion penetration within fifty‑six days of curing and residual strength values consistent with the selected fiber reinforcement [1][2]. Joint layout follows the design plan or, absent a plan, limits panel dimensions to no more than eighteen times the overlay thickness and an aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5, with joints cut one‑third of the slab depth and at least one‑eighth inch wide; continuous base drainage is required, existing joints are cleaned and sealed when an unbonded system is used, and concrete shoulders are installed where original shoulders are concrete or matched to the prevailing shoulder type [1][2]. Typical applications include intersections, steep grades, ramps, and other locations experiencing heavy repeated loads—generally about 2 500 ESALs per day—or where low‑friction surfaces, rideability problems such as rutting, shoving, slippage, or thermal cracking must be corrected; the choice between bonded and unbonded configurations depends on the existing pavement condition and the desired balance of structural capacity, slab thickness, and service life [1][2].

Design inputs and requirements

Designing a concrete overlay starts with gathering site‑specific data that describe the functional context, traffic demand, material condition, environmental constraints, geotechnical capacity, and existing pavement characteristics. The guides identify locations such as intersections, ramp slopes, and bridge decks as common candidates because they frequently exhibit distress like rutting, shoving, slip resistance loss, or cracking that can be remedied with an overlay. Traffic volumes of roughly 2 500 equivalent single‑axle loads per day are cited as a threshold for considering an overlay, and the design must incorporate protective shielding to guard nearby traffic from rebound and dust during placement. Material requirements include ensuring that the concrete or Portland cement concrete material is stored clean and dry, retains a manufacturer‑specified shelf life of at least twelve months, and is accompanied by complete labeling and safety data sheets. For bonded overlays on existing asphalt, the remaining milled asphalt must be at least three inches thick, while the overall overlay thickness cannot be less than one inch; thin bonded overlays are limited to a nominal depth of about two‑and‑a‑half to four inches and conventional bonded overlays require four inches or more. Geotechnical assessment is performed through falling‑weight deflectometer testing of the existing pavement to confirm slab stability and sufficient subgrade support, with any detected slab movement or faulting addressed by stabilization or jacking before overlay placement. Adequate surface and subsurface drainage must be verified, and where deficiencies exist a sub‑drainage system or site‑specific drainage protection such as inlet, scupper, or barrier drain measures is incorporated into the design. Environmental best‑management practices require protecting inlet, scupper and barrier drain structures to prevent overlay material loss, keeping the placement surface dry, and maintaining ambient temperatures between 40 °F and 100 °F during application. Existing‑condition surveys must locate cracks or other defects in the underlying concrete or asphalt, ensure that any prior repairs have cured for at least twenty‑eight days before surface preparation, and confirm that the substrate is dry prior to placement. When the underlying pavement does not meet the prescribed condition—such as insufficient remaining asphalt thickness or unstable concrete—a full‑depth patch using new Portland cement concrete or an asphalt concrete base course is specified before overlay installation. The design of a concrete overlay is controlled by an integrated set of state and federal specifications, design manuals, and performance standards that together prescribe the required activities, material properties, structural analysis, and documentation. In Pennsylvania the principal statutory framework is PennDOT Publication 408 together with the Roadway Construction Standards in Publication 72M, while the broader technical basis for rigid‑pavement overlays is found in the Rigid Pavement specifications, specifically Section 548.3 and its referenced sections such as 501.3, 516, 679, 681 and 316. The overlay must be placed as a Class AA Portland‑cement concrete slab over an existing pavement surface—either asphalt or composite concrete—and the minimum thickness is one inch, with typical design thicknesses ranging from two‑and‑a‑half to four inches for thin bonded overlays and from four to eight inches for conventional overlays. When the specified depth is less than seven inches the mix is required to contain polypropylene or macrofibers that meet ASTM C1116 Type III 4.13 and ASTM C1018 Performance Level 1, with fiber lengths between one and two‑and‑a‑half inches, an aspect ratio not exceeding 150, a dosage limited to five pounds per cubic yard unless a field demonstration shows workability, and a volume content of roughly 0.2–0.5 percent; the fibers must provide a residual flexural strength (f150) equal to at least twenty percent as measured by the Illinois‑modified ASTM C1609 test. Mix design tolerances are limited to plus or minus four percent for cement, plus or minus two percent each for fine and coarse aggregates, plus or minus one percent for water, and plus or minus two percent for any latex admixture, which must conform to AASHTO M 154 and the FHWA‑RD‑78‑35 prequalification criteria when used. For overlays thinner than three inches No. 67 coarse aggregate replaces No. 57, and air‑entraining agents must meet the same standards. Prior to placement the existing pavement is required to be repaired or stabilized through slabjacking, slab stabilization, dowel‑bar retrofit, joint cleaning and resealing, and cracks larger than three‑quarters of an inch must be filled with approved non‑shrink grout; the surface then must be scarified or blasted with equipment capable of at least five thousand pounds per square inch, swept clean, and placed under a protective cover within thirty minutes of paving. For bonded overlays the existing joints are cleaned and sealed and the overlay is placed directly on the slab after a falling‑weight deflectometer test confirms that the underlying concrete is in good‑to‑fair condition; for unbonded systems a separation layer consisting of at least a one‑inch asphalt film or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile overlapped eight inches plus or minus two inches (with no more than three layers) is installed before new joint construction. Joint spacing requirements call for reinforced concrete joints at thirty‑foot intervals and plain‑concrete joints at fifteen‑foot intervals, with coated dowels bonded along their full embedded length; saw cuts are made to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness (or D/3) and a minimum width of one‑eighth inch, and panel dimensions are limited to eighteen times the overlay thickness with an aspect‑ratio ceiling of 1.5, edge slump limits of 0.25 in on shoulders and 0.125 in adjacent to lanes. Temperature control during placement mandates that concrete temperature be maintained between sixty‑five and ninety degrees Fahrenheit while ambient and substrate temperatures remain between forty‑five and eighty‑five degrees; if air temperature falls to forty degrees or lower insulating blankets are applied, and curing must begin within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates, with surface‑temperature change during blanket removal not exceeding forty degrees per hour and all wet‑ and dry‑cure periods kept at or above forty‑five degrees. Curing is achieved either with insulating blankets or by applying a white PAMS curing compound at a rate of one gallon per 150 square feet, producing a film thickness greater than six inches; the compound must be applied immediately after finishing and texturing. Structural design for overlays extending roughly five hundred feet requires a full pavement‑design analysis using DARWin software in accordance with Publication 242, employing rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 to compute ESALs, and determining overlay thickness from Tables 10.8–10.10, rounding to the nearest half‑inch and checking against minimum and maximum limits; bonded overlays on asphalt are designed with the bonded concrete overlay‑mechanistic‑empirical (BCOA‑ME) methodology, using approved software such as BCOA‑ME, ACPA Pavement Designer, or AASHTOWare Pavement ME. The design life for all overlays is required to be between eight and twenty years, and the minimum traffic loading for projects where rutting, shoving, or slip‑resistance problems have been observed is approximately 2,500 equivalent single axle loads per day; early‑opening traffic constraints may invoke liquidated‑damage provisions. Performance testing includes a vertical pull bond test performed according to ASTM C1583 between twenty‑four and seventy‑two hours after each day’s placement, with a minimum average bond strength of 250 psi; a seven‑day pull test must average at least 175 psi with no individual result below 160 psi, and if the initial results fail up to three additional pulls may be made before the overlay must be removed and replaced at no extra cost. The overlay is considered traffic‑ready when a Schmidt hammer reading reaches three thousand psi or after four hours of curing, and cylinder specimens must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 3,600 psi; lot acceptance occurs when the five‑day compressive strength meets or exceeds three thousand psi. Project constraints limit each traffic lane to overlay lots no larger than three thousand square yards, with ramps treated as separate lots; for each lot one core is required (two cores or nondestructive measurement for intersections or lots under three thousand square yards), and any core showing a deficiency greater than one‑quarter inch triggers additional coring at one‑hundred‑foot intervals until the tolerance is met. All designs must be documented on Form D‑4332 for District Engineer review, require BODD certification, annual quality‑assurance reviews, and FHWA approval for Federal‑aid projects unless a waiver is granted; deviations from the manuals are submitted as waivers to BODD, and the entire overlay design package—including the concrete paving job‑mix formula—must be approved by the District Materials Engineer at least two weeks prior to placement. Design methods and criteria

The design of a concrete overlay begins with a comprehensive evaluation of the existing pavement, typically employing a full‑depth deflection test using a falling weight deflectometer to quantify structural condition before deciding on a bonded or unbonded system [1]. For bonded overlays the underlying slab must be in fair‑to‑good condition and can serve as part of the load‑carrying structure; thin bonded sections are limited to 2.5–4 inches while standard bonded sections range from 4 to 8 inches, with the BCOA‑ME mechanistic‑empirical model governing thickness up to a maximum of 6½ inches [1]. Unbonded overlays treat the existing pavement as a stable platform and require a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt or geotextile separator between the old and new concrete, with a separation layer also specified when joint faulting exceeds one‑half inch [1][2]. The procedural framework mandates rehabilitation of all failed transverse joints, cleaning and sealing of joints for unbonded applications, provision of continuous base drainage, placement of a leveling course where needed, installation of a bond breaker if required, and specification of concrete shoulders that match existing conditions; guide‑rail and drainage structures are adjusted as necessary to accommodate the new surface [1]. Prior to placement a pre‑placement quality‑control plan is prepared and a coordination meeting with the Structure Control Engineer must occur at least fourteen calendar days before work begins, during which unstable slabs are stabilized or repaired and cracks larger than three‑quarters of an inch are filled to secure bond integrity [2]. Surface preparation includes scarification and shot blasting no more than twenty‑four hours prior to placement followed by thorough cleaning of the deck, after which a high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate primer is applied at roughly ninety to one hundred square feet per gallon within five minutes of mixing initiator; when joint faulting exceeds one‑half inch a separation layer—either a geotextile with eight‑inch overlap or a nominal one‑inch depth of Superpave asphalt mix—is installed before the concrete overlay is placed [2]. Panel layout follows strict geometric limits: the aspect ratio shall not exceed 1.5, the longer side may be no greater than eighteen times the overlay thickness, and each panel represents no more than three thousand square yards per traffic lane; joints are cut with a green‑cut saw to a depth equal to one‑third of the slab thickness (minimum width one‑eighth inch) and the allowable sawing window is defined using FHWA’s HIPERPAV software or an equivalent approved tool [2]. Dowel baskets are anchored before slip‑form paving proceeds, and screed adjustment matches the surveyed grade [2]. Design calculations are performed in the DARWin pavement‑design software under PennDOT oversight; the process starts by computing future‑traffic pavement thickness using rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 together with guidance in Table 10.8, then determining effective existing thickness through the Condition Survey evaluation method referenced in Table 10.9, and applying the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures [1]. For bonded overlays over asphalt or composite pavements the BCOA‑ME model is invoked to determine required overlay thickness, while daily 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) are calculated in accordance with Chapter 7 methodology; the resulting thickness is rounded up to the nearest half‑inch and checked against minimum and maximum limits given in Table 10.10 [1]. When macro‑fibers are incorporated, residual flexural strength (f150) is measured according to ASTM C1609 and added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength (Feff = MOR + f150); this Feff value is entered into mechanistic‑empirical programs such as BCOA‑ME, ACPA Pavement Designer, or AASHTOWare Pavement ME, and a Residual Strength Estimator spreadsheet assists in predicting f150 from mix and condition data [1]. For bonded concrete overlays placed over milled asphalt, a minimum three‑inch milled asphalt base is required so that the combined system behaves monolithically [1]. The concrete mixture is proportioned by a volumetric‑weight equivalent method with ingredient tolerances of cement ±4 %, fine aggregate ±2 %, coarse aggregate ±2 %, water ±1 % and latex or polymer ±2 %; aggregate moisture content is determined using AASHTO T255 or ASTM C70 to adjust water addition, and a yield test per AASHTO T121 may be performed when directed [2]. Mix selection follows the Superpave mixture‑design procedure outlined in Section 410 and Bulletin 27 and must satisfy Class AA cement‑concrete specifications of Section 501 Table A; for overlays thinner than seven inches virgin polypropylene fibers complying with ASTM C1116 Type III 4.13 and ASTM C1018 performance level 1 are required, with fiber dosage not to exceed five pounds per cubic yard unless field workability is demonstrated, and the quantity is calibrated so that the Illinois‑modified ASTM C1609 residual strength ratio R150,3 reaches at least twenty percent [2]. When the overlay depth is less than three inches No. 67 coarse aggregate replaces the standard No. 57 aggregate, and polymer concrete overlays employ an unsaturated isophthalic polyester‑styrene resin with silane coupling agent at roughly twelve percent of the dry aggregate weight (±1 %); set time is controlled to thirty to ninety minutes and systems must be certified in accordance with Section 106.03(b)3 and independently tested in AASHTO‑certified laboratories for aggregates and mix, and ISO‑certified labs for primer and resin within twelve months of material delivery [2]. Placement is permitted only when concrete temperature is between 65 °F and 90 °F (latex overlays may be placed from 45 °F to 85 °F) and the evaporation rate does not exceed 0.06 lb/ft²·hr as specified in ACI 305R; curing employs a white PAMS liquid membrane applied uniformly at approximately one gallon per 125 ft², with insulating blankets used when air temperature falls to 45 °F or lower, and removal of insulation is governed either by a maximum surface‑temperature change of forty degrees Fahrenheit per hour or by the maturity method correlating accumulated degree‑hours to target compressive strengths according to PTM No. 640 [2]. Performance testing includes vertical pull‑bond tests per ASTM C1583 performed between twenty‑four and seventy‑two hours after curing once a five‑day compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi is achieved; the test must demonstrate a minimum bond strength of 250 psi, with up to three additional pulls permitted if the initial result falls short, and seven‑day bond verification requires an average of no less than 175 psi and no individual result below 160 psi [2]. Compressive‑strength acceptance calls for a five‑day strength of at least 3,000 psi (traffic may be allowed once the slab reaches 3,600 psi), and a Schmidt hammer reading after four hours is also used as an indicator of readiness; chloride‑ion penetration must meet low, very low, or negligible targets within fifty‑six days, verified by AASHTO T277 or T358 [2]. Thickness acceptance is controlled by dividing the paved area into lots no larger than three thousand square yards per lane, selecting a random coring location in each lot according to PTM No. 1, drilling cores following PTM No. 606 (or using nondestructive measurements per PTM No. 605 for small projects), and measuring with an apparatus conforming to PTM No. 614; any measurement that falls short of the specified thickness by more than one‑quarter inch triggers additional cores at 100‑foot intervals, and failure of any core to meet the required depth renders the overlay defective [2]. The overall volume of concrete required is computed simply as overlay thickness multiplied by total placement area, excluding pre‑overlay repairs and transition zones [2].

Surface defects larger than four square inches in area and deeper than one inch must be repaired before any new concrete is placed, and the existing pavement surface must be dry, clean, and not exceed a temperature of 120 °F. Structural section and dimensions

The existing milled asphalt pavement that will receive a concrete overlay must have a minimum thickness of three inches, establishing the lower bound for the underlying pavement condition. The overlay slabs are classified by type; thin bonded overlays are limited to a nominal thickness range of approximately two‑and‑a‑half to four inches, while conventional bonded overlays require at least four inches and may be designed up to about six‑and‑a‑half inches when the BCOA‑ME methodology is employed. When fiber‑reinforced concrete is specified, the slab thickness is typically placed between three and six inches, which falls within the conventional overlay limits. The required fibers are monofilament or bundled monofilament having lengths from one inch to two‑and‑a‑half inches, an aspect ratio not exceeding 150, and may be incorporated at a dosage of up to five pounds per cubic yard provided the mix remains workable. Shoulder provisions for concrete overlays are limited to concrete shoulders or lane widenings that measure between one and two feet; no further shoulder dimensions are prescribed. No explicit dimensional criteria are given for a base, subbase, treated‑soil layer, edge support, or any supplemental reinforcement beyond the fiber requirement, so those elements must be sized in accordance with the overall design specifications of the project. Materials and mixture design

The concrete overlay shall be a Portland‑cement concrete slab that meets the pavement‑concrete requirements of Publication 408 Section 704 and utilizes an optimized aggregate gradation, with precast systems required to satisfy the Class AA cement‑concrete criteria set out in the same section. When rapid‑setting (accelerated) concrete is selected, the mix must be capable of attaining the minimum compressive strength and early‑strength development time prescribed in Publication 408 Section 501, although high‑early‑strength mixes are discouraged because they increase the likelihood of shrinkage cracking. Prior to placement a repair patching material—approved by the Representative and consisting of an asphalt‑based, cementitious, or comparable pavement‑repair material—must be used to fill all surface defects. An interlayer follows, either a geotextile with the prescribed overlap and edge extensions or a one‑inch nominal depth of Superpave asphalt mix as defined in Section 410.3. The overlay also requires a high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate primer that is wax‑free, low‑odor, supplied pre‑mixed with its resin, initiator and promoter, and applied at roughly ninety to one hundred square feet per gallon. The polymer concrete matrix is formed by an unsaturated isophthalic polyester‑styrene copolymer containing at least 1 % by weight of gamma‑methacryloxypropyltrimethoxysilane and compatible with MEKP or CHP initiators, the resin constituting twelve percent ± 1 % of the dry aggregate weight. In addition to the polymer binder, the mix includes Portland cement, fine and coarse aggregates that meet detailed size‑and‑cleanliness tables, water, a latex‑emulsion admixture (a non‑toxic, film‑forming polymeric emulsion in water prequalified per FHWA‑RD‑78‑35), and an air‑entraining admixture conforming to AASHTO M154; all binder components, primers, aggregates and admixtures must be identified on each storage container with the system provider’s name, manufacture date, batch/lot number, trade name, quantity and mixing ratio, stored clean and dry, have a minimum shelf life of twelve months, liquids kept below eighty degrees Fahrenheit and aggregates protected from moisture. The volumetric‑weight mix design permits cement weight to vary from 0 % to +4 % of the target, fine and coarse aggregates limited to ±2 %, water to ±1 %, and latex to ±2 %; the maximum water‑to‑cement ratio is defined in the design together with the exact types of cement and aggregates. For overlay thicknesses less than three inches No. 67 coarse aggregate must replace No. 57, and for overlays thinner than seven inches a Class AA cement concrete incorporating virgin polypropylene fibers is required. Synthetic or steel macrofibers—monofilament or bundled monofilament polypropylene fibers 1 to 2½ inches long with an aspect ratio not exceeding 150—are added in an amount that achieves a residual flexural strength (f₁₅₀) between roughly 100 psi and 225 psi, typically requiring a dosage of 0.2 % to 0.5 % of the concrete volume; the fibers must comply with ASTM C1116 Type III 4.13, meet Performance Level 1 criteria, and be added to attain the Illinois‑modified ASTM C1609 residual strength ratio of twenty percent but not exceeding five pounds per cubic yard unless field testing confirms acceptable workability. Because fiber addition reduces workability, the mix design must allow appropriate adjustments of slump‑reducing or air‑entraining admixtures so that fresh‑mix properties remain within project limits, and fresh concrete may be placed only when its temperature is between sixty‑five and ninety degrees Fahrenheit while the existing surface is below one hundred twenty degrees; latex mixtures must be placed at a plastic temperature of fifty to eighty‑five degrees Fahrenheit, cure temperatures must remain at or above forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit, and the evaporation rate during placement may not exceed 0.06 lb per square foot per hour. Strength requirements include a minimum compressive strength of three‑thousand psi after five days for latex mixes and three‑six hundred psi on cylinder specimens for the hardened overlay, with bond‑strength testing in accordance with ASTM C1583 averaging at least one hundred seventy‑five psi and no individual result below one hundred sixty psi; air‑content limits follow Section 704.1(c)3. The concrete must also satisfy a permeability target: after no more than fifty‑six days of curing it shall exhibit low, very‑low, or negligible chloride‑ion penetration as measured by AASHTO T277 or T358 (a moderate level is limited to 2 800 coulombs). Curing is achieved by applying a white liquid membrane‑forming compound uniformly over all exposed concrete surfaces at not less than one gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 ft² for overlays up to six inches thick and one gallon per 150 ft² for sections thicker than six inches. In unbonded overlay systems a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt separation layer—or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer—must be placed between the existing pavement and the new concrete. Structural details and interfaces

Preparation of the existing pavement includes cleaning and repairing all failed transverse joints, and when the overlay rests on a composite‑base slab a stabilization step is required to re‑establish uniform support together with a continuous pavement‑base drain if needed. For unbonded overlays the existing joints are cleaned, sealed and a separation layer is installed between old and new concrete; this may be a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt film or a non‑woven geotextile interlayer placed flat with edges overlapped about eight inches. All existing transverse and longitudinal joints must be identified, marked and backfilled with an approved joint‑filling material to within ±¼ in of the surface before concrete placement. Joint layout follows the project plans; when no plan is provided a department‑approved pattern limits panel dimensions to no more than eighteen times the overlay thickness and requires the longer‑to‑shorter side ratio not to exceed 1.5 while keeping individual panel aspect ratios close to one. Other guides prescribe joint spacing based on slab thickness, specifying 30 ft spacing for reinforced jointed concrete (RC‑21M) and 15 ft for plain jointed concrete (RC‑27M), with an intermediate joint added when a single‑lane overlay would otherwise exceed roughly 20 ft. Saw‑cut control joints are made with a green‑cut saw to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness and a minimum width of one‑eighth inch for regular joints, or to the full overlay thickness plus one‑half inch when a sealant reservoir is required; transverse cuts are placed directly over existing transverse joints and longitudinal cuts receive a quarter‑inch‑wide sealant reservoir extending the full depth plus half an inch. All joint faces are flushed, filled with backing material and sealed in accordance with standard drawing details. Load‑transfer devices consist of coated dowel bars placed in all transverse joints that have lost load‑transfer capability; the dowels are fully bonded to the existing slab using epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders, while anchored dowel‑basket assemblies may also be used provided vertical and horizontal movement does not exceed one‑quarter inch. Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required for patches longer than about 65 ft, and in fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays saw‑cut contraction joints are positioned so that macrofibers act like tie bars, increasing longitudinal tensile effort by roughly thirty percent; nevertheless conventional dowel bars remain necessary where faulting control is required. When the overlay thickness is less than seven inches synthetic polypropylene fibers meeting ASTM C1116 specifications must be incorporated, using monofilament or bundled fibers 1 to 2½ in long with an aspect ratio not greater than 150, added in a quantity sufficient to achieve a residual strength ratio of 20 % without exceeding five pounds per cubic yard unless a field demonstration shows acceptable workability. Any existing reinforcement bars that become exposed during preparation are abrasive‑blasted clean; epoxy coating of such bars is prohibited. Edge treatment includes edge‑tooling wherever the new slab meets existing joints, metal expansion dams, curbs or previously placed lanes; screed rails and construction bulkheads are isolated from fresh concrete with a pointing trowel while metal expansion dams remain bonded to the wearing surface. Transitions at the limits of the overlay are constructed as indicated on the plans, using hand‑troweling near curbs, scuppers, gutter lines or bridge joints, and texturing is stopped short of such obstructions; a geotextile debonding layer is also placed beneath any transition that rests on existing treated base or concrete. Concrete shoulders are provided when the existing shoulder is concrete, typically as a one‑ to two‑foot lane widening that may be combined with asphalt shoulders, and guide‑rail and drainage structures are adjusted or replaced as needed. A primer or latex grout coat is flooded over every horizontal and vertical surface that will receive the overlay—including drains, parapets, curbs, expansion joints and other adjacent structures—to achieve complete coverage before the overlay is placed [2]. If a geotextile separation membrane is used it is laid flat on the cleaned substrate, overlapped eight inches plus or minus two inches, extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, kept clean and damp but not saturated, protected from traffic, and its surface temperature kept below 120 °F; where an asphalt interlayer is preferred a one‑inch nominal layer may be placed after reducing joint faulting to less than half an inch and anchoring dowel‑bar assemblies so that movement does not exceed a quarter inch in any direction [1][2]. Transverse and longitudinal joints are scribed and saw‑cut to the full thickness of the new concrete plus one‑half inch, with cut width at least as wide as the underlying joint; panel dimensions are limited to eighteen times the overlay thickness and the length‑to‑width ratio is not to exceed 1.5, longitudinal joints being avoided in wheel paths, and joint backing or approved filling material placed within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface for all transverse joints [2]. Edge tooling is applied at every joint, metal expansion dam, curb and previously placed lane; after the mix stiffens screed rails and bulkheads are removed with a pointing trowel, leaving metal expansion dams bonded to the wearing surface, and edge vibration is used adjacent to bulkheads and dams to consolidate material [2]. Load transfer across transverse joints is achieved by dowelling each joint with coated bars anchored in epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders, spaced at 30 feet for reinforced concrete (RC‑21M) and 15 feet for plain concrete, with appropriate seals; for lane reconstructions extending 500 feet or more new joints should be aligned with the existing pattern to preserve continuity [1]. A continuous pavement‑base drain is provided in both bonded and unbonded designs, and any failed transverse joints are rehabilitated to ensure proper drainage and load transfer [1]. Concrete shoulders are required when the original shoulders are concrete; otherwise the new shoulder may match the existing material, typically widened by one to two feet and paired with asphalt shoulders, while guide rails and drainage structures adjoining the overlay are adjusted or replaced as necessary and any required fill widening is incorporated to retain proper shoulder widths and respect bridge clearances [1]. The overlay is cured to a traffic‑ready condition after four hours or when a Schmidt hammer indicates 3,000 psi compressive strength (or 3,600 psi for conventional concrete), and vertical pull‑bond testing per ASTM C1583 is performed between 24 and 72 hours after curing once the overlay reaches at least 3,000 psi; an average bond of 250 psi (minimum 175 psi with no individual result below 160 psi) is required, and locations that fail are repaired with non‑shrink grout before opening to traffic [2]. For polymer‑concrete overlays a dedicated bonding layer consisting of a wax‑free, low‑odor high molecular weight methacrylate primer compatible with the polyester resin binder is applied, with all components supplied by a single system provider and the resin content tightly controlled at approximately 12 % (±1 %) of the dry aggregate weight [2]. Thin Portland‑cement concrete overlays are placed with thicknesses ranging from 2.5 to 4 inches, while thicker cement overlays may be placed at 4 to 8 inches; in either case the bonded interface creates a structural connection that transfers load and prevents slippage or delamination [1][2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Concrete overlay designs incorporate comprehensive water‑management features to protect the new slab and accommodate site‑specific drainage conditions. The design first safeguards existing drainage components by terminating any geotextile interlayer in a drainable layer or at a daylighted location such as a ditch foreslope and by providing protective measures at inlets, scuppers and barrier drains. Existing guide‑rail and other drainage structures that intersect the overlay area are adjusted or replaced so runoff is directed clear of the slab. When subsurface drainage deficiencies are identified, a continuous pavement‑base drain is installed to convey water away from the structure, and additional subgrade drains may be placed to provide adequate subdrainage beneath the overlay. The overlay surface is provided with proper cross‑slope, and for unbonded overlays a bond‑breaker together with a leveling course is applied, which also helps promote runoff and limit moisture intrusion into the underlying pavement. All standing water on the milled or prepared deck must be removed using approved methods; the surface is then kept damp but free of puddles. The clean surface is pre‑wetted for at least one hour, and a moisture test performed in accordance with ASTM D4263 (modified for two hours) confirms that the substrate has reached an acceptable dry condition before concrete placement. For unbonded overlays, existing joints are cleaned and sealed prior to placing the bond‑breaker. The geotextile interlayer itself is dampened but not saturated before overlay placement, and its surface temperature must remain below 120 °F. Concrete placement is limited to concrete temperatures between 65 °F and 90 °F, with ambient and substrate temperatures required to stay between 45 °F and 100 °F; the existing pavement or asphalt surface must be below 120 °F before any material is placed. If forecast air temperatures fall to 40 °F–45 °F (or 45 °F or less), insulating blankets are applied to prevent rapid heat loss, and they may be removed only when the air temperature rises above the threshold or when the concrete reaches an average compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi (or 3,600 psi for higher‑strength mixes), provided that surface temperature change does not exceed 40 °F in any one‑hour period. Fog‑misting, protective covers, wind breaks, sunshades or other misting devices are employed to keep evaporation rates below 0.06 lb/ft²·hr during placement and finishing operations. Liquid materials are stored dry, out of direct sunlight and at temperatures below 80 °F to prevent premature heating. After concrete placement, curing compounds are applied promptly after bleed water dissipates; a saturated burlap water‑cure with continuous misting is used, followed by a dry‑cure under a waterproof cover, with curing temperatures maintained at or above 45 °F and rain exposure prevented through immediate protection. The guides do not prescribe further climate‑specific actions beyond the outlined drainage, temperature, and moisture controls. [1][2]

The concrete overlay is required to meet the geometric, safety and accessibility criteria set out in Chapter 10 of the pavement design guidelines [1]. Prior to placement the existing surface is milled so that a minimum of three inches of asphalt remains, after which an overlay thickness is selected—thin bonded overlays are typically 2½ to 4 inches thick and conventional overlays four inches or greater—to accommodate anticipated traffic volumes of at least 2 500 ESALs per day and to remediate rutting, shoving or slip on steep grades and intersections [1]. The overlay must never be less than one inch in thickness, and the design calls for strict control of thickness, profile, surface tolerance and texture throughout placement [2]. All failed transverse joints are rehabilitated; when an unbonded overlay is used the existing joints are cleaned, sealed and a bond‑breaker layer is installed so that any grade correction is achieved with the concrete itself rather than an asphalt separation layer [1]. Continuous drainage through the pavement base is provided and cross‑slope is corrected to meet standard geometric standards for surface water removal, thereby improving both safety and accessibility [1]. A concrete leveling course restores the required cross‑slope and profile, and a sacrificial quarter‑inch layer may later be diamond‑ground or grooved to increase friction, while an abrasive sand broadcast of at least 2.2 lb per square yard is applied immediately after placement to further enhance surface grip [1][2]. Shoulders that match the existing pavement type—concrete where original shoulders are concrete and asphalt otherwise—provide lateral support for edge loads, preserve the prescribed shoulder width, and supply the space needed for stopped vehicles and emergency access; when widening is required a lane extension of one to two feet may be added while maintaining the required shoulder dimensions [1]. Joint spacing follows pavement type, with typical spacings of 30 ft for jointed reinforced concrete and 15 ft for jointed plain concrete, each equipped with coated dowel bars and appropriate seals; additionally, intermediate transverse joints must be placed so that no panel exceeds fifteen feet in length [1][2]. Transverse joints are cut after the concrete has hardened using a green‑cut saw to at least one‑third of slab depth and a minimum width of one‑eighth inch, longitudinal joints are prohibited in wheel paths, and edge slump is limited to a maximum of one‑quarter inch within six inches of the shoulder [2]. Panel geometry is limited to dimensions no greater than eighteen times the overlay thickness, with the ratio of the longer side to the shorter side not exceeding 1.5 and an aspect ratio as close to unity as practicable [2]. Unstable slabs or slab fragments that move under normal traffic loads must be stabilized or repaired before any interlayer material is installed to ensure uniform support for the new concrete [2]. Screeds are set to match or raise the existing pavement grade as determined from pre‑placement surveys, and all work bridges are kept clear of the fresh latex surface [2][1]. Concrete placement is limited to ambient temperatures between 65 °F and 90 °F, and shielding is required to protect adjacent traffic from rebound, dust and construction activities, with adequate lighting provided whenever work extends beyond daylight hours [2]. The overlay may be opened to traffic only after the concrete attains a compressive strength of at least 3 600 psi and the minimum acceptable bond strength is demonstrated; accelerated concrete methods can permit traffic restoration within four to seven hours after placement, provided those performance criteria are satisfied [2][1]. Guide‑rail and drainage structures are inspected and adjusted or replaced as necessary to retain geometric integrity and user safety, and at intersections the treated length must extend beyond the queue plus braking distance while bridge clearances are verified before opening [1]. For intermittent repairs precast slabs may be used, with slab widths limited to no more than a single lane width and lengths ranging from six to fifteen feet (twenty feet for prestressed units) [1].

Verification and expected performance

Before any concrete is placed, the contractor must submit a comprehensive quality‑control plan that details surface preparation, joint layout, mixing, placement, finishing and curing procedures; this plan is reviewed and approved by the Representative. The existing milled asphalt surface is swept clean and any longitudinal or transverse cracks wider than three‑quarters of an inch are filled with non‑shrink grout approved by the Department. A final cleaning of the substrate occurs no more than thirty minutes before placement, and the substrate temperature is kept below 120 °F at that time. Joint spacing follows the approved layout, with panel dimensions limited to eighteen times the overlay thickness and a length‑to‑width ratio not exceeding 1.5; joints are cut with a green‑cut saw to one‑third of slab depth and a minimum width of one‑eighth inch after the concrete has hardened enough to prevent raveling.

During placement, each lot – defined as no more than twenty cubic yards or a day’s work – is sampled every five cubic yards for plastic air content, temperature and slump, with the Inspector witnessing the tests. The finished thickness must be at least one inch everywhere, and surface‑tolerance and smoothness must meet the limits set in the specifications. Edge slump is limited to one‑quarter inch adjacent to shoulders and one‑eighth inch next to an adjoining lane, and the final finish must be free of honeycomb, segregation or other visible defects.

Curing begins within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates. When ambient temperature is 45 °F or lower, insulating blankets or curing compounds are applied; blankets may be removed only after the concrete surface temperature exceeds 45 °F or when compressive strength surpasses three‑thousand psi, and the temperature change must not exceed forty degrees per hour.

Performance testing continues after placement. Cylindrical specimens are tested in accordance with PTM No. 604, and traffic may be opened only when a minimum compressive strength of 3,600 psi is recorded and the early bond‑strength criteria are satisfied. A Schmidt hammer reading of at least 3,000 psi across the deck, or four hours after finishing, confirms a traffic‑ready condition. Between twenty‑four and seventy‑two hours after placement, a vertical pull‑bond test performed per ASTM C1583 must show a minimum adhesion of 250 psi; if the first result falls short, up to three additional tests are permitted, and the average must still meet the 250 psi threshold or the overlay is deemed defective and must be removed at no cost. At seven days, a bond‑strength test on five random locations per day’s placement must average at least 175 psi with no individual result below 160 psi.

For core verification, the overlay is divided into lots of up to three thousand square yards per traffic lane (ramps are treated as separate lots). An inspector selects a random coring location in each lot, extracts the core according to PTM procedures, refills the vertical surface with the original mix or approved grout and measures depth. Any core that is more than one‑quarter inch short of the required thickness triggers additional cores at 100‑foot intervals; if any core remains deficient, the pavement is considered defective. These combined inspections, tests and documented procedures demonstrate compliance, constructability, performance and consistency with the project requirements. [2]

Concrete overlays are expected to provide a structural service life that ranges from eight to twenty years, a target established by the design criteria and documented through traffic‑based design inputs and agency concurrence forms. [1][2] The anticipated longevity is verified by incorporating current average daily traffic, projected growth rates, and truck percentages into mechanistic‑empirical design models and by completing the required design submission package for agency review. [1] A durable bond between the overlay and the existing pavement is identified as a primary indicator of long‑term durability, and it is measured with vertical pull‑bond tests performed according to ASTM C1583 between twenty‑four and seventy‑two hours after placement, requiring an average strength of at least 250 psi and a seven‑day verification that no individual result falls below 160 psi and the average does not drop beneath 175 psi. [2] Minimum thickness requirements of one inch are confirmed during placement to ensure adequate structural capacity. [2] Strength development is monitored both in the field and laboratory; acceptance cylinders tested per PTM No. 604 must achieve a compressive strength of at least 3,600 psi before traffic opening, while five‑day laboratory specimens must reach a minimum of 3,000 psi, and surface readiness may also be indicated by a calibrated Schmidt hammer reading of 3,000 psi after four hours of curing. [2] When macrofibers are incorporated into the concrete mix, the overlay gains additional structural capacity, exhibits narrower cracks, and improves joint load transfer, thereby extending serviceability and enhancing ride quality. [1] The contribution of fibers to distress resistance is quantified by measuring residual flexural strength at a strain of 150 µε (f150) in accordance with ASTM C1609; this value is added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to produce an effective flexural strength that feeds into thickness and life prediction calculations. [1] Load‑transfer efficiency can increase by approximately thirty percent relative to unreinforced concrete, especially when crack widths exceed one millimeter, and this improvement is evaluated through laboratory slab tests and on‑site monitoring of crack width and joint opening.
