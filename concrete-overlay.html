Concrete Overlay

Part of: Overlay is-subtopic-of
Concrete Overlay is a topic under Overlay.
concrete-overlayconcrete-overlaysconcrete-overlay-s
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-29

Concrete overlay is a rehabilitation technique applied to existing concrete or asphalt pavements that have lost structural capacity due to spalling, cracking, rutting, joint faulting, low‑friction surfaces, drainage problems, or insufficient shoulder width [1][2]. The method places an additional Portland‑cement concrete layer—typically three to six inches thick and often fiber‑reinforced—to raise flexural strength, provide a uniform load‑bearing slab, and deliver a smooth wearing surface that can extend service life eight to twenty years [1][2]. Depending on the condition of the existing deck, the new concrete is either bonded directly to form a monolithic slab or installed over an unbonded separation layer (asphalt film or geotextile) that isolates underlying faults while still allowing joint rehabilitation and shoulder widening [1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Concrete Overlay” also include the following subtopics: Bond Breaker, Bonded Concrete Overlay, and Unbonded Concrete Overlay.

Concrete Overlay employs a Bond Breaker as an unbonded separation layer when the existing pavement lacks structural integrity or exhibits distress that prevents adhesion, creating an Unbonded Concrete Overlay where the new slab acts independently over the old subbase. [1][2] Conversely, when the underlying Portland-cement concrete remains sound but requires surface restoration, the method utilizes a Bonded Concrete Overlay to form a monolithic unit that restores uniform support and flexural strength through direct chemical bonding. [1][2]

Purpose and design objectives

Concrete overlay is applied when an existing pavement—whether concrete or asphalt—has lost the structural capacity and uniform support required for anticipated traffic loads due to various forms of deterioration such as spalling, cracking, rutting, shoving, slippage, thermal cracking, joint faulting, low‑friction surfaces, inadequate drainage, or insufficient shoulder width [1][2]. The primary engineering need addressed by the overlay is to restore a load‑bearing, smooth wearing surface while simultaneously increasing flexural strength and overall load‑carrying capacity of the pavement structure [1][2]. To meet this need the design adds an additional concrete layer typically three to six inches thick of fiber‑reinforced Portland‑cement concrete over asphalt bases, or a thickness sufficient to raise the capacity of existing concrete decks, forming a monolithic slab that provides uniform support and extends service life for at least eight up to twenty years [1]. When the existing pavement is in good‑to‑fair condition the overlay is bonded directly so that the new and old slabs act as a single unit, whereas moderate to severe distress calls for an unbonded system that isolates the overlay from underlying faults while still allowing slab stabilization, joint rehabilitation, and the addition of concrete shoulders or a widened lane of one to two feet with asphalt shoulders [1]. Structural performance criteria require the new concrete to achieve a minimum compressive strength of 3 600 psi and an average bond strength of at least 175 psi, with no individual test result falling below 160 psi, before traffic can be opened to the overlay [2]. To prevent premature or random cracking that would impair serviceability, panel dimensions are limited to no more than eighteen times the overlay thickness and the aspect ratio of any panel is kept as close to one as feasible and not greater than 1.5; saw‑cut joints must be cut to a depth of at least one‑third of slab thickness within twelve inches of edges and at least one‑fourth elsewhere, and cracks occurring outside these joints are deemed defective [2]. For thin bonded overlays placed over asphalt surfaces the mix design must limit chloride ion penetration to low, very low, or negligible levels after 56 days, incorporate macrofibers to meet the specified residual strength (f150), and use No. 67 coarse aggregate when the overlay thickness is under three inches [2]. Surface preparation includes milling away unsound pavement material, stabilizing any moving slabs, filling depressions larger than four square inches and one inch deep, and treating faulted joints by milling those with more than a quarter‑inch of faulting or providing a scratch course when faulting exceeds half an inch; these steps ensure a sound base for the overlay to bond or rest upon [1][2]. The quantity of concrete is calculated from the specified depth and area, with an allowance of up to two percent extra volume to replace material removed during preparation, ensuring that the overlay can be placed without creating voids or unsupported areas [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 463–474)

A concrete overlay is required to restore the functional performance of an existing pavement by eliminating surface distress, improving ride quality, and providing effective removal of surface and subsurface water while delivering a smooth load‑bearing riding surface that meets serviceability requirements throughout its design life [1][2]. To achieve this, the design must rehabilitate any failed transverse joints, clean and seal all existing joints when an unbonded overlay is used, and provide load‑transfer capability through dowel‑bar retrofits or fully bonded dowels spaced at 30 ft for reinforced concrete or 15 ft for plain concrete with coated bars and appropriate joint seals; tie bars are required in patches longer than 65 ft [1]. The overlay thickness is selected by acceptable procedures such as non‑destructive testing, rounded up to the nearest half‑inch, and must fall within the limits prescribed—typically 2.5 to 4 inches for thin‑bonded applications or 4 to 8 inches for conventional bonded designs [1]. In addition, the slab must attain a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi before traffic opening, with a preferred definitive threshold of 3,600 psi, and bond performance is verified by an average tensile‑bond strength of 175 psi and no individual test below 160 psi [2]. Panel geometry is limited to a maximum length equal to eighteen times the overlay thickness and an aspect‑ratio not exceeding 1.5, while joints are cut to one‑third the slab depth with a minimum width of 1⁄8 in, extending through the full thickness plus an additional ½ in without harming dowels, and all joints are sealed with an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant after sawing [2]. When a composite base is present, slab stabilization and uniform support are required together with a continuous pavement‑base drain, and any areas of obvious failure in the base must be removed and replaced with material that supplies adequate structural capacity and facilitates drainage [1]. The design also incorporates concrete shoulders or a widened lane of one to two feet with asphalt shoulders, and provisions are made for adjustment or replacement of guide‑rail and drainage structures as needed, including fill widening to retain shoulder widths and verification of bridge clearances [1]. Durability is addressed by avoiding high early‑strength mixes that are prone to shrinkage cracking; if an accelerated opening is required, a mix capable of reaching the specified compressive strength within four to seven hours may be used, and macrofibers are added at a rate sufficient to achieve the design residual strength (f150) thereby providing additional structural capacity, reducing crack widths, resisting crack growth, and increasing load‑transfer efficiency by roughly thirty percent when crack widths exceed one millimetre [1][2]. When the overlay thickness is under three inches, No. 67 coarse aggregate is specified instead of No. 57 to improve performance, and the concrete mixture must be designed to attain a low‑chloride‑penetration classification (Low, Very Low, or Negligible) within 56 days, with chloride ion migration not exceeding 2,800 coulombs on AASHTO T 277 or the corresponding resistivity limits of T 358 [2]. Fresh concrete temperature is limited to the range of 65 °F to 90 °F and substrate temperature must be below 120 °F at placement; curing compounds are applied within 30 minutes after bleed‑water dissipation, insulation is retained until ambient air exceeds 45 °F or slab strength surpasses 3,000 psi while limiting surface temperature change to no more than 40 °F per hour [2]. Safety objectives are satisfied by enhancing surface friction, ensuring proper shoulder support to reduce edge stresses, limiting slab movement, misalignment and faulting through the reduction in both the number of cracks and their average width, controlling edge slump to a maximum of ¼ in adjacent to shoulders and ⅛ in next to adjoining lanes, and guaranteeing that joint cuts do not compromise dowel integrity while all joints are properly sealed [1][2]. Serviceability goals require that drainage, shoulders and a properly shaped overlay surface be maintained so that the pavement can sustain a minimum design life of eight years and a maximum of twenty years under projected traffic loads, with load‑transfer efficiency preserved throughout the service period and ride quality remaining acceptable for the entire design life [1][2].

Scope and applicability

Concrete overlay is applicable for rehabilitating both existing rigid pavements—such as Portland‑cement concrete slabs, continuously reinforced panels, and composite asphalt‑concrete structures—and asphalt‑surfaced decks, provided the underlying base retains adequate load‑bearing capacity [1][2]. When the existing surface is in good to fair condition and exhibits only limited cracking or faulting, a bonded overlay is appropriate; the new concrete is placed directly on the sound substrate forming a monolithic slab, with thickness as thin as 2½ inches for merely correcting surface distress and up to four inches for typical asphalt decks, while a thicker section of four to eight inches may be used when additional structural capacity or an extended service life of eight to twenty years is desired [1][2]. For bonded applications over asphalt, at least three inches of sound milled material must remain, and if the overlay depth is less than three inches No. 67 coarse aggregate is required instead of the coarser No. 57; the nominal concrete thickness for these bonded overlays generally ranges from 2½ inches to four inches, with fiber‑reinforced variants often specified between three and six inches to achieve higher flexural capacity and residual strengths of roughly one hundred to two hundred twenty‑five psi [1][2]. Fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays incorporating synthetic or steel macrofibers are especially advantageous in high‑traffic or variable‑support situations where a thinner yet high‑capacity slab is needed, delivering enhanced crack control while preserving joint load‑transfer efficiency [1][2]. When the pavement is moderately to severely deteriorated—such as fractured concrete slabs or heavily cracked composite sections—an unbonded overlay is indicated; this system rests on a stabilized platform that includes a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt separation layer or a non‑woven geotextile, incorporates a bond breaker, and may include a leveling course placed within the concrete itself [1]. Unbonded overlays restore structural capacity without transmitting underlying distress and are suitable for correcting failed transverse joints, improving low‑friction surfaces, and addressing rideability problems such as rutting, shoving, slippage, or thermal cracking [1]. Both bonded and unbonded configurations require continuous base drainage, cleaning and sealing of existing joints when an unbonded system is used, and the provision of concrete shoulders that match the prevailing shoulder type [1]. Material specifications call for a Class AA Portland cement concrete mix meeting low‑permeability chloride ion penetration criteria (Low, Very Low, or Negligible) as defined by AASHTO T277/T358; construction temperature limits mandate fresh concrete temperatures between 65 °F and 90 °F, substrate surface temperatures not exceeding 120 °F, and ambient temperatures no lower than 45 °F unless insulating blankets are applied [2]. Quality‑control requirements include three‑day bond strength testing with an average of at least 175 psi and no individual specimen below 160 psi, and traffic may be permitted only after cylinders demonstrate a compressive strength of 3,600 psi [2]. Typical applications encompass intersections, steep grades, ramps, and other locations experiencing heavy repeated loads—generally about 2 500 ESALs per day—or where variable support conditions demand a thinner yet high‑capacity slab; accelerated concrete may be employed for early traffic opening and design follows Chapter 10 guidelines using the standard PCC mix per Publication 408 [1].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 458–469)

Concrete overlays are limited when the existing pavement is either structurally sound but only minimally distressed, in which case surface‑only treatments such as diamond grinding are more suitable than adding a new concrete layer [1]. When wheel ruts exceed half an inch or the required structural capacity cannot be achieved with a thin overlay, designers must turn to alternatives such as transverse grooving, a bonded concrete overlay, or full reconstruction [1]. Sections in which deterioration leaves only narrow strips of original pavement—approximately ten feet between intermittent patches over a length of about five hundred feet—are deemed unsuitable for an overlay and require complete removal and reconstruction [1]. The use of high early‑strength concrete is discouraged because its tendency to develop excessive shrinkage cracking makes accelerated or precast concrete more appropriate when rapid traffic reopening is needed [1]. Pavements exhibiting poor structural condition, including significant deterioration, unstable support, stripping of asphalt, extensive spalling, or unresolved drainage problems, render bonded concrete overlays ineffective and lead designers to consider unbonded overlays, asphalt overlays, crack‑and‑seat treatments, rubblizing, or full reconstruction [1]. When the underlying concrete slab contains extensive cracking or faulting, those defects would be transmitted through a bonded overlay, so an unbonded overlay or repair after correcting the damage is preferred [1]. For existing asphalt decks that suffer considerable cracking, loss of material integrity, or stripping, even fiber‑reinforced bonded overlays are outweighed by the benefits of an unbonded overlay or another rehabilitation method; macrofibers cannot compensate for a poor‑condition substrate [1]. Additionally, if the amount of unsound pavement that must be removed exceeds the modest allowance built into the overlay payment—specifically more than two percent of the calculated overlay quantity—the project falls outside the intended scope of a concrete overlay and another design such as full reconstruction or an alternative rehabilitation method becomes preferable [2]. The specification’s strict 2 % cap on additional concrete therefore serves as a quantitative threshold that, when surpassed, mandates selection of a different rehabilitation strategy [2].

Design inputs and requirements

Designing a concrete overlay requires assembling site‑specific data that describe functional context, traffic demand, material condition, environmental constraints, geotechnical capacity and existing pavement characteristics [1][2]. Typical locations include intersections, ramps or other steep grades where rutting, shoving or surface slippage have been observed, and these sites generally experience daily traffic volumes of at least 2 500 equivalent single‑axle loads [1]. A structural assessment of the current pavement is obtained through falling‑weight deflectometer testing of the existing Portland cement concrete or asphalt surface to confirm slab stability and adequate subgrade support before overlay design proceeds [1]. When the FWD results indicate movement, faulting, or visible rocking of slabs under normal loads, stabilization measures such as slab jacking or full‑depth patching with new PCC or an asphalt concrete base course must be incorporated prior to placing any interlayer material [1][2]. Surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch are required to be filled with an approved patching material before the overlay can be placed, ensuring a uniform substrate for bonding [2]. Joint faulting governs the choice of interlayer; if joint displacement exceeds a quarter‑inch, a geotextile interlayer is specified, whereas faulting greater than half an inch calls for an asphalt interlayer, each with dimensional requirements that include an eight‑inch overlap and a minimum four‑inch free edge extension to preserve drainage [2]. For bonded overlays the remaining asphalt after milling must retain at least three inches of thickness, providing sufficient bond strength for the new concrete layer [1]. Overlay thickness is selected according to type, with thin bonded overlays limited to a nominal depth between about 2½ and 4 inches and conventional bonded overlays requiring four inches or more of concrete cover [1]. Adequate surface and subsurface drainage must be verified; where subsurface drainage problems are identified, a sub‑drainage system should be provided to protect the overlay from moisture accumulation [1]. Environmental constraints also include limiting pavement or fabric surface temperature to no more than 120 °F at the time of placement, preventing thermal distress during curing [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 10 (p. 186); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Concrete‑overlay design for Pennsylvania’s Portland‑cement concrete pavements is governed by an integrated set of state specifications, design manuals, and federal oversight requirements. The core statutory framework is PennDOT Publication 408, which defines the required activities such as slabjacking, slab stabilization, dowel‑bar retrofit, joint repair and resealing, crack sealing, and cross‑stitching. Detailed construction drawings for dowel‑bar retrofit and cross‑stitching are provided in Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards (drawing RC‑26M), while joint construction requirements appear in the standard details RC‑20M, RC‑21M, and RC‑27M. Joint spacing must meet a 30‑foot interval for reinforced concrete or a 15‑foot interval for plain concrete, with coated dowels bonded along their full embedded length. Concrete mix design follows Section 704 of Publication 408 and compressive‑strength timing is governed by Section 501 when accelerated mixes are used.

Design must also satisfy Chapter 10 of the PennDOT design criteria, which mandates rehabilitation of any failed transverse joints where a composite base exists, provision of continuous pavement‑base drainage, installation of concrete shoulders or a widened lane of one to two feet accompanied by asphalt shoulders, and adjustment or replacement of guide‑rail and drainage structures as needed. For unbonded overlays the existing joints are cleaned and sealed, a leveling course is placed, and a separation layer consisting of at least a one‑inch asphalt film or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile is installed before new‑pavement joint construction per Publication 72M.

The choice between bonded and unbonded systems is dictated by a falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD) test on the existing slab; bonded overlays are limited to pavements in good‑to‑fair condition. Bonded overlay thicknesses range from 2½–4 inches for thin layers over asphalt or composite bases up to 4–8 inches for thicker sections, with bond design detailed in Section 545 of Publication 408. Unbonded overlays treat the existing slab as a stable platform and require the specified separation layer.

When an overlay extends roughly 500 feet, a full pavement‑design analysis must be performed using DARWin software in accordance with Publication 242 (Pavement Policy Manual). Traffic loading is calculated with the rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 for ESALs, and the resulting overlay thickness is derived from Tables 10.8–10.10, rounded to the nearest half‑inch and checked against minimum and maximum limits. All designs are documented on Form D‑4332 for District Engineer review, require BODD certification, annual QA reviews, and FHWA approval for Federal‑aid projects unless a waiver is granted; early‑opening traffic constraints invoke liquidated‑damage provisions per Publication 448.

Structural design life must fall between eight and twenty years as prescribed by the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures. Bonded overlays on asphalt employ the bonded concrete overlay‑mechanistic‑empirical (BCOA‑ME) methodology; for thin bonded overlays the use of macro‑fibers is mandated by Publication 408 Section 541, with residual flexural strength (f150) determined in accordance with ASTM C1609 and fiber dosages limited to 0.2–0.5 % by volume. The underlying asphalt must retain at least three inches after milling before concrete placement. Thin‑type overlays are limited to a nominal thickness of 2½–4 inches, while conventional overlays begin at four inches and may increase beyond that value; any overlay not exceeding 6.5 inches must follow the BCOA‑ME procedure using approved software such as BCOA‑ME, ACPA Pavement Designer, or AASHTOWare Pavement ME. Project constraints also require a minimum traffic loading of roughly 2,500 equivalent single axle loads per day for locations such as intersections, steep grades, or ramps where rutting, shoving, or slip‑resistance problems have been observed. Fiber‑reinforced sections typically fall within a three‑to‑six‑inch thickness band. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188)

Design methods and criteria

The design of a concrete overlay begins with a comprehensive evaluation of the existing pavement, typically employing a full‑depth deflection test using a falling weight deflectometer to quantify structural condition before deciding whether a bonded or an unbonded system is appropriate [1]. For bonded overlays the underlying slab must be in fair‑to‑good condition and may be treated as part of the load‑carrying structure, with thin‑bonded sections limited to 2.5–4 inches and standard bonded sections ranging from 4 to 8 inches; the BCOA‑ME mechanistic‑empirical model governs thickness up to a maximum of 6½ inches [1]. Unbonded overlays treat the existing pavement as a stable platform and require a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt or geotextile separator between the old and new concrete, together with cleaning and sealing of all joints [1]. The procedural framework also mandates rehabilitation of failed transverse joints, provision of continuous base drainage, placement of a leveling course where needed, installation of a bond breaker if required, and specification of concrete shoulders that match existing conditions, while guide‑rail and drainage structures are adjusted as necessary to accommodate the new surface [1].

Design calculations are performed in the DARWin pavement‑design software under PennDOT oversight; the process starts by computing future‑traffic pavement thickness using rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 together with guidance in Table 10.8, then determining effective existing thickness through the Condition Survey evaluation method referenced in Table 10.9, and applying the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures [1]. For bonded overlays over asphalt or composite pavements the BCOA‑ME model is invoked, daily 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads are calculated according to Chapter 7 methodology, and the resulting thickness is rounded up to the nearest half‑inch and checked against minimum and maximum limits in Table 10.10 [1]. When macro‑fibers are incorporated, the residual flexural strength (f150) measured by ASTM C1609 is added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength (Feff = MOR + f150); this Feff value is entered into mechanistic‑empirical programs such as BCOA‑ME, ACPA Pavement Designer, or AASHTOWare Pavement ME, and a Residual Strength Estimator spreadsheet assists in predicting f150 from mix and condition data [1]. For bonded overlays placed over milled asphalt, a minimum three‑inch milled asphalt base is required so that the combined system behaves monolithically [1].

A department‑approved quality‑control plan must document every step from surface preparation through testing and be submitted for review before work begins [2]. Existing asphalt surfaces are scarified, shot‑blasted or water‑blasted, cracks larger than three‑quarters inch are filled, and the milled surface is cleaned within thirty minutes of milling; expansion joints are identified and precut, after which full‑depth transverse cuts are made to a depth of at least one‑third the slab thickness within twelve inches of the edge (or one‑fourth elsewhere) with a minimum width of one‑eighth inch, and panel dimensions are limited to no more than eighteen times the overlay thickness with a length‑to‑width ratio not exceeding 1.5, the timing of these cuts being established with FHWA HIPERPAV or equivalent software [2]. Concrete placement is permitted only when concrete temperature lies between 65 °F and 90 °F and the existing surface has been cooled below 120 °F; ambient temperatures at or below 45 °F (or 40 °F in some specifications) require insulating blankets until the maturity method indicates sufficient degree‑hours, with temperature change not to exceed forty degrees per hour during blanket removal [2]. Curing must commence within thirty minutes after bleed water has dissipated, a white curing compound is applied at a rate of one gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 ft², and insulating blankets are used when air temperature falls below the stated limit [2]. Strength development is monitored with the maturity method prescribed in AASHTO T‑413 and PTM 640, correlating degree‑hours to compressive strength, and the overlay may be opened to traffic only after a measured cylinder strength of at least three thousand psi (or three‑thousand six hundred psi where required) is achieved [2]. Bond strength between the new concrete and the existing pavement is verified by ASTM C1583 testing on five random locations per 3,000 yd², demanding an average bond stress of at least 175 psi and no individual result below 160 psi [2]. The mix design incorporates macrofibers whose dosage is selected from Table B to attain a target residual flexural strength (f150) as determined by ASTM C1609‑12 testing at fourteen days, and permeability performance is controlled by designing the mixture to meet Low, Very Low, or Negligible chloride ion penetration within fifty‑six days, evaluated according to AASHTO T277 (maximum 2,800 coulombs) or AASHTO T358 (minimum resistivity of 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4×8 in. cylinders and 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6×12 in. cylinders) [2]. Quality assurance divides the overlay into lots not exceeding three thousand square yards per traffic lane, selects one random coring location per lot in accordance with PTM 1, drills cores per PTM 606 under inspector presence, and for projects smaller than three thousand square yards or at intersections permits two cores or nondestructive thickness measurements according to PTM 605 [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 458–474)

The design of a concrete overlay follows the procedures set out in Chapter 10 and must satisfy the structural design life requirement of at least eight years but not more than twenty years [1]. Prior to any design calculations, falling weight deflectometer testing of the existing Portland‑cement concrete pavement is required to assess its structural capacity and to determine whether a bonded overlay is appropriate; bonded overlays are permitted only when the underlying slab shows limited cracking or faulting and the subgrade provides stable support with functional drainage [1]. When the existing pavement condition is moderate to severe, an unbonded system must be used and shall include a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt (or equivalent geotextile) separation layer placed over cleaned joints [1]. All failed transverse joints must be rehabilitated; for unbonded overlays the joints are first cleaned and sealed, while for bonded overlays they remain intact to achieve monolithic action between old and new slabs [1]. Load transfer across transverse joints is achieved by installing coated dowel bars anchored through the full embedded length, with spacing of thirty feet for jointed reinforced concrete pavements and fifteen feet for jointed plain concrete pavements [1]. The overlay must rest on a uniformly supporting base that complies with Publication 408 specifications, and any areas lacking stable support such as severely crushed sections or joints that have lost load transfer must be removed or repaired before placement [1]. High‑early‑strength mixes are prohibited because of shrinkage risk, and any accelerated concrete must attain the minimum compressive strength within the time limits prescribed in Section 501 [1]. The overlay thickness is limited to a minimum of 2½ inches and a maximum of eight inches; sections between 2½ and 4 inches are classified as thin overlays, while those from 4 to 8 inches are considered standard, and the selected depth must be rounded up to the nearest half‑inch and verified against the minimum and maximum limits in Table 10.10 [1][2]. For bonded concrete overlay on asphalt (BCOA), the milled asphalt substrate must retain at least three inches of thickness after milling, and designs for overlays up to 6.5 inches shall follow the BCOA‑ME mechanistic‑empirical methodology using the associated software; thin BCOA may be as thin as 2½ inches but not less, while conventional BCOA requires a minimum of four inches and is typically applied where traffic demand exceeds 2 500 equivalent single axle loads per day [1]. The work area shall be divided into lots not exceeding three thousand square yards per traffic lane, with ramp sections treated separately, and the thickness of each lot must be verified by a randomly selected core in accordance with PTM No. 605 or an approved nondestructive method; projects at intersections or smaller than the size limit require two cores or an equivalent nondestructive test, and any alternative testing technique must receive written approval from the District Executive [2]. Panel dimensions are constrained so that no single panel exceeds fifteen feet in length, the sum of overall length and width does not surpass eighteen times the overlay thickness, and the aspect ratio (longer dimension divided by shorter) shall not exceed 1.5, with a practical aim toward unity [2]. Saw cuts defining transverse joints must penetrate at least one‑third of the slab depth within twelve inches of any edge and at least one‑quarter of the depth elsewhere, following the approved layout plan [2]. Placement is permitted only when the fresh concrete temperature lies between 65 °F and 90 °F and the prepared asphalt substrate temperature does not exceed 120 °F; fog‑misting or protective covers may be employed to maintain these limits [2]. Edge slump adjacent to shoulders must be limited to one‑quarter inch and to one‑eighth inch next to adjoining lanes [2]. The bond between the overlay and the existing pavement must achieve an average shear strength of at least 175 psi on three representative tests, with no individual test falling below 160 psi [2]. The concrete mix shall employ Class AA cement; if the overlay thickness is less than three inches, No. 67 coarse aggregate replaces No. 57, and for thicknesses under seven inches polypropylene fibers meeting ASTM C1116 Type III 4.13 specifications are required, with an aspect ratio not exceeding 150 and a dosage up to five pounds per cubic yard unless field trials demonstrate safe higher dosages [2]. Synthetic or steel macrofibers may be added in quantities that achieve the prescribed residual strength (f₁₅₀) shown in Table B, verified by a fourteen‑day ASTM C1609‑12 test, and the fiber contribution must yield a residual strength ratio of at least twenty percent; typical fiber dosages range from 0.2 % to 0.5 % by volume, providing a residual strength contribution between 100 and 225 psi that is added to the plain concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength for design [1][2]. Permeability requirements dictate a chloride‑ion penetration rating of Low, Very Low or Negligible after no more than fifty‑six days of curing according to AASHTO T277/T358; if a Moderate rating is pursued it may not exceed 2 800 coulombs [2]. All unstable slabs must be stabilized and any surface defects larger than four square inches or deeper than one inch filled before the interlayer is placed, and concrete shoulders or a widened lane of one to two feet—typically accompanied by asphalt shoulders—must be provided, with any widening filled to retain proper shoulder widths [1]. When a geotextile separation layer is used it may only be installed on joints faulting more than a quarter‑inch, must be overlapped eight ± 2 inches on each side, limited to three layers in any location, extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, kept clean and damp, and never allowed to exceed 120 °F prior to overlay placement [2]. An asphalt interlayer, when specified, shall consist of a one‑inch nominal depth of Superpave mix; joints faulting more than half‑inch must receive a scratch course or milling before concrete placement [2]. Curing requires insulating blankets whenever ambient temperature is forty degrees Fahrenheit or lower; these blankets may be removed only after the concrete surface temperature rises above forty degrees (or air temperature exceeds forty‑five degrees) and the average cylinder compressive strength reaches 3 600 psi, and removal must be performed so that the rate of surface‑temperature change does not exceed forty degrees per hour [2]. Pavement may be opened to traffic after a minimum cylinder strength of three thousand psi is verified by testing or maturity methods, with the higher three‑thousand‑six‑hundred‑psi threshold providing an additional safety margin [2]. The quantity of concrete is calculated from the specified thickness and coverage area, and any supplemental concrete used to replace unsound material removed during preparation may not exceed two percent of that computed volume; requests for additional material must be supported by delivery tickets, waste accounting, and a written justification [2].

Structural section and dimensions

The overlay can only be placed when the existing milled asphalt pavement provides a minimum thickness of three inches. Depending on the type of overlay, the Portland‑cement concrete slab may range from about two and one‑half to four inches for a thin bonded overlay, while a conventional bonded overlay requires at least four inches and can be designed up to roughly six and a half inches when the BCOA‑ME method is applied. When fiber‑reinforced concrete is selected, the slab thickness is typically limited to between three and six inches, which falls within the same conventional limits. For any overlay whose total depth is less than seven inches, the guides require synthetic reinforcement in the form of virgin polypropylene fibers, each fiber being no shorter than one inch and no longer than two and a half inches, with an aspect ratio not exceeding 150, and a maximum dosage of five pounds per cubic yard while delivering at least a twenty‑percent residual strength ratio. In addition to fiber reinforcement, when a geotextile interlayer is employed the material’s edges must be overlapped by approximately eight inches (allowable variation of two inches) and extended a minimum of four inches beyond the pavement edge, with no more than three layers overlapping at any location. Concrete shoulders or lane widenings are limited to a width of one to two feet; no other shoulder dimensions are prescribed. The guides do not prescribe explicit thicknesses for the underlying base, subbase, treated‑soil layer, or edge‑support elements, leaving those layers to be sized according to overall design criteria. Materials and mixture design

The overlay shall be a Portland‑cement concrete slab that meets the pavement‑concrete requirements of Publication 408 Section 704, using an optimized aggregate gradation and classified as Class AA cement‑concrete when a precast system is employed. When the overlay thickness is less than three inches the coarse aggregate shall be No. 67 rather than No. 57 to satisfy the depth‑related gradation requirements. The mix must achieve a minimum compressive strength of three thousand pounds per square inch before traffic opening, with many specifications requiring at least three‑thousand‑six hundred psi and an early‑strength development time consistent with Publication 408 Section 501 if rapid‑setting concrete is used; however, high‑early‑strength mixes are discouraged because they increase the risk of shrinkage cracking. Fresh concrete temperature at placement shall be maintained between sixty‑five and ninety degrees Fahrenheit, and the existing pavement surface temperature must not exceed one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit. Macro‑fibers may be incorporated to improve flexural performance; synthetic or steel fibers are permitted, supplied as monofilament or bundled monofilament with lengths from one to two‑and‑a‑half inches and an aspect ratio not exceeding one‑hundred fifty, and must be 100 % virgin polypropylene when polymeric. Fiber dosage shall be sufficient to obtain a residual flexural strength (f₁₅₀) between roughly one hundred and two hundred twenty‑five psi or a residual strength ratio of about twenty percent after fourteen days as measured by ASTM C1609‑12, which typically translates to a volume fraction of 0.2 % to 0.5 % (approximately five pounds per cubic yard) of concrete; the maximum dosage shall not exceed five pounds per cubic yard unless field testing confirms acceptable workability. Because fiber addition reduces workability, the mix design must allow appropriate adjustments of slump‑reducing or air‑entraining admixtures so that edge slump remains within one‑quarter inch adjacent to shoulders and one‑eighth inch next to adjoining lanes. An unbonded overlay system requires a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt separation layer, or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer, placed between the existing pavement and the new concrete. The curing regimen shall employ a white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene liquid‑membrane‑forming compound applied uniformly at a minimum rate of one gallon per 125 ft² for slabs six inches thick or less and one gallon per 150 ft² for thicker sections, providing continuous solid white opaque coverage on all exposed surfaces. When ambient temperature falls to forty degrees Fahrenheit or lower, insulating blankets must be used to prevent rapid heat loss and may be removed only after the average compressive strength reaches three‑thousand‑six hundred psi or when air temperature rises above forty degrees, with removal staged so that surface temperature does not change more than forty degrees in any one‑hour period. The overlay shall also meet a low to negligible chloride‑ion penetration rating within fifty‑six days as determined by AASHTO T277/T358 procedures. [1][2]

The selection and proportioning of a concrete overlay begin with an assessment of the existing pavement condition and anticipated traffic to determine the required overlay thickness [1]. A minimum milled asphalt base of three inches must be retained after milling before any Portland‑cement concrete is placed, and thin bonded overlays are limited to a nominal concrete depth between two and a half and four inches while conventional overlays start at four inches or greater to provide additional structural capacity [1]. For designs up to six and a half inches thick, the BCOA‑ME methodology and its associated software are employed to balance cement, aggregate, and admixture quantities [1]. When the finished overlay depth is less than three inches, the coarse aggregate must be No. 67 rather than the standard No. 57 to ensure adequate gradation for thin sections [2], and any overlay whose thickness is under seven inches requires a Class AA cement concrete that incorporates virgin polypropylene synthetic fibers supplied as monofilament or bundled monofilament, 1.0–2.5 in long with an aspect ratio not greater than 150 [2]. The fiber dosage is limited to five pounds per cubic yard unless field trials demonstrate acceptable workability at higher rates, and the amount added is calibrated so that a 14‑day ASTM C1609 (Illinois Modified) test yields a residual strength ratio of approximately twenty percent [2]. Concrete mixes are chosen in accordance with the standard Portland‑cement concrete specifications of Publication 408, Section 704, using an optimized aggregate gradation that supplies the necessary structural strength and durability [1]. High‑early‑strength concrete is generally avoided because it can lead to excessive shrinkage cracking; when rapid traffic opening is required, a rapid‑setting or accelerated mix is specified, capable of reaching the minimum compressive strength within four to seven hours, whereas standard mixes typically allow traffic reopening in twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours [1]. Structural performance is further enhanced by first designing the plain‑concrete matrix for the required modulus of rupture and then incorporating macrofibers until the target residual flexural strength (f150) – usually between one hundred and two hundred twenty‑five psi – is achieved, with fiber volume fractions commonly ranging from 0.2 to 0.5 percent by volume based on slab capacity, service life expectations, crack‑width limits, and joint load transfer requirements [1][2]. Trial batches are prepared to verify that fresh‑property criteria are met; water‑reducing admixtures are adjusted to compensate for slump loss up to four inches, and air‑entraining agents are modified as necessary to ensure workability, finishability, and long‑term crack resistance [1]. Durability is addressed by designing the mixture to attain a low, very low, or negligible chloride ion penetration rating after no more than fifty‑six days of curing, as verified by AASHTO T 277 or T 358, with permissible limits of 2,800 coulombs for the rapid chloride migration test and resistivity values not exceeding 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4‑by‑8‑inch cylinders (or 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6‑by‑12‑inch cylinders) [2]. All mix designs must follow the district’s approval process, with a complete Job Mix Formula submitted to the District Materials Engineer at least two weeks before placement for review of aggregate type, fiber content, cement class, water‑cement ratio, admixtures, and durability targets [2]. By adhering to these material selections, proportioning steps, and testing requirements, the overlay satisfies structural strength, resistance to chloride attack, constructability, and long‑term performance objectives.

Structural details and interfaces

Prior to placing a concrete overlay the existing pavement surface is prepared by cleaning and sealing all transverse expansion joints and filling them to within one‑quarter inch of the deck using an approved joint‑backing material; joints exhibiting more than a quarter‑inch of fault are milled, and those exceeding half an inch receive a scratch course or additional milling before the overlay is placed. When the overlay is unbonded, a separation layer is installed between the old slab and the new concrete, which may consist of at least one inch of fine‑graded asphalt film or a non‑woven geotextile interlayer whose edges overlap by about eight inches and extend four inches beyond the pavement edge, with no more than three layers permitted. If the existing pavement rests on a composite base slab, slab stabilization is required to reestablish uniform support, and a continuous pavement‑base drain is provided where necessary. Joint layout follows the practice for new concrete pavements; overlay joints need not coincide with the original transverse joints, but joint spacing is governed by slab thickness and pavement type, requiring 30 ft spacing for reinforced jointed concrete (RC‑21M) and 15 ft spacing for plain jointed concrete (RC‑27M), with an intermediate joint added when a single‑lane overlay would otherwise exceed roughly twenty feet. In the absence of a detailed plan, panel dimensions are limited to a maximum length equal to eighteen times the overlay thickness and an aspect ratio not greater than 1.5; any panel that would exceed fifteen feet or the 18 × thickness limit receives an intermediate transverse joint cut to full overlay depth plus one‑half inch. Existing transverse joints are saw‑cut after fresh concrete placement with a full‑depth cut equal to the overlay thickness plus one‑half inch, the cut width matching or exceeding the underlying crack, and a sealant reservoir of one‑quarter inch is formed in accordance with standard joint drawings. New control joints—both transverse and longitudinal—are created using a green‑cut saw to a minimum depth of one‑third slab thickness (no less than D/3 within twelve inches of an edge and no less than D/4 elsewhere) and a width of at least one‑eighth inch; longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths. All transverse joints that have lost load‑transfer capability are replaced with dowelled joints, the dowels being fully bonded to the existing slab with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders, and coated dowel bars serve as the primary load‑transfer devices. Where patches extend longer than about sixty‑five feet, longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required to maintain continuity across the length of the overlay. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, saw‑cut contraction joints must be placed at the proper depth and timing so that macrofibers act similarly to tie bars, increasing longitudinal tensile effort by roughly thirty percent; nevertheless conventional dowel bars remain necessary in locations where faulting control is required. Reinforcement with synthetic polypropylene fibers is mandated for overlays thinner than seven inches; the fibers must meet ASTM C1116 specifications—monofilament or bundled monofilament 1 to 2½ in long, aspect ratio ≤150, providing a residual strength ratio of 20 %—and are added at a dosage not exceeding five pounds per cubic yard unless field testing demonstrates acceptable workability. Edge preparation requires vertical cut edges at slab limits, with edge slump limited to one‑quarter inch adjacent to shoulders and one‑eighth inch next to adjoining lanes; concrete shoulders of one to two feet are provided when the existing shoulder is concrete, and may be combined with asphalt shoulders, while guide‑rail and drainage structures are adjusted or replaced as needed. Where the overlay meets an adjacent pavement section, concrete‑overlay transitions are constructed; if the transition overlies existing treated base or concrete, a geotextile debonding layer is placed beneath the transition. After curing, all transverse and longitudinal joints are sealed with an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant to ensure joint continuity and water protection. [1][2]

The first step in designing the concrete‑overlay interface is to evaluate the condition of the existing pavement and classify the system as either bonded or unbonded [1][2]. A bonded overlay is selected when core testing confirms that an asphalt slab is free of stripping, any cracking has been repaired, and at least three inches of sound asphalt remain after milling—or when a cement‑concrete or brick substrate retains a minimum of 2½ inches of binder material and exhibits little or no faulting [1][2]. Conversely, an unbonded design is required for pavements in poor condition, and it incorporates a separation layer of at least one inch of fine‑graded asphalt or a non‑woven geotextile placed over the cleaned surface [1][2]. Prior to placement, all loose material is removed and the existing surface is profiled by scarifying, shot‑blasting, water‑blasting, or milling to achieve a clean, roughened condition free of debris, oil, grease, standing water, and temperatures above 120 °F [2]. Cracks larger than three‑quarters inch are filled with an approved non‑shrink grout, surface defects greater than four square inches and deeper than one inch are patched, and any joint faulting exceeding one‑half inch is reduced by a scratch course or additional milling to provide a uniform bearing for the overlay [2]. Existing transverse joints receive joint backing or other approved filling material placed within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface to prevent displacement during paving [2][1]. For bonded overlays, the Portland‑cement concrete slab—typically ranging from 2½ to 8 inches in thickness—is placed directly on the prepared base and allowed to bond, with the overlay thickness determined by mechanistic‑empirical procedures such as DARWin for PCC or BCOA‑ME for thin sections and rounded to the nearest half‑inch within prescribed limits [1][2]. Thin bonded overlays may be 2½ to 4 inches thick, while conventional bonded overlays start at four inches and may extend up to 6½ inches, all following the BCOA‑ME methodology [1]. When an unbonded system is used, a geotextile debonding layer is laid flat over the surface; each sheet overlaps adjacent edges by eight inches (± 2 inches), extends at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, and no more than three layers are permitted in any location [2]. The same geotextile layer provides a transition where the overlay meets treated base course or existing concrete, ensuring continuity of the unbonded interface [2]. Joint layout for both bonded and unbonded overlays follows the project plan, avoiding longitudinal joints in wheel paths; if no layout is provided, panel dimensions are limited to a maximum length of eighteen times the overlay thickness and an aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5, with saw cuts made to a depth equal to the full overlay thickness plus one‑half inch [2][1]. Cut widths must be at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack, and after sawing all joints are sealed to create controlled movement paths and protect the interface from water intrusion [2]. Load transfer across transverse joints is achieved by dowelling each joint with coated bars anchored in epoxy, polyester, or vinylester binders, spaced at 30 feet for reinforced concrete (RC‑21M) and 15 feet for plain concrete, and sealed appropriately [1]. A continuous pavement‑base drain is provided to maintain proper drainage beneath the overlay, and any failed transverse joints are rehabilitated before placement [1]. Edge slumps adjacent to shoulders are limited to one‑quarter inch and to one‑eighth inch next to an adjoining lane to preserve alignment of the interface with adjacent features [2][1]. Where a one‑inch asphalt interlayer is specified, it is placed, compacted with a rubber‑tire roller, and its temperature kept below 120 °F before concrete placement to ensure proper bonding or separation as required [2][1]. Adjacent structures such as guide rails, drainage structures, and bridge clearances are adjusted or replaced as necessary, and shoulder configurations are designed to match the existing material—concrete shoulders when the original shoulders are concrete, otherwise widened by one to two feet with asphalt shoulders—to retain proper widths and functional continuity [1]. Finally, bond‑strength testing performed at seven days must yield an average of at least 175 psi with no individual test below 160 psi; failure to meet these values renders the interface defective [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

The design of a concrete overlay incorporates water‑management features to protect the new slab from moisture and to accommodate site‑specific drainage conditions. When required, a continuous pavement‑base drain is installed to convey water away from the structure, and any guide‑rail or existing drainage components affected by the work are adjusted or replaced so that runoff is directed clear of the slab. Where subsurface drainage deficiencies exist, adequate subdrainage is provided by installing appropriate subgrade drains before the overlay is placed, ensuring a stable support condition and controlling moisture beneath the pavement. For unbonded overlays, all existing joints are cleaned and sealed, a bond‑breaker and leveling course are applied, and cross‑slope is corrected to promote proper runoff and limit moisture intrusion into the underlying pavement. The overlay design also requires that the existing pavement be free of standing water after milling; any water that appears must be removed before placement using Department‑approved drainage measures. If a geotextile interlayer is used, it must terminate in a drainable layer or at a daylighted location so that its free‑drainage capability remains unobstructed, and the fabric should be dampened but not saturated prior to placement. Concrete may only be placed when its temperature is between 65 °F and 90 °F and the surface onto which it is poured is dry and below 120 °F; fog‑misting, protective covers or blankets are employed just before pouring to achieve and maintain these conditions without adding moisture. When forecast ambient temperatures are 45 °F or lower, insulating blankets are required to prevent rapid heat loss, and they remain in place until the air temperature rises above 45 °F or, if temperatures drop to 40 °F, until the slab reaches a compressive strength of at least 3,600 psi (or 3,000 psi as an alternative). Removal of insulation is staged so that the concrete surface temperature does not change more than 40 °F in any one‑hour period. All transverse and longitudinal joints are cleaned of deleterious material and sealed, and curing compounds are applied uniformly to protect the slab from adverse moisture conditions during early strength development. By integrating these drainage provisions, strict moisture control, temperature thresholds, and climate‑responsive insulation practices, the overlay maintains bond integrity and long‑term performance under varying environmental conditions. Verification and expected performance

The completed concrete overlay is accepted only after a systematic series of checks confirms that every aspect of the work conforms to the contract documents and the approved quality‑control plan. First, the material itself must be the specified Class AA cement concrete containing the required polypropylene fibers; the fibers have to be virgin monofilament or bundled, 1.0–2½ inches long with an aspect ratio not exceeding 150, a dosage limited to five pounds per cubic yard unless a field demonstration shows acceptable workability, and the mix must achieve at least a 20 percent residual strength ratio as measured by the Illinois‑modified ASTM C1609 test. Joint layout is verified against the approved plan; all transverse and longitudinal joints are located as shown, spaced according to the plan or an accepted supplemental layout, and saw cuts are made before any sealing to a depth of at least one‑third of slab thickness within 12 inches of each edge and at least one‑fourth elsewhere. The joints are cleaned of deleterious material and sealed, with longitudinal joints avoided in wheel paths. Panel dimensions are measured to ensure that no panel exceeds eighteen times the overlay thickness and that the longer‑to‑shorter side ratio does not exceed 1.5, striving for an aspect ratio as close to unity as practicable. Edge slump is inspected along the outer six inches adjacent to shoulders and adjacent lanes, where it must not exceed a quarter inch next to the shoulder and an eighth inch next to the lane. Prior to placement, backing or filling material must be installed within plus‑or‑minus a quarter inch of the existing pavement surface and protected from displacement. Curing is applied promptly: a white liquid membrane compound or other curing material is spread at the prescribed rate within thirty minutes after bleed water has dissipated, and insulating blankets are used when temperatures are 40 °F or lower, being removed without causing a surface temperature change greater than forty degrees per hour; the curing material remains in place until the slab reaches the minimum compressive strength required for traffic opening. Strength verification includes both compressive‑strength testing of cylinders and bond‑strength testing. After placement, five random bond‑strength tests are performed in the inspector’s presence at seven days according to ASTM C1583 on each 3,000 yd² lot; the average must be at least 175 psi and no individual result may fall below 160 psi for the area to be acceptable. The slab is also tested for compressive strength, with traffic permitted only when the minimum required value—either 3,600 psi as stipulated in some sections or 3,000 psi as indicated in the QC plan—is achieved, the latter being confirmed by cylinder testing or the maturity method (AASHTO T 413). Finally, the overlay is segmented into lots of no more than 3,000 square yards per lane; the inspector randomly selects one coring location per lot (two cores for intersections or smaller areas) and observes core drilling in accordance with PTM No. 606. Core depths are measured, and any deficiency greater than a quarter inch triggers additional coring until the required thickness is confirmed. The drilled holes are cleaned of laitance and filled with the same mix or a non‑shrink grout to verify that constructed thickness matches design. When all material, joint, panel, edge‑slump, backing, curing, strength, bond, and core criteria are satisfied, the Representative authorizes opening to traffic, indicating full compliance with constructability, performance, and project requirements.
