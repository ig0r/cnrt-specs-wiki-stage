Falling Weight Deflectometer

Part of: Pavement Condition Assessment is-subtopic-of
Falling Weight Deflectometer is a topic under Pavement Condition Assessment.
fwd-testingfwd-datafalling-weight-deflectometer-fwd-testingfalling-weight-deflectometerback-calculation
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

The Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) is a calibrated, nondestructive field device that applies a rapid 9,000‑lb impact load to an existing pavement surface and measures the resulting deflections to quantify in‑situ stiffness of the pavement layers and underlying subgrade, thereby providing an estimate of the structure’s effective load‑bearing capacity[1][2]. Deflection data are collected at the load point and at a standard offset (approximately four feet) – typically at lane‑center locations spaced no more than 50 ft apart – and back‑calculated, using AASHTO T 256 and ASTM D4694/D4695 methods, to obtain a subgrade resilient modulus (M_r) that is adjusted by a correction factor of C = 0.25 and halved for static use; when the back‑calculated M_r exceeds 15,000 psi it must be verified by an independent test[1]. The test is applicable to full‑depth asphalt sections, existing asphalt overlays on thin or deteriorated concrete, fractured concrete slabs and, rarely, directly to subgrade, but can only be performed when the pavement surface is not frozen and ambient temperature lies between 45 °F and 90 °F, with equipment calibrated at an SHRP regional facility within the current year[1][2].

Purpose and investigation objectives

The Falling Weight Deflectometer is employed to evaluate the in‑situ stiffness of both pavement layers and the underlying subgrade, thereby representing the effective structural capacity of an existing pavement[1]. It can be applied to a range of pavement structures including full‑depth asphalt sections, existing asphalt overlays on thin concrete pavements, overlays on severely ASR‑degraded concrete, pre‑1945 concrete, fractured PCC slabs and, rarely, directly on subgrade or subbase[1]. The investigation must determine whether the back‑calculated subgrade resilient modulus (M r) is appropriate for design, which requires confirming that accurate pavement layer data are available, that the pavement section does not change frequently, and that the surface is not heavily distressed; if a back‑calculated value exceeds 15 000 psi it must be verified by another test before use[1]. Based on the FWD results the investigation should answer how much effective structural capacity remains in the current pavement and whether that capacity meets the requirements for future traffic loads, what pavement and subgrade characteristics are needed for structural design, and whether the current pavement structure meets the requirements for a structural overlay[1]. For concrete‑pavement restoration projects the study must answer what repair thickness is required, may only be performed under acceptable field conditions—concrete joint testing only when the air temperature does not exceed 70 °F, never on a frozen subgrade, and no more than two years before construction—and should also determine whether concrete joints are performing efficiently and identify which locations and quantities of concrete patching are necessary[1]. The Falling Weight Deflectometer is also used to evaluate the elastic modulus of the fractured concrete layer in rigid pavements, provided the surface is not frozen and ambient temperature is between 45 °F and 90 °F[2]. The investigation must determine whether the average elastic modulus measured over a 1,000‑foot test section falls within the range set out in Table A; if it does not, the breaking pattern must be adjusted and retested[2]. It also asks whether the testing conditions are met and whether the back‑calculated modulus satisfies the specified acceptance criteria[2].

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 10 (pp. 177–179), Ch. 11 (pp. 198–199), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing must be performed on existing Portland cement concrete pavement before any overlay design because it quantifies the current structural condition and dictates whether a bonded or unbonded overlay is appropriate; results enable selection of a rigid bonded overlay for fair‑to‑excellent pavements or an unbonded overlay for moderately to significantly deteriorated sections, thereby informing planning decisions, design thicknesses, and surface preparation steps such as slab stabilization or drainage improvements. For Full Depth Reclamation projects the quantitative stiffness data supplied by FWD can be used to confirm whether an existing pavement has sufficient structural capacity and to calibrate the required layer thicknesses for the reclaimed structure; measurements taken after construction verify that the rebuilt pavement meets the targeted stiffness, informing design validation, future maintenance planning, and confidence in the rehabilitation strategy. The back‑calculated subgrade resilient modulus (Mr) obtained from FWD provides a basis for designing full‑depth asphalt sections, existing asphalt overlays on thin or deteriorated concrete, and other listed conditions; the Mr value must be adjusted with a correction factor of C = 0.25 to align it with laboratory values and the dynamic k derived from back calculation must be halved to obtain a static k for design use, and when the back‑calculated Mr exceeds 15,000 psi the result must be verified by another form of testing before it can influence pavement thickness or rehabilitation decisions. Non‑destructive measurements of structural capacity together with roughness and crack data allow estimation of the Present Serviceability Index (PSI) that ranges from 0 to 5; by comparing the PSI derived from FWD results with the initial and terminal serviceability values defined for each Maintenance Functional Classification, engineers can decide whether a segment requires routine maintenance, surface treatment, overlay or full reconstruction and can verify that design assumptions meet the required terminal serviceability level. When CBR or resilient modulus values are unavailable, the deflection measurements from an FWD test supply the structural data needed for overlay designs and indicate how much patching is required for Concrete Pavement Restoration projects, so having those results before final design helps shape both planning and design decisions; however concrete‑joint testing may only be performed when air temperature does not exceed 70 °F, no testing is allowed if the subgrade is frozen, and the results should not be older than two years before construction because conditions may worsen. FWD testing verifies whether concrete joints have failed and therefore require full‑depth patching, and it also confirms that a pavement is structurally sound enough to qualify for preservation treatments such as ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses or thin asphalt overlays provided the indicated total patching percentage is less than 10 %; these calibrated modulus results guide planners and designers in selecting appropriate rehabilitation methods and determining whether the extent of required patching stays within the cost‑effective limits set by the guidelines. Because patching quantities greater than 10 % of the pavement area are generally not cost‑effective or permitted for Pavement Preservation projects, FWD results help decide whether a preservation treatment is appropriate or if more extensive rehabilitation is warranted and may be used to verify the defined project scope. Falling Weight Deflectometer testing supplies an elastic‑modulus measurement of the fractured concrete layer that is directly compared to the limits in Table A; when the average modulus for a 1,000‑foot‑long test section falls within that range the fracturing pattern is accepted, but if it deviates, adjust the breaking pattern and conduct FWD testing for a new 1,000‑foot‑long test section. The operator records data at intervals of 50 feet or less with a 9,000‑pound drop weight, and provides results to the Representative within seven calendar days before any further fracturing or seating can resume. Because testing is prohibited when the pavement surface is frozen or air temperature falls below 45°F or exceeds 90°F, the modulus values give reliable confirmation that the chosen fracture energy produces a pavement stiffness suitable for subsequent rehabilitation design and construction. Scope and applicability

For existing Portland cement concrete pavements that require a structural evaluation before designing any type of overlay—rigid or flexible—the Falling Weight Deflectometer is appropriate and is required as part of the surface‑preparation process prior to rehabilitation work. [1] In full‑depth reclamation projects the device serves as an in‑situ test to obtain stiffness data on existing pavement materials before the rehabilitation and to determine the stiffness of the newly constructed pavement after construction, making it suitable for evaluating support‑layer conditions and overall structural capacity. [1] The method is permissible for full‑depth asphalt pavement sections and for existing asphalt overlays on thin concrete pavements where the original concrete is less than 8 inches deep or any parabolic sections, as well as for existing asphalt overlays on concrete pavements that suffer severe Alkali‑Silica Reaction degradation, for asphalt overlays on pre‑1945 concrete pavements, and prior to an AC overlay of fractured PCC slab pavement; application directly to subgrade or subbase is allowed only rarely. [1] The back‑calculated resilient modulus derived from FWD data may be unreliable when accurate pavement layer information is unknown, when the pavement section changes frequently over the tested area, or when the current pavement exhibits a fair amount of distress—especially cracking—and any Mr value above 15 000 psi must be verified by another form of testing before use in design. [1] The device is appropriate whenever a structural pavement overlay design is needed and laboratory CBR or resilient modulus values are unavailable, and it is required for Concrete Pavement Restoration projects to size the necessary patching; for CPR work it may be used on concrete joints only when the air temperature does not exceed 70 °F and the subgrade is not frozen, and the test should be performed no more than two years before construction to ensure conditions have not deteriorated. [1] In design guidelines the FWD is applied to rigid (concrete) pavements when full‑depth patching or joint rehabilitation is being considered, verifying that all failed joints have been identified and repaired and confirming structural soundness when total patching does not exceed 10 % of the pavement area. [1] It is employed whenever pavement and subgrade characteristics are needed to support a structural design, especially on concrete pavements where joint efficiency must be evaluated and the locations and quantities of required concrete patching identified. [1] For rigid concrete pavements that have been fractured and seated under crack‑and‑seat or break‑and‑seat rehabilitation methods, the Falling Weight Deflectometer is used to back‑calculate the elastic modulus of the fractured concrete layer; testing must be performed on the fractured and seated surface in the lane centre at intervals of 50 feet or less using a 9,000‑pound drop weight, and it may only be carried out when the pavement is not frozen and the air temperature is between 45 °F and 90 °F. [2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 11 (pp. 198–199), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Back‑calculated subgrade resilient modulus derived from FWD data is limited to a few defined pavement situations and depends on accurate knowledge of the pavement layer structure; it becomes unreliable when the layer information is unknown, when the pavement section changes frequently over the tested length, or when the existing surface exhibits a fair amount of distress such as cracking, which can produce superficially high modulus values. Consequently, if the back‑calculated Mr exceeds 15,000 psi, a different or complementary field test must be performed to verify the value before it is used in design. The Falling Weight Deflectometer cannot be used for concrete pavement joint testing when the air temperature exceeds 70 °F, it cannot operate on a frozen subgrade, and its results become questionable if the test is performed more than two years prior to construction because conditions may worsen; the test is also slow and requires coordinated traffic control. Consequently, another investigation such as using available California Bearing Ratio or Resilient Modulus data should be employed when those laboratory values exist, or a different field method must be chosen whenever temperature, frozen‑subgrade, or timing constraints prevent FWD testing. [1]

Existing information and records

Prior to Falling Weight Deflectometer testing the reviewer should gather a reliable estimate of future traffic loading, obtain the most recent pavement condition survey—including distress documentation such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, drainage issues, edge deterioration, joint spalling and faulting—as recorded in automated videologging surveys, compile any existing non‑destructive deflection testing results including prior FWD measurements and complementary DCP or LWD data that describe current pavement and subgrade stiffness, collect condition‑survey or component‑analysis records that document layer analysis and past repairs, review maintenance history documenting routine preservation actions such as crack sealing performed every 3 to 5 years, joint sealing performed every 5 years, spall repair and patching, examine traffic loading information in the form of Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) values or historic volume data for remaining‑life estimates, and assess sampling plans that detail number, locations, depths and handling of material samples to ensure they meet full‑depth reclamation criteria. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 10 (pp. 177–179), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

In section 5.15, the existing records often rely on traffic estimates, recent visual condition surveys and distress reports but they do not provide quantitative data on the structural stiffness of the pavement layers or the in‑situ strength of the supporting subgrade; these uncertainties must be resolved by field investigation using Falling Weight Deflectometer testing before rehabilitation to supply the needed stiffness information and confirm that the underlying support meets design requirements. In section 6.2, considerable uncertainty may exist regarding the validity of laboratory‑tested CBR values, with records sometimes showing high or low outliers, a wide range of results, or values that are not representative of the soil type, and they often lack accurate pavement layer information when sections change frequently or exhibit cracking; because back calculation of a subgrade resilient modulus from FWD data may be unreliable under those conditions, any back‑calculated Mr value above 15,000 psi must be verified by another form of testing before it is used in the pavement design process. In section 11.7, field investigation with a Falling Weight Deflectometer is needed when the existing records lack California Bearing Ratio or Resilient Modulus values required for an overlay design and when the age of any prior pavement data exceeds two years, creating uncertainty about current subgrade strength; testing concrete joints is only allowed if air temperature does not exceed 70 °F and no FWD testing can be performed on frozen subgrade. In section 13.7, automated pavement condition surveys (STAMPP) may miss certain distress types, leaving the records without reliable subgrade and structural property data needed for design, and they may be inconsistent regarding premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements, aggregate‑related problems such as Vanport Limestone or Alkali Silica Reaction that are not detectable by STAMPP, and the condition of ramp pavements which is not assessed at all; these uncertainties can affect estimates of required concrete patching especially when patching greater than 10 % of the pavement area would be uneconomical, so FWD results verify project scope, joint efficiency and subgrade characteristics that are otherwise uncertain. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 11 (pp. 198–199), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Visual inspection and condition assessment

Falling Weight Deflectometer testing should be conducted on the existing PCCP before an overlay is designed and it helps identify visible problems such as slab movement or faulting, rocking or pumping slabs, extensive surface spalling, cracking that may require full‑depth patching, and subsurface drainage deficiencies. A visual distress survey is required prior to using the FWD so that the most relevant surface problems are identified; the survey should document the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and any drainage issues because these distresses influence both the need for full‑depth reclamation and the interpretation of FWD stiffness results. When a FWD is employed, the presence of visible distress—particularly cracking—should be noted because a fair amount of such distress can make back‑calculated subgrade modulus values unreliable. The observable pavement conditions that should be documented include surface cracking, any patched areas and the depth of ruts, since these features are incorporated into the Present Serviceability Index derived from measurements of roughness, cracks, patches and rut depth. Falling Weight Deflectometer testing is also used to verify the condition of visible defects such as faulted joints, cracks, failed concrete patches, and transverse or longitudinal spalls that are identified for patching, and all failed joints must be verified by FWD before repair. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–151), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Field measurements and testing

Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing is employed both before rehabilitation to obtain the stiffness of existing pavement materials and after construction to determine the stiffness of the new pavement, providing a nondestructive measure of structural capacity [1]. The test forms part of a non‑destructive deflection testing program in which a controlled impact load is applied and the resulting surface deflections are recorded for interpretation of in‑situ subgrade and pavement stiffness, thereby supplying the effective structural capacity needed for overlay design [1]. Deflection measurements are taken at midslab locations that are not cracked under a controlled 9,000 pound drop weight in accordance with ASTM D 4694 and D 4695, and an additional measurement is made at approximately four feet from the centre of the load [1]. The recorded deflections are back‑calculated to obtain the subgrade resilient modulus (M_r), which is then adjusted by a correction factor of C = 0.25 to make it consistent with laboratory values, and any M_r value that exceeds 15,000 psi must be verified by another form of testing before it can be used in pavement design [1]. For slab‑fracturing projects the required nondestructive evaluation is FWD testing performed by a qualified operator, with surface deflections recorded at the lane centreline at spacings of 50 feet or less using the same 9,000‑pound drop weight [2]. The back‑calculation, following AASHTO T 256, ASTM D 4694, D 4695 and D 5858, yields the elastic pavement modulus of the fractured concrete layer [2]. The equipment must be calibrated at a Strategic Highway Research Program regional calibration facility within the current year and may only be used when the pavement is not frozen and ambient temperature is between 45°F and 90°F [2]. Complementary nondestructive field measurements include friction values obtained with a Single Locked Wheel Skid Tester and riding‑quality (roughness) data gathered by High Speed Profilers equipped with laser sensors; together these three inputs—friction, ride quality, and structural capacity from the FWD—are used to assist in selecting rehabilitation alternatives [1]. Subgrade performance may also be evaluated with a dynamic cone penetrometer, which gauges in‑situ strength through penetration resistance, and with a light weight deflectometer, which assesses subgrade stiffness via surface deflection under a lighter load [1]. In concrete pavement applications the FWD is employed to verify that all failed joints have been repaired during full‑depth patching and joint rehabilitation, serving as a nondestructive indication of structural adequacy, and it confirms that the pavement remains structurally sound when total patching percentages are less than ten percent, supporting eligibility for thin overlay treatments [1]. The measured deflection response from the FWD therefore provides the pavement and subgrade characteristics required for structural design and also indicates concrete pavement joint efficiency as well as the locations and quantities of required concrete pavement patching [1].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–151), Ch. 10 (pp. 177–179), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

The required equipment for Falling Weight Deflectometer testing is a calibrated falling weight deflectometer unit, and the test must be performed by a qualified operator; the device shall be calibrated at an SHRP regional calibration facility within the current year, and the manual also states that the FWD is the required equipment [1][2]. Testing is performed with a 9,000‑pound drop weight and deflection measurements are taken at a distance of approximately 4 feet from the centre of the load in accordance with ASTM D 4694 and D 4695, with data recorded in the lane centre [1][2]. The Pavement Policy Manual does not prescribe a specific spacing for FWD measurements [1]. In slab‑fracturing work the Pennsylvania specification requires data to be collected at intervals of 50 feet or less, an initial 1,000‑foot test section to establish the fracture pattern and subsequent verification tests on each 1,000‑foot lot, with a minimum of three lots per lane mile [2]. Results may need adjustment for seasonal effects, and the presence of a very stiff layer within about 15 feet of the subgrade top can produce artificially high back‑calculated moduli; back‑calculation is considered unreliable if accurate pavement layer information is unknown, the section changes frequently, or the pavement exhibits cracking, and any back‑calculated modulus (Mr) above 15,000 psi must be verified by another form of testing [1]. FWD tests may not be performed when the pavement surface is frozen or when ambient temperature falls below 45°F or exceeds 90°F [2].

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Sampling and laboratory investigation

The FWD must be used to sample the fractured concrete layer of the pavement after it has been broken and seated, with the operator back‑calculating its elastic modulus. Data are collected in the center of each travel lane at intervals of 50 feet or less using a 9,000‑pound drop weight, and verification testing continues throughout the fracturing operation by defining a lot as a 1,000‑foot segment; at least three such lots are selected for testing per lane mile. Sampling therefore focuses on the fractured concrete slab, with location spacing no greater than 50 feet and a minimum of three 1,000‑ft sections per mile to provide the required frequency of modulus checks. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

The Falling Weight Deflectometer must carry out testing and a back‑calculation procedure, following AASHTO T 256, ASTM D4694, ASTM D4695 and ASTM D5858, to determine the elastic pavement modulus of the fractured concrete layer; this evaluates the slab’s structural stiffness under the condition that the surface is not frozen and the air temperature stays between 45 °F and 90 °F. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Structural and functional performance

The Falling Weight Deflectometer test requires pavement surface deflection measurements taken at midslab locations that are not cracked under a 9,000‑lb load in accordance with ASTM D 4694/D 4695, plus an additional deflection point located approximately four feet from the centre of the load; these deflections are used to back‑calculate the subgrade resilient modulus Mr for assessing structural capacity. For slab‑fracturing projects the FWD must be scheduled with calibrated equipment and an operator, and it records surface deflection on the fractured and seated pavement at intervals of 50 feet or less using a drop weight of 9,000 pounds; the resulting data are back‑calculated to obtain the elastic pavement modulus of the fractured concrete layer, which is identified as the key measurement for evaluating structural capacity in that context. In addition to deflection, PennDOT’s non‑destructive testing program includes friction values obtained with a Single Locked Wheel Skid Tester and riding quality measured with High Speed Profilers; together these three measurements support evaluation of both the pavement’s structural condition and its functional performance. FWD data are also used to determine pavement and subgrade characteristics necessary for structural design, to indicate concrete‑pavement joint efficiency, and to identify locations and quantities of required concrete patching. Data interpretation and diagnosis

In evaluating a candidate roadway for full‑depth reclamation, visual inspection of distress such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems must be specifically considered in the rehabilitation strategy [1]. Prior to any reconstruction, Falling Weight Deflectometer testing is performed to obtain stiffness values of the existing pavement, while a post‑construction FWD test provides the stiffness of the newly placed layers; these measurements are later combined with laboratory‑derived strengths from sampled material to support interpretation of the back‑calculated modulus [1]. Proper sampling of the candidate roadway is required, and the obtained samples are evaluated in the laboratory for strength and other material properties that feed into the integrated analysis [1]. The integration process assembles visual distress observations, FWD deflection data (load‑point and approximately four‑foot offset), laboratory CBR or resilient‑modulus values, a prescribed correction factor of C = 0.25, any seasonal adjustment, and observed performance trends into a summary report that guides decisions on layer thickness, drainage corrections and stabilization method [1]. Because cracking can render the back‑calculated subgrade resilient modulus unreliable, visual observations must be reviewed before accepting the modulus values, which are only permissible for full‑depth asphalt sections, certain overlays, or direct subgrade tests [1]. When the back‑calculated Mr exceeds 15,000 psi, an additional verification test such as DCP or further laboratory work is required before the value may be used in design; values near 4,000 psi are classified as poor, about 8,000 psi as fair, and close to 16,000 psi as high, prompting a flag for further validation of unusually high results [1]. For slab‑fracturing work on existing concrete pavements, the surface is first wetted to permit a visual inspection that confirms all cracks are visible on the top of the fractured slab [2]. The operator then performs FWD testing at intervals of 50 feet or less using a 9,000‑pound drop weight, and back‑calculates the elastic modulus of the fractured layer with an approved software package in accordance with AASHTO T 256 and related ASTM methods [2]. The obtained modulus values are compared to the range specified in Table A; if they fall within that range the fracture pattern is accepted, otherwise the breaking pattern is adjusted and the FWD test repeated, thereby integrating visual observations, field deflection measurements, analytical back‑calculation results and performance criteria to verify compliance with structural requirements [2].

Findings, confidence, and next actions

The Pavement Policy Manual states that Falling Weight Deflectometer testing can confirm the stiffness of existing pavement before a full‑depth reclamation project and verify the rebuilt layer stiffness after construction, thereby supporting judgments about structural adequacy for anticipated traffic loads (section 5.15) [1]. It also provides a field‑derived estimate of subgrade resilient modulus that may supplement laboratory CBR values when applied to full‑depth asphalt sections or overlays on thin concrete; the back‑calculated Mr must be adjusted with a correction factor of C = 0.25 and converted to a static value by dividing the dynamic k by two, and seasonal effects may also require adjustment (section 6.2) [1]. The test supplies data on pavement structural capacity that can be used as an engineering tool to help choose rehabilitation alternatives and provides objective measurements of load‑bearing performance (section 6.3) [1]. It furnishes the structural data needed for overlay designs when CBR or resilient modulus values are missing and supplies a quantitative basis for determining patch quantities on concrete pavement restoration projects (section 11.7) [1]. FWD can verify that concrete pavements are structurally sound and confirm the presence of failed joints, thereby justifying full‑depth patching and joint rehabilitation when total patching percentage is less than 10 % (section 12.3) [1]. When required patching exceeds 10 % of the pavement area it generally indicates that preservation treatment is not cost‑effective (section 13.7) [1]. Because FWD test results are not required to be addressed in District Interstate plans except when needed to verify project scope, their role in prioritizing work remains limited, leaving some uncertainty about how fully they capture overall pavement condition (section 13.7) [1]. Back‑calculation of a subgrade resilient modulus from FWD data is not an exact science and becomes unreliable if accurate pavement layer information is unknown, if the pavement section changes frequently over the deflection‑tested length, or if the current pavement exhibits distress such as cracking, which can produce superficially high modulus values (section 6.2) [1]. Therefore, if back calculation of Mr yields a value above 15,000 pounds per square inch it must be verified by another form of testing prior to its use in the pavement design process (section 6.2) [1]. Concrete joint tests may only be performed if air temperature does not exceed 70°F and no FWD testing can be performed on frozen subgrade, and results obtained more than two years before construction are discouraged because pavement conditions and design needs may change (section 11.7) [1]. A comprehensive assessment still relies on additional in‑situ tests such as dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer, detailed distress surveys, sampling plans and drainage evaluations to address underlying causes of damage; thus uncertainty remains regarding subgrade strength, moisture effects and other failure mechanisms (section 5.15) [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that Falling Weight Deflectometer testing can be used to back‑calculate the elastic pavement modulus of a fractured concrete layer and verify that the average modulus for a 1,000‑foot test section or each 1,000‑foot lot falls within the range set out in Table A, thereby confirming whether the chosen breaking pattern is acceptable or must be altered (section 522.3) [2]. The method is limited to conditions where the surface is not frozen and air temperature stays between 45°F and 90°F (section 522.3) [2]. It requires equipment calibrated at a SHRP regional facility each year, relies on approved back‑calculation software, and provides only average modulus values measured at intervals of 50 feet or less with a 9,000‑pound drop weight, leaving potential localized variations and temperature‑related testing gaps as sources of uncertainty (section 522.3) [2].

Following an FWD test, the agency should use the measured pavement and subgrade characteristics to confirm structural design inputs and assess concrete joint efficiency, identifying any locations where concrete patching is required; patching must include repair of all failed joints verified by the FWD test and may be performed up to, but not exceeding, 10 % of the pavement area unless a cost‑effectiveness analysis justifies a larger extent, with greater than 10 % generally prompting more extensive rehabilitation rather than preservation actions [1]. When structural deficiencies are identified, a condition‑based rehabilitation plan may call for full‑depth patching of the original pavement with PCC or an approved asphalt concrete base course, slab stabilization or jacking where slab movement or faulting is present, and provision of adequate subdrainage to address subsurface drainage problems; the choice between bonded or unbonded overlays follows the assessed condition, with unbonded overlays requiring a minimum 1‑inch fine‑graded asphalt separation layer (or geotextile) to isolate the new concrete [1]. The next steps after obtaining FWD measurements of existing pavement stiffness include confirming subgrade strength with complementary in‑situ tests such as the dynamic cone penetrometer and light weight deflectometer, verifying that layer thicknesses meet structural requirements, correcting any excess moisture or drainage deficiencies identified by the stiffness data before reconstruction, and collecting representative material samples according to Appendix J for laboratory evaluation and design of the appropriate stabilization mechanism [1]. If the back‑calculated subgrade resilient modulus exceeds 15,000 psi, it must be verified with another form of testing prior to its use in design; the back‑calculated value must then be adjusted with a correction factor C = 0.25 while accounting for seasonal effects, and when laboratory CBR results are uncertain additional laboratory or field testing such as repeat FWD or DCP measurements should be performed to validate the modulus, with low‑CBR zones potentially requiring subgrade improvement measures such as cement or lime stabilization, rock capping, geogrids or geotextiles coordinated with the District Geotechnical Engineer and documented in the pavement design submission [1]. After a concrete slab has been fractured and seated, a minimum 1,000‑foot‑long test section must be evaluated with calibrated FWD equipment under ambient temperatures above 45 °F and below 90 °F; the operator performs back‑calculation of the elastic modulus in accordance with AASHTO T 256, ASTM D4694, D4695 and D5858 using a 9,000‑lb drop weight and records data at intervals of 50 feet or less [2]. The average modulus of the fractured concrete layer is then compared with the limits in Table A, and if it exceeds the upper limit the breaking pattern must be modified by reducing breaking energy and a new test section retested; similarly, each 1,000‑ft lot (at least three per lane mile) is verified by FWD and any lot that fails requires re‑fracturing and repeat testing [2]. Additional corrective actions after successful fracturing and seating include removing exposed reinforcing steel or steel plates, ensuring proper seating before traffic returns, applying a tack coat and placing an asphalt leveling course within one week, and avoiding exposure of the seated surface to freezing conditions [2].
