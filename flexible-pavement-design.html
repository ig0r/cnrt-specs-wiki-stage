Flexible Pavement Design

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Flexible Pavement Design is a topic under Pavement Design.
flexible-pavement-designfull-depth-flexible-pavement-designflexible-designflexible-designsfull-depth-flexible-design
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Flexible pavement design is a methodology that creates a structurally sufficient, cost‑effective roadway by distributing traffic loads through a layered asphalt system to the subgrade while meeting minimum thickness and performance criteria. It hinges on an accurate characterization of the subgrade’s elastic response—typically via resilient modulus obtained from laboratory or CBR testing—and uses the resulting Design Structural Number to derive individual layer thicknesses with either the Specified Thickness or Optimized Thickness methods, applying drainage coefficients of 1.0 and respecting prescribed minimum and maximum course depths [1]. The approach also accounts for climate‑related effects by adjusting the terminal serviceability index for frost‑heave loss, ensuring that the pavement retains an acceptable level of serviceability throughout its design life and satisfies life‑cycle cost objectives as mandated by the 1993 AASHTO Guide and PennDOT procedures [1].

PennDOT publications covering “Flexible Pavement Design” also include the following subtopics: Structural Number.

The Structural Number quantifies the required structural strength for full-depth flexible pavements by summing material-specific coefficients and layer thicknesses to bridge the gap between existing capacity and future traffic demands. [1]

Structural Number

Purpose and design objectives

Flexible pavement design is employed to ensure that a pavement structure can safely distribute traffic loads to the subgrade while meeting minimum thickness and other performance criteria, thereby providing a structurally sufficient pavement in a cost‑effective manner; the required analysis verifies that the proposed pavement section will be structurally adequate for the anticipated traffic and site conditions. It also addresses the need for a pavement structure that can reliably support traffic loads by accurately characterizing the elastic response of the subgrade, so the resilient modulus of the roadbed soils must be determined through laboratory or field testing on representative samples under stress and moisture conditions that reflect seasonal variations, with spring modulus values typically 10 % to 20 % lower than normal/summer values. When the subgrade may be weak, highly variable, or prone to frost‑induced heave, the design must provide a durable load‑carrying structure by selecting appropriate layer thicknesses and materials that prevent premature failure; consequently the design must avoid using resilient modulus values greater than 20 in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation, and it must incorporate stronger base courses or subgrade improvements such as frost‑susceptible soil removal, stabilization, or geogrids to maintain a high degree of serviceability over extended periods. For full‑depth flexible pavements the design is intended to ensure that an acceptable level of serviceability is retained at the end of the design life even after accounting for performance loss caused by frost heave, requiring selection of the terminal serviceability value from Table 6.3 and addition of the calculated loss of serviceability due to frost heave. Finally, the design addresses the need to provide sufficient structural strength by calculating a Design Structural Number that represents the required strength; any thickness‑based SN obtained from the layer design must be greater than this Design SN to be considered structurally adequate, with thicknesses derived using either the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method while all drainage coefficients shall be set to 1.0 and respecting the minimum and maximum course depths, adjustments being made to achieve the lowest total pavement cost. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 134–150), Ch. 9 (pp. 168–173)

The design must achieve a terminal serviceability index that equals the Table 6.3 value plus the loss of serviceability caused by frost heave, which is required for full‑depth flexible pavements. The frost‑heave loss is computed using three inputs—frost‑heave rate, maximum potential serviceability loss derived from the depth of frost penetration, and frost‑heave probability—and must follow the procedure outlined in Appendix G of the 1993 AASHTO Guide. For the probability input the design shall use a recommended range of 25%–75% for all flexible designs. [1]

[1] — Ch. 9 (pp. 168–169)

Scope and applicability

For PennDOT Oversight projects the manual suggests preparing both a rigid and a flexible design alternative, and a DARWin pavement design analysis that includes flexible designs is required whenever a new construction, reconstruction or rehabilitation segment exceeds more than 500 continuous feet on divided roadways, more than 1,000 continuous feet on undivided roadways, or bridge approach paving greater than 1,000 feet; in short‑section projects where the existing pavement is a composite that far exceeds minimum thicknesses or contains elements impractical to replicate, a DARWin analysis—including flexible options—must be performed when directed by the District Pavement Management Engineer; for Federal Oversight projects a flexible design may also be selected as one of the alternatives determined during the Scoping Field View. Flexible pavement design relies on the subgrade’s resilient modulus (Mr) as a direct input, so it is appropriate wherever reliable Mr values can be obtained—either from laboratory testing of representative soil samples or estimated from CBR results using the Heukelom equation—and such laboratory‑derived Mr or CBR data may be used for interstate and expressway projects. The approach may be used for full‑depth asphalt pavement sections and a variety of overlay applications, including existing asphalt overlays on thin concrete pavements (original concrete less than 8 inches in depth or any parabolic sections), overlays on concrete pavements that suffer severe alkali‑silica reaction degradation, overlays on pre‑1945 concrete pavements, and AC overlays of fractured PCC slabs, as well as the rare case of designing directly on subgrade or subbase; for rehabilitation projects where the existing pavement is not removed, the in‑situ density of the subgrade should be considered when converting CBR to resilient modulus, using a multiplier of 1 000 rather than 1 500 with the Heukelom and Klomp equation. Flexible Pavement Design is applied to full‑depth flexible pavements whose structural adequacy is expressed by a design Structural Number that is converted to individual layer thicknesses, and it may use either the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method (the Layered Analysis Thickness Design method must not be used), with all drainage coefficients set to 1.0 and thickness limits governed by the minimum and maximum course depths from Tables 9.4 and 9.5; it is appropriate when the approved structure includes a flexible pavement having either an aggregate/cement or an aggregate/lime/pozzolan base course, which must be offered as bid alternatives. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 134–150), Ch. 9 (pp. 172–173)

In projects that fall under PennDOT Oversight, the manual suggests evaluating one rigid and one flexible design alternative rather than relying solely on a flexible pavement design. For Federal Oversight projects, the specific design alternatives are determined during the Scoping Field View, which may result in selecting a non‑flexible option. When an existing composite pavement greatly exceeds the minimum pavement thickness or incorporates features that cannot be reasonably replicated—such as crack‑and‑seat constructions—the District Pavement Management Engineer may require a DARWin pavement design analysis to achieve a structurally sufficient and cost‑effective solution, which can lead to choosing a different pavement type. If isolated low‑CBR zones are present, if CBR values vary widely across the site, if frost‑susceptible soils present significant frost‑heave risk, or if the subgrade strength falls below a CBR of five, a single uniform flexible pavement design may be inappropriate; designers should consider targeted subgrade improvement, dividing the project into limited subsections with different pavement designs, or employing a rigid pavement structure with an adequate granular foundation to mitigate frost effects. Designers are advised to work with the District Geotechnical Engineer to determine whether subgrade strengthening would be a cost‑effective solution regardless of whether the existing subgrade value is below or above a CBR of 5. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 134–150)

Design inputs and requirements

Flexible pavement design for PennDOT is governed by the 1993 AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures as amended by the Department, and must be performed using AASHTOWare DARWin 3.01 software with input values set out in Chapters 6‑10. The analysis is required whenever the paved length exceeds 500 ft on divided roadways, 1,000 ft on undivided roadways, or 1,000 ft of bridge approach pavement; short‑section designs must either replicate an existing structure that already meets the minimum thicknesses in Tables 8.3 and 9.4 or meet those minima themselves. Design inputs shall include an 18‑kip ESALs over the initial performance period, specified initial and terminal serviceability levels, a chosen reliability level, the overall standard deviation of 0.45, the roadbed soil resilient modulus, and the number of construction stages. The terminal serviceability index is taken from Table 6.3 and must be increased by the loss of serviceability due to frost heave as determined from the 1993 AASHTO Guide Appendix G (Section G.2); the frost‑heave loss is computed using the frost‑heave rate read from Table 9.1 or Table 9.2 according to subgrade soil type, the maximum potential serviceability loss derived from the design freezing index and Figure G.7, and a frost‑heave probability selected within the recommended range of 25 %–75 %, with values obtained from Figure G.8 for the project’s design life. The calculated structural number must be greater than the design structural number; conversion to individual layer thicknesses uses the standard equation with structural coefficients taken from Table 9.3 and all layer drainage coefficients set to 1.0. Thickness selection may only employ the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method available in DARWin, while the Layered Analysis Thickness Design method is prohibited. The allowable thicknesses are further constrained by the minimum and maximum course depths listed in Tables 9.4 and 9.5, and cost considerations for surface, base, and subbase per‑inch rates must be evaluated to achieve the lowest total pavement cost. When the approved structure uses an aggregate/cement or aggregate/lime/pozzolan base, those two base materials must appear in the project bid as alternatives. Subgrade resilient modulus shall be determined in consultation with the District Geotechnical Engineer pursuant to Publication 293 Geotechnical Engineering Manual and used directly in the design. Laboratory determination of Mr shall follow PennDOT’s Moisture Density Test (PTM No. 106), Unconfined Compressive Strength Test (AASHTO T 208), Soil Classification (AASHTO M 145) and Resilient Modulus Test (AASHTO T 307). Laboratory CBR values may be used as an alternative when obtained on soaked samples per AASHTO T 193 and converted to Mr with the Heukelom‑Klomp equation; any CBR value greater than 10 shall be recommended by the District Geotechnical Engineer and approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, while values less than 5 trigger required subgrade improvement justification. No CBR value greater than 20 may be used in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation, which caps the allowable resilient modulus at 30,000 psi. When Mr is back‑calculated from Falling Weight Deflectometer data it shall be multiplied by a correction factor of C = 0.25 and the dynamic k obtained shall be divided by two to obtain a static k; any Mr above 15,000 psi shall be verified by another test before use. Seasonal variations in subgrade modulus shall be accounted for, with spring modulus values typically 10 % to 20 % lower than normal/summer values and frozen values about two times greater. Frost‑susceptible soils shall be removed or isolated with a non‑frost‑susceptible layer. Reconstruction projects shall perform gradation, in‑place moisture content, density, and CBR testing on existing subgrade and borrow material before pavement placement. Project timing constraints require Pavement Preservation projects to be let within 15 months of the Pavement Type Selection approval date; moderate‑complexity projects shall achieve design approval within one year after Design Field View (or environmental) approval and at least six months before the Final Design Office Meeting; most‑complex projects shall do so within two years under the same conditions. If the design year changes by more than three years a new design and life‑cycle cost analysis must be performed. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 134–150), Ch. 9 (pp. 168–173)

Design methods and criteria

Flexible pavement designs for PennDOT are performed using the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures as amended by the Department, and they must be executed with the AASHTOWare® DARWin® 3.01 software; the methodology incorporates traffic inputs, resilient‑modulus assumptions when geotechnical data are unavailable, CBR or Falling Weight Deflectometer results for subgrade characterization, and a life‑cycle cost analysis to evaluate alternatives, and designs are required whenever the construction/reconstruction pavement length is more than 500 continuous feet on divided roadways and 1,000 continuous feet on undivided roadways, with DARWin printouts—including structural calculations and LCCA—forming the formal design package. A preliminary pavement design may be developed prior to receiving the geotechnical report or soils findings by assuming a resilient modulus.

Flexible pavement design relies on the resilient modulus (Mr) of the subgrade, which is obtained either from direct laboratory testing (Moisture Density Test PTM No. 106, Unconfined Compressive Strength AASHTO T 208, Soil Classification AASHTO M 145, and Resilient Modulus Test AASHTO T 307) or estimated from a California Bearing Ratio (CBR) test performed on soaked samples according to AASHTO T 193 and then converted using the empirical relationship Mr = 1500 × CBR; designers shall also evaluate soil gradation through the coefficients of uniformity Cu = D60/D10 and curvature Cc = (D30)^2/(D10·D60) as defined in ASTM D2487, and compute the AASHTO Group Index using GI = (% passing #200 – 35)[0.2 + 0.005(LL – 40)] + 0.01(% passing #200 – 15)(PI – 10), with a simplified form for groups A‑2‑6 and A‑2‑7, while seasonal adjustments (spring values typically 10 % to 20 % lower than normal/summer and frozen values about twice normal/summer) are applied before using Mr directly in the AASHTO flexible pavement design process. When converting CBR to Mr, the Heukelom and Klomp correlation may be modified by multiplying the CBR by 1,000 instead of 1,500 if in‑situ density is significantly less than maximum dry density, and a CBR value greater than 20 shall not be used in this equation; Mr can also be back‑calculated from Falling Weight Deflectometer data with a correction factor of C = 0.25 and the dynamic k value must be divided by two to obtain a static k, while Dynamic Cone Penetration test results are converted to Mr through the same CBR relationship, and rock‑capped sections are modeled with CBR = 20 and lime or cement stabilized layers use a structural coefficient of 0.12 in the design calculations; frost effects are addressed by applying the AASHTO frost‑heave calculator (Appendix G) as an additional tool.

The full‑depth flexible pavement design follows the procedures in the 1993 AASHTO Guide for Design of Pavement Structures and requires a set of input values that include 18‑kip ESALs over the initial performance period, the initial serviceability, the terminal serviceability, the reliability level, the overall standard deviation, the roadbed soil resilient modulus, and the number of construction stages.

In full‑depth flexible pavement design the terminal serviceability index is taken from Table 6.3 and increased by the loss of serviceability caused by frost heave; because DARWin does not compute that loss, it must be obtained from Appendix G (Section G.2) using three empirically based inputs—a frost‑heave rate selected from Tables 9.1 or 9.2 according to subgrade soil type, the maximum potential serviceability loss derived from the design freezing index via Figure 9.1 and Figure G.7, and an estimated frost‑heave probability normally recommended between 25 % and 75 %; the required loss is read from Figure G.8, and a PennDOT online Frost Heave spreadsheet tool may be used to automate these calculations.

After entering all required variables, the user obtains the Design Structural Number (SN) by activating the calculate function; SN must be converted to individual layer thicknesses using the equation SN = a₁d₁ + a₂d₂m₂ + a₃d₃m₃ + … aₙdₙmₙ. The resulting thicknesses are derived with either the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method supplied in DARWin, and the Layered Analysis Thickness Design method shall not be used; structural coefficients aᵢ are taken from Table 9.3 and all drainage coefficients mᵢ shall be set to 1.0, with the design bounded by the minimum and maximum course depths listed in Tables 9.4 and 9.5, while cost‑effective adjustments consider per‑inch costs, lift depth limits, and the structural coefficients. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 134–150), Ch. 9 (pp. 168–173)

The coefficient of uniformity Cu is determined by the ratio of D60 to D10:

Cu=D60D10

The formula computes the coefficient of uniformity from the percent passing specific sieves. As D60 grows, the result rises. As D10 grows, the result falls.

If the design year used differs by more than 3 years from the approved design year, a new design and life‑cycle cost analysis must be prepared. Preservation projects shall be let within 15 months of Pavement Type Selection approval; moderate‑complexity projects shall obtain design approval within 1 year after Design Field View (and at least 6 months before the Final Design Office Meeting); most‑complex projects shall obtain approval within 2 years after Design Field View (and at least 6 months before the Final Design Office Meeting). In flexible pavement design the subgrade must be characterized by a resilient modulus that is obtained in consultation with the District Geotechnical Engineer and may be derived from laboratory CBR tests using Mr = 1500 × CBR; seasonal variations are assumed, with spring modulus values 10% to 20% lower than normal/summer values and frozen values typically twice as high. Any subgrade CBR greater than 10 shall be recommended by the District Geotechnical Engineer and approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, while a CBR less than 5 must trigger justification for significant undercutting or other improvement methods, and all laboratory CBR results shall be verified against companion test data such as soil classification, gradation, percent passing #200 sieve, Atterberg limits, maximum dry density, optimum moisture content and Group Index to confirm the subgrade can achieve the estimated Mr. The flexible pavement design must be based on a subgrade CBR value that reflects the weakest soil type, and no CBR value greater than 20 may be used in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation which limits the resulting resilient modulus to 30 000 psi; consequently Mr values between 20 000 and 30 000 psi should be applied with extreme caution and any back‑calculated Mr above 15 000 psi must be verified by another test before use. When Falling Weight Deflectometer data are used, the back‑calculated modulus shall be adjusted with a correction factor of C = 0.25 (or less if a stiff layer is present) and converted to a static k value by dividing the dynamic k by two. For subgrades with CBR at or below 5, subgrade strengthening options such as cement or lime stabilization, rock capping modeled with CBR = 20, or geogrid/geotextile installation shall be evaluated, and the design plans must specify a minimum acceptable resilient modulus for the roadbed soil. For full‑depth flexible pavement design the terminal serviceability index shall be taken as the value listed in Table 6.3 plus the loss of serviceability caused by frost heave; this loss is calculated from the 1993 AASHTO Guide using three inputs—frost‑heave rate determined from Table 9.1 or Table 9.2 based on subgrade soil type, maximum potential serviceability loss derived from frost penetration depth, and frost‑heave probability which shall be selected in the range of 25%–75% for all flexible designs—and because DARWin does not compute the frost‑heave loss, the designer must determine it manually by following the chart procedures. The design should use an overall standard deviation of 0.45 for flexible pavement design as specified in subsection 9.5 of Chapter 9 and referenced to Section 6.5. For a full‑depth flexible pavement the calculated structural number obtained from the thickness design shall exceed the required Design SN to be considered structurally adequate; this calculation shall use either the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method in DARWin and may not employ the Layered Analysis Thickness Design method. All layer drainage coefficients mi shall be set to 1.0, structural coefficients ai are taken from Table 9.3, and the design is limited by the minimum and maximum course depths given in Tables 9.4 and 9.5 while seeking the lowest total pavement cost. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 134–150), Ch. 9 (pp. 168–173)

Materials and mixture design

In the subgrade soils portion of flexible pavement design (section 6.2) the designer shall obtain a resilient modulus for the roadbed soil, either from laboratory Mr tests or by estimating it from CBR using the relationship Mr =1500×CBR, and shall consult with the District Geotechnical Engineer in that determination. The required CBR test results must be supported by companion laboratory data; any CBR greater than 10 or less than 5 shall be recommended by the District Geotechnical Engineer and approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, while no CBR value greater than twenty may be used in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation. The design also must specify a minimum resilient modulus for the roadbed soil and require that all borrow material meet this same modulus. When subgrade strengthening is required, rock capping shall be modeled with a CBR of twenty and lime or cement stabilization shall be represented by a structural coefficient of 0.12 together with the project‑specific CBR for the underlying soil. The specification shall include the soil classification, gradation criteria (for gravels Cu ≥ 4 and 1 ≤ Cc ≤ 3, for sands Cu ≥ 6 and 1 ≤ Cc ≤ 3), percent passing the #200 sieve, Atterberg limits (liquid limit, plastic limit, plasticity index), AASHTO Group Index, maximum dry density and optimum moisture content. Seasonal effects shall be noted by stating that spring modulus values are 10 % to 20 % lower than normal or summer values and that frozen subgrade modulus values are typically two times greater than normal/summer values. For separation a Class 4 geotextile Type A shall be placed across the full lane width of at least eight feet, and where the subgrade CBR is less than three a Class 4 Type C geotextile shall be provided to give both separation and reinforcement. Cement or lime stabilization rates shall be derived from laboratory testing of soil gradation, liquid limit, plasticity index, shrinkage limit, unconfined compressive strength, dry density, sulfate/chloride content and organic content.

In the structural number portion of flexible pavement design (section 9.8) the engineer must specify a structural coefficient a_i for each layer taken from Table 9.3 and set every layer drainage coefficient m_i to 1.0. The specification shall also identify the surface, base and subbase material types together with their cost per inch, and the minimum and maximum lift depths drawn from Tables 9.4 and 9.5 so that thicknesses can be selected to achieve the required structural number while minimizing total cost. When a flexible pavement includes a base course, the bid shall present both an aggregate‑cement option and an aggregate‑lime‑pozzolan option as alternatives. [1]

Structural details and interfaces

Flexible pavement design requires that the interface between the subgrade and overlying aggregate layers be stabilized, for which geogrids may be placed at that boundary. Separation of the subgrade from the subbase is achieved with Class 4 geotextile fabrics; Type A layer‑separation fabric shall be used on all completed subgrades across the full lane width of eight feet or greater, while Type C stabilization fabric is specified when the underlying CBR is less than three. Where rock excavation is needed, the Soil Survey Report must evaluate the rock conditions and prescribe a recommended depth of subbase or other treatment for each rock cut, and gradual granular transitions are employed at abrupt changes in subgrade support to maintain uniform conditions. Drainage, environment, and accessibility

In the pavement design analysis/submittal (section 6.1), the typical section drawing must depict all edge drains so that drainage features are incorporated into the structural layout, and the geotechnical report is required to provide a Frost Heave Probability as described in Section 9.3.3 to address moisture‑related climate effects on subgrade performance; for temporary crossovers and similar provisions the design should consider seasonal factors, service length, and truck traffic percentages. In evaluating subgrade soils (section 6.2), flexible pavement design shall obtain seasonal resilient modulus values from laboratory or field testing that simulate stress and moisture conditions of distinct seasons; spring modulus values are 10% to 20% lower than normal/summer values, while frozen subgrade modulus values are typically two times greater than the normal/summer values. The design must also recognize that soils with high fines content are usually more moisture sensitive, harder to compact, and exhibit a higher susceptibility to frost heave, so their classification, gradation, Atterberg limits, and group index should be documented to guide appropriate subgrade improvement or drainage measures. Flexible pavement designs should incorporate measures that control moisture and protect against freeze‑thaw effects by removing soils highly susceptible to frost heave and replacing them with a non‑frost‑susceptible layer thick enough to insulate the roadbed from frost penetration, while also providing a stronger base course to sustain reduced bearing support. The design should include pavement base drains or other appropriate drains to improve drainage, especially when subgrade conditions vary, and when the project is divided into subsections with differing thicknesses the drainage beneath the newly constructed pavements must be effectively accommodated. Subgrade improvement decisions such as rock capping of 1 to 3 feet deep where needed should be coordinated early with the geotechnical team to ensure that moisture, climate‑related frost action and other environmental factors are addressed in the pavement structure. For full‑depth flexible pavement design (section 9.3) the terminal serviceability index is increased by the loss of serviceability caused by frost heave; this loss is obtained by first selecting a frost‑heave rate from Table 9.1 or Table 9.2 according to subgrade soil type, then determining the depth of frost penetration using the Design Freezing Index and reading the maximum potential serviceability loss on the FAIR drainage quality line, and finally applying an estimated frost‑heave probability that should be chosen in the recommended range of 25%–75%; the resulting value (for example, 3.0 plus the calculated loss) is used as the terminal serviceability for the design. Verification and expected performance

The completed flexible pavement design shall be run in DARWin 3.01 and shall be compared to an AASHTOWare Pavement ME design performed by Central Office, with any recommended changes incorporated before final submission; the design package must include all supporting documentation listed (cover letter, Form D‑4332, typical section drawing, DARWin printouts, traffic and geotechnical data, LCCA, etc.) so that reviewers can verify compliance with minimum thickness tables, constructability details such as edge drains and widening, performance predictions from the life‑cycle cost analysis, and alignment with the project’s scope, schedule and cost limits. The District Executive’s approval of short‑section projects and the required timing—approval within 1 year for moderate complexity or within 2 years for most complex projects after a Design Field View and at least 6 months before the Final Design Office Meeting—provide additional constructability and consistency checks. For a full‑depth flexible pavement the design performance is expressed as a terminal serviceability index that must be met by adding the calculated loss of serviceability due to frost heave to the base value from Table 6.3; for example, an Interstate design might target a terminal index of 3.75 (3.0 + 0.75). The frost‑heave loss is quantified by determining the frost‑heave rate from the appropriate subgrade table, estimating the maximum potential serviceability loss using the depth of frost penetration derived from the Design Freezing Index, and applying a frost‑heave probability (recommended 25%–75%) on Figure G.8 of the 1993 AASHTO Guide; the resulting ∆PSIFH is then added to the base serviceability value to obtain the terminal index.
