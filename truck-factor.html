Truck Factor

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Truck Factor is a topic under Pavement Design.
average-initial-truck-factoraverage-initial-truck-factorsesal-truck-factoresals-truck
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

The Average Initial Truck Factor is a numerical factor that converts anticipated truck traffic into an equivalent number of single‑axle load repetitions (ESALs), enabling engineers to predict cumulative pavement damage and size structures to meet serviceability goals without premature failure. It is entered as a required input to the rigorous 18‑kip ESAL calculation in DARWin, with values taken from Table 7.1 (or Table 7.2 when the Simple Method applies) and applied throughout overlay designs to quantify structural demand. By representing the initial load‑carrying capacity of existing pavements, the factor guides the selection of overlay thicknesses that will accommodate future truck traffic without early distress [1].

Purpose and design objectives

The Average Initial Truck Factor is used to translate the anticipated truck traffic into equivalent single‑axle load repetitions, thereby allowing engineers to predict cumulative damage and size pavement structures so that serviceability goals are met without premature failure. In the traffic analysis for pavement design (section 7.1) it serves as an input to the rigorous 18‑kip ESAL calculation that quantifies how many ESALs each truck class contributes over the performance period. For the design of an asphalt concrete overlay on jointed plain concrete pavement (section 10.4) the factor is employed to quantify the initial load‑carrying capacity of the existing rigid pavement so that the required overlay thickness can be determined to accommodate future truck traffic without premature distress. Likewise, in the AC overlay of an AC/PCC composite pavement (section 10.5) engineers first determine the pavement thickness for future traffic and then compute ESAL values using the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors, which translates anticipated truck loading into equivalent repetitions to prevent premature distress. In the unbonded PCC overlay of an AC pavement (section 10.9) the factor is used to quantify the structural demand imposed by anticipated trucks, enabling calculation of ESALs that drive selection of a PCC overlay thickness capable of providing the required structural strength for future traffic. [1]

[1] — Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157), Ch. 10 (pp. 181–186)

In the unbonded PCC overlay design, the only objective explicitly tied to the Truck Factor is structural: the overlay must be designed to provide structural strength, and the calculation of equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) shall use the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (p. 186)

Scope and applicability

The Average Initial Truck Factor is employed in the ESAL calculation whenever the Rigorous Method is selected, which applies to all functional classifications of highways. For new construction or rehabilitation on collectors and local roads the Simple Method may be used, provided that the layer thicknesses determined with the Simple Method do not exceed the minimum layer thicknesses for that roadway type; in those cases the Average Initial Truck Factor values from Table 7.2 are applied. When a cross‑route interchange project has a projected design‑year ADT greater than 8,000, the Rigorous Method (and thus the truck factor) must be used instead of the Simple Method.

In overlay designs the factor is appropriate for flexible asphalt‑concrete (AC) overlays on existing AC pavements; it is used in the ESAL calculations required by the DARWin overlay design workflow and the values are taken from Table 7.1 as part of the future‑traffic structural number computation. The same factor may be used in the AC Overlay of Fractured PCC Slab method, which can be applied to design an AC overlay for either a cracked‑and‑seated or rubblized jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) or jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP). For rigid pavements the Average Initial Truck Factor should also be used when designing an AC overlay on jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP), jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) or continuously reinforced concrete pavement (CRCP); it should also be used if the existing structure consists of concrete that currently carries an asphalt overlay that will be removed prior to placing a new AC overlay, with the rigid‑pavement values taken from Table 7.1.

The factor shall be utilized by the AC Overlay of AC/PCC (composite) design method when a previously overlaid concrete roadway is to receive an asphalt overlay without removing an existing asphalt overlay; the rigid‑pavement values from Table 7.1 are used to calculate ESALs for the existing concrete structure before determining the required asphalt thickness.

For concrete‑only overlays, the Average Initial Truck Factor is applied when calculating equivalent single axle loads for bonded Portland cement concrete (PCC) overlays over existing concrete slabs that are not severely cracked or faulted; such bonded overlays should only be utilized where the existing concrete pavement does not exhibit a great extent of cracking and/or faulting, and the rigid‑pavement values from Table 7.1 are used in this context. The Department encourages the use of unbonded Portland cement concrete (PCC) overlays where appropriate; the factor is applied for unbonded PCC overlays over existing concrete pavement, including projects involving full‑depth repairs or visibly rocking slabs where the existing concrete can serve as a relatively uniform subbase, with the rigid‑pavement values from Table 7.1 employed. Finally, when an unbonded PCC overlay is placed over an asphalt‑concrete pavement, the design approach calls for using the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 to calculate ESALs for future traffic, making the factor appropriate in this structural overlay application. [1]

[1] — Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157), Ch. 10 (pp. 180–186)

The Simple (short) method is preferred when the project is a collector or local road and the pavement layer thicknesses calculated with the Simple method stay within the minimum thickness limits for that roadway type, or when a rehabilitation’s required structural capacity does not exceed the existing pavement’s capacity; in those cases the Average Initial Truck Factor need not be used. The Simple method may not be applied to any other functional classifications, and if the thickness limits are exceeded for new construction the Rigorous method (which uses the truck factor) must be employed. For cross routes that intersect limited‑access freeways, the minimum daily 18‑kip ESAL values can be used instead of a full truck‑factor calculation provided the design‑year ADT does not exceed eight thousand. When the existing concrete slab shows extensive cracking, faulting, or other significant distress, a bonded PCC overlay that depends on the Average Initial Truck Factor is not advisable; under those conditions another design method such as an unbonded overlay should be considered because substantial distress would transmit through the overlay if left unrepaired. [1]

[1] — Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157), Ch. 10 (p. 183)

Design inputs and requirements

The Average Initial Truck Factor is defined by the ESAL factors given in Table 7.1 (or Table 7.2 when the Simple Method is employed) and must be used with DARWin’s rigorous 18‑kip ESAL calculation that employs the compound growth rate method; the annual percent growth in truck factor shall be set to 0% unless historical evidence or loading studies justify otherwise. The Simple Method may only be applied on collectors and local roads, and for cross routes that have a design‑year ADT greater than 8,000 the rigorous method must be used. When designing an AC overlay of existing asphalt pavement, the flexible‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors listed in Table 7.1 must be used to compute ESALs and are applied throughout the overlay structural number and thickness calculations described in Chapter 10 Section 10.2, with future traffic inputs taken from Table 10.7 and the existing pavement evaluated by Component or Non‑Destructive Testing methods. In an AC overlay over an AC/PCC composite pavement, the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors listed in Table 7.1 are required for all ESAL calculations, thereby governing truck loading in that design. The same requirement to use the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 applies when performing ESAL calculations for bonded PCC overlays of PCC pavement (Section 10.6), unbonded PCC overlays of PCC pavement (Section 10.7), and unbonded PCC overlays of AC pavement (Section 10.9), directly controlling how truck loading is accounted for in each of those designs. [1]

[1] — Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157), Ch. 10 (pp. 180–186)

Design methods and criteria

In the traffic analysis for pavement design (section 7.1) the Truck Factor, expressed as average initial ESALs per truck, is entered into DARWin as part of the rigorous 18‑kip ESAL calculation. The user supplies the ESAL factor from Table 7.1 for each vehicle class and must employ the compound growth‑rate calculation method required by DARWin; when the Simple Method is selected for collectors or local roads the Annual Truck Factor Growth Rate Percentage should be inputted as 0 % and the average initial values are taken from Table 7.2. For a flexible‑pavement AC overlay (section 10.2) the flexible pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 are used to convert traffic into ESALs for the overlay design, and DARWin evaluates the resulting structural requirements by computing an effective existing Structural Number with either the Component Method or the Non‑Destructive Testing Method. In the Component Method the depths of all pavement layers and their corresponding structural coefficients from Table 9.3 are entered so that the structural number equation in Section 9.8 can be applied with drainage coefficients set to 1.0; DARWin also provides secondary dialog boxes for calculating the future‑traffic Structural Number and for performing the thickness design. For a jointed plain concrete‑pavement (JPCP) overlay (section 10.4) the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors listed in Table 7.1 are inserted into the ESAL calculations required for the overlay design, and the resulting ESAL values are used in DARWin’s secondary screen that follows the same workflow as the rigid‑pavement design process to determine the needed AC overlay thickness for future traffic. For an AC/PCC composite pavement overlay (section 10.5) the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 serve as inputs to the ESAL calculations that establish the required overlay thickness; no additional equations, models, or empirical relationships for the Truck Factor are provided beyond this usage. For a bonded PCC overlay on PCC pavement (section 10.6) the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 are used as inputs for ESAL calculations, after which DARWin first calculates the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic and then computes the Effective Existing Thickness via the Condition Survey evaluation method before determining the required bonded PCC overlay thickness. For an unbonded PCC overlay on PCC pavement (section 10.7) the only step that explicitly involves the Truck Factor is to apply the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 as the input for ESAL calculations, which are then used together with the pavement‑thickness tables to establish the required overlay thickness. For an unbonded PCC overlay on AC pavement (section 10.9) the procedure likewise requires taking the rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors listed in Table 7.1 and applying them as inputs when calculating equivalent single axle loads for the future traffic analysis; the required PCC overlay thickness is equal to the calculated Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic. [1]

The Average Initial Truck Factor used in the ESAL calculation must be taken from the ESAL factors listed for each vehicle class (Table 7.1 for the Rigorous method or Table 7.2 when the Simple Method is applied). The annual growth rate for the truck factor shall be entered as 0% unless specific historical evidence or loading studies justify a different value, and this 0% rate is required for both the Rigorous and Simple methods. These inputs are then used in DARWin with the compound growth calculation type. [1]

[1] — Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157)

Structural details and interfaces

In section 10.6, the interface for a bonded PCC overlay is created by directly bonding the new concrete to the existing concrete pavement so that the overlay and the existing concrete act as a single monolithic slab; the design must rely on the condition of the underlying pavement and should only be utilized where the existing concrete does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting, and the design also incorporates rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 for the ESAL calculations that drive thickness selection. In section 10.7, the separation of the layers of concrete can be accomplished with a geotextile interlayer or asphalt concrete; when specifying a geotextile interlayer, mill concrete joints with greater than 1/4‑inch faulting, and if joint faulting exceeds 1/2 inch, place a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix at the joint or mill/grind the joints to reduce faulting, ensure proper drainage of the interlayer by daylighting the interlayer or installing a vertical subdrain, and the interlayer must extend through the shoulder when shoulders are constructed or reconstructed and tied to the mainline. [1]
