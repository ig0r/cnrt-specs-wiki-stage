Air Entrainment

Part of: Mix Design is-subtopic-of
Air Entrainment is a topic under Mix Design.
air-entrainment
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Air entrainment is the intentional introduction of microscopic air bubbles into fresh pavement concrete by adding an air‑entraining admixture—or, where appropriate, a lightweight aggregate—to achieve a prescribed plastic‑state air content [1]. For conventional cement concrete the target is about six percent air, whereas mixes used for AAAP, slip‑form and form paving are designed for roughly seven percent, with an allowable deviation of ±1.5 % (broader bands are permitted for self‑consolidating mixes) [1]. The resulting air‑void system is retained through placement and curing so that the hardened concrete contains a total entrapped‑air volume typically between 3.5 % and 7.5 % (up to 8.5 % for special mixes) and exhibits a spacing factor near 0.008 in and a specific surface of about 630 in²/in³, characteristics that provide the freeze–thaw durability required of pavement structures while still meeting strength and density requirements [1].

Material purpose and applicability

Air entrainment in pavement concrete is the intentional introduction of microscopic air bubbles into the fresh mix by means of an air‑entraining admixture, or alternatively a suitable lightweight aggregate, to achieve a prescribed level of air content. For conventional cement concrete the target plastic‑state air content is 6 %, while mixes used for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and AA Form Paving are designed for 7 % air in the fresh state. The admixture is added according to the procedure specified in Section 704.1(e) and the resulting air voids—both entrapped and deliberately entrained—are retained through placement and curing so that the hardened concrete exhibits a total air content within defined limits, typically 3.5 % to 7.5 % for regular mixes and 4.5 % to 8.5 % for the special mixes. This controlled air‑void system provides the durability required of pavement structures, particularly by improving resistance to freeze–thaw cycling, while still allowing the mix to meet the specified strength and density requirements; it is noted that the use of the entraining agent may modestly reduce some performance values. [1]

[1] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 181–182), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–652)

Composition and characteristics

Air entrainment is produced by adding a solution of an air‑entraining admixture to the concrete batch, which creates a controlled population of microscopic air voids. In the plastic state the mix is targeted to contain about six percent air for ordinary cement concrete and roughly seven percent for AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving and related pavement concretes; self‑consolidating mixes may be allowed a broader band of seven percent plus or minus two percent, while quality‑control specifications often require seven percent ± 1.5 %. After hardening the total entrapped air must remain within defined limits—approximately 3.5 to 7.5 % for standard concrete and 4.5 to 8.5 % for the pavement mixes. The resulting air‑void system is characterized by a spacing factor near 0.008 in and a specific surface of about 630 in² per cubic inch, values that provide the freeze‑thaw durability required in pavements. Compliance is verified by testing each truckload for plastic air content together with slump and temperature; any batch falling outside the prescribed range is rejected. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 436–437), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–693)

Air entrainment in pavement concrete is highly sensitive to any alteration in the mix composition, the origin of its constituents, or the way it is processed. The primary lever for achieving the desired level of entrained air is the dosage of an air‑entraining admixture; this dosage is set so that the plastic‑state air content reaches a target of 6 % for ordinary cement concrete and 7 % for AAAP and related pavements, with an allowable deviation of plus or minus 1.5 %. Because the amount of entrained air in the hardened slab reflects the mix composition, any change in aggregate proportions that also modifies the quantity of cementitious material or water necessitates a new mix design and can shift the plastic‑air level away from its target. Likewise, variations in source aggregates or processing conditions manifest as deviations in measured entrained‑air content; the specification requires the hardened concrete to retain an overall air content roughly between 3.5 % and 8.5 %, with a more restrictive band of 3.5 % to 7.5 % cited for certain mixes. When measurements fall outside these limits, a written evaluation and corrective action are mandated, and paving may be halted until an acceptable solution is approved. To guard against inadvertent degradation of durability, the quality‑control plan obliges testing each truck for plastic air, rejecting any batch whose measured value does not meet the 7 % ±1.5 % criterion, and adjusting aggregate gradations as needed to stay within prescribed limits. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 436–439), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–652)

Key material properties

Air entrainment performance depends on an integrated set of physical, mechanical, chemical, and thermal characteristics. The foremost physical attribute is the volume of microscopic air bubbles introduced into the fresh mix, known as plastic air content. Standard pavement mixes target a plastic air content of six percent, while AAAP, slip‑form, and form paving mixes aim for seven percent; these values are verified with AASHTO T152/T196 meters. After curing, the hardened concrete must retain an entrapped‑air volume generally between three point five and seven point five percent, extending to up to eight point five percent for the special mixes, and a typical overall air content of about seven percent with a tolerance of plus or minus two percent. The geometry of the void system is defined by a spacing factor near 0.008 in and a specific surface of roughly 630 in²/in³, both essential to resist freeze‑thaw damage. Density testing according to AASHTO T121 provides an additional physical measure of the void structure.

Mechanically, the mix must achieve sufficient compressive strength despite the incorporated air. A 28‑day compressive strength test on cylinders, performed in accordance with PTM No. 604 or equivalent procedures, is required; the average result must meet a minimum of about three thousand pounds per square inch and satisfy a quality index of at least fifty‑five percent for Class AA concrete. Both the compressive strength and the plastic‑air measurement are taken from each sublot to confirm uniformity.

Chemically, the water‑cement ratio is controlled between 0.37 and 0.42, a range that promotes both adequate strength development and stable air retention. When the air‑entrained concrete will be in contact with metal reinforcement or other aggressive agents, additional durability checks such as resistivity, pH, and chloride content are performed to ensure corrosion resistance.

Thermally, the temperature of the fresh mix at placement and during early curing is closely monitored because temperatures above roughly eighty degrees Fahrenheit can destabilize the air bubbles. Calibrated thermometers and ASTM C1064 temperature testing are employed, and a concrete‑temperature control plan is required to keep the mix within acceptable limits.

All of these parameters—air content, spacing factor, specific surface, density, compressive strength, water‑cement ratio, chemical durability indices, and placement temperature—must be measured and maintained within their prescribed thresholds for air‑entrained concrete to deliver the intended freeze‑thaw durability in pavement applications. [1]

[1] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 181–182), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 380–441), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–693)

The air‑entrainment quality of a concrete pavement is established by first measuring the percentage of entrained air in the fresh mix. Calibrated air meters that meet AASHTO T 152 for stone‑gravel aggregates or AASHTO T 196 for slag and lightweight coarse aggregate are used on samples taken from each sublot before placement; the devices must be calibrated before use and periodically recalibrated, with testing performed at least once per 50 cubic yards or on the first truck of every lot. The measured plastic‑air content is compared to mix‑specific targets—generally 6 % for standard cement mixes and 7 % for AAAP, slip‑form, and other air‑entrained pavements—with an allowable deviation of ±1.5 % (±2 % for self‑consolidating mixes). Acceptance requires the averaged result to fall within the Class AA range of 5.0 % to 8.5 % when the percent weight of limestone in the aggregate is 55 % or greater; any value outside this tolerance triggers additional testing, corrective action, or rejection of the truck until three consecutive passes are achieved.

When hardened concrete must be evaluated, the air‑void system is characterized according to ASTM C457. This analysis provides a total air content that must lie within defined limits—3.5–7.5 % for standard mixes and 4.5–8.5 % for air‑entrained mixes—and yields an air‑spacing factor not exceeding approximately 0.008 in together with a specific surface of at least about 630 in²/in³. These parameters, combined with freeze‑thaw durability testing per ASTM C666 (requiring a durability factor of 80 or greater), are used to classify the entrained‑air quality needed for resistance to freezing cycles.

In addition to on‑site plastic‑air measurements, representative core samples are submitted for laboratory verification. One out of every ten cores is sent as an information specimen to Laboratory Testing Services, where the air content is measured in accordance with PTM No. 623. The laboratory results, together with recorded density and strength data from the mix design, complete the characterization of the entrained‑air system for the pavement. [1]

[1] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 181–182), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 379–441), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–693)

Durability and deterioration

Air‑entrainment is principally employed to protect concrete pavements from freeze‑thaw damage, a key deterioration mechanism that can cause cracking and loss of structural integrity when water within the matrix expands upon freezing. To achieve the required resistance, the mix must attain a durability factor of at least 80 as defined by ASTM C666, which serves as the benchmark for acceptable performance under repeated freezing and thawing cycles. In the fresh state, self‑consolidating mixes are required to contain roughly seven percent air, with an allowable variation of plus or minus two percent, ensuring that enough microscopic voids are present to accommodate ice formation without generating excessive internal stresses. Once the pavement has hardened, the entrapped air must be maintained within a tighter band of approximately three and a half percent to eight and a half percent; values outside this interval signal a heightened risk of reduced freeze‑thaw resistance and other durability losses. Should measurements fall below or exceed these limits, a written evaluation outlining the problem and a proposed corrective action must be submitted within five days. If no remedial steps are taken promptly, authorities may suspend paving operations until an acceptable solution is agreed upon, underscoring the critical role of precise air‑content control in preserving long‑term pavement durability. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–439), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (p. 693)

Material interactions and compatibility

The specification permits air‑entraining admixtures only from approved manufacturers and controls chemical compatibility by limiting sulfate content to less than 1000 ppm and sulfide content to less than 200 ppm in the cured backfill. It also requires corrosion‑resistance testing for mixes that contact exposed metal, including a minimum resistivity of 2000 ohm‑centimeters and a pH range of 5.0 to 9.5. No additional compatibility issues such as organics, clays, reactive aggregates, other contaminants, dissimilar materials, or shrinkage restraint are addressed in the document. [1]

[1] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 181–182)

Influence of production and placement

Achieving and preserving the intended air‑void system in pavement concrete begins with disciplined mixing practices. The project must start each batch using the mixer type and procedure specified for the job, and the dosage of air‑entraining admixture is calibrated so that the plastic‑state air content reaches the design target—6 % for conventional cement concrete or 7 % for AAAP, slip‑form, and form paving mixes—and remains within a ±1.5 % tolerance. Any batch whose measured plastic air falls outside these limits is rejected before it can be placed, ensuring that the protective bubbles required for freeze‑thaw durability are present from the outset.

During placement, each truckload is sampled for its plastic air content; loads that do not meet the 7 % ±1.5 % criterion are excluded from the pour and must be retested until three consecutive passes are recorded. A concrete temperature control plan further safeguards the entrained air by limiting the concrete temperature to below 80 °F at the point of placement, preventing premature loss or coalescence of bubbles. Uniform alignment checks during discharge help maintain consistent distribution of the air‑void system throughout the pavement.

Finishing operations must be carried out without adding water or other finishing aids to the surface, because excess moisture can collapse the delicate air voids and diminish both strength and durability. Compaction is not addressed in these sections, indicating that no specific compaction requirements are prescribed for preserving entrained air beyond standard practices.

Curing exerts a decisive influence on the long‑term performance of the air‑entrained concrete. Proper curing methods—particularly those that protect joints from moisture loss during and after joint sawing—are mandated to prevent surface cracking, honeycombing, or other defects that would erode the protective effect of the entrained air. Trial specimens are also cured under controlled conditions; when deficiencies are suspected, the hardened concrete must be evaluated for spacing factor and specific surface in accordance with ASTM C457 to verify that the air‑void system remains within acceptable limits. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 436–441), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–652)

The durability that air entrainment provides to pavement concrete is most strongly affected by any construction‑related deviation that moves the actual air content or void structure away from the design targets. The mix must be placed with a plastic‑state air content of roughly seven percent for AAAP mixes (six percent for standard cement concrete) and this value must be kept within about plus or minus one and a half to two percentage points; any lot that falls outside that band is rejected on site, typically by sampling the first truck of each lot. Even when the plastic‑state target is met, the hardened concrete must retain a total air content between three and a half percent and seven and a half percent for standard mixes, or four and a half to eight and a half percent for AAAP mixes, and it must develop an air‑void system whose spacing factor is near 0.008 in and whose specific surface approaches 630 in²/in³. Failure to achieve these limits creates larger voids that accelerate freeze‑thaw damage.

Equally critical are uniformity requirements for strength and thickness. Each sublot must produce compressive‑strength specimens that, together with the air‑content results, give a percentage‑within‑limits (PWL) of at least fifty‑five percent; any lower PWL signals a defect that can erode long‑term performance. Core depths must not differ from the design thickness by more than half an inch on average, and no individual sublot may be thinner than that tolerance. Surface imperfections—rain damage, improper joint work, inadequate curing, poor final finish, cracking, honeycombing, depressions, high points, or edge slumping—also compromise the retained air‑void system and are treated as defects unless satisfactorily repaired.

Finally, the concrete must pass a freeze‑thaw durability test with a minimum factor of eighty, and routine slump, air, and temperature testing must be performed at the frequencies prescribed in the specifications. Together, these construction variations constitute the primary threats to the long‑term effectiveness of air entrainment. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 380–441), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–693)

Selection, specification, and improvement

Selection of an air‑entraining system is driven first by the target percentage of entrapped air that must be achieved in the fresh mix. For ordinary Portland cement concrete the specification calls for a plastic‑state air content of about six percent, whereas mixes designated AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving or AA Form Paving require roughly seven percent and Class AA concrete is limited to a range of approximately five to eight point five percent. The allowable deviation from the target is generally plus or minus one point five percent (or up to plus or minus two percent in some specifications), and the mix must be adjusted with an admixture dosage determined according to AASHTO T152 for stone‑gravel aggregates or T196 for slag or lightweight coarse aggregate, without applying any aggregate correction factor. After curing, the total air retained in the hardened concrete is required to lie between three point five percent and seven point five percent for standard mixes and between four point five percent and eight point five percent for AAAP‑type mixes; a department requirement for rigid pavement expands this overall envelope to three point five percent–eight point five percent. When durability is a concern, the hardened air‑void system must also satisfy a spacing factor near 0.008 in and a specific surface of about 630 in²/in³, and the concrete must achieve a minimum freeze‑thaw durability factor of eighty as measured by ASTM C666.

In addition to the air‑content limits, the mix must meet other performance criteria that influence admixture selection. The plastic‑water‑loss of the fresh concrete must be at least fifty‑five percent, and the twenty‑eight day compressive strength must satisfy the project’s strength requirement. For applications such as flowable backfill, the air‑entraining admixture itself must be sourced from a manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15 and explicitly approved for that use; its inclusion is reflected in the mix design and any change triggers a revised design because the agent can affect achievable density values.

The specification is enforced through a quality‑control plan that requires sampling of each truck or sublot before placement. The first sample must meet the prescribed plastic air content, and any lot falling outside the limit is rejected; subsequent trucks are retested until three consecutive samples satisfy the requirement. Failure to achieve compliance within five days obliges the contractor to submit a written evaluation and corrective proposal, and continued non‑conformance may result in a stop‑work order. [1]

[1] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 181–182), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 380–441), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–693)

Improving the performance of air‑entrained concrete for pavement hinges on a tightly coordinated mix design, judicious use of admixtures and supplementary cementitious materials, and an extensive quality‑control program that monitors both fresh and hardened properties. The mix must contain a correctly dosed air‑entraining admixture; the required dosage is established by AASHTO T 152 or T 196 testing on plastic concrete and must be maintained within ±1.5 % of the target air content, typically 7 % ± 2 %. For flowable backfill the admixture should be sourced from a manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15 and used with an appropriate lightweight aggregate. Adjustments to fly ash or slag levels—reducing or increasing them when Type IP or Type IS cement is employed—help preserve the intended density, strength, and air‑void system. Water‑reducing admixtures, including high‑range superplasticizers, permit higher slump values (up to 5 inches without reducers, 6½ inches with conventional reducers, and 8 inches with superplasticizers) while keeping the plastic‑state air content within the specified range. A modest addition of mineral filler in place of fine aggregate can further stabilise entrained air.

The specification mandates a detailed quality‑control plan that defines testing frequencies, corrective actions, and backup equipment. All air meters conforming to AASHTO T 196/T 152 must be calibrated no more than two weeks before placement and recalibrated in the presence of the inspector every two weeks; any failure to meet limits triggers increased sampling, including testing each truck until three consecutive passes are recorded. Each sublot is required to provide a compressive‑strength specimen and a plastic‑air test, with acceptance based on at least 55 % of results falling within prescribed limits and statistical quality indices derived from mean and standard deviation. The first truck of every lot must meet the target air content (7 % ± 1.5 %); trucks outside this band are rejected and subsequent loads retested. Concrete temperature is controlled so that placement temperatures do not exceed 80 °F, and daily aggregate‑gradation checks allow proportion adjustments to maintain consistency.

Hardened‑concrete air‑void parameters are verified by ASTM C457, requiring a spacing factor near 0.008 in and a specific surface of roughly 630 in²/in³. Freeze‑thaw durability is confirmed with ASTM C666 (Procedure A or B) demanding a minimum durability factor of 80. If hardened‑air measurements fall outside the acceptable range of 3.5 % to 8.5 %, a written evaluation and corrective proposal must be submitted within five days, and paving may be halted until an approved solution is implemented. In addition, one out of every ten pavement cores must be submitted to Laboratory Testing Services for an entrained‑air evaluation in accordance with PTM No. 623; any core whose 28‑day compressive strength is measured below 3,500 psi triggers an immediate investigation of the concrete production process to identify and correct the cause of low strength. [1]

[1] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 181–182), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 379–441), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–693)

Performance evaluation and implications

Air‑entrainment performance is verified through a coordinated program of field measurements, laboratory analyses, and long‑term durability testing. In the field, calibrated air meters that conform to AASHTO T 196 or T 152 are used to determine plastic‑air content on the first three trucks of each lot and thereafter at least once for every 50 cubic yards placed; the first truck of each lot and any sublot cores are also sampled. The target fresh‑air value is generally about 7 % with an allowable tolerance of ±1.5 % (±2 % for self‑consolidating mixes), and stone or gravel aggregates must achieve a performance quality level of at least 55 %. When measured values fall outside these limits the truck or sublot is rejected and corrective action is required.

Core specimens extracted from each sublot are dispatched to Laboratory Technical Services, where hardened‑air content is determined according to PTM No. 623 (or ASTM C457 for a full air‑void analysis). Acceptance criteria for the hardened air void system typically require a total air content between 3.5 % and 7.5 %, with some provisions permitting up to 8.5 %. The same cores are used to produce representative cylinders that are tested for compressive strength at ages of 3, 7, 14, 28 (and, when specified, 56) days in accordance with PTM No. 604/606; the 28‑day strength results provide additional evidence that the entrained air does not compromise structural performance. Mix‑design submissions must include measured air content together with density and strength data.

Long‑term durability is demonstrated by subjecting hardened concrete to freeze‑thaw cycling in accordance with ASTM C666 (Procedure A or B) and requiring a durability factor of at least 80, thereby confirming that the retained air void system provides resistance to repeated freezing and thawing. This combination of on‑site fresh‑concrete testing, core‑based laboratory evaluation of hardened‑air content and compressive strength, and accelerated freeze‑thaw performance constitutes the complete suite of evidence used to evaluate whether concrete meets the required level of air entrainment for pavement applications. [1]

[1] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 181–182), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 379–441), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–693)

Design specifications for pavement concrete require a target air content in the fresh mix—typically six percent for standard cement concrete and seven percent for AAAP, slip‑form, and form paving mixes—with an allowable tolerance of plus or minus one point five percent. During construction the plastic‑state air content is measured, and after curing the hardened material must retain a total air content within defined limits: three point five to seven point five percent for most mixes and four point five to eight point five percent for AAAP‑type pavements. In addition, the air‑void system must meet a maximum spacing factor of about 0.008 in and a minimum specific surface of roughly 630 in²/in³. These criteria ensure that sufficient microscopic bubbles are present to relieve freeze‑thaw stresses, thereby enhancing durability and reducing long‑term maintenance requirements.

Acceptance testing is performed on each sublot; the percentage of air content must meet the specification with a percent‑within‑limits (PWL) value of at least fifty‑five percent. The Representative evaluates this alongside twenty‑eight day compressive strength on a lot‑by‑lot basis, and any batch whose plastic‑air measurement falls outside the allowable range is rejected and not placed. This testing regime embeds air‑entrainment performance into design verification, construction quality control, and subsequent maintenance planning.

For self‑consolidating concrete mixes the specification calls for a plastic air content around seven percent plus or minus two percent, which creates uniformly distributed microscopic bubbles that act as pressure‑relief sites during freezing. Meeting this target is directly linked to achieving a minimum durability factor of eighty in ASTM C666 freeze‑thaw testing, confirming resistance to cracking and surface scaling under cyclic temperature changes.

Air‑entraining admixtures are mandated for flowable backfill mixes; their inclusion can lower density and strength values, so designers must adjust mix proportions or select compatible lightweight aggregates to satisfy required structural performance. Consequently, the mix design process must explicitly account for air entrainment to ensure that the final pavement material meets both durability and load‑bearing criteria.

If core testing of hardened concrete shows an entrapped‑air content below three point five percent or above eight point five percent, a written evaluation and corrective plan must be submitted within five days. Failure to take corrective action can result in a stop order on paving operations until an acceptable solution is agreed upon. This requirement ties the material’s air‑entrainment properties directly to construction scheduling, acceptance decisions, and long‑term maintenance strategies, underscoring the critical role of controlled air content in achieving durable, high‑performing pavements.
