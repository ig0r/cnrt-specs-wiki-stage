Air Entrainment

Part of: Mix Design is-subtopic-of
Air Entrainment is a topic under Mix Design.
air-entrainment
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-30

Air entrainment is the intentional incorporation of microscopic air bubbles into rigid‑pavement concrete by adding a specially formulated air‑entraining admixture to the mix, which creates a controlled volume of “plastic” air in the fresh state—typically about six percent for ordinary cement concrete and roughly seven percent for AAAP and related pavement mixes[1]. These entrained voids are required to persist after hardening within prescribed limits (approximately 3.5–7.5 % total air for standard mixes and up to 8.5 % for special blends) and to exhibit an air‑void geometry characterized by a spacing factor near 0.008 in and a specific surface around 630 in² per cubic inch, thereby providing space for water expansion during freezing and protecting the cement matrix from freeze–thaw damage[1]. Maintaining the plastic‑state air content within a tight tolerance (±1.5 to ±2 %) through calibrated admixture dosing and rigorous quality‑control sampling ensures that the microscopic bubble network functions as a durability measure, triggering corrective action if the measured content falls outside the acceptable range[1].

PennDOT publications covering “Air Entrainment” also include the following subtopics: Air Content.

Air Entrainment relies on Air Content as the measurable proportion of voids that must be maintained within specified tolerances during fresh concrete testing. [1]

Air Content

Material purpose and applicability

Air entrainment is the intentional incorporation of microscopic air bubbles into rigid‑pavement concrete by means of an air‑entraining admixture added to the mix. In the plastic state the admixture creates a controlled volume of air, typically about six percent for ordinary cement concrete and roughly seven percent for AAAP and related pavement mixes. After hardening the entrained voids are required to remain within prescribed limits—generally three point five to seven point five percent for standard mixes and four point five to eight point five percent for AAAP, slip‑form paving, and form paving blends, with an overall acceptable range of about three point five to eight point five percent. Maintaining the air content in this band functions as a durability measure; the dispersed voids provide space for water to expand during freezing, thereby protecting the cement matrix from freeze‑thaw damage and serving as a performance check that triggers corrective action if the measured content falls outside the limits. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 388–389), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–666)

Composition and characteristics

Air‑entrained concrete is defined by the intentional incorporation of an air‑entraining admixture into a conventional cementitious mix composed of Portland cement, fine and coarse aggregates, and water. The admixture creates microscopic bubbles—referred to as plastic air—in the fresh mixture, raising the measured air content in the plastic state to about six percent for ordinary cement concrete and roughly seven percent for AAAP and related paving mixes. In self‑consolidating or flowable backfill formulations the target is commonly set at seven percent with an allowable deviation of plus or minus two percent, while quality‑control procedures often require a tighter tolerance of ±1.5 percent on the first truck sampled from each lot. After hardening these entrained voids must develop an air‑void system characterized by a spacing factor near 0.008 in and a specific surface close to 630 in² per cubic inch, ensuring that the hardened‑state air content remains within the prescribed range for durability. The defining constituents therefore include the cementitious binder (Portland cement alone or combined with supplementary cementitious material), the aggregates, water, and the air‑entraining agents; optional components such as bottom ash or other chemical admixtures may be present but do not alter the essential characteristic of a controlled microscopic air network that imparts freeze‑thaw resistance. [1]

[1] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (p. 186), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 448–449), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 661–707)

Air‑entrainment performance is highly sensitive to the exact makeup of the concrete mix, the origin of its constituents, the mixing method, and the proportioning of each ingredient. For ordinary cement concrete the specification calls for a plastic‑state air content of 6 %, whereas mixes used for AAAP, slip‑form paving and fixed‑form paving require about 7 % air in the fresh state. This target is obtained by dosing an air‑entraining admixture solution; the required dosage is calculated according to AASHTO T 152 for stone and gravel aggregates or AASHTO T 196 for slag or lightweight coarse aggregate, with the explicit instruction not to apply any aggregate correction factor. The resulting plastic‑state air includes both entrapped and true entrained bubbles, and the hardened concrete must ultimately contain between 3.5 % and 7.5 % total air.

Because the balance between entrapped and entrained voids depends on cement type, supplementary cementitious materials, and aggregate source, any change in these components can shift the spacing factor and specific surface of the air‑void system. The specification limits the maximum spacing factor to roughly 0.008 in and requires a minimum specific surface of about 630 in²/in³; therefore adjustments in composition must be evaluated through trial batches to ensure that these void parameters remain within range.

During production, variations such as altering aggregate proportions, modifying cementitious material or water content, or switching between plant‑mixed and truck‑mixed batches directly affect the plastic air level. The quality‑control plan mandates sampling the first truck of each lot and accepting it only if its measured plastic air falls within 7 % ± 1.5 %. Trucks that fail this criterion are rejected until three consecutive passes are recorded. When aggregate proportions are changed to satisfy gradation limits (as referenced in PTM No. 529), any accompanying changes in cement or water require a new mix design and additional trial concrete batches.

In summary, every variation in composition, source material, processing method, or proportioning must be examined through controlled testing and, when necessary, corrected by adjusting admixture dosage or redesigning the mix to preserve the prescribed air‑void characteristics that provide durability under freeze–thaw conditions. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 448–449), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–666)

Key material properties

The effectiveness of air‑entrainment in pavement concrete is governed by a tightly defined suite of physical, mechanical, chemical and thermal attributes. Physically, the fresh mix must contain a prescribed volume of microscopic air bubbles—commonly six percent for ordinary cement concrete and seven percent for AAAP, slip‑form and fixed‑form pavements—with an allowable deviation that ranges from plus or minus one point five to two percent depending on the specification. After hardening, the total entrained‑air content must remain within narrow limits: three and a half to seven and a half percent for standard mixes and four and a half to eight and a half percent for the special pavement blends. In addition, the hardened air‑void system must satisfy an average spacing factor of roughly 0.008 in and a specific surface area near 630 in² per cubic inch, ensuring that the bubbles are sufficiently fine and evenly distributed to protect against freeze–thaw damage.

Mechanically, the concrete must still meet all conventional strength and workability criteria. The mix design is required to demonstrate appropriate density and compressive‑strength values, typically verified by slump, air and temperature tests on the fresh material and by confirming that the 28‑day compressive strength meets the project minimums. Freeze‑thaw durability is quantified by an ASTM C666 durability factor, which must reach at least eighty for the mix to be considered acceptable.

Chemically, the cured concrete should exhibit high electrical resistivity—greater than two thousand ohm‑centimeters after a sixty‑day cure—to limit corrosion currents, and its pH must stay within a neutral envelope of roughly five to nine point five. Chloride, sulfate and sulfide concentrations are required to remain below the limits set for the pavement system, and an air‑entraining admixture approved by a listed manufacturer (as identified in Bulletin 15) is mandated to achieve these chemical targets reliably.

Thermally, placement temperature control is essential because temperatures above eighty degrees Fahrenheit can cause premature loss of entrained air. Consequently, a concrete‑temperature‑control plan must be prepared and implemented whenever the mix temperature approaches this threshold, ensuring that the air‑void structure is preserved during curing. [1]

[1] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 186–187), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 388–449), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–707)

The air‑entrainment characteristics of a concrete mix are first quantified while the material is still plastic. For conventional cement concrete the target total air content in the fresh state is 6 % and for mixes such as AAAP or self‑consolidating concrete the target is about 7 %, with an allowable deviation of ±1.5 % (or ±2 % for certain self‑consolidating specifications). The required dosage of air‑entraining admixture is determined according to AASHTO T152 when stone or gravel aggregates are used and according to AASHTO T196 for slag or lightweight coarse aggregates, and the specification explicitly forbids applying any aggregate correction factor. Routine plastic‑mix testing—slump, air, and temperature measurements performed at the frequencies prescribed in Section 704 and Publication 145—provides the measured fresh‑air percentage, which must fall within the specified band before the mix is accepted. In practice the first truck of each lot is sampled; if its plastic air content does not meet the 7 % ± 1.5 % criterion the truck is rejected and subsequent trucks are retested until three consecutive passes are recorded. When hardened concrete is later examined, the air‑void system is characterized in accordance with ASTM C457. This evaluation determines the spacing factor, which should be approximately 0.008 in, and the specific surface area, targeted near 630 in²/in³, while confirming that the total hardened‑state air content lies within the defined limits—generally 3.5–7.5 % for standard mixes and 4.5–8.5 % for AAAP blends. In addition to the bulk‑mix measurements, cores are extracted from each sublot and sent to Laboratory Testing Services for an entrained‑air analysis under PTM No. 623. The resulting air content, expressed as a percent of concrete volume, must be between roughly 3.5 % and 8.5 %; any value outside this interval triggers a written evaluation of the problem and a corrective‑action plan to be submitted within five days. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 388–452), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–707)

Behavior and mechanisms

The behavior of air entrainment in pavement concrete is governed chemically by the dosage of an air‑entraining admixture added to the mix, physically by the presence of both entrapped and deliberately entrained air bubbles within the plastic concrete, and mechanically by how the concrete is mixed—using the specified mixer type and mixing procedure—which influences the distribution and stability of those air voids. [1]

[1] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–666)

Durability and deterioration

Air entrainment must be precisely controlled because the durability of rigid pavement concrete hinges on achieving both a target air content and an appropriate air‑void geometry. When the total air retained in hardened concrete falls outside the prescribed limits—generally 3.5 % to 7.5 % for most mixes and up to 8.5 % for AAAP pavings—the material becomes susceptible to freeze–thaw damage, prompting a written assessment and corrective plan. An insufficient or excessive air volume compromises the protective void system; similarly, an improper void structure characterized by a spacing factor larger than about 0.008 in or a specific surface lower than roughly 630 in²/in³ reduces resistance to cyclic freezing and thawing. In self‑consolidating concrete, the plastic‑state air content is targeted at approximately seven percent with a tolerance of ±2 %; deviation beyond this range also undermines freeze–thaw performance. The resistance to repeated freezing and thawing is quantified by a durability factor obtained from ASTM C666 procedures, which must reach at least eighty; values below this threshold indicate heightened vulnerability to deterioration. Moreover, low chloride ion penetration (rated Low, Very Low, or Negligible after 56 days per AASHTO T 277/T 358) and limited shrinkage are required to prevent permeability‑driven damage that could erode the benefits of entrained air. Continuous monitoring through core sampling and evaluation of entrained‑air content ensures compliance; any measurement below 3.5 % or above 8.5 % triggers immediate reporting and remedial action, reflecting the critical link between air‑entrainment quality and long‑term pavement durability. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 388–389), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–707)

Material interactions and compatibility

Air entrainment is introduced by dosing an air‑entraining admixture until the mix reaches the prescribed plastic‑state air content (6 % for ordinary cement concrete, 7 % for AAAP and related pavements). The amount of admixture required is determined from test methods that differ for stone or gravel aggregates versus slag or lightweight coarse aggregates, showing a direct interaction between the entrained‑air system and the type and absorption characteristics of the aggregate. Maintaining this air content creates a stable void structure whose spacing factor and specific surface must fall within defined limits in the hardened concrete, thereby enhancing durability without altering the cement factor or other binder proportions. [1]

[1] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–666)

The specification for flowable backfill containing air‑entraining admixtures includes explicit limits to avoid compatibility problems. Sulfate content in the cured material must stay below one thousand ppm, and corrosion resistance is addressed by requiring a resistivity greater than two thousand ohm‑centimeters, a pH between 5.0 and 9.5, and chloride levels under two hundred ppm; sulfide limits are also set at two hundred ppm. No other compatibility issues such as organics, clays, reactive aggregates, or shrinkage restraint are mentioned in the source. [1]

[1] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 186–187)

Influence of production and placement

Effective control of the air‑entrainment system begins with disciplined mixing practices. Trial batches are prepared for each concrete class, and a full‑size batch is mixed using the intended equipment to verify that the dosage of the air‑entraining admixture yields the target plastic‑state air content—typically six percent, rising to seven percent for AAAP pavements—and remains within a plus‑or‑minus 1.5 percent tolerance. If these targets are not met, the mixing procedure must be adjusted before production proceeds. During construction the first truck of each lot is sampled for its plastic air content; any batch that falls outside the seven‑percent target with the same ±1.5 percent allowance is rejected, reinforcing the mix‑air control established during batching.

Placement and compaction further influence the balance between entrained and inadvertently trapped air. The hardened concrete must achieve a total‑air content that lies within the specified limits—3.5 to 7.5 percent for most mixes and 4.5 to 8.5 percent for AAAP pavements—and any deviation prompts additional air‑void system testing. Proper compaction helps keep entrapped air low, preserving the designed void structure essential for freeze‑thaw durability. Temperature control at placement is also mandated; if concrete temperature exceeds eighty degrees Fahrenheit, corrective actions are required to protect the air‑void integrity.

Finishing operations must be carried out so that the air‑void system remains stable after the surface is formed. Although specific finishing techniques are not detailed, protecting joints during and after sawing is emphasized as a critical step in maintaining the intended performance of the air‑entrained pavement.

Curing practices complete the sequence by safeguarding the durability benefits conferred by the entrained air. A prescribed curing method that shields joints from moisture loss is required to preserve the stability of the void system. Moreover, the concrete must develop strength at a rate that yields a 28‑day to 7‑day compressive‑strength ratio of at least 1.20, while shrinkage and permeability controls are applied to further ensure long‑term performance under freeze‑thaw conditions. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 448–449), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–666)

The long‑term durability of air‑entrained concrete is governed primarily by how closely the construction process adheres to the prescribed air‑content limits and the quality of the resulting void system. When the fresh mix does not achieve the target plastic‑air level—6 % for standard cement concrete or 7 % for AAAP, slip‑form and fixed‑form paving, with an acceptable tolerance of roughly plus or minus one and a half percent—the protective bubble network is inadequately formed. Likewise, if hardened concrete contains total air outside the allowed range (3.5 % to 7.5 % for ordinary mixes, 4.5 % to 8.5 % for the special mixes) or exhibits an oversized spacing factor above about 0.008 in. or a specific surface below roughly 630 in²/in³, the air‑void structure is compromised and resistance to freeze‑thaw cycles declines.

A second source of defect stems from how the air‑entraining admixture is measured and introduced during batching. Dosing errors that exceed the ±3 % calibration tolerance, loss of interlock control on the measuring device, or a dispensing system that cannot detect flow or keep the solution properly agitated produce inconsistent concentrations throughout the batch. Using separate admixture solutions without proper sequencing, or allowing the bulk supply tank to run empty during a day’s operations, also creates dosage fluctuations that erode durability.

Finally, failure to meet the minimum freeze‑thaw durability factor of 80 (ASTM C666 Procedure A or B) indicates that the entrained‑air system is not performing as intended. Immediate core testing and a written corrective action within five days are required when measured air content falls below 3.5 % or rises above 8.5 %, ensuring that any deviation is addressed before further deterioration occurs. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 388–449), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–707)

Selection, specification, and improvement

The selection and specification of an air‑entraining admixture for pavement concrete are driven by a series of quantitative criteria that begin with the desired air content in the fresh mix. Standard cement concrete is required to achieve roughly six percent plastic‑state air, whereas mixes designated for AAAP, slip‑form or fixed‑form paving must target about seven percent. For self‑consolidating pavement concrete the target is also around seven percent but may be allowed a broader tolerance of plus or minus two percent. In most quality‑control plans the acceptable fresh‑mix range is tightened to seven percent with a tolerance of plus or minus one and a half percent, and any batch that falls outside this band must be rejected and retested until three consecutive passes are recorded.

The amount of admixture added is determined by the procedures in AASHTO T152 for stone and gravel aggregates and AASHTO T196 for other aggregates, with the dosage required to maintain the specified plastic‑state air content within a tolerance of plus or minus 1.5 percent during placement. Once the concrete has hardened, the total entrapped‑air must lie within prescribed limits: generally between three point five and seven point five percent, while pavement mixes may be allowed up to eight point five percent. An overall acceptable hardened‑air range is therefore roughly three point five to eight point five percent; values outside this interval trigger a mandatory written evaluation of the cause and a corrective plan submitted within five days, and failure to act can result in a stop‑work order from the district executive.

In addition to air content, durability requirements impose further constraints. For self‑consolidating mixes the freeze‑thaw durability factor must be at least eighty; failure to meet either the air‑content range or this durability threshold leads to rejection of the batch. Finally, the admixture itself must be sourced from a manufacturer listed on Bulletin 15 and formulated for the specific application, such as flowable backfill. Ongoing quality assurance is reinforced by daily truck sampling of the first load in each lot, with non‑conforming trucks excluded from incorporation until compliance is demonstrated. [1]

[1] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 186–187), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 388–452), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–707)

To achieve reliable air‑entrainment in pavement concrete the mix must be designed and controlled at several stages. The primary modification is the use of a correctly dosed air‑entraining admixture, supplied from an approved manufacturer (Bulletin 15 for flowable backfill) and introduced with the mixing water through a calibrated visual measuring device that is accurate to ±3 % and equipped with interlocks that prevent discharge during charging cycles. Continuous agitation of the bulk tank and flow‑indication sensors ensure uniform solution delivery, while in‑mixer moisture probes in fully automated batch plants keep the water‑to‑cement ratio stable and protect bubble stability.

The target plastic‑air content is generally 7 % with an allowable variance of ±1.5 % (or ±2 % where specified). Fresh‑mix testing of slump, air, temperature and plastic‑air content on the first truck of each lot must fall within this band; any lot that fails is rejected and subsequent trucks are retested until three consecutive passes are recorded. The dosage of admixture is calibrated by AASHTO T152 or T196 tests, applied without aggregate correction factors, to maintain the plastic‑air content within ±1.5 % of the 7 % target.

Supplementary cementitious materials such as slag cement, fly ash or silica fume may be incorporated under the cement‑factor provisions; they enhance durability while preserving the required air‑void characteristics. Complementary admixtures—water‑reducing or retarding agents—can be used together with the air‑entraining agent to retain workability without collapsing the entrained bubbles. A modest amount of aggregate mineral filler may replace fine aggregate to improve mix optimization and promote uniform air distribution.

Aggregate quality is another critical control. Gravels must not exceed 3 % absorption and other aggregates 2 %; higher‑absorption coarse aggregates are permissible only when mix‑design specimens satisfy freeze‑thaw criteria of at least 300 cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80 (ASTM C666). Maintaining aggregate temperatures between 40 °F and 100 °F at charge, and limiting mixing water temperature to no more than 150 °F, further protects the intended air content under varying weather.

In the hardened state the air‑void system is verified according to ASTM C457. The spacing factor should be approximately 0.008 in., the specific surface about 630 in²/in³, and total air within prescribed limits. Core samples submitted for entrained‑air evaluation must show a content between 3.5 % and 8.5 %; values outside this band trigger an immediate written analysis of cause and corrective plan to be provided within five days, with the possibility of halting paving operations until an acceptable remedy is agreed upon.

When the specified air‑entrainment targets cannot be met, the mix may be adjusted by substituting acceptable materials or revising the mixing procedure, and any change in material source requires a revised mix design and certification before use. Together, these dosage controls, additive selections, aggregate specifications, automated batching, and rigorous fresh‑ and hardened‑state testing constitute a comprehensive quality‑control framework that improves air‑entrainment performance and mitigates its known weaknesses. Performance evaluation and implications

Evaluation of air‑entrainment in pavement concrete relies on a hierarchy of laboratory, field, and long‑term performance tests. In the laboratory, fresh‑mix air content is measured on site according to AASHTO T152 for mixes containing stone or gravel and AASHTO T196 when slag or lightweight aggregate is used; the target is 6 % for conventional cement concrete and about 7 % for AAAP or self‑consolidating mixtures, with an allowable deviation of ±1.5 %. Additional fresh‑state checks required by Section 704 and Publication 145 include slump, temperature and a plastic‑air content tolerance of roughly 7 % ± 2 % for self‑consolidating concrete. When the mix design is being approved, air content is recorded together with density and compressive strength, and the admixture must be sourced from a manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15. If any doubt remains after placement, hardened‑state air‑void analysis per ASTM C457 is performed to verify that total air, spacing factor (≈0.008 in) and specific surface (≈630 in²/in³) meet the specified limits.

Field verification is achieved by sampling each delivery. The first truck of a lot is tested for plastic‑air content; any unit falling outside the 7 % ± 1.5 % band is rejected and subsequent trucks are retested until three consecutive passes are obtained before concrete can be placed. In addition, information cores are taken from every sublot—or at least one in ten—and sent to Laboratory Testing Services for analysis under PTM No. 623. The measured entrained‑air content must lie between 3.5 % and 8.5 %; results outside this interval trigger a written evaluation and corrective action within five days.

Long‑term durability is demonstrated through freeze‑thaw cycling according to ASTM C666 (Procedure A or B). A minimum durability factor of 80 confirms that the entrained air will protect the pavement concrete against repeated freezing and thawing. [1]

[1] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 186–187), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 388–452), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–707)

Air‑entraining admixture is specified so that the plastic concrete contains a prescribed amount of air—typically 6 % for conventional cement concrete and 7 % for AAAP, slip‑form and fixed‑form paving mixes. This creates a stable void system whose spacing factor of roughly 0.008 in and specific surface near 630 in²/in³ satisfy the limits required for hardened concrete. The resulting total air content must lie between about 3.5 % and 7.5 % for standard mixes, or between 4.5 % and 8.5 % for AAAP mixes, and designers are required to hold the plastic‑state air within a ±1.5 % tolerance during batching. When the hardened concrete falls outside the 3.5 %–8.5 % band, a written problem assessment and corrective proposal must be submitted within five days; failure to resolve the issue permits the District Executive to suspend paving until an acceptable remedy is agreed upon. For self‑consolidating concrete the target plastic air content is 7 % ±2 %, and the mix must achieve at least an 80 durability factor in ASTM C666 freeze‑thaw testing, a performance that stems from microscopic bubbles that relieve internal pressure during freezing cycles and thereby markedly improve pavement durability under cyclic freeze‑thaw conditions. Consequently, designers embed these air‑content targets into mix designs, quality‑control plans require sampling of the first truck of each lot and rejection of any batch that does not meet the 7 % ±1.5 % requirement, and subsequent trucks are retested until three consecutive passes are recorded. By maintaining the prescribed void structure and air content, the pavement exhibits reduced permeability, enhanced resistance to freeze‑thaw damage, extended service life, and consequently lower long‑term maintenance demands.
