Asphalt Base Course

Part of: Base Course is-subtopic-of
Asphalt Base Course is a topic under Base Course.

Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

The Asphalt Base Course (ABC) is the structural layer placed beneath surface courses of new or reconstructed pavements whose total base‑course thickness exceeds six inches, often incorporating an Asphalt Rich Base Course in the lower three to six inches of the first lift [1][2]. Designed with a Superpave mix limited to less than 0.3 million ESALs, 2.5 % air voids and a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25 mm, it provides the highest tensile strength at the pavement bottom to resist bottom‑up cracking and moisture‑induced damage while contributing to the effective modulus of subgrade reaction used in k‑value calculations for rigid pavements [1][2]. The course must be placed as a uniformly compacted, non‑segregated layer achieving at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density and cured until its average moisture content falls below 2 %, requirements that prevent distortion, raveling and loss of support for subsequent layers [2]. In permeable applications it serves as an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base over a 2A subbase, providing load‑supporting drainage while maintaining free‑draining characteristics [1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

The Asphalt Base Course is employed in new or reconstructed pavements whose total base‑course thickness exceeds six inches; for those cases the Asphalt Rich Base Course (ARBC) is specified for the bottom three to six inches of the first lift and the ARBC mix must be a < 0.3 million ESAL design with 2.5% air voids and a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25 mm. [1] By providing the highest tensile strength at the pavement’s bottom, this layer is intended to improve resistance to bottom‑up cracking and to mitigate moisture‑damage deterioration of the lower pavement during its service life. [1] In rigid pavement design the Asphalt Base Course (ATPB) is required as the base layer over a 2A Subbase to contribute to the effective modulus of subgrade reaction, which governs how the pavement supports traffic loads. [1] By being included in the weighted calculation of the k‑value, the ATPB helps correct for loss of support caused by erosion or pumping and ensures that the ratio of subbase to roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed four, preventing an artificial design condition. [1] The Asphalt Base Course must be placed so that it forms a uniformly compacted layer achieving at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density and is cured until its average moisture content is less than 2 %, requirements intended to prevent distortion, raveling and other damage and thereby provide a stable foundation for subsequent pavement layers. [2] The specification prohibits placement on wet prepared surfaces or when the air or surface temperature is 35 °F or lower, mandates that any compacted depth exceeding the limits for a given mix be built in two or more layers each between 3 inches and 6 inches thick, requires surface irregularities not to exceed 1/4 inch, and declares the pavement defective if the constructed depth is deficient by ½ inch or more unless corrective action is taken. [2] In a full‑depth reclamation the Asphalt Base Course is designed to create a chemically or asphalt‑stabilized layer that meets strict density and strength criteria so it can safely carry traffic loads; the material must be compacted to at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum density at optimum moisture content and must develop a 7‑day unconfined compressive strength of 300 to 500 psi for roads to be surfaced with less than a 3‑inch overlay (or 280 to 500 psi for thicker overlays) before unlimited traffic is permitted. [2] For an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base Course the intent is to supply a load‑supporting, permeable foundation while preventing loss of drainage and aggregate damage; therefore placement on unstable, frozen or sub‑35 °F surfaces is prohibited, the mix must be produced below 320 °F, compaction is limited to four passes with 8‑to‑10‑ton steel‑wheel rollers, the material may not be compacted to the point that it ceases to be free draining or the aggregate is crushed, and rolling must be performed as soon as the mat has cooled sufficiently to avoid shoving or lateral movement. [2] When used to replace a deficient flexible base, the Asphalt Base Course is installed to provide a uniformly compacted, non‑segregated layer that restores proper support and load distribution over the subgrade or subbase after unsuitable material has been removed; this requires compacting the existing subgrade or subbase before placing the base course material, placing the asphalt base course in a manner that does not cause segregation, and compacting to the density specified by use of approved compaction equipment. [2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 111–113), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 215–236)

The Asphalt Base Course, when employed as a Superpave paving course, must be designed to resist permanent deformation (rutting) and low‑temperature thermal cracking under the projected traffic loads. For base layers thicker than six inches in which an Asphalt Rich Base Course is specified, the design shall call for air voids of 2.5 % so that tensile strength at the bottom of the pavement section is maximized, thereby promoting better resistance to bottom‑up cracking and moisture damage throughout its service life. Shoulder upgrades shall require a minimum thickness of 4 inches of Asphalt Base Course or an equivalent recycled material, and the depth used for lane widening shall be based on the existing pavement structure and ESALs and should not exceed that existing depth. The base course must be compacted to a minimum density of 96 % of the PTM No. 402 target density in layers that are between 2 inches and 5 inches thick. When placed as part of a Superpave mix, the material shall be installed in two or more layers of approximately equal compacted depth that stay within the prescribed limits; for a Superpave 25.0 mm mix no layer shall be less than 3 inches nor more than 6 inches, and for a Superpave 37.5 mm mix no layer shall be less than 4 inches nor more than 8 inches when the total indicated compacted depth exceeds those maxima. Surface smoothness shall be achieved such that the average gap measured under a 12‑ft straight edge does not exceed ½ inch per 3,000 sq yd, and surface irregularities are limited to at most ¼ inch; the constructed depth may not be deficient by ½ inch or more from the design depth, otherwise the pavement is deemed defective. The placed material shall be cured until the average moisture content is less than 2 % or, when emulsified asphalt is used, for at least two weeks before any wearing or binder course is placed. During full‑depth reclamation the base course must reach a minimum density of at least 95 % of the laboratory‑compacted maximum density at optimum moisture content, with compaction verified by a 300‑foot control strip and ten random nuclear density tests per lot. Structural performance shall be demonstrated by achieving an indirect tensile strength of at least 50 psi for asphalt‑stabilized mixes or an unconfined compressive strength between 300 and 500 psi (or 280 to 500 psi depending on overlay thickness) for chemically stabilized mixes, while strengths above 900 psi shall be avoided. Durability shall be supported by maintaining moisture content at the optimum level but not exceeding it by more than 3 % during compaction and by keeping the cured layer damp through daily water application or an emulsified asphalt fog seal until the strength requirement is met. Placement shall not occur on wet prepared surfaces or when the air or surface temperature is 35 °F or lower, and the Asphalt Treated Permeable Base Course shall be prohibited on unstable or frozen surfaces, below 35 °F, or during rain. Completed portions of the base course shall be protected from marring, distortion, and other damage with immediate repair of any damage; traffic shall be controlled during construction, using a pilot car when raveling is likely, and only light vehicles may be allowed after proof rolling with the heaviest expected vehicle once seven‑day strength has been achieved. Appropriate traffic control signs shall be posted and any observed damage during limited use shall be promptly repaired to ensure safety and serviceability. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, an Asphalt Rich Base Course (ARBC) may be employed on any new or reconstructed pavement whose total base‑course thickness exceeds six inches; it is placed as the bottom three to six inches of the first lift and must be a mix with less than 0.3 million ESALs (50 gyrations), design air voids of 2.5 %, and a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25.0 mm. In the same manual, an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base (ATPB) is identified as appropriate for the base layer of rigid‑pavement designs on Interstate and Expressway projects when it is installed over a 2A Subbase; in those cases ATPB can be bid as an alternate to a Cement Treated Permeable Base, particularly where a drainage layer is required and positive outlet drainage can be achieved. The manual also states that Asphalt Base Course may be used for shoulder upgrade projects coded 7216 or 7233 provided the improvement width is at least two feet and the design specifies a minimum depth of four inches; a recycled equivalent may substitute for new asphalt base in those instances. Pennsylvania’s specifications permit cold‑recycled asphalt base courses under Section 341 when reclaimed pavement material is stabilized with an emulsified or foamed asphalt agent, provided placement occurs outside the prohibited seasonal windows (no placement from September 1 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0, 2‑0, 3‑0, 4‑0, 9‑0 and 10‑0, and no placement from October 1 to April 30 in all other districts) and the ambient temperature is at least 45 °F and rising after a minimum of 24 hours without freezing; the placed layer must be between two and five inches thick and compacted to at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density. The same specification notes that cold‑recycled asphalt base course is also appropriate for on‑grade pavement projects that call for a CRABC treatment, where construction utilizes emulsified or foamed asphalt together with reclaimed asphalt pavement, reclaimed asphalt millings, cement or virgin aggregates. For central‑plant mixed cold‑recycled asphalt base courses described in Section 342, the material blend may consist of reclaimed asphalt pavement, reclaimed asphalt millings, virgin aggregates or cement combined with an emulsified or foamed asphalt stabilizing agent, and mixing can be performed continuously or in batch at a plant. An Asphalt Treated Permeable Base Course (ATPBC) under Section 360 may be placed only when the underlying surface is stable and not frozen, the air temperature is at least 35 °F, rain is absent, the mix is produced below 320 °F, placement occurs within eight hours of production, and compaction is achieved with steel‑wheel rollers weighing eight to ten tons. The specifications allow Asphalt Base Course to be used on paved shoulders classified as Type 1, Type 1‑F, Type 1‑S or Type 1‑SP when the design follows the Superpave asphalt mixture procedure of Section 313.2 (or 313.3 for Type 1‑SP) using a PG 64S‑22 binder; it is limited to traffic levels of less than 0.3 million ESALs unless otherwise indicated, and for Type 1‑SP the base forms part of a combination base course together with an aggregate base as described in Section 350.3. For rigid‑pavement relief joint applications specified in Section 504, Asphalt Base Course is appropriate when the project specifies PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement and anticipates traffic levels from three to less than ten million ESALs, or when the design explicitly calls for its use. Finally, the specifications state that Asphalt Base Course may be employed in flexible base replacement projects described in Section 316 whenever an existing pavement is being replaced and the design requires a Superpave asphalt mixture to serve as the base course layer. [1][2]

When the projected 20‑year design ESALs are between 0.3 and less than 3.0 million, a district may consider an alternate design to the standard Asphalt Base Course if asphalt cracking is identified as a regional problem, if the average truck speed exceeds 30 mph, or if rutting is not regarded as a concern; under those circumstances the conventional base layer can be replaced by another approach [1]. Conversely, where frequent stopping conditions, intersections, or steep hills produce slow‑moving truck traffic, the guidance advises retaining the conventional Asphalt Base Course rather than adopting an alternative design [1]. For any new or reconstructed pavement whose total base‑course thickness is greater than 6 inches, the specification requires the use of an Asphalt Rich Base Course instead of a standard asphalt base layer, thereby precluding the standard Asphalt Base Course in that situation [1]. Cold‑recycled asphalt base course cannot be placed from September 1 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0, 2‑0, 3‑0, 4‑0, 9‑0, and 10‑0, nor from October 1 to April 30 in all other districts; projects falling within those prohibited periods must look to a different pavement design approach [2]. Placement of cold‑recycled asphalt base course is only permissible with written approval when no freezing temperatures have occurred for the preceding 24 hours and when the ambient air temperature is at least 45 °F and rising, so projects that cannot satisfy either condition should consider an alternative design [2]. Asphalt treated permeable base course (ATPBC) may not be placed on surfaces whose subgrade is unstable, frozen, or whose temperature is below 35 °F, nor when the ambient air temperature is below 35 °F or rain is occurring; under those ground‑condition or weather constraints an alternate base design must be used [2]. If the mix cannot be produced at a temperature below 320 °F, if it cannot be placed within eight hours of production, or if stockpiling cannot be avoided, the specification directs that another design approach be favored [2]. The Asphalt Base Course is limited to a Superpave mixture with performance grade PG 64S‑22 for traffic levels of less than 0.3 million ESALs (or as otherwise indicated), so projects exceeding this traffic level or requiring a different performance grade must employ an alternative design [2]. Additionally, the Asphalt Base Course material is prescribed only where the projected traffic demand lies between three and less than ten million ESALs; consequently, if the anticipated cumulative load is below three million ESALs or reaches ten million or more, a different pavement design should be considered [2].

Design inputs and requirements

The Asphalt Base Course design must incorporate material information such as the subbase type and its thickness, which affect the effective modulus of subgrade reaction; existing‑condition data include the loss of support factor for each layer, while geotechnical inputs require the roadbed soil resilient modulus and any depth to a rigid foundation within 10 feet that could influence k. Environmental conditions are reflected by selecting an appropriate base elastic modulus—15,000 psi for spring thaw, 30,000 psi for normal (summer/fall), or 50,000 psi for frozen periods—and adjusting it so the subbase‑to‑soil modulus ratio does not exceed four. These factors together determine the weighted average loss of support used in calculating the effective k‑value for the base course. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162)

The Superpave volumetric mixture design system requires the specification of a performance‑grade binder and the calculation of twenty‑year design‑life equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) for each paving course, and the project should generally be limited to no more than three individual mix designs, with five permitted for major projects. For standard construction base courses the mixture shall employ a PG 64S‑22 binder and is limited to traffic volumes of three to less than ten million ESALs, while Type 1‑SP paved shoulders are limited to less than 0.3 million ESALs unless otherwise directed. Placement may not occur on prepared surfaces that are wet or when the air or surface temperature is 35 °F or lower; limited quantities may be placed if work is halted by weather. If the estimated ESAL range exceeds ten million, the district should specify a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder for the wearing course, while base courses do not need to be changed. When the total Asphalt Base Course thickness is greater than six inches, an Asphalt Recycled Base Course (ARBC) shall be used for the bottom three to six inches of the first layer, and all ARBC materials are limited to less than 0.3 million ESAL mix designs with design air voids of 2.5 % and a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25.0 mm. For a Superpave 25.0 mm asphalt base course whose indicated compacted depth exceeds six inches, the material must be placed in two or more layers of approximately equal compacted depth, with each layer not less than three inches and not more than six inches; similarly, a Superpave 37.5 mm base course exceeding eight inches requires layers between four and eight inches. Surface irregularities greater than one‑quarter inch render the pavement defective, and a depth deficiency of one‑half inch or more that cannot be satisfactorily corrected is also considered a defect; verification is performed by drilling full‑depth cores at one random location per 3 000 square yards in accordance with PTM No. 1 and measuring per PTM No. 737. Compaction of the base course shall achieve a minimum of 96 % of the target density defined by PTM 402, verified for each 3 000‑square‑yard area in accordance with AASHTO T 310. For cold‑recycled asphalt base course placement equipment capable of automatically metering liquids within ± 2.0 % by weight shall be used, an asphalt paver as specified in Section 413,3(e)1 shall place the mix, and all machinery shall be maintained per Section 108.05(c). Placement of cold‑recycled material is prohibited from 1 September to 30 April in Districts 1‑0 through 4‑0, 9‑0, 10‑0 and from 1 October to 30 April elsewhere unless written approval is obtained, and ambient temperature must be at least 45 °F and rising with no freezing for 24 hours. The moisture content for compaction must achieve the optimum moisture content determined from the project mix design but may not exceed it by more than three percent, measured by ASTM D2216, D44643 or AASHTO T 265, and curing continues until moisture content falls below two percent and at least two weeks before placement of the wearing course for emulsified material. Full‑depth reclamation requires compaction to at least 95 % of the laboratory‑compacted maximum density at optimum moisture content as defined in PTM 106, verified on a 300‑foot control strip with nuclear gauge readings and at least ten random tests per PTM 1. Surface tolerances for reclamation limit irregularities to no more than one‑and‑a‑half inches and average unevenness to less than one‑half inch over a ten‑foot template, and a minimum final cross‑slope of one‑quarter inch per foot shall be provided. Strength criteria demand an indirect tensile strength of at least fifty pounds per square inch when tested according to Bulletin 27, and a seven‑day unconfined compressive strength of three hundred to five hundred psi (or two hundred eighty to five hundred psi for overlays three inches or greater) for chemically stabilized material before traffic may be opened. For Interstate and Expressway rigid pavement designs the Asphalt Base Course must be an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base (ATPB) or Cement Treated Permeable Base (CTPB) placed over a 2A Subbase, with ATPB and CTPB bid as alternates, and Open Graded Subbase is prohibited for concrete pavement projects; only ATPB or CTPB shall be used when a drainage layer is specified. Placement of an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base Course shall occur on stable, unfrozen surfaces with air temperatures above 35 °F, rain placement is prohibited, the production temperature must be below 320 °F, and the material must be placed within eight hours of manufacture. Compaction of the permeable base shall be performed with steel‑wheel power rollers weighing eight to ten tons in maximum four‑inch lifts, allowing the mix to cool to one hundred degrees Fahrenheit before subsequent layers, and four roller passes shall be applied without crushing aggregate or impairing drainage. When calculating the effective subgrade reaction modulus, the base elastic modulus to use is fifteen thousand pounds per square inch for spring‑thaw conditions, thirty thousand psi for normal conditions, and fifty thousand psi for frozen conditions, and these values should be adjusted so that the ratio of the subbase to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed four. Shoulder upgrade projects identified by codes 7216 and 7233 require the work to include at least a four‑inch depth of Asphalt Base Course or an equivalent recycled material. [1][2]

Design methods and criteria

The design of an Asphalt Base Course utilizes the Superpave volumetric asphalt mixture design procedure, which serves as the primary design tool for selecting aggregate gradations and binder content through a volumetric mixture design developed with the Superpave Gyratory Compactor (SGC) and may incorporate prescribed manufactured additives or plant process modifications [2]. It also requires specifying a performance‑grade (PG) binder and calculating the 20‑year design life equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) so that the project can be placed in one of five ESAL ranges, with those values driving the selection of binder grade and mix proportions [1]. When an Asphalt Rich Base Course is used for base layers thicker than six inches, the mix must be a < 0.3 million ESAL (50 gyrations) design with design air voids of 2.5 % and a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25.0 mm to achieve high tensile strength at the bottom of the pavement [1]. In rigid‑pavement analysis the Asphalt Base Course is treated together with the subbase when computing the effective modulus of subgrade reaction, using a weighted loss‑of‑support factor calculated as (LS₁·D₁)/(D₁+D₂)+(LS₂·D₂)/(D₁+D₂) [1]. The base elastic modulus to be used is 15 000 psi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30 000 psi for normal (summer and fall) conditions, and 50 000 psi for frozen conditions, but these values should be adjusted so that the ratio of the subbase to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed 4; subbase type, thickness and depth to a rigid foundation are also considered in the effective k‑value calculation [1]. For cold‑recycled asphalt base courses the construction sequence consists of milling to an indicated depth, blending emulsified or foamed asphalt stabilizing agents with reclaimed asphalt pavement, reclaimed asphalt material, cement or virgin aggregates, placing and compacting the mixture, and then conducting acceptance testing; the mixing and placement equipment must be capable of automatically metering liquids within a variation of not more than ± 2.0 % by weight [2]. Compacted layers shall be provided between 2 inches to 5 inches in depth, and each layer shall be compacted to at least 96 % of the PTM No. 402 target density for each 3 000‑square‑yard area [2]. Surface smoothness is verified with a twelve‑foot straight edge over three locations per 3 000 sq yd and any average gap exceeding one‑half inch must be corrected, while the stabilized mix shall cure for at least two weeks or until the average moisture content falls below 2 % before the wearing course is placed [2]. Placement is prohibited on prepared surfaces that are wet or when the air or surface temperature is 35 °F or lower and may not occur during rain [2]. When the indicated compacted depth of a Superpave 25.0 mm asphalt base course exceeds six inches, the material must be placed in two or more layers of approximately equal compacted depth with no layer less than three inches or more than six inches; when the indicated compacted depth of a Superpave 37.5 mm asphalt base course exceeds eight inches, it must be placed in two or more layers of roughly equal compacted depth with no layer less than four inches or more than eight inches [2]. The pavement is defective if surface irregularities exceed one‑quarter inch, and depth control is verified by drilling full‑depth cores at one random location per 3 000 sq yd according to PTM No. 1; any core showing a deficiency of one‑half inch or more that cannot be satisfactorily corrected is considered defective [2]. For full‑depth reclamation the construction sequence follows pulverization, mixing reclaimed material with aggregates, RAP, stabilizing additives and water as defined by the mix design, then compacting to at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum density at optimum moisture content according to PTM No. 106 using a roller pattern verified on a three‑hundred‑foot control strip with nuclear gauge readings according to PTM No. 402; moisture content shall be determined in accordance with ASTM D2216, ASTM D44643 or AASHTO T 265 and shall not exceed the optimum by more than 3 % [2]. Strength acceptance requires three ASTM D1633 Proctor specimens that after a seven‑day cure meet either an indirect tensile strength of 50 psi for asphalt‑stabilized mixes (Bulletin 27) or an unconfined compressive strength between 300 and 500 psi (or 280–500 psi for thicker overlays) [2]. Emulsion or foamed mixes may be optionally compacted in a Superpave Gyratory Compactor to two to three inches using fifty gyrations [2]. For asphalt‑treated permeable base course the mix shall be produced at a temperature below 320 °F, placed within eight hours of production, spread with a slip‑form paver or mechanical spreader in lifts not exceeding four inches, and compacted with an eight‑ton to ten‑ton steel‑wheel roller applying four passes while avoiding over‑compaction that would impede drainage; surface smoothness is checked with a ten‑foot straightedge and irregularities greater than one‑half inch are corrected, and depth verification requires a six‑inch diameter test hole for each 3 000 sq yd of completed base [2]. The performance grade specified for the Superpave design of the Asphalt Base Course in Type 1‑SP shoulders is PG 64S‑22 applied to pavements with less than 0.3 million ESALs or as otherwise indicated [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

The Superpave system must be used to design the asphalt base course so that the mixture resists the climatic and traffic conditions of the project location, selecting an appropriate PG‑binder and ESAL range for the expected loads (section 5.9) [1]. When the total base course thickness exceeds 6 inches, an Asphalt Rich Base Course shall be placed in the bottom 3 to 6 inches of the first lift; ARBC mix designs are limited to less than 0.3 million ESAL, have design air voids of 2.5 % and a nominal maximum aggregate size of 25.0 mm, which provides the highest tensile strength at the bottom of the pavement section and improves moisture‑damage resistance during service life (section 5.9) [1]. For rigid pavement designs that include an asphalt base course, the design shall treat the base as part of the combined subbase when computing the effective k‑value (section 8.6) [1]. An Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base shall be specified over a 2A Subbase, and the base elastic modulus used in the effective k‑value calculation shall be set to 15,000 psi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30,000 psi for normal summer/fall conditions, and 50,000 psi for frozen winter conditions, with adjustments permitted so that the subbase‑to‑soil resilient modulus ratio does not exceed 4 (section 8.6) [1]. For Cold Recycled Asphalt Base Course the mix must contain sufficient liquids and water may be added at the mill head with a calibrated meter to keep moisture suitable for compaction; if density targets are not met the moisture content is adjusted and the layer re‑compacted, and after placement the material shall be cured until the average moisture content is less than 2 % before any wearing or binder course is installed (section 341) [2]. The rate of strength gain may vary with climatic conditions—favorable weather (no rain, sunshine, low humidity, high temperature) can accelerate cure while less favorable conditions may extend the required curing period from a few days up to two weeks (section 344) [2]. Verification and expected performance

For cold‑recycled asphalt base courses, verification begins with confirming that compaction reaches at least 96 % of the PTM 402 target density and that in‑place density is measured on every 3,000 sq yd segment according to AASHTO T 310; any segment falling below this limit must be scarified, moisture adjusted and re‑compacted, with a new target density established if necessary. Surface smoothness and grade are then checked with a 12‑ft straight edge at three locations within each 3,000 sq yd area and the average gap is required to be no more than ½ inch before correction. Milling depth is recorded at least once per 3,000 sq yd during pulverization to ensure the specified depth is achieved, and prior to placing the wearing or binder course the base must be cured until moisture content is less than 2 %, verified by extracting three random 1,200‑gram samples. For standard superpave asphalt base courses, verification is performed by the Inspector through random full‑depth coring at one location per 3,000 sq yd and any additional locations suspected to be deficient; core depth is measured according to PTM No. 737 and compared with the indicated compacted depth. Any surface irregularity exceeding ¼ inch or a depth deficiency of ½ inch or more that cannot be satisfactorily corrected is deemed defective. The contractor must control the loose depth of each lift so that the final compacted depth meets the design within tolerance, backfill, compact and seal the core holes with the same mix, and promptly correct any deficient areas longitudinally and transversely until the depth shortfall is reduced to less than ½ inch. For full‑depth reclamation bases, verification starts with establishing a compaction control strip of at least 300 ft and using nuclear density gauges to demonstrate a minimum density of at least 95 % of the laboratory‑compacted maximum density; if the strip does not meet this value, additional passes are made and a new strip may be constructed requiring an average density that is 98 % of the original strip density. Surface tolerance on each 3,000 sq yd lot is checked by measuring the average irregularity over a 10‑ft template and correcting any area where the irregularity exceeds ½ inch while also confirming a minimum cross‑slope of ¼‑inch per foot. Strength testing of laboratory specimens requires that asphalt‑stabilized material must reach a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi and chemically stabilized material must achieve an unconfined compressive strength of 300 to 500 psi (or 280 to 500 psi for thicker overlays) after seven days; averages below these limits trigger rework. Acceptance is granted only when each lot satisfies the density, surface‑tolerance, and strength criteria. For asphalt‑treated permeable base courses, verification by the Representative combines surface‑tolerance checks with depth testing. Surface irregularities at suspected locations, transverse joints and paving notches are examined with a 10‑ft straightedge held parallel to the centerline and moved in stages no more than 5 ft apart; any deviation greater than ½ inch must be corrected by loosening or adding material without grinding or milling. Depth compliance is confirmed by digging or drilling one 6‑inch diameter test hole to the full ATPBC depth for each 3,000 sq yd (or less) of finished base, and the Representative may require additional holes where deficiencies are suspected; sections that are short by ½ inch or more are removed and replaced until the depth is within ½ inch of the design. Compaction quality is also checked by confirming that exactly four roller passes have been applied and that no further passes are used except to eliminate surface creases, while avoiding over‑compaction that would prevent free drainage or crush aggregate. The Asphalt Base Course in a full‑depth reclamation is evaluated by the density, strength, and surface‑smoothness criteria set out in Section 344.3(e). The reclaimed material is compacted to a minimum density of at least 95 % of the laboratory maximum dry density at optimum moisture content, with nuclear density readings taken on a control strip; it achieves a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi for asphalt‑stabilized mixes or an unconfined compressive strength of 300–500 psi (or 280 psi for thinner overlays) after seven days, and any surface irregularity exceeding ½ inch over a 10‑foot template is corrected. Acceptance requires at least ten random density tests per lot, the specified strength test results, and compliance with the surface tolerance limits. [2]
