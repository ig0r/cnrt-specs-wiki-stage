Joint Deterioration

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Joint Deterioration is a topic under Distress.
deteriorated-jointsfailed-transverse-joints
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Joint deterioration refers to the loss of integrity in concrete pavement joints, where the joint material becomes crushed or otherwise degraded; it is typically more severe at the slab bottom than near the surface and can extend beyond the mid‑slab depth, especially at the intersection of transverse and longitudinal joints [1]. The distress appears in Portland‑cement concrete systems—including plain slabs, jointed reinforced slabs, and adjacent shoulders—and is also addressed for asphalt overlays where longitudinal and transverse joints are sealed or sawed and sealed during overlay placement [1][2]. This degradation reduces the pavement’s ability to share loads, leading to diminished slab structural capacity, increased faulting, and degraded ride quality [1].

Description and identification

Joint deterioration refers to the loss of integrity in concrete pavement joints, where the joint material becomes crushed or otherwise degraded. The condition is typically more severe at the slab bottom than at the surface, and it can extend beyond the mid‑slab depth, especially at the intersection of transverse and longitudinal joints. When such deterioration is present, full‑depth corner repairs are recommended and all crushed or deteriorated joints must be removed during patching. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89)

In section 4.1 Concrete Pavement Restoration, joint deterioration is first spotted through a visual inventory of the pavement in which the exact quantities of visible distress should be measured and tabulated for each joint. To separate true joint problems from other load‑ or climate‑related cracks, cores should be taken near deteriorated joints and at random locations to determine subbase or subgrade soil conditions and the extent of deterioration below the pavement surface; deflection tests, faulting measurements, or other non‑destructive techniques should be used to identify which joints require replacement. In section 4.2 Concrete Pavement Patching, a joint is identified as deteriorated when it is crushed or otherwise damaged, shows loss of load transfer, and the damage extends beyond the mid‑slab depth—often greater at the slab bottom than near the surface—and is especially evident where transverse and longitudinal joints intersect. This condition is distinguished from superficial spalling that occurs only in the top half of the slab depth and is not caused by severe joint problems; partial‑depth repairs should only be made where such spalling occurs, whereas all crushed or deteriorated joints should also be removed and full‑depth corner repairs … should be considered where joint deterioration exists at the intersection of transverse and longitudinal joints and extends beyond the mid‑slab depth. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–89)

Where it occurs

Joint Deterioration appears in Portland‑cement concrete pavement systems, including plain concrete slabs, jointed reinforced slabs, and the adjacent concrete shoulders [1][2]. The distress is also addressed for asphalt pavements, where longitudinal and transverse joints are sealed as part of asphalt joint and crack sealing treatments [1]. When an asphalt overlay is placed over existing joints—whether the underlying surface is concrete or a previously sawed‑and‑sealed asphalt overlay—the same treatment is required, with saw‑and‑seal operations specified for those overlays [1]. Unbonded concrete overlays are required to have cleaning and sealing of all existing joints before placement, extending consideration of joint deterioration to such overlay structures [1]. For rehabilitation projects that involve a concrete overlay or an asphalt overlay applied to an existing concrete pavement, joint cleaning and sealing may also be performed on shoulders after diamond grinding as part of the restoration process [1]. In partial‑depth repair of rigid pavements where delamination extends through a transverse or longitudinal joint, the specification calls for removal of unsound concrete to at least 2 inches depth and, when the repair abuts an existing joint, placement of ½ inch expansion joint material in transverse joints or ¼ inch pre‑formed cellular polystyrene in longitudinal joints [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 417–418)

Joint Deterioration is reported to appear at the longitudinal and transverse joints of concrete pavements, including the pavement‑shoulder interface, indicating that the distress is localized to joint locations rather than slab interiors or subgrade. The distress also manifests where joints intersect corners, such that deterioration at the intersection of transverse and longitudinal joints may extend deeper toward the slab bottom and can require full‑depth corner repairs when it passes beyond mid‑slab depth. In addition, Joint Deterioration is observed to occur at locations where joints meet cracks, with delamination that can extend through a pavement joint. The delamination may be treated as a partial‑depth repair that preserves the joint by establishing two separate repair areas, each laid out rectangularly and extending approximately 3 inches beyond the edges of the delaminated zone. Each repair area must have minimum dimensions of 15 inches in length by 10 inches in width by 2 inches in depth, and when such areas are less than 24 inches apart they shall be combined into a single partial‑depth repair area. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 417–418)

Measurement, survey and extent

Joint deterioration is quantified by measuring faulting, which is the vertical offset between adjacent slabs, and by conducting pavement deflection tests at the joint locations. The field crew uses faulting measurement devices and standard deflection testing equipment to record the vertical displacement or surface curvature across each joint, performing the test directly on the joint to determine whether it should be replaced or stabilized. Joint Deterioration is also evaluated by sounding the concrete to determine the extent of delamination; this is done with a steel bar, drag chains, or a hammer while a Representative is present to observe the assessment. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 417–418)

Effects on performance

Joint deterioration in concrete pavements reduces slab structural capacity because loss of load transfer diminishes the ability of the pavement to share loads, and this condition therefore requires full‑depth patching with properly dowelled joints to restore proper load sharing. When joint damage is severe enough to extend beyond the mid‑slab depth, full‑depth corner repairs as specified in Publication 408, Specifications, Section 516, should be considered where joint deterioration exists at the intersection of transverse and longitudinal joints and extends beyond the mid‑slab depth. To reestablish support around patches and maintain structural capacity, the guidelines in section 3.3 prescribe rehabilitating failed transverse joints, or when that has already been done cleaning and resealing all joints, and installing dowel bar retrofits at mid‑panel cracks and transverse joints. Uniform support must be provided to the overlay, so any crushed or deteriorated joints should also be removed; otherwise ride quality degrades and additional leveling material becomes necessary. When ride quality is compromised, diamond grinding may be applied to improve ride quality as per Publication 408, Specifications, Section 514, except for concrete pavements constructed with Vanport Limestone aggregate; however, the process removes the effective mortar layer and the skid resistance of the exposed Vanport Limestone is susceptible to rapid decline, which can affect safety. By addressing joint deterioration through these specified remedial actions—rehabilitation, cleaning, resealing, dowel bar retrofits, full‑depth patching or corner repairs, removal of deteriorated joints, and selective grinding—the pavement’s durability and service life are preserved. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89)

When joints in a Portland cement concrete pavement deteriorate, the damage often starts at the slab bottom and can spread upward, eventually causing loss of load‑transfer capacity; if this loss of load transfer occurs, only full‑depth patching or full‑depth corner repairs—required when joint deterioration exists at the intersection of transverse and longitudinal joints and extends beyond the mid‑slab depth—can restore performance. Partial‑depth repair is limited to spalling confined to the top half of the slab and may not be used when severe joint problems are present, because such problems can only be corrected by full‑depth patching. Moreover, if intermittent patches leave ten feet or less of original pavement between them over a length of about ±500 feet, the entire section should be removed and reconstructed, indicating that unchecked joint deterioration can force large‑scale reconstruction. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89)

Repairs and treatments

In the asphalt‑overlay rehabilitation strategy (section 3.3 A) longitudinal and transverse joint cleaning and sealing are prescribed to treat deteriorated joints by removing debris and applying sealant as part of the overlay process. [1] The same strategy also requires sawing and sealing the overlay over existing transverse and patch joints to address joint deterioration that persists after the new asphalt is placed. [1] When a bonded concrete overlay is selected and a composite base is present, rehabilitation of all failed transverse joints is required to treat joint failure in that situation. [1] The concrete‑rehabilitation strategy (section 3.3 B) calls for rehabilitating all failed transverse joints when they have not yet been repaired, thereby addressing joint deterioration. [1] If those transverse joints have already been treated, the alternative prescribed is to clean and reseal all joints, which also targets joint deterioration. [1] Section 3.3 B further requires cleaning and sealing of all longitudinal joints and pavement‑shoulder joints as a means of treating joint deterioration. [1] A dowel‑bar retrofit at mid‑panel cracks and transverse joints is recommended in section 3.3 B to restore joint function, addressing joint deterioration associated with loss of load transfer. [1] Section 4.1 on concrete pavement restoration mandates that joint rehabilitation and joint cleaning/sealing be performed after diamond grinding so that the sealant reservoir has the proper shape factor and is protected from damage, thereby treating joint deterioration under those post‑grinding conditions. [1] Slab stabilization, including slabjacking, may also be employed in section 4.1 for joints identified by deflection, faulting or other non‑destructive testing as needing replacement, which addresses joint deterioration where such testing indicates a problem. [1] Section 4.2 prescribes full‑depth concrete pavement patching for badly deteriorated pavement and broken slabs and requires removal of all crushed or deteriorated joints, treating joint deterioration that extends through the slab depth when the entire deteriorated area must be removed. [1] Full‑depth corner repairs (Publication 408 Section 516) are specified in section 4.2 for situations where joint deterioration exists at the intersection of transverse and longitudinal joints and reaches beyond the mid‑slab depth, thereby addressing that specific form of joint failure. [1] Partial‑depth repairs may be employed only when spalling is limited to the top half of the slab and is not caused by severe joint problems such as loss of load transfer, in which case full‑depth patching would be required; this condition limits partial‑depth repair use to relatively minor surface distress. [1] The partial‑depth repair procedure described in section 525.3 of Publication 408 addresses joint deterioration when delamination extends through a pavement joint by requiring the joint to be retained as two separate minimum repair areas unless a detailed re‑establishment procedure is included in the QC Plan. [2] If the partial‑depth repair abuts an existing transverse joint, section 525.3 mandates installation of 1/2‑inch expansion joint material within the joint. [2] When the repair abuts an existing longitudinal joint, the same section requires placement of 1/4‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene in the joint, each extending at least 1/2 inch below the bottom of the repair whenever possible. [2] Where the repair material can be sawed without damage, section 525.3 directs that the joint be re‑established by sawing through the full depth of the repair before sealing as directed in sections 512.3 and 521.3, thereby treating joint deterioration through a controlled re‑creation of the joint geometry. [2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 417–418)

For concrete rehabilitation strategies, a joint qualifies for full rehabilitation only when it is classified as a failed transverse joint; joints that are not deemed failed are limited to cleaning and resealing, which also applies to longitudinal and pavement‑shoulder joints. In the concrete pavement patching guidelines, full‑depth corner repairs are indicated only when joint deterioration at a transverse‑longitudinal intersection extends beyond the mid‑slab depth. Partial‑depth repairs may be used only for spalling confined to the top half of the slab and not caused by severe joint problems such as loss of load transfer; in those latter cases full‑depth patching is required. If a significant length of roadway (±500 feet) would need intermittent patches leaving ten feet or less of original pavement between them, the section must be removed and reconstructed rather than patched. For concrete pavement partial‑depth repair, when joint deterioration appears as delamination that extends through a pavement joint the treatment is limited to a partial‑depth repair that must preserve the joint by creating two separate minimum repair areas; each area must be at least 15 in long, 10 in wide and 2 in deep and the removal depth may not exceed half the slab thickness. If the exposed dowel bars in the transverse joint show only minor corrosion, a bond‑breaking material may be applied. When the Representative judges the dowel bars to be misaligned or extensively deteriorated, the partial‑depth repair is ruled out and a full‑depth concrete patch must be constructed. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 417–418)

Prevention in design and construction

Cleaning and sealing longitudinal and transverse concrete joints (and pavement‑shoulder joints) is recommended to limit moisture intrusion, loss of support, and sealant failure, thereby curbing joint deterioration; this measure appears under the Asphalt Overlay strategy in the initial‑cost guidelines and within the concrete rehabilitation guidelines of section 3.3 B, and it is required after diamond grinding in the Concrete Pavement Restoration guidance (§4.1). Where additional protection is deemed necessary, heavy‑duty membranes may be applied as part of the same Asphalt Overlay context to further reduce joint distress. Sawing and sealing the overlay over existing transverse and patch joints is prescribed for overlays placed on concrete or previously sealed asphalt surfaces, addressing deterioration of those specific joint types during installation. Rehabilitation of all failed transverse joints must be performed if they have not already been repaired; otherwise cleaning and resealing of all joints is required to limit further joint deterioration. A dowel bar retrofit at mid‑panel cracks and transverse joints is required to restore load transfer across cracked joints and mitigate joint distress. In concrete pavement patching, every patch joint must be dowelled and the dowels fully bonded to the existing slab with epoxy, polyester or vinyl‑ester binders so that load transfer at transverse joints is maintained, directly mitigating loss of load transfer that characterizes joint deterioration for full‑depth patches. When reconstruction of all lanes extends 500 feet or more, the design must follow Publication 72M specifications (RC‑21M for JRCP or RC‑27M for JPCP), which prescribe 30‑foot or 15‑foot joint spacing, perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals; compliance prevents joint distress caused by inadequate spacing or poor load transfer. If only a single lane of 500 feet or more is rebuilt, the new joints must be aligned with existing ones and an intermediate joint inserted whenever the pre‑existing spacing exceeds 20 feet, thereby avoiding excessive joint spacing that can lead to deterioration. Designers are required to select the appropriate New Pavement Joint item—skewed or non‑skewed—as called for in Publication 72M/408, ensuring correct joint orientation and load transfer for any patch. To limit joint deterioration during partial‑depth repairs, when a repair abuts an existing transverse joint the specification requires installation of 1/2″ expansion joint material in the joint; when it abuts an existing longitudinal joint, 1/4″ preformed cellular polystyrene shall be installed, both extending at least 1/2 inch below the bottom of the repair whenever possible, thereby preserving joint function and preventing mortar intrusion. If delamination extends through a pavement joint, the joint must be maintained as two separate minimum repair areas unless the QC Plan provides a detailed procedure for re‑establishing the joint, thereby addressing joint separation and crack propagation. When the repair material can be sawed without damage, the joint should be re‑established by sawing through the full depth of the repair and then sealed according to Section 512.3 for longitudinal joints and Section 521.3 for transverse joints, further reducing moisture ingress and loss of joint integrity. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 417–418)

For asphalt overlay on existing concrete, longitudinal and transverse joint cleaning and sealing is required to prevent further deterioration of those joints (Section 3.3 Guidelines for Developing Initial Costs – A) [1]. When the overlay crosses existing transverse or patch joints, saw‑cutting and sealing the overlay over those joints is mandated to address joint deterioration that could develop beneath the new asphalt layer (Section 3.3 Guidelines for Developing Initial Costs – A) [1]. For concrete pavement rehabilitation or when a concrete overlay is applied, any failed transverse joint must be rehabilitated; if the joint has not yet failed, cleaning and resealing of all joints—including longitudinal and pavement/shoulder joints—is required to prevent further joint deterioration (Section 3.3 Guidelines for Developing Initial Costs – B) [1]. A dowel bar retrofit at mid‑panel cracks and transverse joints is prescribed to maintain joint performance (Section 3.3 Guidelines for Developing Initial Costs – B) [1]. When an unbonded overlay is used, cleaning and sealing all existing joints applies specifically to that overlay condition to prevent deterioration (Section 3.3 Guidelines for Developing Initial Costs – B) [1]. After diamond grinding, joint cleaning and sealing—or rehabilitation—must be performed so the sealant reservoir attains the proper shape factor, a sequencing intended to prevent damage to the sealant and thus curb further joint deterioration (Section 4.1 Concrete Pavement Restoration) [1]. In full‑depth concrete pavement patching, dowelling all patch joints and fully bonding the dowels with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders is required to preserve load transfer at transverse joints, thereby preventing loss of transfer that leads to joint deterioration (Section 4.2 Concrete Pavement Patching) [1]. For full‑width reconstruction of 500 ft or more, designs must follow RC‑21M (JRCP) or RC‑27M (JPCP); these specifications require perpendicular joints spaced 30 ft (JRCP) or 15 ft (JPCP) together with coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, a measure that prevents crushed or poorly transferring joints (Section 4.2 Concrete Pavement Patching) [1]. When only one lane is reconstructed for 500 ft or more, new joints must be aligned with existing joints and an intermediate joint placed wherever existing spacing exceeds 20 ft, addressing excessive joint spacing that can cause deterioration (Section 4.2 Concrete Pavement Patching) [1]. Any joints that have lost load transfer or are crushed must be removed and replaced during patching for overlay preparation to avoid further joint distress in the overlay zone (Section 4.2 Concrete Pavement Patching) [1]. If delamination extends through a pavement joint, the repair shall be performed by maintaining the joint as two separate minimum repair areas, which is intended to protect the adjacent joint and crack from mortar intrusion; this applies only when the QC Plan does not already contain a detailed re‑establishment procedure (Section 525.3 Construction) [2]. When a partial‑depth repair abuts an existing transverse joint, 1/2 in expansion joint material shall be installed in the joint, extending at least 1/2 in below the repair when possible, to preserve joint function (Section 525.3 Construction) [2]. When a partial‑depth repair abuts an existing longitudinal joint, 1/4 in preformed cellular polystyrene shall be placed in the joint for the same purpose of preserving joint function (Section 525.3 Construction) [2]. The joint may be re‑established by full‑depth sawing of the repair material when it can be sawed without damage (Section 525.3 Construction) [2]. Longitudinal joints shall be sealed as specified in Section 512.3 and transverse joints shall be sealed as specified in Section 521.3 to prevent further deterioration (Section 525.3 Construction) [2].
