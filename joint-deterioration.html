Joint Deterioration

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Joint Deterioration is a topic under Distress.
deteriorated-jointsfailed-transverse-joints
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Joint deterioration refers to loss of integrity in concrete pavement joints, where the joint material becomes crushed or otherwise degraded, appearing more severe at the slab bottom than at the surface and often extending beyond the mid‑slab depth, particularly where transverse and longitudinal joints intersect [1]. The condition is observed in Portland‑cement concrete pavement systems—including jointed plain (JPCP) and jointed reinforced (JRCP) slabs, their shoulders, and at corner locations where joints meet cracks or the pavement‑shoulder interface—manifesting as delamination that propagates through the joints [1][2]. This degradation reduces slab structural capacity by diminishing load‑transfer capability, which can lead to compromised ride quality and may ultimately require full‑depth corner repairs or reconstruction when it extends beyond mid‑slab depth [1].

Description and identification

Joint deterioration refers to the loss of integrity in concrete pavement joints, where the joint material becomes crushed or otherwise degraded. The condition is typically more severe at the slab bottom than at the surface, and it can extend beyond the mid‑slab depth, especially at the intersection of transverse and longitudinal joints. When such deterioration is present, full‑depth corner repairs are recommended and all crushed or deteriorated joints must be removed during patching. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89)

In the Pavement Policy Manual’s section 4.1 on Concrete Pavement Restoration, joint deterioration is first spotted through a visual inventory of the pavement in which the exact quantities of visible distress are measured and tabulated for each joint. To separate true joint problems from other load‑ or climate‑related cracks, cores are taken near deteriorated joints and at random locations to determine subbase or subgrade soil conditions and the extent of deterioration below the pavement surface; deflection tests, faulting measurements, or other non‑destructive techniques are then used to identify which joints require replacement. In section 4.2 on Concrete Pavement Patching, a joint is identified as deteriorated when it is crushed or otherwise damaged, shows loss of load transfer, and the damage extends beyond the mid‑slab depth—often greater at the slab bottom than near the surface—and is especially evident where transverse and longitudinal joints intersect. This condition is distinguished from superficial spalling that occurs only in the top half of the slab depth and is not caused by severe joint problems; partial‑depth repairs are limited to such spalling, whereas all crushed or deteriorated joints must be removed and full‑depth corner repairs considered where joint deterioration exists at the intersection of transverse and longitudinal joints and extends beyond the mid‑slab depth. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specification identifies joint deterioration by sounding the concrete with a steel bar, drag chains, or a hammer to locate delamination. Once delamination is found, rectangular repair limits are laid out to extend approximately 3 inches beyond the edges of the delaminated area. When the delamination is determined to extend through a pavement joint, it is distinguished from other delaminations by the requirement to maintain the joint with two separate minimum repair areas, each at least 15 inches long by 10 inches wide by 2 inches deep, rather than treating it as a single patch. If such joint‑crossing delamination occurs, the repair plan must specifically address reestablishing or protecting the joint according to Section 525.3(e). [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 427–428)

Where it occurs

In section 3.3 GUIDELINES FOR DEVELOPING INITIAL COSTS > A. Asphalt Rehabilitation Strategies of the Pavement Policy Manual, Joint Deterioration is addressed for both concrete and asphalt pavements, with longitudinal and transverse joint cleaning and sealing required for concrete pavements and asphalt joint and crack sealing required for asphalt pavements; the same treatment is also required when an asphalt overlay is placed over existing joints, with saw‑and‑seal operations specified for asphalt overlays on concrete or on previously sawed and sealed asphalt overlay [1]. In section 3.3 GUIDELINES FOR DEVELOPING INITIAL COSTS > B. Concrete Rehabilitation Strategies of the same manual, Joint Deterioration is addressed only for concrete‑type structures, calling for rehabilitation of failed transverse joints on plain concrete pavements, cleaning and sealing all longitudinal joints and pavement/shoulder joints, and requiring similar joint work—cleaning and sealing existing joints—for unbonded concrete overlays; thus the distress is considered in plain concrete slabs, bonded or unbonded concrete overlays, and reconstruction methods that involve concrete [1]. Section 4.1 CONCRETE PAVEMENT RESTORATION (CPR) of the Pavement Policy Manual notes that Joint Deterioration appears in Portland‑cement concrete pavement systems, manifesting in the joints of concrete slabs, and because the rehabilitation guidance treats it together with concrete‑or‑asphalt overlays, the condition can be addressed when either a concrete overlay or an asphalt overlay is applied to the existing concrete structure; joint cleaning and sealing may also be performed on shoulders after diamond grinding [1]. Section 4.2 CONCRETE PAVEMENT PATCHING of the same manual states that Joint Deterioration is addressed only for Portland‑cement concrete pavements, including jointed plain (JPCP) and jointed reinforced (JRCP) slabs and the concrete shoulders adjacent to them; the guidelines require removal of all crushed or deteriorated joints and full‑depth corner repairs where joint deterioration exists at transverse‑longitudinal intersections extending beyond mid‑slab depth, and when a concrete pavement is to receive an asphalt overlay or an unbonded concrete overlay any joints that have lost load transfer or are crushed must be replaced before the overlay is placed [1]. In SECTION 525—CONCRETE PAVEMENT PARTIAL‑DEPTH REPAIR of Publication 408, the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifies that Joint Deterioration is addressed only for concrete (rigid) pavements; when delamination extends through a transverse or longitudinal pavement joint it must be treated with the partial‑depth repair procedures of Section 525.3, maintaining the joint with two separate minimum repair areas, installing 1/2‑inch expansion joint material in any abutting transverse joint and 1/4‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene in any abutting longitudinal joint, and sealing the joints as required by Sections 512.3 and 521.3 [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 427–428)

Joint Deterioration is described as a distress that appears at the longitudinal and transverse joints of concrete pavements. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications characterize the same distress as delamination that propagates through the pavement joints, confirming that it occurs at transverse and longitudinal joints (and any adjacent cracks). The Pavement Policy Manual further notes that the distress also manifests at corner locations where transverse and longitudinal joints intersect, sometimes requiring full‑depth corner repairs when the deterioration extends beyond mid‑slab depth. It is also reported to occur at the pavement‑shoulder interface. Additionally, the manual indicates that joint deterioration tends to be deeper toward the slab bottom than near the surface, especially where joints meet cracks. According to the Pennsylvania specification, because the delamination is confined to the joint system, the distress does not occur in slab interiors, wheel paths, or subgrade layers. When delamination extends through a joint, the specification requires maintaining the joint with two separate minimum repair areas unless a QC plan provides an alternative method to re‑establish and protect the joint and adjacent crack. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 427–428)

Measurement, survey and extent

In the Pavement Policy Manual, joint deterioration is quantified by measuring faulting, which is defined as the vertical offset between adjacent slabs, and by conducting pavement deflection tests at the joint locations; the field crew uses faulting measurement devices and standard deflection testing equipment to record the vertical displacement or surface curvature across each joint, performing the test directly on the joint to determine whether it should be replaced or stabilized [1]. In the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, joint deterioration is quantified by measuring the extent of concrete delamination at a joint [2]. The measurement procedure calls for sounding the concrete with a steel bar, drag chains, or a hammer in the presence of the Representative to locate the damaged zone [2]. After the delaminated area is identified, rectangular repair limits are laid out extending approximately 3 inches beyond the edges, and the resulting area must be at least 15 inches long, 10 inches wide, and 2 inches deep [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 427–428)

Effects on performance

Joint deterioration in concrete pavements reduces slab structural capacity because loss of load transfer diminishes the ability of the pavement to share loads, and this condition therefore requires full‑depth patching with properly dowelled joints to restore proper load sharing. When joint damage is severe enough to extend beyond the mid‑slab depth, full‑depth corner repairs as specified in Publication 408, Specifications, Section 516, should be considered where joint deterioration exists at the intersection of transverse and longitudinal joints and extends beyond the mid‑slab depth. To reestablish support around patches and maintain structural capacity, the guidelines in section 3.3 prescribe rehabilitating failed transverse joints, or when that has already been done cleaning and resealing all joints, and installing dowel bar retrofits at mid‑panel cracks and transverse joints. Uniform support must be provided to the overlay, so any crushed or deteriorated joints should also be removed; otherwise ride quality degrades and additional leveling material becomes necessary. When ride quality is compromised, diamond grinding may be applied to improve ride quality as per Publication 408, Specifications, Section 514, except for concrete pavements constructed with Vanport Limestone aggregate; however, the process removes the effective mortar layer and the skid resistance of the exposed Vanport Limestone is susceptible to rapid decline, which can affect safety. By addressing joint deterioration through these specified remedial actions—rehabilitation, cleaning, resealing, dowel bar retrofits, full‑depth patching or corner repairs, removal of deteriorated joints, and selective grinding—the pavement’s durability and service life are preserved. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89)

When joints in a Portland cement concrete pavement deteriorate, the damage often starts at the slab bottom and can spread upward, eventually causing loss of load‑transfer capacity; if this loss of load transfer occurs, only full‑depth patching or full‑depth corner repairs—required when joint deterioration exists at the intersection of transverse and longitudinal joints and extends beyond the mid‑slab depth—can restore performance. Partial‑depth repair is limited to spalling confined to the top half of the slab and may not be used when severe joint problems are present, because such problems can only be corrected by full‑depth patching. Moreover, if intermittent patches leave ten feet or less of original pavement between them over a length of about ±500 feet, the entire section should be removed and reconstructed, indicating that unchecked joint deterioration can force large‑scale reconstruction. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89)

Repairs and treatments

In the Asphalt Overlay strategy described in section 3.3 A, longitudinal and transverse joint cleaning and sealing of concrete pavements is called for to treat deteriorated joints by removing debris and applying sealant as part of the overlay process. The same section also requires sawing and sealing the overlay over existing transverse and patch joints to address joint deterioration that persists after the new asphalt is placed. When a bonded concrete overlay is selected, the guideline specifies rehabilitation of all failed transverse joints if a composite base is present, thereby treating joint failure in that situation. The Concrete Rehabilitation Strategies of section 3.3 B state that joint deterioration is addressed by rehabilitating all failed transverse joints when they have not yet been repaired; if they have already been treated the alternative is to clean and reseal all joints. That section also calls for cleaning and sealing all longitudinal joints and pavement‑shoulder joints, and recommends a dowel‑bar retrofit at mid‑panel cracks and transverse joints to restore joint function. Section 4.1 on Concrete Pavement Restoration requires that joint rehabilitation and joint cleaning/sealing be performed after diamond grinding so the sealant reservoir has the proper shape factor and is protected from damage. Slab stabilization, including slabjacking, may also be used for joints identified by deflection, faulting or other non‑destructive testing as needing replacement, thereby addressing joint deterioration where the tests indicate a problem. Section 4.2 on Concrete Pavement Patching prescribes full‑depth concrete pavement patching for badly deteriorated pavement and broken slabs and requires removal of all crushed or deteriorated joints, treating joint deterioration that extends through the slab depth when the entire deteriorated area must be removed. Full‑depth corner repairs (Publication 408 Section 516) are used specifically where joint deterioration exists at the intersection of transverse and longitudinal joints and reaches beyond the mid‑slab depth. Partial‑depth repairs may be employed only when spalling is limited to the top half of the slab and is not caused by severe joint problems such as loss of load transfer. Partial‑depth repair of concrete pavements addresses joint deterioration by preserving the joint when delamination runs through it; in that case the source requires maintaining the joint with two separate minimum repair areas unless a detailed re‑establishment procedure is included in the QC Plan. When the partial‑depth repair abuts an existing transverse joint, the treatment calls for installing 1/2‑inch expansion joint material in the joint. When it abuts an existing longitudinal joint, 1/4‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene is installed; both materials should extend at least 1/2 inch below the bottom of the repair whenever possible. If the repair material can be sawed without damage, the joint may be re‑established by sawing through the full depth of the repair, after which longitudinal and transverse joints are sealed per Sections 512.3 and 521.3 respectively. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 427–428)

For concrete rehabilitation strategies, a joint is eligible for full rehabilitation only when it is classified as a failed transverse joint; joints that are not deemed failed are limited to cleaning and resealing, which also applies to longitudinal and pavement‑shoulder joints. Full‑depth corner repairs are indicated only when joint deterioration at a transverse‑longitudinal intersection extends beyond the mid‑slab depth. Partial‑depth repairs may be used only where spalling is confined to the top half of the slab depth and is not caused by severe joint problems such as loss of load transfer; in those latter cases full‑depth patching is required. If a significant length of roadway, approximately ±500 feet, would require intermittent patches leaving ten feet or less of original pavement between them, the pavement section should be removed and reconstructed for its entire length rather than patched. For concrete pavement partial‑depth repair, the treatment is allowed when delamination reaches a joint only if the joint is preserved by creating two separate minimum repair areas, each at least 15 in long, 10 in wide and 2 in deep, and the quality‑control plan contains a detailed procedure for reestablishing the joint; otherwise a full‑depth patch must be used. The removal depth for the partial‑depth repair may not be less than 2 in nor exceed half of the slab thickness. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 427–428)

Prevention in design and construction

Cleaning and sealing longitudinal and transverse concrete joints as well as pavement‑shoulder joints is recommended to limit moisture intrusion, loss of support, and sealant failure, thereby curbing joint deterioration; this measure appears under the Asphalt Overlay strategy (item 1.f) in the initial‑cost guidelines and within the concrete rehabilitation guidelines of section 3.3 B, and it is required after diamond grinding in the Concrete Pavement Restoration guidance (§4.1). [1] Similarly, asphalt joint and crack sealing on asphalt pavements is listed in item 1.f of the Asphalt Overlay strategy to reduce joint distress. [1] When additional protection is deemed necessary, heavy‑duty membranes may be applied as part of the same Asphalt Overlay context. [1] Sawing and sealing the overlay over existing transverse and patch joints is prescribed for overlays placed on concrete or previously sealed asphalt surfaces (item 1.h) to address deterioration of those specific joint types during installation. [1] During concrete pavement rehabilitation, any failed transverse joints should be rehabilitated if they have not already been repaired, and all joints should be cleaned and resealed; this is intended to limit further joint deterioration (section 3.3 B). [1] A dowel bar retrofit at mid‑panel cracks and transverse joints is required in that same section to restore load transfer across cracked joints and mitigate joint distress. [1] In the Concrete Pavement Patching guidelines (subsection 4.2), every patch joint must be dowelled and the dowels fully bonded to the existing slab with epoxy, polyester or vinyl‑ester binders so that load transfer at transverse joints is maintained, directly mitigating the loss of load transfer that characterizes joint deterioration for full‑depth patches. [1] When reconstruction of all lanes extends 500 feet or more, the design must follow Publication 72M specifications (RC‑21M for JRCP or RC‑27M for JPCP), which prescribe 30‑foot or 15‑foot joint spacing, perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals; compliance with these requirements prevents joint distress caused by inadequate spacing or poor load transfer. [1] If only a single lane of 500 feet or more is rebuilt, the new joints must be aligned with existing ones and an intermediate joint inserted whenever the pre‑existing spacing exceeds 20 feet, thereby avoiding excessive joint spacing that can lead to deterioration. [1] Designers are also required to select the appropriate New Pavement Joint item—skewed or non‑skewed—as called for in Publication 72M/408, ensuring correct joint orientation and load transfer for any patch. [1] For a partial‑depth repair that abuts an existing transverse joint, a 1/2‑inch expansion joint material must be installed in the joint; for a repair that meets an existing longitudinal joint, a 1/4‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene must be installed, and both fillers should extend at least 1/2 inch below the bottom of the repair whenever possible, thereby helping to maintain joint integrity and limit further deterioration. [2] If the repair material can be sawed without damage, the joint should be re‑established by sawing through the full depth of the repair, after which the repaired joints are sealed according to Sections 512.3 (longitudinal) and 521.3 (transverse). [2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 427–428)

Longitudinal and transverse joint cleaning and sealing of concrete pavements is required when an asphalt overlay is placed on existing concrete, a practice intended to prevent further deterioration of those joints. The same guidance mandates saw‑cutting and sealing the overlay over existing transverse and patch joints whenever the overlay will cross such joints, thereby addressing joint deterioration that could develop beneath the new asphalt layer. For concrete rehabilitation, any failed transverse joint shall be rehabilitated as part of concrete pavement rehabilitation or when a concrete overlay is applied; if the joint has not yet failed, cleaning and resealing of all joints—including longitudinal and pavement/shoulder joints—shall be carried out to prevent further joint deterioration. When an unbonded overlay is used, cleaning and sealing all existing joints applies specifically to that overlay condition. Joint cleaning and sealing—or rehabilitation—must be performed after diamond grinding so that the sealant reservoir attains the proper shape factor, a sequencing intended to prevent damage to the sealant and thus curb further joint deterioration. For full‑depth concrete pavement patching, dowelling all patch joints and fully bonding the dowels with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders is required to preserve load transfer at transverse joints, thereby preventing loss of transfer that leads to joint deterioration. Full‑width reconstruction of 500 ft or more must follow RC‑21M (JRCP) or RC‑27M (JPCP); these specifications require perpendicular joints spaced 30 ft (JRCP) or 15 ft (JPCP) together with coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, a measure that prevents crushed or poorly transferring joints. When only one lane is reconstructed for 500 ft or more the new joints must be aligned with the existing joints and an intermediate joint shall be placed wherever existing spacing exceeds 20 ft, addressing excessive joint spacing that can cause deterioration. Any joints that have lost load transfer or are crushed shall be removed and replaced during patching for overlay preparation to avoid further joint distress in the overlay zone. For partial‑depth repairs where delamination extends through a pavement joint, the repair must preserve the joint by using two separate minimum repair areas—or include a detailed re‑establishment procedure in the QC Plan—to keep mortar from intruding and protect adjacent cracks; this applies under Section 525.3(c) for any partial‑depth repair where the joint is affected. If a partial‑depth repair abuts an existing transverse joint, the specification requires installing 1/2‑inch expansion joint material that extends at least 1/2 inch below the bottom of the repair, a measure intended to maintain joint movement capacity and prevent joint deterioration. If the partial‑depth repair abuts an existing longitudinal joint, the specification requires installing 1/4‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene with the same extension, also intended to preserve joint movement and prevent deterioration. When the repair material can be sawed without damage, the joint should be re‑established by sawing through its full depth and then sealed per Sections 512.3 (longitudinal) and 521.3 (transverse), addressing water infiltration that could accelerate joint distress. [1][2]
