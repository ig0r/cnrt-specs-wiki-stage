Texturing

Part of: Skid Resistance is-subtopic-of
Texturing is a topic under Skid Resistance.
texturingpavement-surface-texturemacrotexture
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Texturing is a mechanically applied macro‑texture for concrete pavements, created either by diamond grooving or as part of a Next Generation Concrete Surface (NGCS) treatment that cuts deeper channels into the slab [1][2]. Its purpose is to provide water drainage from beneath tires—thereby increasing skid resistance, reducing hydroplaning and wet‑weather crashes—and, when NGCS is used, to lower pavement‑generated noise [1]. The process is carried out after floating and straight‑edge correction while the concrete remains plastic, using a self‑propelled machine equipped with a turf‑drag, broom or spring‑steel tine that maintains constant pressure and travel speed to produce longitudinal grooves roughly 1/8 in wide, 1/8–3/16 in deep and spaced ½–¾ in apart [2].

Purpose and applicability

Texturing—implemented as diamond grooving or as part of a Next Generation Concrete Surface (NGCS) treatment—creates macro‑texture on concrete pavements by cutting deeper channels with diamond tools. The resulting texture lets water escape from beneath tires, which reduces the chances of skidding, hydroplaning and wet‑weather crashes while also increasing pavement friction and lateral stability; NGCS further uses this texture to lower road noise. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 93–94)

Texturing is appropriate for concrete pavements when the project calls for increased skid resistance, improved water drainage, or reduction of pavement‑generated noise [1]. For slip‑form placement of plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 501), texture shall be applied after floating and straight‑edge testing while the concrete remains plastic, using a self‑propelled turf‑drag or broom that stays within 5 inches but not closer than 3 inches of the pavement edges and produces longitudinal grooves about 1/8 inch wide (± 1/32 inch), 1/8 to 3/16 inch deep, with a nominal centre‑to‑centre spacing of ½ to ¾ inch [2]. When a pavement has a radius less than 50 feet and the achieved mean texture depth does not reach at least 0.040 inch, additional mechanical grooving perpendicular to traffic shall be required, indicating that simple longitudinal texturing alone is insufficient [2]. For concrete patches, the final finish must be textured so that it corresponds with the surrounding pavement; if the existing surface has been diamond‑ground, the patch shall receive longitudinal texture, but when the contract specifies grinding of the entire pavement surface, matching wheel ruts and texturing the patch are not required, showing that full‑pavement grinding supersedes localized texturing [2]. Diamond grinding is recommended when the surface requires re‑profiling, removal of rutting, restoration of texture, or a quieter ride; total grinding is preferred unless an isolated low spot would demand excessive grinding of surrounding pavement, indicating that grinding rather than texturing should be selected for those performance goals [1]. Diamond grooving should be employed to provide macrotexture that allows water to escape from beneath tires; longitudinal grooves are the economical choice for most situations, while transverse grooves are reserved only where additional braking traction is needed, and grooving may follow diamond grinding if ride quality must first be addressed, demonstrating that grooving—not texturing—is the appropriate technique when macro‑drainage or extra braking performance is required [1]. Next Generation Concrete Surface (NGCS) texturing, which combines flush grinding with longitudinal grooving, is suitable on new or existing pavements where road‑noise concerns exist, the aggregate is polishing‑susceptible, or a full‑pavement texture is desired; however, conventional diamond grinding must first remove any surface irregularities greater than 1/4 inch before NGCS can be performed, indicating that preliminary grinding precedes this specialized texturing method [1]. For slip‑form pavements where longitudinal grooves are not feasible, transverse texture may be applied on shoulders, gore areas, sidewalks or other narrow sections, producing rectangular grooves 3/32 to 3/16 inch wide and 1/8 to 3/16 inch deep; hand methods may be approved where self‑propelled equipment cannot reach, showing alternative texturing approaches for constrained locations [2]. If the initial texture produced by the equipment does not meet the required quality, placement of concrete shall be stopped until the equipment deficiency or malfunction is corrected, underscoring that unsatisfactory texturing results trigger a pause in construction rather than proceeding with an alternate method [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 93–94); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–412)

Required inputs

Texturing of concrete pavements is contingent upon the original concrete mix, specifically requiring the use of a particular coarse aggregate together with natural sand; the hardness of that coarse aggregate influences the suitability for texturing, as polishing‑susceptible aggregates generally lead to the selection of longitudinal grooving or next‑generation concrete surface (NGCS) treatments [1]. The work must be performed where a positive cross slope allows water to drain from the grooves and shoulders, and isolated low spots need not be ground if doing so would require excessive grinding of surrounding pavement; for NGCS any surface irregularities greater than ¼ inch must be corrected by conventional diamond grinding before texturing [1]. Under the plain or reinforced cement concrete pavement specifications, texturing is to be carried out after floating and straight‑edge correction, when excess moisture has disappeared but before the concrete becomes non‑plastic; at that stage a turf drag, broom device, or spring‑steel tine mounted on self‑propelled equipment with external alignment control is used [2]. The equipment must maintain continuous contact pressure, travel at a constant speed, operate within 5 inches of the pavement edge but not closer than 3 inches, and produce longitudinal grooves that are 1/8 inch (± 1/32 inch) wide and 1/8 to 3/16 inch deep with a nominal centre‑to‑centre spacing of ½ to ¾ inch [2]. For small or confined areas where self‑propelled machines cannot be used, hand‑applied drag or broom methods may be approved, and any unsatisfactory result requires stopping concrete placement until the equipment issue is corrected [2]. When the pavement serves traffic with a horizontal radius under 50 feet, the finished surface must achieve a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 inch as measured by ASTM E965 in the presence of the Representative [2]. For NGCS texturing, a self‑propelled grinding machine weighing at least 40,000 lb and equipped with a minimum 4‑ft wide head fitted with diamond grinding blades of 1/8‑inch thickness spaced by 1/32‑inch spacers is required; the grooving blades must be ¼‑inch to 3⁄8‑inch taller than the grinding blades and arranged about ½ inch centre‑to‑centre to cut longitudinal grooves 1/8‑inch to 3/16‑inch deep [2]. The pavement surface must be textured in the longitudinal direction with a maximum differential of ¼‑inch at joints or cracks, cross‑slope deviations not greater than ⅛‑inch over any 12‑ft measured with a straightedge, and feather passes extending at least 2 feet onto shoulders for a minimum length of 100 feet to ensure proper drainage [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 93–94); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–399)

Design for diamond grinding and grooving must reference Publication 408 sections—Section 514 for grinding, Section 510 for longitudinal grooves, and Section 517 for transverse grooves—and the plans shall clearly show grinding or grooving limits together with transition or stop lines at bridges and ramps. A positive cross‑slope is required to provide drainage for pavement and shoulder areas. When next‑generation concrete surface (NGCS) is used, conventional diamond grinding is recommended to remove any significant roughness, correct faulting, or surface irregularities greater than ¼ inch before texturing. Longitudinal grooving is preferred because it is less time‑consuming; transverse grooving may be specified only where braking traction is needed. For mechanically textured concrete pavements the design calls for conventional diamond‑ground surfaces identified as 408/514 and longitudinally diamond‑grooved surfaces identified as 408/510, with grooves spaced at 3/4 inch center‑to‑center, which governs the texture geometry. Any concrete slurry generated during texturing must be removed in accordance with Publication 408 Section 104.06 to prevent slurry from entering drainage structures or waterways; this establishes a pre‑construction procedural control. Under slip‑form paving (Section 501) the texturing operation shall follow floating and straight‑edge correction and shall be performed with a spring‑steel tine device that maintains constant contact pressure and travel speed; the tines must operate within five inches but not closer than three inches of the pavement edges. The spring‑steel tine device shall create longitudinal grooves parallel to the centerline having a width of one‑eighth inch ± one‑thirty‑second inch, a depth ranging from one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths inch, and a nominal centre‑to‑centre spacing of one‑half to three‑quarters inch. Where transverse texture is required the grooves shall be rectangular, three‑thirty‑seconds to three‑sixteenths inch wide and one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths inch deep. The finished surface must achieve a minimum mean texture depth of 0.040 inch, which shall be verified by ASTM E965 testing in the presence of the Representative at no fewer than four locations per lane; any location below this limit shall trigger additional testing every fifty feet and remedial grooving to the specified dimensions. Under Section 509 the next‑generation concrete surface texturing shall employ a power‑driven, self‑propelled machine equipped with diamond blades that must weigh at least forty‑thousand pounds and possess a grinding head of minimum four‑foot width. Grinding shall be executed so that the differential between adjacent sides of joints and cracks does not exceed one‑sixteenth inch, and no depressions or slope misalignment greater than one‑eighth inch in twelve feet—measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge placed perpendicular to the centerline—shall be permitted; overlapping passes may not exceed one inch. Longitudinal grooves shall be spaced approximately one‑half inch centre‑to‑centre and shall produce depths of one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths inch, with groove‑forming blades one‑quarter to three‑eighths inch taller in diameter than the grinding blades; ridges on the finished surface may not exceed one‑eighth inch and at least ninety‑eight percent of the pavement surface must be textured. A feather pass of minimum one hundred feet extending at least two feet onto adjacent shoulders shall be provided for drainage, and before full production a three‑hundred‑foot test section in a single lane shall be constructed and approved. For concrete pavement patching (Section 516) the final finish of patches that are not being overlaid shall be textured to correspond with the texture of the surrounding pavement, and when the existing surface has been diamond‑ground the patch shall be textured longitudinally; no explicit dimensional tolerances or pre‑construction test procedures are prescribed for the texture itself. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 93–95); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–412)

Equipment and personnel

For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 501), texturing preparation begins with a fully energized, self‑propelled slip‑form paving machine equipped with side forms and automatic alignment controls that also vibrates or tampes the concrete across the full width and depth. After placement, consolidation is achieved by striking off and screeding the concrete with a finishing machine equipped with a rubber‑tired wheel and a transverse finishing unit to produce a uniform textured surface, followed by floating with mechanical floats. Initial texturing is then applied using a turf drag, broom or spring‑steel tine device mounted on self‑propelled equipment that maintains constant pressure, alignment and travel speed while keeping the tines 3 inches to 5 inches from the edge. Curing may be performed either with white polyethylene sheets or by applying a monomolecular film using a pressure spray tank with an adjustable nozzle, atomizing sprayer, tank agitator and protective hood to lay a continuous film without marring the surface. Final acceptance testing includes checking edge slump and surface tolerance with a 12‑foot straightedge, measuring macrotexture depth per ASTM E965 at four locations per lane, and verifying tie‑bar pull‑out resistance using a center‑pull hydraulic jack with a load‑measuring gauge capable of 12,000 lb or 1/32 in slippage. For next generation concrete surface texturing (Section 509), the operation relies on a power‑driven, self‑propelled machine fitted with diamond blades for grinding and grooving; the equipment must weigh at least 40,000 lb and carry a grinding head no less than 4 ft wide. The same machine produces longitudinal grooves in either a single‑pass or two‑pass sequence, using guides to keep the texture parallel to the centerline. Acceptance of the process requires constructing a 300‑foot test section in one lane to verify that the equipment and procedures achieve an acceptable surface. For bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavement (Section 540), preparation of the existing surface uses a profile mill, and pneumatic hammers may be employed for saw cutting and chipping where the mill cannot reach; after milling the surface is cleaned with a compressed‑air stream delivering at least 100 psi that is free of oil or moisture. Placement of the concrete is followed by finishing and texturing, after which curing materials are applied typically via a spray system that provides a uniform white opaque coating at a minimum rate of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 ft²; insulating blankets are laid when air temperature is 45°F or less, with removal allowed only when the concrete surface temperature change does not exceed 40°F in any one‑hour period and compressive strength has reached at least 3,000 psi. Testing of the finished pavement requires coring equipment conforming to PTM No. 606 for extracting cores, a measuring apparatus per PTM No. 614 for depth verification, and either cylinder compression testing according to PTM No. 604 or maturity monitoring in accordance with AASHTO T 413 to confirm the required strength before opening to traffic. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–461)

Texturing must be performed by an operator who can run a self‑propelled texturing machine equipped with external alignment control, keeping the turf drag, broom or tine device continuously in contact with the slab and applying constant down pressure while maintaining a steady travel speed; the operator also provides positive elevation control of the device. When equipment cannot reach confined zones or fails, crew members may use approved hand‑texturing methods, and work must be stopped if unsatisfactory results are obtained. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

Work sequence

Before any mechanically textured concrete pavement work can be undertaken, the existing surface must first receive conventional diamond grinding to remove significant roughness, correct faulting, and eliminate surface irregularities greater than 1/4 inch; this preparatory grind establishes the ride‑quality baseline required for subsequent texturing operations such as longitudinal or transverse grooving or Next Generation Concrete Surface (NGCS) applied in either a single‑pass or two‑pass mode. In addition, all concrete slurry and any residue must be removed and managed according to Publication 408 specifications; the slurry may not be discharged into drainage structures, wetlands, waterways, or the Department’s right‑of‑way and must be disposed of as directed in Section 104.06. For slip‑form paved concrete pavements, texturing cannot begin until the crew has completed the final strike‑off and consolidation steps, performed floating, carried out straightedge testing and corrected any surface irregularities, ensured that all excess moisture has disappeared, and kept the concrete in its plastic condition; only when these actions are finished may a turf drag or broom be used to create the required texture. Prior to grinding and grooving of a Next Generation Concrete Surface, a 300‑foot test section must be constructed in a single lane to demonstrate the equipment and procedures, and work may not proceed until that test section has been approved—or waived by written District Executive approval. When applying a bonded concrete overlay over an asphalt‑surfaced pavement, construction‑related work—including texturing—may commence only after a comprehensive quality‑control plan has been submitted and accepted, the existing asphalt surface has been profile milled to the depth shown in the plans, cracks larger than 3/4 inch have been filled with approved grout, standing water eliminated, the milled surface cleaned of loose debris, a final cleaning performed no more than 30 minutes before concrete placement, and the asphalt temperature verified to be less than 120 °F immediately prior to placing the overlay. For concrete pavement patches, the patch surface must first be finished to match the existing pavement cross‑section and any wheel ruts (or tapered for Type C patches) in accordance with the final finish procedure; only after this final finish is completed may the patch be textured to correspond with the surrounding pavement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 93–95); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–461)

Texturing begins with diamond grinding the concrete pavement using diamond‑cutting equipment to re‑profile the surface and, when ride quality is a concern, to remove any significant roughness, correct faulting, or surface irregularities greater than 1/4 inch before further treatment; grooving is then cut with a diamond‑grooving saw to provide macrotexture for water drainage, and grooving should occur after diamond grinding if diamond grinding is needed for ride quality [1]. For Next Generation Concrete Surface (NGCS) the contractor may either perform a single‑pass operation that combines diamond grinding and grooving in one pass, or follow a two‑pass operation that first diamonds the pavement and subsequently grooves it [1]. After floating, straightedge testing and surface corrections have been completed while the concrete is still plastic, an initial texture is applied using a turf‑drag or broom device to create striations parallel to the centerline; this is done on self‑propelled equipment with external alignment control, constant contact area, positive elevation control, down pressure and steady travel speed, although hand methods may be approved for small or irregular areas [2]. The final texture is then produced with a spring‑steel tine device positioned between 5 inches and 3 inches from the pavement edges to cut parallel grooves that are 1/8 inch (± 1/32) wide, 1/8 to 3/16 inch deep and spaced ½ to ¾ inch center‑to‑center [2]. If unsatisfactory results are obtained, placement of concrete must be stopped until the equipment deficiency or malfunction is corrected [2]. For NGCS work a power‑driven self‑propelled machine with diamond blades that weighs at least 40,000 lb and has a minimum 4‑ft grinding head is selected; the contractor then decides whether to achieve NGCS in a single‑pass or two‑pass operation [2]. In a single‑pass approach the blade stack consists of 1/8‑in blades spaced 1/32 in apart with grooving blades ¼‑to‑⅜ in taller to cut longitudinal grooves 1/8‑to‑3/16 in deep at about ½‑in centre spacing [2]. In a two‑pass method the surface is first ground with the same flat blade stack and later the grooves of the same dimensions are added [2]. After the main grinding/grooving passes a feather pass extending at least 2 ft onto shoulders for a minimum length of 100 ft is made to provide drainage, ensuring no slope deviation greater than 1/8 in in 12 ft and maintaining a maximum 1/16‑in differential across joints [2]. A 300‑ft test section is constructed and approved before full work proceeds; slurry is continuously removed and the pavement cleaned before traffic reopens, with no unground areas allowed and overlap limited to 1 in [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 93–94); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–399)

For slip‑form paving of plain or reinforced cement concrete (Section 501), the initial texturing must be performed only after floating, straightedge testing and any surface corrections have been completed, once all excess moisture has disappeared, and it must occur before the concrete reaches a non‑plastic condition; if unsatisfactory results are obtained, placement shall be stopped until the equipment deficiency or malfunction is corrected. For bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavement (Section 540), curing materials must be applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation after finishing and texturing, and final surface cleaning cannot occur earlier than 30 minutes before the overlay is placed; when curing covers are removed to permit joint sawing, the removal period may not exceed 1/2 hour, after which the cover must be replaced or the slab protected. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–461)

Staging and logistics

Work zones for texturing are controlled by patching one lane at a time when adjacent lanes would be affected, with surface markings placed before sawing and traffic protected from drop‑off conditions; excavated areas may not remain un‑patched for more than 48 hours (or over weekends/holidays) and after saw cutting the pavement traffic may use the patched area only up to a maximum of 72 hours. Prior to concrete placement, the contractor must have adequate equipment, trained personnel, and enough hauling units scheduled to keep placement continuous, and once the patch is finished the surface is textured to match the surrounding pavement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–412)

During mechanical texturing, the concrete slurry generated by diamond grinding raises surface‑water pH to 10 or 11 and creates turbidity, so management shall include measures to prevent the slurry from entering drainage structures, stormwater control structures, wetlands, or waterways, and the slurry is not to be wasted within the Department’s right‑of‑way; removal of the slurry or residue is required by Publication 408 specifications. In addition, slurry generated during grinding must not be allowed to flow across lanes occupied by traffic or into gutters or other drainage facilities. Texturing in the slip‑form paving method must be performed while the surface is still plastic, after excess moisture has disappeared and before the concrete becomes non‑plastic, and it may only be done during hours of natural light unless an acceptable lighting system is provided. If rain becomes imminent before the concrete has set, paving operations—including texturing—must be stopped and the surface protected with cover material. The concrete temperature may not exceed 160 °F during curing, which also limits when texturing can occur. When ambient air temperature is forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or less, insulating blankets are required to prevent rapid heat loss, and they may only be removed when the air temperature exceeds forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or when the average compressive strength of the concrete exceeds three thousand pounds per square inch, provided that the rate of temperature change at the concrete surface does not exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit within any one‑hour period. Immediately before placing a concrete overlay, the existing asphalt surface must be less than one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit and kept free of standing water. The diamond‑grinding operation required by Section 509 must employ a power‑driven, self‑propelled machine that weighs a minimum of 40,000 pounds and has a grinding head at least four feet in width, so the site must be able to accommodate this heavy, wide equipment. The spring‑steel tine device used for longitudinal texture must stay within five inches but not closer than three inches from pavement edges. Self‑propelled texturing machines must maintain constant contact with the surface and a constant travel speed to achieve uniform grooves. No depressions or misalignment of slope greater than 1/8‑inch in twelve feet may be allowed when measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge placed perpendicular to the centreline. Proper drainage is provided by making a feather pass a minimum of two feet onto the adjacent shoulder, gore area, auxiliary lane, or ramp lane, and each feather pass must be at least one hundred feet long. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (p. 95); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–461)

Quality control and acceptance

For slip‑form paving of plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements, the final surface finish is accepted when the longitudinal texture grooves are 1/8 inch wide with a tolerance of ± 1/32 inch, have a depth ranging from 1/8 to 3/16 inch, and are spaced nominally 1/2 to 3/4 inch centre‑to‑centre; the transverse texture grooves must be rectangular, 3/32 to 3/16 inch wide and 1/8 to 3/16 inch deep; macrotexture must achieve a Mean Texture Depth of at least 0.040 inch as measured in accordance with ASTM E965 on a minimum of four test locations per lane, and if any determined MTD is less than 0.040 inch additional tests are required every 50 feet together with corrective mechanical grooving that reproduces the same groove dimensions and provides grooves spaced 1½ inch centre‑to‑centre. When texturing a next‑generation concrete surface, the inspector must verify that the grinding machine weighs at least 40,000 lb and is equipped with a grinding head of no less than 4 ft width, that all blades are straight with no bowing as confirmed by a 4‑ft straightedge, and that joint and crack edges do not exceed a differential of 1/16 inch. The pavement slope must be free of depressions or misalignment greater than 1/8 inch over any 12‑ft span measured perpendicular to the centreline. Longitudinal grooves produced by the grinding operation should be spaced about 0.5 inch centre‑to‑centre, have depths between 1/8 and 3/16 inch, and overlap between passes must be limited to 1 inch. The final surface finish must exhibit ridges that do not exceed 1/8 inch, at least 98 percent of the area must be textured, and ride quality measured by IRI shall be ≤70 in/mile for speeds greater than 45 mph or ≤90 in/mile for speeds of 45 mph or less. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–399)

After the final texturing operation, the Representative performs a macro‑texture depth test (ASTM E965) on the pavement; at least four test locations are measured in each lane. If any measured mean texture depth is less than 0.040 in, additional measurements must be made every 50 ft to locate and assess deficient macrotexture. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

Under Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, the final texturing must be performed with a spring‑steel tine device that creates longitudinal grooves 1/8 inch (± 1/32 inch) wide, 1/8 to 3/16 inch deep and spaced ½ to ¾ inch center‑to‑center; for low‑radius traffic the pavement must also achieve a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 inch as measured by ASTM E965, and any area below that threshold requires additional testing and, if still deficient, mechanical grooving of rectangular grooves with the same width and depth limits and 1½‑inch spacing at no extra cost; when texturing equipment produces unsatisfactory results the concrete placement must be stopped until the equipment deficiency or malfunction is corrected. Under Section 509—Next Generation Concrete Surface, the texturing (NGCS) is accepted only when the longitudinal grinding produces no more than a 1/16‑inch differential across adjacent sides of joints or cracks, when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge there are no depressions or slope misalignments greater than 1/8 inch in 12 feet, and the finished surface contains no ridges that exceed 1/8 inch while at least 98 % of the pavement is textured; any pass overlap must not exceed 1 inch, and if any of these tolerances are exceeded the non‑conforming area must be reground to meet the criteria before the work can be considered complete. Under Section 516—Concrete Pavement Patching, patches must be textured so that their texture matches that of the surrounding pavement; any deviation in surface level greater than 3/16 inch measured with a 12‑foot straightedge is not acceptable and must be corrected by grinding at no additional cost, and if after grinding the surface still exceeds this limit the patch area is considered defective and must be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department. Safety and environmental controls

Under section 4.7 DIAMOND GRINDING AND GROOVING > MECHANICALLY TEXTURED CONCRETE PAVEMENTS, the texturing process generates a concrete slurry that can raise the pH of surface water to 10 or 11 and create turbidity, which constitutes an environmental hazard for the public if it reaches drainage structures, wetlands, or waterways. The same section requires removal of all slurry or residue in accordance with Publication 408 specifications and disposal as directed in Section 104.06, and expressly prohibits waste of slurry within the Department’s right‑of‑way to protect both workers handling the alkaline material and the surrounding public. In SECTION 509—NEXT GENERATION CONCRETE SURFACE and SECTION 540—BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF ASPHALT‑SURFACED PAVEMENT, the texturing operation employs a power‑driven, self‑propelled grinding machine that must weigh at least 40,000 lb and carries diamond blades, creating a moving heavy‑equipment hazard for operators and any personnel working near the grinder. The specification mandates continuous removal of slurry or residue resulting from the diamond grinding and grooving and expressly forbids allowing slurry to flow across lanes occupied by traffic or into gutters or other drainage facilities, indicating a slip or vehicle‑damage hazard for the public if not controlled. Workers must guard against standing water on the milled surface because any residual water is prohibited and can create slip hazards; oil or grease drippings from equipment must also be kept off the cleaned surface to prevent contamination that could lead to bonding problems and present a slip risk for personnel and later road users. When compressed‑air streams are used, the air must be free of oil, moisture, and other contaminants and have a minimum pressure of 100 psi measured at the source; otherwise workers may be exposed to inhalation hazards or equipment failure. Immediately before placing the concrete overlay, the asphalt temperature must be less than 120°F, so exposure to higher temperatures could cause heat stress or burns for both crew and the public if traffic is allowed before cooling. Curing, protection, and handoff

Following the final texturing pass on a plain or reinforced cement‑concrete pavement constructed by the slip‑form method (Section 501), curing must be initiated immediately; the curing material is applied within thirty minutes after bleed water has dissipated and remains in place on both the surface and the sides until the slab reaches the compressive‑strength level required for traffic opening as defined in Section 501.3(q). The contractor may select any approved method, such as white polyethylene sheeting overlapped at least twelve inches and sealed at the edges, burlap‑backed sheets, a liquid membrane‑forming compound applied at the manufacturer’s rate, or a monomolecular film, and if normal curing is postponed an intermediate film must be sprayed; all covers are to remain undisturbed, the concrete temperature may not exceed 160 °F during curing, and emergency curing material shall be available. For a bonded concrete overlay over asphalt‑surfaced pavement (Section 540), curing materials also must be placed within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and retained until the slab attains the minimum compressive strength required for traffic opening. When the air temperature is forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or less, insulating blankets are installed to prevent rapid heat loss and may be removed only when the air temperature exceeds forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or when the average compressive strength exceeds three thousand pounds per square inch, provided that the concrete surface temperature does not change more than forty degrees Fahrenheit in any one‑hour period. The curing compound is spread uniformly so as to give a solid white opaque cover at a minimum rate of one gallon (± 0.1 gallon) per 125 square feet, and a second equal application may be made after sawing if conditions warrant. For concrete pavement patching (Section 516), after the surface has been textured to match the surrounding pavement the fresh concrete must first be protected from traffic drop‑off conditions and rain in accordance with Section 901.3(j) and Section 501.3(r); the date of placement is then scribed on the fresh surface before a curing membrane is applied. A Type 2 white pigmented liquid membrane‑forming curing compound may be used in place of the standard white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene (PAMS) compound as permitted by Section 501.3(l), and rapid‑set patch repairs are cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. [2]

In Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, after the final texturing is completed a curing material must be applied within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and kept in place until the slab reaches the compressive strength required for opening to traffic under Section 501.3(q). The pavement may then be opened to construction equipment or users only if second‑stage sawing has not been performed, an oversized backer rod (width plus 50 %) is installed and maintained near the surface, the initial concrete strength has been attained, and all curing requirements have been satisfied; the Representative will authorize opening once the required strength—either the Table D value for normal‑strength pavements or at least 1,200 psi for accelerated‑strength pavements—is confirmed. In Section 509—Next Generation Concrete Surface, work completed under Texturing may carry construction traffic or be opened to users only after the NGCS has been finished in accordance with all acceptance criteria and the pavement surface has been cleaned of slurry. The final surface must be true to grade, contain no ridges that exceed 1/8 inch, achieve at least 98 % texture coverage, and meet the IRI limit of 70 in/mile when posted speeds are greater than 45 mph or 90 in/mile when posted speeds are less than or equal to 45 mph; only after these tolerances are verified and the surface is cleaned can traffic be allowed. In Section 540—Bonded Concrete Overlay of Asphalt‑Surfac ed Pavement, after finishing and texturing a curing material must be applied within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and kept in place until the concrete reaches the strength needed for traffic opening; the Representative will allow construction traffic or public use only when the concrete has attained the minimum required compressive strength of 3,000 psi, verified by cylinder testing per PTM No. 604 or by the maturity method per AASHTO T 413. Expected performance and maintenance

In the slip‑form paving method described in Section 501.3(g), texture work is deemed defective when the measured mean texture depth (MTD) is less than 0.040 inches; this condition triggers additional testing and requires mandatory mechanical grooving to achieve the specified macrotexture. The finished surface must also remain within a 3/16‑inch tolerance when inspected with a straightedge placed parallel or perpendicular to the centerline, and any depressions of 3/16 inch or greater between adjacent lanes are considered defective and must be removed by grinding or cutting. Moreover, if unsatisfactory results are obtained from the texture equipment, placement of concrete shall be stopped until the equipment deficiency or malfunction is corrected. Under the requirements for next‑generation concrete surfaces in Section 509.3, failures may arise when grinding equipment that causes spalls at joints or cracks, or fractures surface aggregates, is used; such spalling constitutes non‑compliance. The ground surface must be smooth and contain no ridges that exceed 1/8 inch, and slope misalignment greater than 1/8 inch over a 12‑foot length or a differential larger than 1/16‑inch between adjacent sides of joints and cracks also constitute non‑compliance. Localized depressions resulting from subsidence, edge slump, or other causes may be present but are excluded from this requirement when approved. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–399)

During service the texture must be monitored by testing macrotexture depth; the specification requires a minimum mean texture depth of 0.040 in for radii under 50 ft, with at least four test locations per lane and additional checks every 50 ft if the measured MTD is below that value. When the MTD falls short, the pavement must be mechanically grooved perpendicular to traffic using rectangular grooves 1/8 in (± 1/32 in) wide, 1/8‑to‑3/16 in deep and spaced 1½ in center‑to‑center while avoiding joints. Preventive actions also include keeping the texturing device free of hardened concrete particles and halting placement if unsatisfactory texture results appear until the equipment is corrected. After texturing, routine monitoring must verify that joint and crack differentials do not exceed the maximum 1/16‑inch tolerance, that cross‑slope depressions or misalignments are no greater than 1/8 inch over a 12‑foot straightedge, and that the ground surface contains no ridges exceeding 1/8 inch; any non‑compliant area is required to be reground. Equipment blades should be inspected with a 4‑ft straightedge for bowing before each pass, slurry must be removed continuously, and the pavement cleaned before traffic reopens. Following completion of finishing and texturing, a curing material must be applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and left in place until the concrete reaches the minimum compressive strength required for opening to traffic as defined in Section 540.3(q); the comprehensive QC plan also requires monitoring of concrete temperature throughout placement and curing to provide early detection of conditions that could affect texture performance.
