Texturing

Part of: Skid Resistance is-subtopic-of
Texturing is a topic under Skid Resistance.
texturingpavement-surface-texturemacrotexture
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Texturing—whether executed as diamond grooving or as part of a Next Generation Concrete Surface (NGCS) treatment—creates macro‑texture on concrete pavements by cutting deeper, regularly spaced channels that allow water to escape from beneath tires, thereby reducing skidding, hydroplaning and wet‑weather crashes while also increasing friction, lateral stability and, in the case of NGCS, lowering road noise[1][2]. The process is applied to concrete slabs that contain a coarse aggregate, have a positive cross‑slope for drainage, and are still plastic; a self‑propelled machine equipped with a turf drag, broom or spring‑steel tine creates longitudinal grooves typically one‑eighth inch wide and one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths inch deep, spaced half to three‑quarters inch apart[2]. Acceptance hinges on achieving a minimum mean texture depth of 0.040 inches as measured by ASTM E965 across multiple locations, with any deficiency requiring additional mechanical grooving to meet that macro‑texture criterion[1][2].

Purpose and applicability

Texturing—whether performed as diamond grooving or as part of a Next Generation Concrete Surface treatment—creates macro‑texture on concrete pavements by cutting deeper channels that allow water to escape from beneath tires, which reduces the chances of skidding, hydroplaning and wet‑weather crashes while increasing pavement friction and lateral stability. The same roughened surface also satisfies the required macro‑texture depth, specifically achieving a minimum Mean Texture Depth of 0.040 inches for lanes whose horizontal curvature radius is less than 50 ft; if this depth is not attained the pavement is deemed deficient in macrotexture and would fail the acceptance criteria. In addition, the NGCS application of texturing is intended to lower road noise, addressing the problem of excessive traffic sound. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 93–94); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–367)

Texturing is appropriate for concrete pavements that require improved friction, drainage, or noise reduction; diamond grinding should be used when the surface needs re‑profiling, rut removal, texture restoration, or a quieter ride, and total grinding is preferred unless an isolated low spot would demand excessive grinding of surrounding pavement [1]. Diamond grooving is applied to provide macrotexture for water escape, with longitudinal grooves being the economical choice for most situations and transverse grooves reserved only where additional braking traction is needed; grooving may follow diamond grinding if ride quality must first be addressed [1]. Next Generation Concrete Surface (NGCS) texturing—flush grinding combined with longitudinal grooving—is appropriate on new or existing pavements where road‑noise concerns exist, the aggregate is polishing‑susceptible, or a full‑pavement texture is desired, but conventional diamond grinding must first remove any surface irregularities greater than 1/4 inch before NGCS can be performed [1]. Texturing is also appropriate after floating, straightedge testing and surface corrections have been completed, once excess moisture has disappeared and before the concrete becomes non‑plastic; at that point a self‑propelled machine with a turf drag or broom creates longitudinal grooves parallel to the centerline, while transverse texture may be applied on shoulders, gore areas, sidewalks or any pavement specifically approved by the Representative [2]. Hand‑applied texturing may be used only where self‑propelled equipment cannot reach such as small, irregular or confined zones, or when the equipment breaks down [2]. If the measured mean texture depth is less than 0.040 inches, the specification requires mechanically grooving the deficient surface rather than relying on standard texturing, and placement must be stopped until the equipment problem is corrected if unsatisfactory results are obtained [2]. For concrete pavement patches that are not being overlaid, texturing is required for Type A and B patches so that the patch surface corresponds to the texture of the surrounding pavement; when the existing pavement has been diamond ground, the patch should be textured longitudinally [2]. However, if the contract includes grinding of the pavement surface, matching wheel ruts and any texturing of the patch surface is not necessary because grinding replaces the need for a separate texturing step [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 93–94); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–402)

Required inputs

Texturing of concrete pavements is contingent upon the original concrete mix, specifically requiring a coarse aggregate and natural sand as defined in the mix design, and the hardness of that coarse aggregate influences the selection of texturing method, with polishing‑susceptible aggregates leading to longitudinal grooving or next‑generation concrete surface (NGCS) treatment. [1] The work must be performed on pavements that provide a positive cross slope so that water can drain from the grooves created by the texture. [1] When an isolated low spot is encountered, grinding to meet the low spot is not required if doing so would demand excessive grinding of the surrounding pavement. [1] For NGCS applications any surface irregularities exceeding one‑quarter inch must be removed by conventional diamond grinding before the texturing operation can commence. [1] Under the Pennsylvania specifications, initial texturing is to be carried out only after floating, straightedge testing and all required surface corrections have been completed, once all excess moisture has disappeared and while the concrete remains plastic (i.e., before it becomes non‑plastic). [2] The texture‑producing device may be a turf drag, broom, or spring‑steel tine mounted on self‑propelled equipment that maintains continuous contact, down pressure and constant travel speed, and the device must operate between three inches and five inches from the pavement edge; no water or monomolecular film may be added to the concrete surface during this operation. [2] The longitudinal grooves produced shall be one‑eighth inch wide (± one‑thirty‑second inch), with a depth ranging from one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths inch and spaced center‑to‑center between one half and three quarters inch; transverse grooves, when used, shall be rectangular, three‑thirty‑seconds to three‑sixteenths inch wide and one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths inch deep. [2] For pavements whose horizontal curvature radius is less than fifty feet the finished surface must achieve a mean texture depth of at least zero point zero four zero inches as measured by ASTM E965. [2] The final texturing operation requires a power‑driven, self‑propelled machine weighing no less than forty thousand pounds and equipped with a diamond grinding head at least four feet wide; the blade stack shall include one‑eighth inch grinding blades spaced one‑thirty‑second inch apart together with grooving blades that are one‑quarter to three‑eighths inch taller, producing longitudinal grooves approximately one half inch center‑to‑center and one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths inch deep. [2] The process shall be performed longitudinally with a maximum differential of one‑sixteenth inch across joints or cracks, cross‑slope deviations not greater than one‑eighth inch in twelve feet, and a feather pass extending at least two feet onto shoulders (minimum one hundred foot length) to preserve drainage; any area exceeding these tolerances must be reground. [2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 93–94); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–390)

Design for diamond grinding and grooving shall reference the appropriate sections of Publication 408—Section 514 for grinding, Section 510 for longitudinal grooves, and Section 517 for transverse grooves, and the construction plans must clearly show grinding or grooving limits together with transition or stop lines at bridges and ramps. A positive cross‑slope is required to provide drainage for both pavement and shoulder areas. When next‑generation concrete surface (NGCS) is used it is recommended to employ conventional diamond grinding to remove any significant roughness, correct faulting, or surface irregularities greater than ¼ inch prior to texturing. Longitudinal grooving is preferred because it is less time‑consuming, while transverse grooving may be specified only where braking traction is needed. For mechanically textured concrete pavements the design calls for conventional diamond‑ground surfaces identified as 408/514 and longitudinally diamond‑grooved surfaces identified as 408/510, with grooves spaced at 3/4 inch center‑to‑center. Any concrete slurry generated during texturing must be removed in accordance with Publication 408 Section 104.06 to prevent slurry from entering drainage structures or waterways; this establishes a pre‑construction procedural control. For slip‑form concrete pavements the texturing operation is performed after final finishing using a spring‑steel tine device that shall be positioned between three and five inches from the pavement edges. The device creates parallel grooves whose width shall be one‑eighth inch (± one‑thirty‑second inch), depth shall be between one‑eighth and three‑sixteenths inch, and nominal center‑to‑center spacing shall be from one‑half to three‑quarters inch. Where transverse texture is required on shoulders or other narrow areas the grooves shall be rectangular, with width ranging from three‑thirty‑seconds to three‑sixteenths inch and depth from one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths inch, applied in a single pass without overlap. For pavements having a horizontal curvature radius of less than fifty feet the finished surface must achieve a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 inch; this shall be verified by ASTM E965 testing on a minimum of four locations per lane and, if any location falls below the value, additional tests shall be made every fifty feet. For next‑generation concrete surfaces the texturing is achieved with a self‑propelled diamond grinding machine that must weigh at least forty thousand pounds and carry a grinding head at least four feet wide. The blade stack shall employ one‑eighth‑inch blades spaced one‑thirty‑second inch apart for the ground surface together with longitudinal grooving blades that are one‑quarter to three‑eighths inch taller, producing grooves one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths inch deep and spaced about one‑half inch center‑to‑center. The grinding operation shall limit joint differentials to not more than one‑sixteenth inch, restrict slope deviations to not exceed one‑eighth inch in twelve feet as measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge, keep overlap between passes to no more than one inch while providing at least a two‑foot feather pass of minimum one hundred foot length for drainage. Prior to full production a three‑hundred‑foot single‑lane test section shall be constructed and approved; the completed surface shall have ridges not exceeding one‑eighth inch and shall be textured over at least ninety‑eight percent of the pavement. For concrete pavement patches the texture shall correspond to that of the surrounding pavement, and when the existing surface has been diamond ground the patch texture shall be applied longitudinally. The finished patch surface shall be evaluated with a twelve‑foot straightedge and any high points or depressions of three‑sixteenths inch or more shall be corrected by grinding before the patch is opened to traffic. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 93–95); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–457)

Equipment and personnel

Preparation and placement rely on a fully energized self‑propelled slip‑form paving machine with side forms, automatic alignment controls and vibrating or tamping elements that run continuously. After the concrete is placed it is struck off and consolidated by a finishing machine equipped with a rubber‑tired wheel spreader and transverse screed, with optional rigid manual screeds and mechanical floats for floating. The final texture is applied while the surface is still plastic using self‑propelled texturing equipment carrying turf drags, brooms or spring‑steel tine devices (hand methods may be approved) to produce the required longitudinal or transverse grooves. Curing is accomplished by applying a monomolecular film or liquid membrane with a self‑propelled mechanical spreader or an atomizing spray system that includes a tank agitator and hood, and protective sheets or burlap are placed over the surface and sides as needed. Surface tolerances and macrotexture are verified with a 12‑foot straightedge and ASTM E965 macrotexture testing equipment, while tie‑bar pull‑out capacity is checked using a center‑pull hydraulic jack, load gauge and bearing ring. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–367)

Texturing must be performed by operators who can run self‑propelled texturing machines equipped with external alignment control, keeping the texture device in continuous contact with the concrete, applying positive elevation and down pressure, and maintaining a constant travel speed to produce uniform grooves. When pavement width or site conditions prevent use of such equipment, personnel may employ approved hand‑texturing methods. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–367)

Work sequence

For any mechanically textured concrete pavement work, including conventional diamond grinding, diamond grooving, or Next Generation Concrete Surface, the existing pavement must first be subjected to conventional diamond grinding to remove significant roughness, correct faulting, and eliminate surface irregularities greater than 1/4 inch, establishing the required ride‑quality baseline before any subsequent texturing can occur. Only after that preparatory grind is completed may longitudinal or transverse grooving be cut, and NGCS may then be performed either in a single‑pass (combined grind‑groove) or two‑pass (grind then groove) operation. Prior to commencing any mechanically textured concrete pavement activity, all concrete slurry and residue must be removed and managed in accordance with Publication 408 specifications; the slurry may not be discharged into drainage structures, wetlands, waterways, or the Department’s right‑of‑way and must be disposed of as directed in Section 104.06. For slip‑form paving (Section 501.3(g)), the concrete must first be struck off and consolidated, then floated, followed by straightedge testing and correction of any low or high spots; only after those operations are finished, all excess moisture has disappeared, and while the concrete remains plastic may the initial texture be placed with a turf‑drag or broom device to produce striations parallel with the centerline. For Next Generation Concrete Surface (Section 509.3), a 300‑foot test section must be constructed in a single lane to demonstrate that the equipment and procedures will provide an acceptable surface, and work cannot proceed until that test section has been approved; the requirement may be waived only with written approval from the District Executive. For bonded concrete overlays (Section 545.3), the existing pavement must first undergo complete surface preparation consisting of scarification or milling followed by shot‑blasting or water‑blasting to create a roughened surface, thorough removal of all loose debris, and protection from oil or grease; joint backing or approved filling material must be installed within ±¼ inch of the surface in every existing transverse joint, the concrete overlay must already be placed and finished, and its temperature reduced to less than 120°F using fog‑misting, covers or blankets as required before any finishing and texturing operations may begin. For concrete pavement patching (Section 516.3), the patch surface must first be finished to duplicate the existing pavement cross‑section; Type A and B patches must reproduce any wheel ruts, while Type C patches must have wheel ruts tapered to a straight cross‑slope with a minimum transition length of 20 feet, and only after this final surface finish is completed may the patch be textured to correspond with the surrounding pavement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 93–95); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–457)

Under the slip‑form paving method described in Section 501.3(g), after concrete placement the crew strikes off and consolidates the slab, floats any open‑textured areas, and then uses a 12‑foot straightedge to test and correct low or high spots while the surface remains plastic. Once excess moisture has disappeared and before the concrete becomes non‑plastic, an initial texture is applied with a turf drag or broom to produce longitudinal striations parallel with the centerline. A self‑propelled spring‑steel tine device is then run within three to five inches of the edges to cut parallel grooves that are 1/8 inch (± 1/32) wide, 1/8 to 3/16 inch deep and spaced 1/2 to 3/4 inch center‑to‑center. For shoulders, gore areas or other narrow sections a transverse texture is applied in one pass without overlap using rectangular grooves that are 3/32 to 3/16 inch wide and 1/8 to 3/16 inch deep. The finished surface is then checked for macrotexture; if the mean texture depth is less than 0.040 inch per ASTM E965, additional perpendicular grooving of 1/8 inch (± 1/32) width, 1/8 to 3/16 inch depth and 1½‑inch spacing is performed. For Next Generation Concrete Surface (NGCS) texturing the contractor may either perform a single‑pass operation that combines diamond grinding and grooving in one pass, or follow a two‑pass operation that first diamonds the pavement and subsequently grooves it. Texturing begins with diamond grinding the concrete pavement using diamond‑cutting equipment to re‑profile the surface and, when ride quality is a concern, to remove any significant roughness, correct faulting, or surface irregularities greater than 1/4 inch before further treatment. Under the next‑generation concrete surface procedure in Section 509.3, a power‑driven, self‑propelled machine equipped with diamond blades that weighs at least 40,000 lb and has a grinding head no less than 4 ft wide is used; the pavement is ground either in a single‑pass or two‑pass operation as determined by equipment capability. For a single‑pass the operator stacks 1/8‑inch grinding blades separated by 1/32‑inch spacers on the 4‑ft head and adds grooving blades that are 1/4 inch to 3/8 inch taller, spaced about ½ inch center‑to‑center, producing longitudinal grooves 1/8‑inch to 3/16‑inch deep while ensuring no overlap greater than 1 inch and maintaining a maximum 1/16‑inch differential at joints and cracks. For a two‑pass the pavement is first ground with the same blade stack and then the grooves are created in a second pass using 1/8‑inch wide blades of the same depth and spacing. After grinding, a feather pass extending a minimum of 2 feet onto adjacent shoulders or ramps is performed for drainage, with a minimum length of 100 ft, while continuously removing slurry so it does not flow across traffic lanes. A 300‑ft test section is constructed for approval before full work, at least 98 % of the pavement surface must be verified to be textured, and ride quality is confirmed to meet the maximum IRI of 70 in/mile when posted speeds exceed 45 mph or 90 in/mile when they are 45 mph or less. The source specifies that grooving should occur after diamond grinding if diamond grinding is needed for ride quality. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 93–94); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–390)

Within the slip‑form paving method for plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 501), the specification requires that after the slab has been floated, straightedge‑tested and any surface corrections made, all excess moisture must have disappeared before the initial texturing operation is started, and the texturing must be applied while the concrete is still plastic, i.e., before it becomes non‑plastic. In the same context, a 12‑foot straightedge test must be performed while the concrete remains plastic and any low spots must be corrected immediately before proceeding to strike‑off, consolidation or refinish. For bonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545), once finishing and texturing are completed, curing material must be applied within 30 minutes of the dissipation of bleed water, and the final surface cleaning performed in front of the paver may not occur earlier than 30 minutes before the concrete overlay/inlay is placed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–457)

Staging and logistics

For slip‑form paving under Section 501, texturing shall be performed after floating, straightedge testing and surface corrections have been completed, when all excess moisture has disappeared but before the concrete becomes non‑plastic. The tine or broom must stay within 5 inches but not closer than 3 inches of the pavement edge, so sufficient lane width is required; confined zones may only be textured by hand methods. The operation should occur during natural‑light hours unless an acceptable lighting system is provided. Any imminent rain requires stopping paving and protecting the surface before texturing can proceed. For next‑generation concrete surfaces, a power‑driven, self‑propelled grinding machine that weighs at least 40,000 pounds and is equipped with a grinding head no less than 4 feet wide must be used, therefore the work site must provide sufficient clearance for this heavy, wide equipment. Proper drainage is mandated by making a feather pass a minimum of 2 feet onto adjacent shoulders, gore areas, auxiliary lanes or ramp lanes. Slurry generated during diamond grinding must be removed continuously so it does not flow across occupied traffic lanes or into gutters or other drainage facilities. For bonded concrete overlays, texturing must be scheduled when the concrete temperature at placement is between 65 °F and 90 °F and the prepared surface is below 120 °F before the overlay is placed. If forecasts call for air temperatures of 45 °F or less, insulating blankets must be provided to prevent rapid heat loss; they may be removed only when the air temperature exceeds 45 °F or when the average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, provided the surface temperature does not change more than 40 °F in any one‑hour period. All standing water must be drained or removed before final cleaning, which has to occur no more than 30 minutes prior to placement so that oil, grease or other deleterious material cannot contaminate the textured surface. The site must accommodate a self‑propelled mechanical scarifier and shot‑blasting or water‑blasting equipment to uniformly remove the existing surface. During mechanical texturing, concrete slurry generated by diamond grinding raises surface‑water pH to 10 or 11 and creates turbidity; consequently management shall include measures to prevent the slurry from entering drainage structures, stormwater control structures, wetlands or waterways. The slurry is not to be wasted within the Department’s right‑of‑way, and removal of the slurry or residue is required by Publication 408 specifications. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (p. 95); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–457)

Quality control and acceptance

For slip‑form paving under Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, texturing is performed after floating and straightedge testing while the concrete is still plastic using a spring steel tine device mounted on self‑propelled equipment with external alignment control, maintaining constant down pressure and travel speed and keeping the device between 3 inches and 5 inches from the pavement edge. The longitudinal texture must produce parallel grooves that are 1/8 inch (± 1/32 inch) wide, 1/8 to 3/16 inch deep, with a nominal center‑to‑center spacing of 1/2 to 3/4 inch. Where transverse texture is required, the grooves must be rectangular, 3/32 inch to 3/16 inch wide and 1/8 to 3/16 inch deep across the full width. The finished surface also has to meet a minimum mean texture depth of 0.040 inches as measured by ASTM E965 in at least four locations per lane, with additional tests every 50 feet if any measurement is less than 0.040 inches and corrective mechanical grooving performed to the same longitudinal groove dimensions. Inspection of the texturing operation under Section 509—Next Generation Concrete Surface must verify that the grinding equipment meets the minimum weight of 40,000 lb and a minimum 4‑ft head width, that all blades are straight across the contact surface using a 4‑ft straightedge with no bowing, that the differential at joints or cracks does not exceed 1/16‑inch, and that cross‑slope deviations are not greater than 1/8 inch over a 12‑ft straightedge. The feather pass must extend at least 2 ft onto adjacent shoulders and be at least 100 ft long, while the longitudinal grooves must be spaced about 0.5 in center‑to‑center, be 1/8‑ to 3/16‑inch deep (and 1/8‑inch wide in two‑pass work), and ridges may not exceed 1/8 inch; at least 98 % of the surface must meet these texture criteria. Finally, ride quality is tested by IRI, which shall not exceed 70 in/mile where posted speeds are greater than 45 mph and shall not exceed 90 in/mile where speeds are 45 mph or less. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–390)

After the final texturing operation, macro‑texture depth is measured in accordance with ASTM E965 while a Representative is present; at least four test locations are required for each lane. If any measured mean texture depth (MTD) is found to be less than 0.040 inches, further measurements must be taken every 50 feet to locate the extent of the deficient macro‑texture. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–367)

Under the slip‑form paving method specified in Section 501, the final texturing shall be produced with a spring‑steel tine device that creates longitudinal grooves no closer than three inches and no farther than five inches from the pavement edge; each groove shall be one‑eighth inch (± one‑thirty‑second inch) wide, one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths inch deep, and spaced one‑half to three‑quarters inch center‑to‑center. The finished surface must achieve a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 inches; if testing shows an MTD below this value, additional tests are required and the pavement must be mechanically grooved with rectangular cuts that meet the same width and depth tolerances and are spaced one‑and‑a‑half inch center‑to‑center while observing joint clearance limits. When any texture result is unsatisfactory, concrete placement shall be stopped until equipment deficiencies are corrected, correction methods must be submitted to and approved by the District Executive, and defective pavement must be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department.

For next‑generation concrete surfaces in Section 509, the pavement shall be accepted only if the differential between adjacent sides of joints and cracks does not exceed one‑sixteenth inch, the cross‑slope misalignment is no greater than one‑eighth inch over a twelve‑foot straightedge measured perpendicular to the centerline, ridges on the ground surface do not exceed one‑eighth inch, and at least ninety‑eight percent of the surface is textured. Any area that exceeds these tolerances is considered non‑conforming and must be reground, with slurry removed before traffic is allowed.

In Section 545, the final finish for a bonded concrete overlay must meet edge‑slump limits such that the maximum edge slump in the outside six inches next to the shoulder is one‑quarter inch and the maximum edge slump in the outside six inches next to an adjacent lane is one‑eighth inch. If the overlay fails these limits or exhibits other nonconforming conditions such as improper depth, excessive air content, insufficient bond strength, rain‑induced surface defects, honeycombing, or any finish that cannot be repaired, it is considered defective and must be removed and replaced in accordance with Section 545.4(c) Table A and the replacement procedures of Section 501.3(t), with a minimum removal length of six feet between transverse joints.

For concrete pavement patches covered by Section 516, the surface must be textured to match the surrounding pavement, and any deviation measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge may not exceed three‑sixteenths inch in the longitudinal direction or across patch joints; high points or depressions greater than that must be corrected by grinding at no additional cost, retested, and if the surface still exceeds the limit the area is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department. [2]

Safety and environmental controls

Texturing of concrete pavements generates a wet concrete slurry whose alkalinity can raise the pH of surface water to 10 or 11 and produce turbidity, creating an environmental hazard for the public if the material reaches drainage structures, wetlands, or waterways [1]. To protect workers who handle this alkaline material, the procedure mandates that all slurry or residue be removed in accordance with Publication 408 specifications and disposed of as directed in Section 104.06, and it expressly prohibits wasting slurry within the Department’s right‑of‑way [1]. During the diamond‑grinding operation the slurry must be continuously removed; allowing it to flow onto lanes that are occupied by traffic or into gutters or other drainage facilities is forbidden because such discharge could endanger motorists, so the pavement must be cleaned before reopening to traffic [2]. The specification does not identify additional worker‑specific hazards beyond those associated with handling the alkaline slurry [2].

Curing, protection, and handoff

In the Slip‑Form Paving Method described in Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, after the final texturing is completed the curing materials must be applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and must be left in place until the slab reaches the strength required for opening to traffic as defined in Section 501.3(q); the same curing method shall be used throughout the work unless the Representative directs a change, and any of the approved methods—white polyethylene sheeting, burlap‑backed covers, PAMS liquid membrane, or an intermediate monomolecular film applied as an intermediate cure—may be employed provided they are maintained without marring the surface. In Section 545—Bonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfaced Pavements, after finishing and texturing the overlay the curing materials must also be applied within 30 minutes after bleed water has dissipated and must remain until the pavement attains the strength required for traffic opening. When the forecast air temperature is 45 °F or less, insulating blankets shall be placed to prevent rapid heat loss and may be removed only when the temperature rises above 45 °F or when the average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, provided the surface temperature does not change more than 40 °F in any one‑hour period. A curing compound is then spread uniformly over all exposed concrete at a minimum rate of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 square feet, forming an opaque white film. In Section 516—Concrete Pavement Patching, after the surface has been textured to match the surrounding pavement the patch is covered with a liquid‑membrane forming curing compound as required by Section 501.3(l); a Type 2 white pigmented curing compound may be used in lieu of the standard white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene product. The work must also be protected from rain in accordance with Section 501.3(r) and rapid‑set repairs are cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, after which the Representative will permit traffic only when cylinder tests show a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi as specified by PTM No. 604. Under the slip‑form paving method for plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 501, 501.3(g)), the Contractor may open the textured pavement to construction equipment or traffic provided that second‑stage sawing has not been performed, any joints that have been widened are sealed and a backer rod is installed and maintained near the surface, the concrete has attained its initial strength, all curing requirements are satisfied, and the equipment used conforms to vehicle code limits. For bonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545, 545.3), curing materials must be applied after finishing and texturing operations within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and must remain on the surface until the pavement reaches the required compressive strength of 3,600 pounds per square inch and meets the minimum bond‑strength criteria in Section 545.3(e); only then may construction traffic be permitted or the pavement be opened to users as determined by the Representative. Expected performance and maintenance

During slip‑form paving, if the final texturing operation produces unsatisfactory groove uniformity the specification requires that concrete placement be stopped until the equipment deficiency or malfunction is corrected. In addition, macrotexture must achieve a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 inches; when testing shows an MTD less than 0.040 inches the contractor must conduct additional tests every 50 feet to determine the extent of deficient macrotexture and mechanically groove the deficient area perpendicular to the roadway at no additional cost to the Department. With next‑generation concrete surface (NGCS) texturing, use of a power‑driven self‑propelled grinder that does not meet the minimum 40,000‑pound weight and 4‑foot head width can cause spalls at joints or cracks and fracture of surface aggregates. The process must keep any fault at joints or cracks within a maximum 1/16‑inch differential between adjacent sides, and slope misalignment may not exceed 1/8 inch in 12 feet as measured with a straightedge. Depressed pavement areas that arise from subsidence, edge slump, or other localized causes are considered distressed areas and must be excluded unless specifically approved. After the finishing and texturing steps of a bonded concrete overlay, failures may appear as surface defects resulting from rain, an improper final finish, or honey‑combing, and edge slump that exceeds the allowable limits—more than 1/4 inch in the outer six inches next to a shoulder or more than 1/8 inch in the outer six inches next to an adjacent lane. Moreover, any condition that renders the overlay defective in depth, air content, or bond strength is regarded as a failure of the texturing treatment. [2]

For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 501), macrotexture depth shall be tested according to ASTM E965 in the presence of the Representative at a minimum of four locations per lane; if any determined MTD is less than 0.040 inches, additional tests shall be conducted every 50 feet to determine the extent of deficient macrotexture and the deficient pavement must be mechanically grooved perpendicular to the roadway at no additional cost using the specified groove dimensions; unsatisfactory texturing results shall require stopping concrete placement until the equipment deficiency or malfunction is corrected; when diamond‑grinding surface corrections are made in hardened concrete for smoothness, no additional texturing is required; cracks or spalls that appear before final inspection must be repaired according to Table A at no cost to the Department. For next‑generation concrete surfaces (Section 509), the contractor shall monitor joint and crack differentials so that the differential between adjacent sides does not exceed 1/16‑inch, shall verify cross‑slope with a 12‑foot straightedge so that any depression does not exceed 1/8‑inch in 12 feet, and shall provide a feather pass at least 2 feet onto shoulders to preserve drainage; any area that fails these checks shall be reground as a repair; after grinding the surface shall be cleaned of slurry before traffic is allowed and the final texture shall cover at least 98 % of the pavement with ridges not exceeding 1/8‑inch. For bonded concrete overlays (Section 545), curing material shall be applied within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and shall remain in place until the concrete reaches the required strength; the prepared surface shall then be monitored by a 7‑day bond‑strength test per ASTM C1583 and the area shall be deemed defective if the average of three tests falls below 175 psi or any individual result is under 160 psi, which shall trigger additional testing and, when necessary, removal and replacement of the deficient overlay segment.
