Permeability

Part of: Subsurface Drainage is-subtopic-of
Permeability is a topic under Subsurface Drainage.
permeability
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

Permeability is the capacity of pavement materials—whether granular base or sub‑base aggregates or cementitious mixes—to permit water, or in the case of concrete, chloride ions, to pass through their internal void structure[1][2]. In granular layers this capacity derives from relatively large inter‑particle voids that enable rapid gravity‑driven drainage and suppress capillary rise, while in concrete it is quantified by the measured chloride‑ion penetration after a 56‑day (or less) cure using AASHTO T 277 or T 358, with required classifications of Low, Very Low, or Negligible (or a limited Moderate rating)[1][2]. The purpose is to remove moisture quickly so as to protect the subgrade and limit chloride‑induced deterioration, and it is defined by performance thresholds such as a void content of about 20 % ± 5 % and an infiltration rate exceeding 200 in/hr for pervious concrete, or a chloride‑penetration charge not exceeding 2,800 coulombs (or resistivity ≥15.6 kΩ·cm) when Moderate is permitted[1][2].

Material purpose and applicability

Within the Pavement Policy Manual’s drainage discussion, permeability is described as the ability of base and sub‑base materials to readily allow water to flow through them, a property that should be highly permeable so that gravity‑drainable water will be removed rapidly, helping keep moisture out of the underlying subgrade, reducing capillary rise, and supporting longer pavement life and more uniform performance. In the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications for cement concrete (Section 704), permeability is defined as the concrete’s susceptibility to chloride‑ion ingress, and its function is to limit that ingress by requiring the mix design to achieve a Low, Very Low, or Negligible chloride‑ion penetration rating after a curing period of 56 days or less, as measured by AASHTO T 277 or AASHTO T 358, thereby protecting the pavement from chloride‑induced deterioration. The same definition and functional requirement apply to thin bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements in Section 541 of the specifications, where permeability denotes the overlay’s ability to allow chloride ions to pass through, and the design must likewise achieve a Low, Very Low, or Negligible level of chloride‑ion penetration after 56 days or less according to AASHTO T 277 or AASHTO T 358 so that ingress of chlorides is limited and the durability of the concrete layer is protected. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 463), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–666)

Composition and characteristics

In section 2.3 DRAINAGE of the Pavement Policy Manual, permeability is defined by base and subbase materials that are highly permeable, meaning they readily allow water to flow through their granular structure [1]. The defining physical characteristic in that context is a relatively large void space between particles, which prevents capillary rise because the openings are too big to wick water upward [1]. Because permeability varies with particle‑size distribution, gradation is used as an indicator test, and particle shape can also influence the degree of flow [1]. In SECTION 704—CEMENT CONCRETE (c) Design Basis of the Pennsylvania DOT specifications, permeability of the specified concrete mix designs is expressed by the level of chloride‑ion penetration achieved after curing, measured with AASHTO T 277 (charge in coulombs) or AASHTO T 358 (resistivity in kΩ·cm) [2]. For AAAP and AA Slip Form or Fixed Form pavements the mixture must attain a “Low”, “Very Low” or “Negligible” classification within 56 days, while a “Moderate” rating is only permissible if the charge does not exceed 2,800 coulombs or the resistivity is at least 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4‑by‑8‑in cylinders (or 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6‑by‑12‑in cylinders) [2]. The permeability outcome depends on concrete constituents—including cement factor, supplementary cementitious materials such as slag, fly ash or silica fume, air‑entraining admixture to achieve the specified air content, and aggregate gradation—as defined in the mix design [2]. In SECTION 541—THIN BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF ASPHALT‑SURFACED PAVEMENT, permeability for the thin bonded concrete overlay is likewise defined by the mixture’s ability to limit chloride‑ion penetration as measured by AASHTO T 277 or T 358 after curing [2]. The concrete must be formulated with Portland cement, synthetic or steel macrofibers, and when the overlay depth is less than 3 inches, No. 67 coarse aggregate so that the resulting mix achieves a target classification of Low, Very Low, or Negligible chloride ion penetration within a 56‑day (or less) curing period [2]. If a Moderate classification is accepted, the measured charge passed must not exceed 2,800 coulombs, and the electrical resistance must not exceed 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4‑by‑8‑in cylinders or 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6‑by‑12‑in cylinders [2]. In SECTION 520—PERVIOUS CONCRETE PAVEMENT SYSTEM, permeability of a pervious concrete pavement is defined by its internal void structure, which must achieve a void content of 20% ± 5% [2]. The ability of water to flow through those voids must exceed 200 inches per hour as measured by ASTM C1701 [2]. The hardened density of the cured concrete also influences permeability; it must not exceed 130 pounds per cubic foot, with a tolerance of ± 5 pounds per cubic foot from the test‑section average [2]. Surface finishing operations are required to keep the voids open—steel troweling may be used only when the surface voids remain open and the density and infiltration criteria are met [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 419–463), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–666)

Permeability depends on the size and connectivity of voids, so changes in material composition, source characteristics, processing methods, or proportioning can alter how easily water flows through a base or subbase. [1] Even when gradation (particle‑size distribution) is held constant, differences such as particle shape may cause noticeable variations in permeability. [1] Designers sometimes sacrifice some drainability to obtain a more stable work platform while the pavement layers are being built. [1] In cement concrete, variations in the concrete constituents such as using different cement types, replacing Portland cement with allowable percentages of slag, fly ash, or silica fume, and sourcing aggregates from alternate suppliers can change the mix’s ability to satisfy the permeability requirement that the mixture achieve a target chloride ion penetration classified as Low, Very Low, or Negligible after a 56‑day or less curing period. [2] When the initial design does not meet this criterion, the specification permits furnishing other acceptable materials or making necessary changes in the mixing procedure and component proportions to conform to the required permeability performance. [2] If a Moderate level of chloride ion penetration is considered, it must not exceed 2,800 coulombs per AASHTO T 277 Table 1 or be at least 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4‑by‑8‑in cylinders (or 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6‑by‑12‑in cylinders) according to AASHTO T 358, ensuring that any compositional or processing adjustments still keep the permeability within these defined limits. [2] In pervious concrete pavement systems, variations in the concrete mix such as changes in aggregate source, water‑to‑cement ratio, or proportioning of coarse and fine aggregates directly alter the void structure, which the specification limits to a target of 20% ± 5%. [2] When the void content falls outside this band, the ability of water to flow through the pavement is reduced and infiltration rates may drop below the required performance. [2] Likewise, processing steps such as mixing speed, placement within 90 minutes of adding water, and the type of finishing equipment used affect compaction and thus the achieved density and voids, influencing whether the average infiltration measured on test sections can exceed 200 inches per hour as mandated. [2] Maintaining an undisturbed, minimally compacted subgrade also supports the intended permeability by preventing loss of surface voids. [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 419–421), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–666)

Key material properties

Permeability performance in pavement systems is governed first by the physical characteristics of the base and subbase aggregates; a particle‑size distribution that creates relatively large interparticle voids and favorable particle shape allows water to flow readily while those larger voids also suppress capillary rise. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifies that pervious concrete must achieve a void structure of 20% ± 5%, a hardened density not exceeding 130 pounds per cubic foot with a tolerance of ± 5 lb/ft³, and an infiltration capacity that must exceed 200 inches per hour for test sections constructed in accordance with the relevant section. Designers may adjust subbase slope to enhance gravity‑driven drainage, further improving the effective permeability of the pavement system. The material must be sufficiently stable for construction equipment, so some drainability may be sacrificed to provide a more robust work platform while the pavement layers are being built. In cementitious pavements the mixture is required to contain an air content of 6.0% in the plastic state (7.0% for AAAP and related pavings), a property that contributes to the interconnected pore network needed for water passage. Overdesign compressive strengths shall be at least 1,000 pounds per square inch, except for AAAP, AAAP LW, AA Paving, and ASC where the minimum is 500 pounds per square inch, ensuring that the structural capacity does not compromise the void architecture essential for permeability. The chemical durability of permeable concrete is addressed by targeting a chloride ion penetration classification of Low, Very Low, or Negligible after a curing period of 56 days or less, as measured by AASHTO T277 or AASHTO T358. A Moderate rating for chloride migration is permissible only if the measured charge does not exceed 2,800 coulombs on T277 or the electrical resistance does not exceed 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4‑inch by 8‑inch cylinders and 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6‑inch by 12‑inch cylinders on T358. Thermal considerations also affect permeability performance; pervious concrete shall not be placed if projected air temperatures are below 40 °F, and the pavement may not be opened to traffic until it has cured for at least 7 days with ambient temperatures exceeding 55 °F. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 419–463), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–666)

PennDOT characterizes the permeability of base and sub‑base aggregates qualitatively as the ability to readily allow water to flow through, noting that highly permeable aggregates provide rapid gravity drainage and reduce capillary rise; because direct field measurement of permeability is not easily performed, the agency relies on particle‑size distribution (gradation) as an indicator test to classify material permeability [1]. In cementitious pavement layers the permeability of the mixture is assessed by chloride‑ion penetration testing after a curing period that does not exceed 56 days, using either AASHTO T 277 or AASHTO T 358. The test outcomes are placed into one of four classes—Low, Very Low, Negligible, or Moderate—and Low, Very Low, and Negligible classifications are required, while a Moderate classification may be accepted only if the rapid‑chloride‑permeability charge does not exceed 2,800 coulombs, or if the resistivity is at least 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4‑in × 8‑in cylinders and 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6‑in × 12‑in cylinders [2]. The same chloride‑ion penetration criteria are applied to thin bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements, with the identical limits on charge and resistivity governing acceptance of a Moderate class [2]. For pervious concrete pavement systems permeability is quantified by infiltration testing performed in accordance with ASTM C1701; test sections must achieve an average infiltration rate that exceeds 200 inches per hour, and this minimum average applies to each 10 000 sq ft of installed concrete. Void content, measured according to ASTM C1754, is also reported, with a target void structure of 20 % ± 5 %, because it directly influences infiltration capacity [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 419–463), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–666)

Behavior and mechanisms

Base and subbase materials should be highly permeable so that gravity‑drainable water is removed rapidly, and the larger voids inherent in such mixes do not wick water upward by capillary action. Under construction loads the design may intentionally reduce drainability to obtain a more stable work platform, indicating that permeability can be compromised for load stability. Moisture‑related distress such as shrinkage or thermal cracking is not slowed by drainage, but effective permeability prevents moisture‑associated damage adjacent to those cracks. The placement of pervious concrete must be protected by strict construction controls; air temperatures projected to fall below 40 °F at any time during the 24‑hour period after placement prohibit placement. Rainfall before finishing and curing requires an immediate work stoppage, formation of a construction joint, and protective sheeting because rain can cause loss of mortar at the surface that jeopardizes void openness. After placement smooth steel‑wheeled rollers are required while pneumatic tire rollers are excluded to avoid sealing the void structure. The pavement cannot be opened to vehicular traffic until it has cured for at least 7 days during which the ambient temperature has exceeded 55 °F, ensuring that loading does not damage the permeable matrix. In‑service performance is verified by infiltration testing and any area that fails to meet a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour must be removed and replaced. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 419–421)

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), permeability in base and subbase materials is controlled primarily by physical mechanisms such as gravity‑driven flow of free water and capillary action that retains water in fine soils [1]. The same manual states that the mechanical characteristics of the aggregate—particle size distribution, shape, and resulting void geometry—determine how readily water can pass through; larger voids produced by well‑graded, highly permeable aggregates promote rapid drainage and suppress capillarity, whereas finer, poorly graded mixes impede flow [1]. The manual further notes that the slope of subbase layers mechanically influences the rate at which gravity can remove water, with steeper slopes increasing drainage speed [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification 408 (2026) specifies for pervious concrete that permeability is governed by its physical void structure, requiring a target void content of 20 % ± 5 % and an undisturbed, un‑compacted subgrade from which accumulated fines must be removed to keep the aggregate matrix open [2]. That specification also identifies mechanical actions during construction as controlling flow paths: only smooth steel‑wheeled rollers operated in static mode may be used, pneumatic tire rollers are prohibited, surface finishing must avoid sealing the voids, and the hardened density of the concrete shall not exceed 130 lb/ft³ to preserve porosity [2]. Finally, the Pennsylvania specification mandates that permeability be verified by infiltration testing, with the average infiltration rate for test sections required to exceed 200 in/hr, confirming that the assembled pore network remains functional [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 419–421)

Durability and deterioration

Permeability can deteriorate when drain outlets become crushed or plugged, which impedes the flow of water through the drainage system [1]. Fine soils may retain water by capillary action that draws moisture upward and reduces effective void space, further limiting permeability [1]. Design compromises that sacrifice some drainability for a more stable construction platform also lower permeability [1]. Inadequate gradation may produce smaller inter‑particle openings that limit water movement [1]. These mechanisms can lead to insufficient removal of surface and subsurface water, increasing the risk of moisture‑related pavement distress such as rutting, loss of support, and frost heave [1]. In cement concrete mixes the principal durability concern for permeability is chloride ion penetration, and designs must achieve a target of Low, Very Low, or Negligible after a 56‑day or less curing period in accordance with AASHTO T 277 or T 358 [2]. If a Moderate rating is used it may only be accepted when the charge passed does not exceed 2,800 coulombs or when the resistivity meets at least 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑inch by 8‑inch cylinders and 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑inch by 12‑inch cylinders [2]. Excessive chloride ingress indicates higher permeability and can promote reinforcement corrosion [2]. Shrinkage that exceeds the specified limit of 500 microstrain could induce cracking that further compromises low‑permeability performance [2]. For pervious concrete pavement systems permeability can deteriorate when rainfall occurs before curing because the rain may cause loss of mortar at the surface; such loss is evaluated after curing and repaired or removed at the discretion of the Representative [2]. Permeability also declines if fines generated by saw‑cut construction joints are not removed, and the specification calls for those fines to be removed by vacuum to avoid clogging [2]. The use of pneumatic tire rollers is prohibited because they can seal the voids [2]. Accordingly, any pervious concrete that does not achieve a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour must be removed and replaced [2]. Curing must begin within 20 minutes and the surface shall be covered with polyethylene sheeting that is maintained for a minimum of 7 days to preserve permeability [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 419–421), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–666)

Materials that consist of coarse, well‑graded particles with larger voids tend to be less prone to permeability loss because water can flow rapidly by gravity and capillary wicking is avoided [1]. In contrast, fine soils or base layers with very small openings retain water as capillary moisture that can be drawn upward and impede drainage [1]. For pervious concrete, preserving a void structure of about 20 % ± 5 % helps maintain permeability, and an average infiltration rate that exceeds 200 inches per hour is required to meet performance criteria [2]. The use of smooth steel‑wheeled rollers rather than pneumatic tire rollers, and the avoidance of any finishing operation that seals surface voids, are construction practices that protect the intended void space [2]. Exposure conditions that increase susceptibility include high water tables intersecting the subbase, ponded surface water that infiltrates the pavement structure, and drain outlets that become crushed or plugged, all of which reduce the ability of water to escape [1]. Air temperatures projected to fall below 40 °F during the first 24 hours after placement, or any rain occurring within that period, can cause surface mortar loss and accelerate permeability deterioration [2]. The accumulation of fine particles generated by saw‑cut joints, if not removed, can clog the voids and further diminish permeability [2]. Proper curing—covering the pavement with polyethylene for at least seven days while ambient temperatures exceed 55 °F—reduces the risk of deterioration [2]. Design measures such as maximizing subbase slope, providing adequate outlet spacing, and protecting drains from damage help maintain high permeability and limit deterioration [1].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 419–421)

Material interactions and compatibility

Permeability performance of cement concrete must consider chemical compatibility; sulfate attack is mitigated by designing the mix as sulfate‑resistant concrete per ACI 201.2, while chloride ingress that can lead to corrosion is limited by requiring a target chloride ion penetration classified as Low, Very Low, or Negligible after 56 days or less according to AASHTO T 277 or T 358. In addition, shrinkage restraint is controlled by limiting the 28‑day shrinkage to not exceed 500 microstrain (or up to 550 microstrain with approval). The source does not mention organics, clays, reactive aggregates, other contaminants, dissimilar materials, or further chemical attack beyond these specified risks. [2]

[2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–666)

Influence of production and placement

Within the cement‑concrete design of Section 704, proper mixing and curing directly control the measured permeability as expressed by chloride‑ion penetration. Trial mixtures are required for each concrete class; test specimens are molded and cured, and if the requirements of Table A cannot be achieved, necessary changes in the mixing procedure must be made before production. At the start of construction a full‑size batch is mixed with the type of mixer and the mixing procedure planned for the project and this batch provides the basis for final adjustment of the accepted design. The concrete mixture shall be designed to meet a target chloride‑ion penetration classified as Low, Very Low, or Negligible after a curing period of 56 days or less in accordance with AASHTO T 277 or AASHTO T 358. In the thin bonded concrete overlays covered by Section 541, the mixing stage must be controlled so that the resulting mix achieves the permeability criterion expressed by low chloride‑ion penetration; the design calls for a target of Low, Very Low, or Negligible after a curing period of 56 days or less. The district may also accept a Moderate penetration provided the measured values do not exceed 2,800 coulombs on AASHTO T 277 or the resistance limits of 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑inch by 8‑inch cylinders and 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑inch by 12‑inch cylinders on AASHTO T 358, indicating that mix composition and curing duration directly govern permeability performance. For pervious concrete pavement systems described in Section 520, mixing, placement, compaction, finishing and curing are all required to preserve the void structure that governs permeability. The mix must be rotated at 70 to 100 revolutions and placed no later than 90 minutes after the final introduction of any mix water; the subgrade is wetted but not left with standing water and the concrete is spread without segregation. Compaction is limited to smooth steel‑wheeled rollers operated in static mode for one to four passes, and pneumatic‑tire rollers shall not be used. The final surface finish is achieved with a maximum 2‑ton roller so that surface voids remain open; when these practices are followed the resulting void content stays near 20 % ± 5 % and infiltration rates above 200 in/hr can be attained. Curing must begin within 20 minutes of concrete discharge, the pavement surface must be completely covered with polyethylene sheeting, and curing is to be maintained for a minimum of 7 days, which develops strength while keeping the pore network intact. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 419–463), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–666)

Construction‑related defects that most affect long‑term permeability are those that reduce or block water flow through the base and subbase layers, such as crushing or plugging of base drain outlets which prevents the drainage system from functioning properly [1]. Using base or subbase materials with insufficient intrinsic permeability—because the gradation is inappropriate or particle shape limits void size—also limits gravity‑drainable flow [1]. In some cases permeability is deliberately reduced to obtain a more stable work platform during construction, which can compromise long‑term water movement [1]. For cement concrete pavements, variations that alter the air‑void structure or seal the intended void network most affect long‑term permeability; the hardened concrete must have a total air content between 3.5 % and 7.5 %, inclusive, and exceeding this range creates additional pathways for fluid movement [2]. The spacing factor must not exceed approximately 0.008 inch, and the specific surface must not fall below roughly 630 in²/in³; violations of these limits also generate extra flow paths [2]. Achieving the specified chloride‑ion penetration rating of Low, Very Low, or Negligible within a 56‑day cure is required, and exceeding 2,800 coulombs on AASHTO T 277 or failing to meet the prescribed kΩ·cm values on T 358 prevents meeting this threshold, thereby increasing permeability over time [2]. For pervious concrete pavement systems, long‑term permeability is most sensitive to failure to achieve a void structure of 20 % ± 5 %; finishing methods that close surface pores—such as the use of pneumatic tire rollers or excessive steel troweling—can seal surface voids and cause infiltration rates to fall below the required minimum of 200 in/hr [2]. Introducing fines, for example from saw‑cut joints that are not vacuumed out, or allowing fine material from a compacted subgrade to migrate into the pavement, fills the void network and degrades performance [2]. Curing must begin within 20 minutes of discharge and be maintained for at least 7 days; delayed or interrupted curing can cause mortar loss and surface sealing that reduces permeability [2].

Selection, specification, and improvement

Selection of permeability for base and sub‑base layers is guided by the need for materials that are highly permeable so that gravity‑drainable water can be removed rapidly, and because larger voids do not wick water upward, capillary rise is avoided [1]. The specification also calls for a balance between drainage performance and construction stability, allowing some reduction in drainability when a more stable work platform is required during pavement construction [1]. Because field measurement of permeability is difficult, PennDOT uses particle‑size distribution (gradation) as an indicator test to infer the material’s ability to transmit water [1]. For AAAP cement‑concrete mixtures used in Section 704 and for AA Slip Form and AA Fixed Form paving mixtures, permeability is expressed through a target chloride‑ion penetration that shall be classified as Low, Very Low, or Negligible after a curing period of 56 days or less, evaluated with AASHTO T 277 or AASHTO T 358 [2]. The same permeability criteria apply to thin bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavement in Section 541, which also must meet the Low/Very Low/Negligible target under the identical test methods and curing period [2]. When a Moderate classification is permitted, the mix must not exceed 2,800 coulombs on the AASHTO T 277 test and must have an AASHTO T 358 resistivity of at least 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑inch × 8‑inch cylinders or at least 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑inch × 12‑inch cylinders [2]. For pervious concrete pavement systems in Section 520, permeability is demonstrated by an average infiltration rate that must exceed 200 inches per hour when measured in accordance with ASTM C1701; any installed concrete that fails to meet this minimum must be removed and replaced [2]. The design void structure for the pervious concrete shall be about 20 % ± 5 %, and the hardened density of test sections is used as the basis of acceptance with a tolerance of ± 5 lb/ft³ and not to exceed 130 lb/ft³ [2].

Performance evaluation and implications

In PennDOT practice, because permeability is not easily measured in the field, a laboratory particle‑size distribution (gradation) test is used as the primary indicator of how readily water can flow through base and subbase materials when designing drainage systems [1]. For cement‑based pavements covered by Sections 704 and 541, permeability is evaluated in the laboratory by measuring chloride‑ion penetration on cured specimens that have been conditioned for 56 days or less, using either AASHTO T 277 or AASHTO T 358, and the mix must achieve a classification of Low, Very Low, or Negligible [2]. If a Moderate classification is permitted, the results may not exceed 2,800 coulombs according to Table 1 of AASHTO T 277, and the resistivity values must be at least 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑in × 8‑in cylinders or at least 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑in × 12‑in cylinders as specified in Table 1 of AASHTO T 358 [2]. The same sections do not prescribe any field or long‑term performance evidence for evaluating permeability of those cement‑based pavements [2]. For pervious concrete pavement systems described in Section 520, laboratory assessment includes determination of void structure with a target of 20 % ± 5 % and hardened density whose average must be within ±5 lb/ft³ and not exceed 130 lb/ft³, both measured according to ASTM C1754 [2]. Field verification is performed by constructing two test sections of at least 225 ft² each and conducting infiltration testing in accordance with ASTM C1701; the average infiltration rate for the two sections must be greater than 200 inches per hour [2]. Ongoing in‑place performance is monitored by additional ASTM C1701 infiltration tests on three locations for every 10,000 ft² of installed pavement, and any area that does not meet the minimum average rate of 200 in/hr shall be removed and replaced [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 419–463), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 663–666)

Drainage is identified as the most important consideration during field view, design, construction, and maintenance, so base and subbase materials should be highly permeable, allowing water to flow rapidly by gravity and preventing capillary wicking. This high permeability removes water from beneath the pavement surface, reducing loss of subgrade support and thus increasing the aggregate base’s resistance to traffic‑induced deflection while also limiting frost heave, rutting, and other moisture‑related distresses that would otherwise shorten pavement life and degrade ride quality. Designers may sacrifice some drainability to provide a stable work platform during construction, and because permeability is not easily measured in the field, gradation is used as an indicator even though similar gradations can produce different permeabilities. In the pervious concrete pavement system, permeability is governed by a void content of 20 % ± 5 % and a hardened density that may not exceed 130 lb/ft³ with a tolerance of ± 5 lb/ft³. These parameters are linked to an infiltration capacity that must be demonstrated by test sections achieving an average infiltration rate exceeding 200 inches per hour, and the same threshold triggers replacement if later in‑place testing falls below the minimum. Preservation of the prescribed void structure is achieved by using smooth steel‑wheeled rollers, maintaining an undisturbed subgrade, and protecting surface voids during finishing. Ongoing maintenance calls for periodic infiltration testing every 10 000 sq ft and removal or replacement of any sections that do not meet the minimum average infiltration rate. For cement concrete, the design basis requires that the concrete mixture be engineered so that chloride‑ion penetration measured after a curing period of 56 days or less meets the Low, Very Low, or Negligible category according to AASHTO T 277 or T 358, thereby protecting the pavement’s durability against chloride‑induced corrosion and reducing future maintenance needs. If a design yields only Moderate penetration, approval may still be granted provided the test results stay within the specified limits of not exceeding 2,800 coulombs on AASHTO T 277 or being greater than or equal to 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4‑by‑8 in cylinders and 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6‑by‑12 in cylinders on AASHTO T 358.
