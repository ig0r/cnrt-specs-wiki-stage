Permeability

Part of: Subsurface Drainage is-subtopic-of
Permeability is a topic under Subsurface Drainage.
permeability
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Permeability in pavement engineering is the ability of a material to readily transmit fluids through its internal void structure. In granular base and sub‑base layers this means that large inter‑particle voids created by an appropriate gradation allow gravity‑driven water to flow quickly, suppressing capillary rise and protecting the underlying subgrade from moisture‑related distress [1]. For cementitious pavements the term denotes the mix’s capacity to restrict chloride‑ion ingress, requiring a design that achieves a Low, Very Low or Negligible penetration rating after 56 days as measured by AASHTO T 277 (≤2 800 coulombs) or T 358 (minimum resistivity of 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4‑in × 8‑in cylinders and 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6‑in × 12‑in cylinders), while pervious concrete relies on a void content of about 20 % ±5 % that yields an infiltration capacity exceeding 200 in/hr [2].

Material purpose and applicability

Permeability in pavement engineering is described for different materials. For base and sub‑base layers, the Pavement Policy Manual (section 2.3 DRAINAGE) defines permeability as the ability of those materials to readily allow water to flow through them; when these layers are highly permeable they enable gravity‑drainable water to be removed rapidly, keep moisture out of the underlying subgrade, reduce capillary rise, and thereby support longer pavement life and more uniform performance. [1] In cement‑concrete addressed in SECTION 704—CEMENT CONCRETE of the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, permeability refers to the mixture’s ability to limit chloride ion penetration; the mix must be designed so that after a curing period of 56 days or less the chloride ion penetration is classified as Low, Very Low, or Negligible according to AASHTO T 277 or AASHTO T 358, and by meeting this permeability target the material helps control ingress of chlorides, protecting the concrete from corrosion‑related deterioration. [2] The same definition applies to the thin bonded concrete overlay covered by SECTION 541—THIN BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF ASPHALT‑SURFACED PAVEMENT, where the concrete mixture must be designed so that chloride ions penetrate only at a Low, Very low, or Negligible level after a 56 day or less curing period as verified by AASHTO T 277 or AASHTO T 358; this serves to control chloride ingress, protect the overlay from deterioration and satisfy the mix‑design acceptance criteria, although the DME/DMM may also accept a Moderate penetration level under certain conditions. [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 451), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–652)

Composition and characteristics

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, permeability in pavement drainage is defined by base and subbase materials that should be highly permeable, meaning they readily allow water to flow through their granular structure so that gravity‑drainable water will be removed rapidly [1]. The defining physical characteristic cited by the same manual is a relatively large void space between particles, which prevents capillary rise because the openings are too big to wick water upward [1]. Because permeability varies with particle‑size distribution, the manual notes that gradation (particle‑size distribution) is used as an indicator test, and it also acknowledges that particle shape can influence the degree of flow [1].

In the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, Section 704 defines the permeability of cement concrete by the level of chloride‑ion penetration achieved after a curing period of 56 days or less, as measured with AASHTO T 277 or AASHTO T 358; the mixture must be designed to attain a classification of Low, Very Low, or Negligible penetration [2]. If a Moderate rating is accepted, the test results may not exceed 2,800 coulombs on T 277 or must meet a resistivity of at least 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑in × 8‑in cylinders and 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑in × 12‑in cylinders on T 358, making the measured chloride‑ion resistance the defining physical characteristic [2].

Section 541 of the same Pennsylvania specifications applies an identical definition of permeability to a thin bonded concrete overlay, requiring the concrete mix’s ability to limit chloride‑ion migration after a curing period of 56 days or less to a Low, Very Low, or Negligible level as measured by AASHTO T 277 or T 358; the acceptable limits are not to exceed 2,800 coulombs per the T 277 test and not to exceed 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑in × 8‑in cylinders and 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑in × 12‑in cylinders per the T 358 test [2]. This performance is achieved with a Portland‑cement concrete that includes synthetic or steel macrofibers to meet the design residual strength f150, and when the overlay depth is less than 3 inches it uses No. 67 coarse aggregate [2].

Section 520 of the Pennsylvania specifications defines the permeability of a pervious concrete pavement system by its internal void structure; the mix must achieve a void content of 20 % ±5 % as measured by ASTM C‑1754 and, for test sections, must exceed an infiltration capacity of 200 inches per hour according to ASTM C‑1701 [2]. The material composition specified includes a coarse aggregate base over a minimum two‑inch fine‑aggregate layer wrapped in non‑woven geotextile, while the subgrade is excavated to remain undisturbed and un‑compacted so that its inherent permeability is preserved [2]. Density limits also constrain the mix; the hardened density of the pavement may not exceed 130 lb/ft³ with a tolerance of ±5 lb/ft³, ensuring that sufficient void space remains open after finishing [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 410–451), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–652)

Permeability of pavement layers is sensitive to any alteration in material composition, source characteristics, processing methods, or proportioning [1][2]. In the base and sub‑base context described by the Pavement Policy Manual (section 2.3 DRAINAGE), even when particle‑size distribution remains unchanged, differences such as particle shape may cause noticeable variations in permeability, and designers sometimes sacrifice some drainability to obtain a more stable work platform during construction [1]. For cement concrete pavements, the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications (section 704.1 GENERAL‑(c) Design Basis) state that variations in the cement factor, supplemental cementitious material type or amount, aggregate source, and mixing procedure directly influence permeability because the mix must be designed to achieve a target chloride‑ion penetration classified as Low, Very Low, or Negligible after a 56‑day (or less) cure according to AASHTO T 277 or T 358; if those targets cannot be met, the designer must furnish alternative acceptable materials or modify the mixing process to conform, and when aggregates are sourced consistently—e.g., AAAP with No. 57’s versus No. 8’s—repeat permeability testing may be waived provided the same aggregate source is used [2].

Key material properties

In the Pavement Policy Manual (section 2.3 DRAINAGE) permeability performance is said to depend primarily on the physical characteristics of the base and subbase aggregates, specifically a particle‑size distribution that creates relatively large interparticle voids and favorable particle shape, which allow water to flow readily and suppress capillary rise [1]. The manual also notes that designers may accept reduced drainability in order to obtain a more stable work platform for construction equipment, indicating a trade‑off between physical stability and permeability [1]. Additionally, the subbase slope is adjusted to enhance gravity‑driven drainage, further improving the effective permeability of the pavement system under the conditions described in that section [1]. The permeability of cementitious pavements is governed primarily by the chemical resistance of the mix to chloride ion ingress; the mixture shall achieve a Low, Very low, or Negligible penetration rating after a curing period of 56 day or less as measured by AASHTO T 277 or AASHTO T 358 [2]. Under AASHTO T 277 the charge passed must not exceed 2,800 coulombs, and under AASHTO T 358 the resistivity must not exceed 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑inch × 8‑inch cylinders or 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑inch × 12‑inch cylinders [2]. Adequate air content, targeted at 6.0 % in the plastic state and maintained between 3.5 % and 7.5 % after hardening, provides the pore structure needed for fluid movement [2]. A void structure of 20 % ± 5 % defines the size of the flow channels that convey water through the concrete matrix [2]. Mechanical performance is reflected by a 28‑day to 7‑day compressive strength ratio of at least 1.20 and by shrinkage that does not exceed 500 microstrain, both of which influence the stability of the permeable network under load and curing [2]. Hardened density shall be controlled within a tolerance of ±5 lb/ft³ and shall not exceed 130 lb/ft³ [2]. Infiltration capacity must exceed 200 in/hr as verified by ASTM C‑1701 testing, directly indicating the material’s ability to transmit water [2]. Placement and service are governed by thermal conditions that require air temperatures not fall below 40 °F during installation and ambient temperatures to have exceeded 55 °F before traffic is permitted after a minimum seven‑day cure [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 410–451), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–652)

Permeability in pavement base and subbase materials is described qualitatively as the ability to readily allow water to flow through, with highly permeable aggregates providing rapid gravity drainage and reducing capillary rise [1]. Because permeability is not easily measured in the field, PennDOT uses gradation (particle‑size distribution) as an indicator test to classify material permeability [1]. In cement concrete, permeability is evaluated by measuring chloride‑ion penetration after a curing period of fifty‑six days or less using AASHTO T 277 (charge in coulombs) or AASHTO T 358 (electrical resistivity) [2]. The concrete mix must be designed to achieve a target classification of Low, Very Low, or Negligible penetration [2]. If a Moderate level is accepted, the test results may not exceed 2,800 coulombs according to Table 1 of AASHTO T 277, and the resistivity must be greater than or equal to 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑inch × 8‑inch cylinders and greater than or equal to 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑inch × 12‑inch cylinders as specified in Table 1 of AASHTO T 358 [2]. Thin bonded concrete overlays are likewise evaluated by measuring chloride‑ion penetration after a curing period of fifty‑six days or less with either AASHTO T 277 or AASHTO T 358, and the overlay mix must be designed to meet the same target classification of Low, Very low, or Negligible [2]. When the AASHTO T 277 test is used, the charge passed may not exceed 2,800 coulombs; when the AASHTO T 358 test is used, the resistivity may not exceed 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑inch × 8‑inch cylinders or 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑inch × 12‑inch cylinders [2]. For pervious concrete pavement systems, permeability is quantified by infiltration testing performed in accordance with ASTM C 1701 on both laboratory test sections and field locations; the average infiltration rate must exceed 200 inches per hour for test sections and also meet a minimum average of 200 inches per hour for each ten‑thousand square‑feet area placed [2]. Void content is measured using ASTM C 1754 with a target void structure of 20 % ±5 % [2]. Hardened density is likewise determined by ASTM C 1754 with an acceptance tolerance of ±5 pounds per cubic foot and not to exceed 130 pounds per cubic foot [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 410–451), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–652)

Behavior and mechanisms

Permeability of base and subbase materials is intended to be high so that gravity‑drainable water is removed rapidly, which limits capillary rise because larger voids do not wick water upward. Under construction loads the design may intentionally reduce drainability to obtain a more stable work platform, indicating that permeability can be compromised for load stability. Moisture‑related distress such as shrinkage or thermal cracking is not slowed by drainage, but effective permeability prevents moisture‑associated damage adjacent to those cracks. Chemical exposure and other service conditions are not addressed in the source. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59)

For base and subbase layers in permeable pavements, permeability is controlled primarily by physical mechanisms such as gravity‑driven flow of free water and capillary action that retains water in fine soils [1]. The mechanical characteristics of the aggregate—particle size distribution, shape, and resulting void geometry—determine how readily water can pass through; larger voids produced by well‑graded, highly permeable aggregates promote rapid drainage and suppress capillarity, whereas finer, poorly graded mixes impede flow [1]. In addition, the slope of subbase layers mechanically influences the rate at which gravity can remove water, with steeper slopes increasing drainage speed [1]. In cement‑concrete pavements, permeability is governed primarily by the mix‑design constituents that create the pore structure, where the cement factor and any supplementary cementitious material (SCM) replacements dictate the chemical composition of the binder and the amount of air‑entraining admixture controls the physical air‑void system in both the plastic and hardened states [2]. These combined physical and chemical characteristics determine how readily chloride ions can penetrate, and the design must achieve a target of Low, Very Low, or Negligible penetration after a 56‑day (or less) curing period as specified by AASHTO T 277 or T 358 [2]. The total air content in the hardened concrete must be between 3.5 % and 7.5 %, inclusive, and the minimum cement factor listed in Table A must be used unless exceptions apply; Portland cement may be replaced with SCM(s) provided the maximum replacement by mass percentages in Table G, Prevention Level Z are not exceeded [2]. For pervious concrete pavement systems, permeability is governed chiefly by the physical void structure, and maintaining a void content around 20 % ± 5 % creates the interconnected pores that allow water to flow while keeping those surface voids open during finishing preserves the designed infiltration capacity [2]. Mechanical actions such as using smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode with one to four passes, avoiding pneumatic tire rollers, and limiting compaction of the subgrade by excavating to undisturbed soil all serve to retain the intended pore network, while removal of fines from construction joints prevents clogging that would otherwise reduce flow [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 410–411), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–652)

Durability and deterioration

Permeability may deteriorate when base drain outlets become crushed or plugged, which impedes water flow through the drainage system [1]. Fine soils can retain water by capillary action that draws moisture upward and reduces effective void space, further limiting permeability [1]. Design compromises that sacrifice some drainability to obtain a more stable construction platform also lower permeability [1]. Inadequate gradation may produce smaller inter‑particle openings that restrict water movement [1]. These mechanisms can lead to insufficient removal of surface and subsurface water, increasing the risk of moisture‑related pavement distress such as rutting, loss of support, and frost heave [1]. For pervious concrete, infiltration capacity can be compromised when fine sediments infiltrate the void structure, for example soils migrating from the detention basin if the bottom is not protected [2]. Fines generated by saw‑cut joints that are left in place also clog the voids [2]. Rainfall occurring before the concrete has cured may cause loss of surface mortar, further reducing infiltration capacity [2]. Equipment that seals the voids—such as pneumatic tire rollers or excessive steel troweling—diminishes permeability [2]. If clogging causes the measured infiltration rate to fall below the required 200 inches per hour, the concrete must be removed and replaced [2]. Maintaining proper subgrade preparation, protecting the detention base with a minimum of 2 inches of fine aggregate, and promptly vacuuming saw‑cut fines are essential actions to preserve designed infiltration performance [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 410–411)

Materials with coarse, well‑graded particles and larger voids are less prone to permeability loss because they allow water to flow rapidly by gravity and avoid capillary wicking. In contrast, fine soils or bases with very small openings retain water as capillary moisture that can be drawn upward and impede drainage. The void network in pervious concrete should be maintained at about 20 % ± 5 %, because preserving this open‑pore structure reduces susceptibility to permeability deterioration. Infiltration of fine materials into the pavement system, such as fines generated by saw‑cut joints, must be removed to avoid clogging and loss of infiltration capacity. High water tables that intersect the subbase increase susceptibility because they provide a source of moisture that can saturate the base and reduce drainage. Ponded surface water that infiltrates the pavement structure also raises the risk of permeability loss. Drain outlets that become crushed or plugged reduce the ability of water to escape, thereby increasing deterioration potential. Protecting drain outlets from crushing or plugging helps maintain high permeability and limit deterioration. Exposure to low air temperatures is a vulnerability; pervious concrete must not be placed if projected air temperature falls below 40 °F during the first 24 hours, because cold conditions can impair early strength development needed to resist mortar loss. Curing must be protected for a minimum of 7 days to develop sufficient strength that resists mortar loss and preserves permeability. Rainfall before final finishing can wash out mortar and reduce infiltration, so construction schedules should avoid rain events in that period. The pavement must achieve an average infiltration rate exceeding 200 inches per hour; values below this threshold indicate deterioration of permeability and trigger replacement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 410–411)

Material interactions and compatibility

Permeability of the cement concrete is controlled through the mix‑design constituents; the specified requirement is that the mixture achieve a Low, Very Low or Negligible chloride‑ion penetration after 56 days or less as measured by AASHTO T 277/T 358. Achieving this level depends on the selected cement factor and any supplementary cementitious material (SCM) replacements—slag cement must be at least 25 % of the cement, fly ash at least 15 %, and silica fume between 5‑10 %—as well as on aggregate gradation, absorption and moisture condition, and the air‑entraining admixture used to obtain the prescribed plastic‑state air content. For AA slip‑form and AA form paving the same permeability target applies, linking binder composition, aggregate characteristics and air content directly to the concrete’s ability to limit ion migration. [2]

[2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–652)

The source identifies two compatibility considerations for permeability: sulfate exposure requires the concrete to be designed as sulfate‑resistant in accordance with ACI 201.2, and shrinkage restraint is limited by a maximum 28‑day microstrain of 500 unless an exception up to 550 microstrains is approved; no other listed agents such as organics, clays, reactive aggregates, contaminants, dissimilar materials, corrosion or broader chemical attack are addressed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–652)

Influence of production and placement

In SECTION 704—CEMENT CONCRETE, achieving the specified permeability of the concrete depends on controlling the mix design and the curing duration; trial mixtures are made and, if the requirements cannot be achieved, changes in the mixing procedure must be altered or alternative materials used to satisfy the permeability criteria. The mixture is then required to reach a low, very low, or negligible chloride ion penetration after a 56‑day or less curing period as defined by AASHTO test methods, and proper curing therefore directly influences whether the concrete attains the intended permeable performance. In SECTION 541—THIN BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF ASPHALT‑SURFACED PAVEMENT, the mix design must be engineered to achieve low permeability as indicated by a target chloride ion penetration classified as Low, Very low, or Negligible, and this performance is evaluated after a curing period of up to 56 days; thus both the composition of the mixture and the duration of cure directly influence the final permeability. If a design yields only Moderate chloride ion penetration, it may still be accepted provided the measured values do not exceed the limits set by AASHTO T 277 (2,800 coulombs) or AASHTO T 358 (15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑inch × 8‑inch cylinders and 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑inch × 12‑inch cylinders). The source does not specify any effects of placement, compaction, or finishing on permeability. In SECTION 520—PERVIOUS CONCRETE PAVEMENT SYSTEM, proper mixing and timely placement—concrete must be placed within 90 minutes after the last water is added—and depositing the mix on a saturated (but not standing‑water) subgrade without segregation set the foundation for the intended void structure; limited compaction with smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode for one to four passes, and the prohibition of pneumatic tire rollers, help keep the void content at 20 % ±5 % and prevent the hardened density from exceeding 130 lb/ft³ (within a ±5 lb/ft³ tolerance). A gentle final surface finish that leaves surface voids open combined with immediate curing—starting within 20 minutes of discharge, fully covering with polyethylene sheeting, and maintaining it for at least 7 days—preserves the designed porosity so that infiltration rates must exceed 200 in/hr, thereby delivering the required permeability performance. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 410–451), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 650–652)

Construction‑related defects that most affect the long‑term permeability of a pavement are those that reduce or block the ability of water to flow through the base and subbase layers; crushing or plugging of base drain outlets prevents the drainage system from functioning properly, while using materials with insufficient intrinsic permeability—whether because the gradation is inappropriate or particle shape limits void size—limits gravity‑drainable flow. In some cases permeability is deliberately reduced to gain a more stable work platform during construction, which can also compromise long‑term water movement. Long‑term permeability is most sensitive to construction actions that alter the intended void network; excessive compaction or sealing of surface voids—such as using rollers heavier than the specified maximum 2‑ton roller, pneumatic tire rollers, or steel troweling that closes pores—directly reduces infiltration rates, and failure to remove fines generated by saw‑cut joints can clog the voids and lower performance. Maintaining the designed void structure (±5 % of the target) and achieving an average infiltration rate above 200 in/hr on test sections are required thresholds, and deviations in these parameters during placement, finishing, or curing—e.g., delayed curing beyond 7 days or exposure to rain before the polyethylene sheet is applied—introduce defects that compromise permeability over time. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 410–411)

Selection, specification, and improvement

For base and subbase layers, the selection criterion is that materials should be highly permeable so that gravity‑drainable water will be removed rapidly [1]. High permeability also prevents capillary rise because the larger voids do not wick water upward [1]. Some drainability may be sacrificed to provide a more stable work platform while the pavement layers are being built [1]. PennDOT uses particle‑size distribution (gradation) as an indicator test to infer permeability [1]. For AAAP, AA Slip Form Paving, and AA Form Pavement concrete under Section 704, permeability is specified through a target chloride‑ion penetration class that shall be Low, Very Low, or Negligible after a curing period of 56 days or less as measured by AASHTO T 277 or AASHTO T 358 [2]. The district materials engineer or district maintenance manager may also accept a Moderate penetration rating provided the test results do not exceed 2,800 coulombs on AASHTO T 277 or meet the minimum resistivity of 15.6 kΩ‑cm for 4‑in × 8‑in cylinders and 12.3 kΩ‑cm for 6‑in × 12‑in cylinders on AASHTO T 358 [2]. For thin bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavement, permeability is likewise controlled by specifying the same target chloride‑ion penetration classes (Low, Very Low, or Negligible) after a 56‑day or less curing period measured by the same tests, with the option for a Moderate classification if approved [2]. For pervious concrete pavement systems covered by Section 520, permeability is expressed through an infiltration rate requirement such that test sections constructed to the approved mix design must be evaluated in accordance with ASTM C 1701 and achieve an average infiltration value that exceeds 200 inches per hour, and the installed pavement must likewise demonstrate a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour during in‑place testing [2]. Because water flow is governed by the concrete’s internal void structure, the mix must also produce a void content of 20 % ±5 % and a hardened density that does not exceed 130 lb/ft³ (±5 lb/ft³) [2].

Designers should select base and subbase aggregates that are intrinsically highly permeable, providing large inter‑particle voids that promote rapid gravity drainage and suppress capillary wicking; where work platform stability is a concern a modest reduction in permeability may be accepted but must be balanced with adequate gradation testing, which PennDOT employs as an indicator of permeability because direct field measurement is difficult. Incorporating lateral underdrains at wet subgrade zones and at cut‑to‑fill transitions further assists water removal, while maximizing the slope of subbase layers and spacing drains closely together increases the rate at which water is evacuated from the pavement structure. For cement concrete pavements, permeability can be improved by incorporating supplementary cementitious materials; at least one of slag cement (Grade 100 or higher) at a minimum of 25 %, fly ash (Type C or F) at a minimum of 15 %, or silica fume at 5‑10 % may replace part of the Portland cement and help achieve low chloride ion penetration. The mix must also include an air‑entraining admixture sized to obtain the specified plastic‑state air content—6.0 % for general concrete or 7.0 % for AAAP and related mixes—within a tolerance of ± 1.5 %, preserving a pore structure that supports permeability. Trial batches are required, and mixture‑qualification testing such as anti‑washout admixture dosage per PTM No. 641 and chloride ion penetration tests (AASHTO T 277 or T 358) after 56 days or less must be performed; if the Table A criteria are not met, alternative materials or adjustments to the mixing procedure must be made. When a thin bonded concrete overlay over asphalt‑surfaced pavement is less than 3 inches thick, the mix should use No. 67 coarse aggregate instead of No. 57, and synthetic or steel macrofibers may be added at a dosage that achieves the required residual strength (f150) according to Table A. In pervious concrete pavement systems, high permeability is maintained through several construction‑level controls: test sections must demonstrate an average infiltration rate that exceeds 200 inches per hour and a void content of 20 % ± 5 %; hardened density may not exceed 130 lb/ft³ and must be within ±5 lb/ft³. Subgrade preparation requires undisturbed, minimally compacted soil with any fine material scarified away, and the detention‑basin bottom must be protected by at least two inches of fine aggregate together with a non‑woven geotextile to keep sediments from clogging the system. Surface finishing shall use smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode for one to four passes—pneumatic tire rollers shall not be used—and final finishing may employ no more than a 2‑ton roller or steel troweling provided the voids remain unobstructed. Rapid curing is achieved by beginning curing within 20 minutes of concrete discharge, completely covering the pavement surface with polyethylene sheeting and maintaining the cover for a minimum of seven days to prevent premature drying or sealing of the pores. Performance evaluation and implications

Because permeability is not easily measured in the field, PennDOT relies on a laboratory particle‑size distribution (gradation) test as the primary indicator of how readily water can flow through base and subbase materials when designing drainage systems [1]. For cement‑concrete mixes specified in Section 704, permeability is evaluated by laboratory chloride‑ion penetration testing on specimens cured for 56 days or less, using either AASHTO T 277 (Rapid Chloride Permeability Test) or AASHTO T 358 [2]. The design target shall be a Low, Very Low, or Negligible level of ion penetration; if a Moderate level is proposed, the T 277 result may not exceed 2,800 coulombs and the T 358 result must meet minimum resistivity values of 15.6 kΩ·cm for a 4‑in × 8‑in cylinder and 12.3 kΩ·cm for a 6‑in × 12‑in cylinder [2]. For thin bonded concrete overlays covered in Section 541, permeability is likewise evaluated by laboratory chloride‑ion penetration testing after a curing period of 56 days or less with AASHTO T 277 or AASHTO T 358, and the required performance shall be Low, Very Low, or Negligible penetration with the same limits of not exceeding 2,800 coulombs for T 277 and not exceeding 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4‑in × 8‑in cylinders and 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6‑in × 12‑in cylinders under T 358 [2]. For pervious concrete pavement systems described in Section 520, permeability is verified through field infiltration testing performed on constructed test sections in accordance with ASTM C 1701 [2]. Each test section shall be at least 225 ft², and the average infiltration rate of the two sections must exceed 200 inches per hour [2]. In addition, for every 10,000 ft² of placed concrete three field locations are selected and tested per ASTM C 1701, and any area that does not achieve a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour shall be removed and replaced [2].

Drainage is identified as the most important consideration during field view, design, construction, and maintenance of a pavement structure. Base and sub‑base materials should be highly permeable so that water can flow rapidly by gravity, which removes moisture from beneath the surface, reduces loss of subgrade support, increases resistance of the aggregate base to traffic‑induced deflection, and limits frost heave, rutting, and other moisture‑related distresses that would otherwise shorten pavement life and degrade ride quality. Designers may sacrifice some drainability to provide a stable work platform during construction, and because permeability is not easily measured in the field, gradation is used as an indicator even though similar gradations can produce different permeabilities. In cement‑based pavement systems permeability is treated as a primary design criterion; the concrete mix must achieve a Low, Very Low, or Negligible chloride‑ion penetration after a curing period of fifty‑six days or less as measured by AASHTO T 277 or AASHTO T 358. If a Moderate rating is permitted, the charge may not exceed 2,800 coulombs on T 277 or the resistivity must be at least 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4‑inch × 8‑inch cylinders and 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6‑inch × 12‑inch cylinders. The thin bonded concrete overlay over asphalt‑surfaced pavement follows the same requirement, with the DME/DMM allowing a Moderate level only when the test results meet the same 2,800‑coulomb or the 15.6‑kΩ·cm (4×8) and 12.3‑kΩ·cm (6×12) limits; producers must perform these permeability tests before submitting a mix design to ensure durability against chloride ingress. Pervious concrete pavement systems are required to achieve an average infiltration rate of at least two hundred inches per hour, and designers therefore specify test sections and ongoing field testing to verify that the void structure is approximately twenty percent ± five percent and that the hardened density does not exceed one hundred thirty pounds per cubic foot with a tolerance of plus or minus five pounds per cubic foot. Construction practices are mandated to preserve those voids, including using smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode for one to four passes, avoiding pneumatic‑tire rollers, and finishing without sealing surface voids; curing must continue for a minimum of seven days and joints must be cut properly to prevent premature loss of infiltration capacity. Maintenance includes periodic infiltration testing every ten thousand square feet and removal and replacement of pervious concrete that does not meet or exceed the minimum average infiltration rate of two hundred inches per hour, thereby sustaining long‑term performance and durability.
