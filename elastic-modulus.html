Elastic Modulus

Part of: Portland Cement Concrete is-subtopic-of
Elastic Modulus is a topic under Portland Cement Concrete.
elastic-modulusconcrete-elastic-modulus
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Elastic modulus (Ec) is the measure of concrete’s stiffness or rigidity, expressed in pounds per square inch as a 28‑day mean value that quantifies how much a slab will deform under traffic loads[1]. For normal‑weight Portland cement concrete it is linked to compressive strength through the ACI correlation Ec = 57,000 (f′c)^0.5, giving a standard mean of about 4 × 10⁶ psi for Class AA paving concrete unless local strength data justify another figure[1]. In existing pavements the modulus is back‑calculated from falling‑weight deflectometer tests performed with a 9,000‑lb drop weight under controlled temperature conditions (45–90 °F), and the resulting average must fall within prescribed limits to be accepted[2].

Material purpose and applicability

Elastic modulus, expressed for concrete as the 28‑day mean elastic modulus Ec, quantifies the material’s stiffness or rigidity. In rigid pavement design it is used as a key input property to represent how the concrete slab will deform under traffic loads; the AASHTO procedures normally employ a mean value of 4,000,000 pounds per square inch for Class AA paving concrete unless local compressive‑strength data justify a different figure. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (p. 161)

Composition and characteristics

The elastic modulus of concrete, denoted Ec and expressed in pounds per square inch, quantifies the material’s stiffness or rigidity. For normal weight Portland Cement Concrete the ACI recommends the correlation Ec = 57,000 (f’c)^{0.5}, where f’c is the 28‑day compressive strength in pounds per square inch. Using a design compressive strength of 3500 psi for Class AA paving concrete gives an Ec of 3.4 × 10^6 psi, while the mean value adopted in the 1993 AASHTO procedures is 4,000,000 psi corresponding to a mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (p. 161)

Key material properties

In rigid pavement design the stiffness of concrete, expressed as its elastic modulus, is primarily linked to the material’s compressive strength; the ACI correlation shows that Ec increases with the square‑root of f′c, so a 28‑day compressive strength of 3500 psi for Class AA concrete yields an Ec around 3.4×10⁶ psi and the mean value adopted for Class AA concrete is 4×10⁶ psi corresponding to a mean compressive strength of about 4925 psi, a relationship that applies to normal‑weight Portland cement concrete used in pavement slabs and for which no chemical or thermal properties are mentioned as influencing Ec [1]. In the slab‑fracturing procedure the elastic modulus of the fractured concrete layer is obtained with a falling‑weight deflectometer that applies a 9,000 lb drop weight, so the mechanical loading condition of the test directly governs the measured modulus; additionally, the test may only be performed when the pavement surface is not frozen and the air temperature is at least 45 °F but does not exceed 90 °F, indicating that thermal conditions are critical to obtaining a reliable elastic‑modulus value, while no chemical or other material properties are specified in this section [2].

[1] — Ch. 8 (p. 161); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) defines the concrete elastic modulus, Ec, through the ACI‑recommended correlation Ec = 57,000 (f′c)^0.5 where f′c is the 28‑day compressive strength in psi [1]. Applying a 28‑day design strength of 3,500 psi for Class AA paving concrete therefore yields an Ec of 3.4 × 10^6 psi [1]. AASHTO’s rigid pavement design procedures, however, adopt a mean Ec of 4,000,000 psi that corresponds to a mean compressive strength of 4,925 psi for Class AA concrete unless local historical data indicate significantly different strengths [1]. For an existing fractured concrete slab the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifies measurement of the elastic modulus by Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing with a 9,000‑lb drop weight, provided that the surface is not frozen and air temperature is between 45 °F and 90 °F [2]. The operator then back‑calculates the pavement modulus using the procedures of AASHTO T 256 together with ASTM D4694, D4695 and D5858, employing approved software such as MODULUS or EVERCALC [2]. The resulting average elastic modulus is characterized by comparison with the acceptable range set out in Table A for the specified treatment; if the average falls within that range the value is accepted, otherwise the fracturing pattern must be adjusted and the test repeated [2].

Behavior and mechanisms

The elastic modulus of a fractured concrete slab is obtained after fracturing and seating by using falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD) testing, and the measured average must fall within the range specified in Table A for that treatment. Moisture is introduced only to wet the fractured surface so that the crack pattern can be inspected visually; however, roller seating shall not be carried out under wet conditions. Temperature limits are imposed on modulus measurement: FWD testing shall not be performed when the pavement surface is frozen or when the air temperature falls below 45 °F or exceeds 90 °F, indicating that modulus values are considered reliable only within this thermal window. The source does not address chemical exposure or other service‑condition effects. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

The elastic modulus of a fractured concrete slab is primarily governed by mechanical actions applied during the fracturing process; the impact force and resulting breaking energy dictated by the chosen fracturing pattern directly influence the stiffness measured by FWD back‑calculation, so that when the average modulus exceeds the upper limit in Table A the pattern must be altered to reduce breaking energy. The requirement that a minimum 1,000‑foot‑long test section be used to establish the optimal pattern and verify that the average elastic modulus falls within the range specified in Table A further ties the measured modulus to these mechanical controls. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Durability and deterioration

Improper fracturing or seating actions that cause surface spalling, punched holes, shattering of concrete, or inadequate contact pressure can degrade the stiffness of the slab, reflected as an elastic modulus outside the acceptable range; consequently the average elastic modulus of the fractured layer must remain within the limits set in Table A, otherwise the breaking pattern must be altered and the section re‑fractured. Additionally, wet conditions during roller seating, exposure to freezing temperatures before overlay, and soil liquefaction from unsuitable sonic vibratory equipment are durability concerns that can prevent proper consolidation and thus affect the measured modulus. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Influence of production and placement

Construction variations that most affect the long‑term elastic modulus are those that alter the fracture quality or seating of the concrete slab. Using impact equipment that creates extensive surface spalling, punches holes, or shatters the pavement can raise the measured modulus beyond the specified range, while an inappropriate breaking energy—either too high or too low—requires adjustment of the fracturing pattern to keep the average modulus within Table A limits. Proper seating with a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller and the required number of passes (at least three for Crack‑and‑Seat, at least five for Break‑and‑Seat) is also critical; insufficient contact pressure or using prohibited vibratory rollers can leave the slab inadequately compacted and lower its modulus. Finally, leaving exposed reinforcing steel or steel plates in the fractured surface must be avoided because they constitute defects that can distort the back‑calculated elastic modulus. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Selection, specification, and improvement

In rigid pavement design the elastic modulus of concrete is selected by first relating it to the 28‑day structural design compressive strength using the ACI recommended correlation E_c = 57,000 (f’_c)^0.5; for Class AA paving concrete the mean compressive strength that corresponds to the standard mean modulus is 4,925 pounds per square inch, giving a mean elastic modulus of 4,000,000 pounds per square inch, and this value should be used in all rigid pavement designs unless district historical data show mean compressive strengths significantly different than 4,925 psi, in which case the modulus must be adjusted accordingly [1]. In slab‑fracturing operations for existing concrete pavements the elastic modulus of the fractured layer must fall within the allowable range shown in Table A of Section 522.1, and this requirement applies to both crack‑and‑seat and break‑and‑seat methods and is identical for unreinforced and reinforced slabs [2]. During construction the average modulus for each 1,000‑ft test section is obtained by back‑calculating falling‑weight deflectometer data and compared with those limits, and any value exceeding the upper limit triggers a modification of the breaking pattern (reduced energy) and retesting [2]. The modulus determination must be made with a calibrated FWD that uses a 9,000‑lb drop weight, samples at intervals of 50 feet or less, and is conducted only when the pavement surface is not frozen and the air temperature is between 45°F and 90°F, in accordance with AASHTO T 256, ASTM D4694, D4695 and D5858 using approved back‑calculation software [2].

To keep the elastic modulus of a fractured concrete slab within the specified range, the procedure calls for establishing at least one 1,000‑foot test section to fine‑tune the breaking pattern; if the measured modulus exceeds the upper limit, the pattern must be altered to use reduced breaking energy and retested. Seating the broken pieces with a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic tire roller inflated to at least 100 psi and applying the required number of passes (three for Crack‑and‑Seat, five for Break‑and‑Seat) provides consistent compaction that stabilizes modulus values. Continuous quality control is achieved by conducting falling weight deflectometer tests in accordance with AASHTO T 256, ASTM D4694, D4695 and D5858 only when the surface temperature is between 45 °F and 90 °F and using calibrated equipment, while backfilling to the slab bottom with Subbase No. 2A and topping with Flexible Base Replacement further supports modulus performance. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Performance evaluation and implications

Evaluation of the elastic modulus for rigid pavements relies on laboratory measurements, specifically the 28‑day mean elastic modulus of the concrete slab, which quantifies stiffness after curing. The primary design input is obtained from the 28‑day structural design compressive strength and entered into the American Concrete Institute correlation Ec = 57 000 (f’c)^0.5. For Class AA paving concrete the mean elastic modulus used in designs is 4 × 10^6 pounds per square inch, a value that corresponds with a mean compressive strength of 4,925 pounds per square inch; this laboratory‑derived figure may be adjusted only if district historical data show mean compressive strengths significantly different than 4,925 pounds per square inch. The source does not mention any field or long‑term performance tests for evaluating elastic modulus. Evaluation of elastic modulus for fractured concrete pavements is performed with a falling weight deflectometer (FWD) as stipulated in Section 522(e); the operator conducts FWD testing and back‑calculation to determine the elastic pavement modulus, using calibrated equipment from an SHRP regional facility and approved software such as MODULUS or EVERCALC. Tests are carried out on a 1,000‑foot‑long test section with data collected at intervals of 50 feet or less in the lane center using a drop weight of 9,000 pounds, provided that the pavement surface is not frozen and air temperature is between 45 °F and 90 °F. The 1,000‑foot‑long test section will be deemed satisfactory if the average elastic modulus of the fractured concrete layer falls within the range specified in Table A; otherwise the breaking pattern is adjusted and the section retested.[1][1][1][1][2][2][2]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 160–161); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

After the concrete slabs are fractured and seated, a falling weight deflectometer is used to back‑calculate the average elastic pavement modulus of the fractured layer; this measured stiffness must fall within the range set out in Table A for the treatment to be accepted. When the average modulus meets that range, the chosen breaking pattern is deemed satisfactory, but if the average exceeds the upper limit the breaking energy must be reduced and a new fracturing pattern tested, ensuring the repaired pavement has an appropriate level of stiffness to perform under traffic loads. By using the elastic modulus as a quantitative acceptance criterion, engineers can directly control the structural behavior, durability, and subsequent maintenance needs of the rehabilitated rigid pavement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)
