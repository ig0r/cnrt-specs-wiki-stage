Water Infiltration

Part of: Subsurface Drainage is-subtopic-of
Water Infiltration is a topic under Subsurface Drainage.
water-infiltrationinfiltrationexcess-moisture
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

Water infiltration denotes the unwanted movement of water, chlorides, and incompressible materials into a pavement structure; uncontrolled entry compromises material integrity and reduces structural capacity, so design requirements call for cleaning and sealing all joints, cracks, and disturbed surfaces to keep the subgrade stable and the existing drainage system functional [1]. In pervious concrete and asphalt systems the horizontal surface is deliberately porous, allowing storm‑water to pass through into an underlying open‑graded angular stone layer that provides temporary detention while pipe underdrains and properly shaped, fine‑graded embankments convey water away, establishing a continuously positive drainage condition [2]. Performance is verified by ASTM C1701 infiltration testing with an average rate of at least 200 in/hr, and construction controls such as limiting compaction to four static‑roller passes and sealing all constructed or disturbed surfaces each workday are mandated to preserve porosity and prevent water entry [2].

Purpose and design objectives

Water infiltration is addressed in full‑depth reclamation projects by requiring that joints and cracks be sealed or rehabilitated so that water, chlorides, and incompressible materials cannot enter the pavement structure; this prevents moisture‑related distress by keeping the underlying subgrade stable and ensuring the existing drainage system remains functional [1]. The guidance further states that excess water within any part of the pavement structure, including the subgrade, is detrimental because it can cause loss of material integrity and reduce structural capacity, making a well‑drained pavement essential for durable performance and mandating identification and correction of pre‑existing drainage problems before reconstruction [1]. In pervious concrete pavement systems, the horizontal concrete surface is designed to be permeable to water and air so that storm‑water can pass through into the subsoil, while an underlying open‑graded angular stone layer provides temporary detention; this configuration manages storm‑water and prevents water from accumulating in structural layers where it could cause distress [2]. Pervious asphalt pavement systems employ a similar approach with a permeable asphalt surface and an open‑graded angular stone layer, require infiltration testing per ASTM C1701 to show an average rate exceeding 200 inches per hour, and limit compaction passes to no more than four to avoid over‑compaction that would eliminate porosity, thereby keeping the pavement free‑draining and protecting structural layers from moisture‑induced damage [2]. Embankment and fill layers are shaped to drain during construction, fine‑graded, and sealed each workday with a smooth‑drum roller to promote continuous positive drainage and reduce water entering the pavement structure, which helps prevent instability of wet or saturated foundations [2]. Finally, subgrade construction calls for compacting and proof‑rolling followed by sealing all constructed or disturbed surfaces with a static‑mode smooth‑drum steel wheel roller to maintain a continuously positive drainage condition and avoid distress such as stripping, pumping, or structural damage caused by water infiltration [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 150–172), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 307–310), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 418)

The design must keep water from entering the pavement structure—future infiltration of water, chlorides, and incompressible materials into the pavement structure must be prevented—and it must rely on a functional existing drainage system to achieve that goal [1]. For pervious concrete pavement the design must achieve a functional water‑infiltration performance that exceeds 200 inches per hour, as demonstrated by two test sections whose average infiltration value is required to be greater than 200 in/hr according to ASTM C1701, and for each installed 10,000 square‑foot area the minimum average infiltration rate shall be 200 in/hr; any portion not meeting this threshold shall be removed and replaced [2]. The pervious asphalt pavement system shall be constructed on a prepared surface so that the surface layer provides a horizontal plane permeable to water and air, thereby allowing storm‑water infiltration into the subsoil as a functional objective, and includes a second layer of open‑graded, angular stone used for temporary storm‑water detention [2]. Perforated or porous pipes must be sized to provide at least 1.4 square inches of water inlet area per linear foot and must achieve a minimum infiltration rate of 10 gallons per minute per linear foot as defined in Section 610.2(c) and (e) [2]. Structurally the pipe material shall pass the strength test prescribed in ASTM D2412, meet the stiffness values given for each pipe type, and possess a minimum tensile strength of 1,200 psi and crush strength of 40 psi for prefabricated base drains, ensuring it can sustain construction handling and permanent loading without deformation [2]. The pipe shall be flattenable to specified percentages without cracking and must provide a flow capacity of at least 15 gallons per minute per foot of width and minimum open‑core areas of 6 % on the pavement side face, guaranteeing continued drainage performance under traffic loads [2]. Durability is addressed by selecting materials that exhibit a water absorption of no more than 0.01 % after 24 hours and demonstrate fungus resistance with no growth [2]. Subgrade preparation shall leave the soil undisturbed and as permeable as possible, limit compaction to not more than four passes so that surface porosity is retained, and avoid over‑compacting that would prevent free draining or crush aggregate; compacted thickness of pervious asphalt sections may not be more than 1/4‑inch less than the design thickness [2]. Fine grading shall remove low areas and seal the embankment or fill surface at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage and reduce water infiltration, while soil moisture shall be maintained within –3 % of optimum content, Type 1 granular material within ±2 % of optimum moisture content, and Type 2 kept surface‑damp but not wet [2]. All constructed or disturbed subgrade surfaces shall be sealed each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage and minimize infiltration of surface water; after the completed subgrade has been in place for more than 60 days or at least one winter season, it must be compacted and proof rolled before placing a Class 4, Type A geotextile over the entire lane width (8 ft or greater) [2]. The geotextile shall run continuously longitudinally with its transverse edges placed between a minimum of 6 inches and a maximum of 12 inches from the interior edge of the pavement base drain, and any damaged or unsatisfactory areas must be reshaped and recompacted before placement [2]. These measures support functional drainage, preserve structural integrity, enhance durability by limiting water entry, and maintain serviceability by keeping surface irregularities within the allowable ½‑inch tolerance [2].

Scope and applicability

Water‑infiltration design is appropriate for rigid pervious concrete pavements constructed under Section 520 and for pervious asphalt pavement systems constructed under Section 420. In the concrete case, plant‑mixed pervious concrete is placed on a prepared surface that has been scarified at least six inches deep and cleared of fine material so that the subgrade remains as permeable as possible; the pavement must incorporate a horizontal pervious concrete surface layer and an underlying open‑graded angular stone layer intended for temporary storm‑water detention. The concrete mix shall achieve a void structure of 20 % ± 5 % and a hardened density not to exceed 130 lb/ft³ (± 5 lb/ft³). Test sections of at least 225 ft² are required, and for each 10 000 ft² of installed concrete infiltration testing in accordance with ASTM C1701 must be performed on three areas; any portion that does not meet a minimum average infiltration rate exceeding 200 in/hr must be removed and replaced. For pervious asphalt, plant‑mixed asphalt concrete is similarly placed on a prepared subgrade that is left undisturbed and un‑compacted, with an open‑graded angular stone layer beneath a horizontal pervious asphalt surface layer to provide temporary detention. Placement of the asphalt mixture is prohibited when air or surface temperature is below 50 °F, and compaction is limited to no more than four passes with smooth steel‑wheeled rollers operated in static mode so that porosity is preserved; the subgrade must remain as permeable as possible by avoiding compaction before installation of geotextile and stone bed. As with concrete, test sections must achieve an average infiltration rate exceeding 200 in/hr. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 307–310), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 418–421)

When excess moisture is present in the pavement structure—including a wet subgrade—or when drainage problems have not been corrected, the suitability of full‑depth reclamation (FDR) diminishes and other rehabilitation strategies become more appropriate. Likewise, if the observed distress is primarily surface‑related rather than requiring improvement of the support layers, an alternative design approach should be favored over FDR because it targets different failure mechanisms. Design inputs and requirements

Water infiltration in Portland cement concrete (PCC) pavements is governed by the joint sealing requirements set out in Publication 408 Specifications, notably Sections 512 and 521 for cleaning and sealing of all joints and cracks before patching, and Section 469 for overlay situations. The specifications call for constructing a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir at pavement/shoulder joints and sealing it with rubberized or asphalt joint sealing material. Transverse joint reservoirs must meet the shape factor defined in Section 521 and illustrated in Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards RC‑20M. For overlays, badly spalled joints must be cleaned and filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material as required by Section 469. Compliance with these standards together with a stable subgrade and functional drainage is required to prevent infiltration of water, chlorides, and incompressible materials into the pavement structure. Under Section 520—Pervious Concrete Pavement System, water‑infiltration performance is controlled by ASTM C1701 testing; both test sections must be infiltrated according to this method and the average infiltration value must exceed 200 inches per hour. For field construction, infiltration tests are required on three areas selected according to PTM No 1 for every 10,000 sq ft of pervious concrete placed using ASTM C1701. Any pavement that does not meet a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour must be removed and replaced. Under Section 420—Pervious Asphalt Pavement System, water‑infiltration performance is governed by a mandatory test‑section program in which two test sections at least 225 ft² each are constructed on the approved JMF and evaluated according to ASTM C1701. The average infiltration rate for the pair is required to exceed 200 inches per hour. Subgrade preparation requirements also control infiltration: excavation must be to undisturbed soil without compaction, fine material must be removed, scarification to a minimum depth of six inches is required, and heavy equipment traffic is prohibited before placement of geotextile and stone bed so the base remains as permeable as possible. Placement is further constrained by a temperature limit that pervious mixtures may not be placed when air or surface temperatures are below 50 °F. Compaction must be limited to no more than four passes with a steel‑wheeled static roller to avoid reducing porosity. Under Section 610—Pipe Undrain and Pavement Base Drain, circular pipe shall have a minimum inside diameter of 4 inches and be sized in increments of at least 1 inch; semi‑circular pipe shall average an inside diameter of 4 5/8 inches. Perforations must provide at least 1.4 square inches of water inlet area per linear foot with hole diameters no smaller than 3⁄16 inch and no larger than 3⁄8 inch. Perforated or porous pipe shall deliver a minimum infiltration rate of 10 gallons per minute per linear foot as measured by AASHTO M 176M. Pipe material must satisfy ASTM D2412 strength testing with a stiffness of at least 35 psi at 5 % deflection for sizes from 4 to 10 inches. Under Section 206—Placement and Compaction of Embankment and Fill, layers must be shaped to drain during construction, fine‑graded and sealed with a smooth‑drum roller at the end of each workday to promote continuous positive drainage, moisture content of soil must be kept within –3 % of optimum, Type 1 granular material within ±2 % of optimum, and compaction targets must be at least 97 % of maximum dry density (100 % for the top three feet). Under Section 210—Subgrade, sealing of all constructed or disturbed surfaces at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel‑wheel roller operated in static mode is required to maintain positive drainage and limit water infiltration. If the completed subgrade remains in place for more than 60 days or at least one winter season, it must be compacted and proof rolled before installing a Class 4, Type A geotextile separator and subbase. The geotextile shall be placed continuously across the entire lane width of eight feet or greater. [1][2]

Design methods and criteria

To limit future infiltration of water, chlorides, and incompressible materials into the pavement structure, the CPR guidelines prescribe a series of joint‑focused procedures [1]. Joint rehabilitation creates new sealant reservoirs and reseals transverse or expansion joints [1]. Longitudinal joint cleaning and sealing is required on every CPR project [1]. Pavement‑shoulder joint sealing constructs a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir and applies rubberized/asphalt sealant [1]. Transverse joint cleaning and sealing must satisfy the shape‑factor requirements of Section 521 [1]. Crack sealing follows the specifications for full‑depth or partial‑depth cracks [1]. Dowel bar retrofits install epoxy‑coated dowels across cracks or joints to restore load transfer [1]. Cross‑stitching places epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle across longitudinal cracks or joints to achieve near‑complete load transfer [1]. For pervious concrete pavements the specification requires constructing two test sections and measuring their infiltration rates with ASTM C1701; the average of the two values must exceed 200 inches per hour [2]. In addition, a field verification is performed on three locations selected per 10 000 square feet of installed pervious concrete, also using ASTM C1701, and any area that does not meet the minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour must be removed and replaced [2]. For pervious asphalt pavement two test sections of at least 225 square feet each are built on the prepared subgrade, tested in accordance with ASTM C1701, and the pair’s average infiltration rate must exceed 200 inches per hour [2]. The constructed thickness may not be more than ¼‑inch less than the design thickness and compaction is limited to no more than four passes of a static steel‑wheeled roller so that surface porosity is retained [2]. Pipe underdrains are required to provide a minimum water inlet area of 1.4 square inches per linear foot, achieved by perforating each pipe with circular holes whose nominal diameters are not less than 3⁄16 inch and not greater than 3⁄8 inch or slotted openings no wider than 1⁄8 inch; all perforations must be cut cleanly to avoid flow restriction [2]. The assembled pipe must deliver at least 10 gallons per minute per linear foot as determined by AASHTO M 176M, and pipe strength is verified with the parallel‑plate load test prescribed in ASTM D2412 [2]. During construction of embankments or fills, layers are shaped to drain, fine‑graded to eliminate low spots, and sealed each workday with a smooth‑drum roller to promote continuous positive drainage and reduce water entry [2]. Moisture conditions are controlled by maintaining native soil within –3 % of its optimum moisture content, Type 1 granular material within ±2 % of its optimum moisture content, and Type 2 granular material in a surface‑damp (not wet) condition at the time of compaction [2]. Subgrade work is similarly protected; after each day’s construction or disturbance the surface is sealed with a smooth‑drum steel‑wheel roller operated in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage and minimize infiltration [2]. Once the subgrade has been compacted and proof‑rolled as required by Section 206.3(a)1, a Class 4, Type A geotextile is placed over the entire lane width of eight feet or greater before installing the subbase [2].

The pavement design must prevent future infiltration of water, as well as chlorides and incompressible materials, into the pavement structure by cleaning and sealing all joints and cracks in accordance with the joint‑sealing sections; no specific numeric thresholds or safety factors are prescribed for water infiltration in this guidance. [1] In pervious concrete pavement systems, infiltration performance is required to be demonstrated through testing: each of two test sections shall be tested according to ASTM C1701 and the average of both infiltration values must exceed 200 inches per hour, and an in‑place infiltration test on three areas per 10 000 square feet of installed pavement shall achieve a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour. [2] The same concrete system also requires that the void structure be 20 % ± 5 % as measured by ASTM C1754. Structural section and dimensions

The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) specifies that to prevent water infiltration a sealant reservoir measuring 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch shall be constructed at the pavement‑shoulder joint; it does not provide any dimensional requirements for the pavement layer, base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab, edge‑support, or reinforcement [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification 408/2026, Section 520.3 for pervious concrete pavement, requires that the test‑section thickness may not be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness, that the exterior formed surface at the edge must have a radius of at least ¼ inch, and that core acceptance is limited to an average core length of 3/8 inch plus ¾ inch with no individual core permitted to be less than –½ inch; the specification does not prescribe separate dimensions for the base, subbase, treated‑soil, shoulder, edge‑support, or reinforcement [2].

Materials and mixture design

To prevent water infiltration the pavement design shall require sealing of all joints and cracks using a rubberized joint‑sealing material as prescribed in Publication 408 Sections 512 and 521 [1]. Where joints are badly spalled, the voids shall be cleaned and filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material or an equivalent product in accordance with Publication 408 Section 469 [1]. At pavement‑shoulder interfaces a sealant reservoir sized at 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch shall be constructed and the joint sealed with rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material, while transverse‑joint reservoirs must satisfy the shape‑factor criteria set forth in Publication 408 Section 521 [1]. For pervious concrete pavement systems each test section shall be placed using the approved mix design and shall achieve a void structure of 20 % ± 5 % as measured by ASTM C1754 [2]. The average hardened density of the two test sections shall not exceed 130 lb/ft³ and must lie within a tolerance of ± 5 lb/ft³ [2]. Section thickness may be no more than one‑quarter inch less than the design thickness [2]. Infiltration testing performed in accordance with ASTM C1701 shall produce an average rate greater than 200 inches per hour for the two test sections, and this average shall serve as the acceptance criterion for the remainder of the project [2]. The pipe‑underdrain material specification requires circular pipes to have a minimum inside diameter of 4 inches and to be sized in increments of at least 1 inch (or semicircular pipe an average inside diameter of 4 5/8 in) [2]. Pipes shall be perforated to provide at least 1.4 sq in of inlet area per linear foot using clean circular holes nominally between 3⁄16 in and 3⁄8 in (or slotted holes not exceeding 1⁄8 in wide), and must achieve a minimum infiltration rate of 10 gallons per minute per linear foot as measured by AASHTO M 176M [2]. Pipe stiffness shall meet the listed PSI values at the specified deflection, for example 30 psi at 10 % deflection for PE pipe 4‑10 in and 35 psi at 5 % deflection for PVC pipe 4‑10 in [2]. Prefabricated pavement base drains shall employ an inert plastic core with a tensile strength of at least 1,200 psi, water absorption not greater than 0.01 % after 24 h, crush strength of at least 40 psi, a flow rate of at least 15 gpm per foot width, and must provide a minimum open‑core area of 6 % on the pavement side face (3 % on the shoulder side) [2]. The surrounding backfill shall consist of fine aggregate Type II sand meeting Section 703.1 grading and soundness limits, coarse aggregate Type C or better with No. 57 for Type I backfill and No. 8 for Type II backfill, and a non‑woven needle‑punched geotextile fabric conforming to Table A [2].

In SECTION 520—PERVIOUS CONCRETE PAVEMENT SYSTEM, the mix intended to provide structural integrity, durability, constructability and performance is first validated by constructing two test sections of at least 225 ft² each using the approved design. Each concrete section must have a void structure of 20% ± 5%, a hardened density that does not exceed 130 lb/ft³ with a tolerance of ± 5 lb/ft³, and a thickness that cannot be more than 1/4‑inch less than the design thickness. The average infiltration rate measured on the test sections in accordance with ASTM C1701 must exceed 200 in/hr before production proceeds, and core sampling together with periodic infiltration testing is required to confirm that the installed pavement continues to meet these void, density and permeability criteria throughout construction. In SECTION 420—PERVIOUS ASPHALT PAVEMENT SYSTEM, the mixture shall be an approved JMF and is placed in test sections of at least 225 ft² each. The average infiltration of those sections must exceed 200 in/hr and the compacted thickness may not be more than 1/4‑inch less than the design thickness. The subgrade should remain as undisturbed and permeable as possible, with fine‑aggregate bedding and coarse‑aggregate lifts installed per the drawings, and placement shall be avoided when air or surface temperature is lower than 50 °F. Compaction is limited to a maximum of four passes with a 10‑ton steel‑wheeled static roller to preserve surface porosity, and over‑compaction that eliminates free drainage must be avoided. [2]

Structural details and interfaces

To control water infiltration in Portland cement concrete (PCC) pavements, all transverse, longitudinal, and pavement‑shoulder joints as well as full‑depth cracks shall be cleaned and sealed with rubberized joint sealant or asphalt wearing‑course material when an overlay is planned, and the sealing must include constructing a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir for pavement/shoulder joints while ensuring that the transverse‑joint reservoir meets the shape‑factor requirements of Specification Section 521 [1]. Joint rehabilitation Type 1 (sawcut joints) or Type 2 (metal plate joints) should be performed on joints that are just beginning to show distress, and dowel bar retrofits using epoxy‑coated smooth dowels and cross‑stitching may be employed to restore load transfer when faulting or poor load transfer is observed, provided the existing drainage remains functional [1]. When spalling extends beyond the top half of a slab, partial‑depth spall repairs per Specification Section 525 or full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs per Specification Section 519 are required [1]. For pervious concrete pavement constructed under Section 520.3, construction joints shall be placed at the locations specified by the design and may be formed either by tooling into the fresh concrete to approximately one‑quarter of the slab thickness with a pervious‑concrete cross‑rolling tool or by saw cutting after the concrete has attained sufficient compressive strength, and any fines generated by saw cutting must be removed by vacuum to avoid clogging [2]. The exterior formed edges of the pervious concrete shall be rounded to a radius not less than one‑quarter inch [2]. The pavement base drain component of the water‑infiltration system shall incorporate a continuous geotextile wrap placed completely around the drainage core with its seam running longitudinally on the shoulder side face and preferably near the midpoint, and if an adhesive is used to fabricate the seam it shall be a non‑water‑soluble type [2]. Standard fittings and splice connections shall be provided along the drain, each of sufficient strength to resist construction handling and permanent loading, and any nonstandard fitting or connection shall receive approval [2]. To limit water infiltration at the subgrade level, all disturbed subgrade surfaces must be sealed at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage [2]. After subgrade acceptance, a Class 4, Type A geotextile shall be placed over the entire lane width of eight feet or greater before the subbase is installed; the geotextile must run continuously in the longitudinal direction with its transverse edges extending between six inches and twelve inches from the interior edge of the pavement base drain and may not be disturbed or damaged during placement of the pavement base drain [2].

To limit water infiltration at pavement/shoulder joints, the Pavement Policy Manual requires cleaning and sealing all joints and cracks with the specified rubberized joint sealing material before any repair that does not involve overlaying; a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir must be provided for each joint and the transverse joint sealant reservoir must satisfy the shape‑factor criteria of the relevant specification, while maintaining functionality of existing drainage and stability of the subgrade [1]. Full‑depth reclamation designs must incorporate effective drainage so that water does not accumulate at any layer or the subgrade; existing drainage problems shall be identified and corrected before placement of the reclaimed layer, and wet subgrade locations shall be located and provided with appropriate drainage measures to prevent excess moisture from entering interlayer interfaces and to protect material integrity and structural capacity [1]. In pervious‑concrete pavement systems (Section 520) the subgrade is excavated to undisturbed soil and left un‑compacted to retain maximum permeability; when fine material accumulates it shall be removed and the underlying soil scarified to a minimum depth of 6 inches [2]. The interface with a detention basin in that system is protected by placing a minimum of 2 inches of fine aggregate on the base, wrapping the sides with non‑woven geotextile fabric, and then adding coarse aggregate in lifts not exceeding 8 inches, each lift being compacted as specified [2]. Construction joints in pervious concrete shall be cut to approximately one‑quarter slab thickness and any generated fines shall be vacuum‑removed so that joint voids remain open, while exterior edges shall be rounded to at least a quarter‑inch radius to avoid water ponding [2]. For pipe‑underdrain and pavement‑base drain systems (Section 610) circular drainage pipes must have a minimum inside diameter of 4 inches and be perforated so that the water inlet area is at least 1.4 square inches per linear foot, with hole diameters between 3/16 inch and 3/8 inch (or limited slotted openings); the assembled system shall achieve a minimum infiltration rate of 10 gallons per minute per linear foot of pipe [2]. The drainage core in that system shall be completely wrapped in non‑woven needle‑punched geotextile with the seam run along the length and attached to protrusions on the pavement side face to ensure unobstructed inflow through at least half of the geotextile area, and the surrounding backfill shall consist of fine aggregate Type A sand as specified [2]. In pervious‑asphalt pavement systems (Section 420) a plant‑mixed asphalt concrete surface layer is placed on a prepared subgrade to form a horizontal plane that is permeable to water and air, allowing stormwater to infiltrate into the subsoil; beneath this surface an open‑graded angular stone layer provides temporary detention of the infiltrated water, creating the functional interface between layers that supports water infiltration [2]. All existing vertical surfaces such as curbs, structures, gutters and adjoining pavements that will contact the pervious asphalt must receive a thin, uniform coating of an approved emulsified‑asphalt product applied in two or more coats before the wearing course is placed, and no tack coat is permitted between lifts of the pervious asphalt [2]. The subgrade beneath pervious pavement shall be left undisturbed and as permeable as possible; any eroded areas shall be scarified to at least six inches depth and repaired without compaction prior to placement of geotextile and stone‑bed, and compaction of the pervious layer shall be limited to no more than four passes of a 10‑ton steel‑wheeled static roller or equivalent vibratory roller operating in static mode, with the surface required to remain free‑draining; over‑compaction that crushes aggregate or eliminates drainage is prohibited [2]. During construction of embankments or fills (Section 206) each compacted layer shall be formed so that it drains, then fine‑graded to eliminate low spots and sealed at the end of each workday with a smooth drum roller to maintain continuous positive drainage and limit water infiltration; edges may not contain furrows that would block free surface‑water drainage [2]. In subgrade construction (Section 210) the subgrade shall be compacted and then sealed each day with a smooth‑drum steel‑wheel roller operated in static mode so that drainage remains positive; after the subgrade has been in place for more than 60 days or at least one winter season it shall be re‑compacted and proof rolled before a Class 4, Type A geotextile is laid over the entire lane width, the geotextile being installed continuously in the longitudinal direction and positioned transversely between 6 inches and 12 inches from the interior edge of any pavement base drain, with care taken not to damage the fabric during base‑drain installation [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

In section 4.3 JOINTS of the Pavement Policy Manual, controlling water infiltration begins with verifying that the subgrade is stable and that the existing drainage system is functional; this prevents moisture from entering the pavement structure. All joints and cracks that will be patched but not overlaid should be cleaned and sealed with the specified rubberized joint sealing material, creating effective barriers against water, chlorides, and incompressible materials. For pavement‑shoulder interfaces the seal includes a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir filled with rubberized or asphalt sealant, which further limits infiltration under varying environmental conditions. In section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES > B, any existing drainage problems must be identified and corrected before constructing the reclaimed pavement layer; wet subgrade locations should be identified and effective drainage installed prior to FDR. Other water‑related damage within the existing pavement layers should be evaluated to determine its source and a corrective solution applied before reclamation because excess moisture in the structure, including the subgrade, is detrimental to material integrity and structural capacity. In pervious concrete systems, drainage and moisture control begin with excavation to undisturbed soil without compaction so the subgrade remains as permeable as possible, and a detention basin whose bottom is covered with a minimum of 2 inches of fine aggregate and wrapped in non‑woven geotextile to keep sediment out of the storage area. Before placement the subgrade or subbase must be wetted until it is saturated but without standing water, ensuring adequate moisture for bonding while preserving voids for infiltration. Placement is prohibited when air temperatures are projected to fall below 40 °F at any time during the first 24‑hour period after concrete placement or when rain is forecast before final finishing and curing; if rainfall does occur production must stop, a construction joint formed, and polyethylene sheeting applied, and after placement the pavement is cured under polyethylene sheeting for a minimum of 7 days with edges secured against wind. Design for water infiltration must incorporate drainage elements that meet the underdrain specifications of Section 610, where pipes shall have a minimum inside diameter of 4 inches (circular) or an average inside diameter of 4 5/8 inches (semi‑circular), be perforated to provide at least 1.4 sq in per linear foot of inlet area, and must achieve a minimum infiltration rate of 10 gallons per minute per linear foot as determined by AASHTO M 176M; prefabricated pavement base drains shall use an inert plastic core wrapped in non‑woven geotextile, provide at least 6 % open core area on the pavement side face and a flow capacity of not less than 15 gallons per minute per foot of width, with unobstructed inflow through a minimum of 50 % of the geotextile area on that face, and the surrounding backfill shall be fine aggregate Type II backfill consisting of natural or manufactured Type A sand as specified in Section 703.1, ensuring moisture is directed away from the pavement structure. During embankment or fill construction, layers are shaped to drain and edges are not allowed to have furrows that prohibit free drainage; fine grading removes low areas and the surface is sealed each workday by rolling with a smooth drum roller to promote continuous positive drainage and reduce water infiltration, while compaction is performed at moisture levels specified for each material—soil is maintained within -3 % of optimum moisture content, Type 1 granular material within ±2 % of optimum moisture content, and Type 2 kept surface damp but not wet—to achieve a stable, non‑rutting condition that resists water entry, and where the foundation is saturated, swampy, or otherwise unstable the specification requires constructing the embankment with rock to the indicated elevation, providing a more water‑resistant base. Design for pervious asphalt pavement systems preserves a permeable subgrade by excavating to undisturbed soil and scarifying any eroded areas to at least 6 inches depth while avoiding heavy equipment traffic before the geotextile and stone bed are placed, and a detention basin must be protected with a minimum 2‑inch fine‑aggregate layer and wrapped in non‑woven geotextile; pervious pavement mixtures shall not be placed when air or surface temperature is lower than 50 °F, and compaction shall be limited to no more than four roller passes so that the surface remains free draining. To limit water infiltration the subgrade must be sealed each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode so that drainage remains continuous and surface water entry is minimized; after the completed subgrade has been in place for more than 60 days or at least one winter season it shall be compacted and proof rolled again before any geotextile is installed, a Class 4, Type A geotextile must be placed over the entire lane width of 8 feet or greater before placing the subbase, and the geotextile shall be installed continuously in a longitudinal direction with its transverse edges extending between a minimum of 6 inches and a maximum of 12 inches from the interior edge of the pavement base drain. Verification and expected performance

The water‑infiltration system is verified through a series of acceptance tests that address compliance, constructability, performance and consistency with the project specifications for each component. For pervious concrete pavement (Section 520), two test sections at least 225 ft² are constructed using the approved mix and placement procedures; each section’s thickness may not be more than ¼‑inch less than the design thickness, the void structure must be 20 % ± 5 %, and the hardened density must fall within a tolerance of ± 5 lb/ft³ and not exceed 130 lb/ft³. Both sections are tested in accordance with ASTM C1701 and the average infiltration rate must exceed 200 inches per hour, providing a direct compliance check on permeability. After the full pavement is placed, three 4‑inch‑diameter cores per 5,000 ft² are removed and tested for oven‑dry density and void content, which must be within ± 5 % of the average voids measured in the accepted test sections, and infiltration tests on three locations per 10,000 ft² are performed according to ASTM C1701 with any area not meeting a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour removed and replaced. All results, including thickness, density, void content and infiltration values, are submitted for Representative acceptance.

For the pipe underdrain and pavement base drain (Section 610), verification begins by confirming that the installed pipe matches the type and size called for in Section 610.2(a)1, with a minimum inside diameter of four inches sized in one‑inch increments. The perforations must provide at least 1.4 square‑inches of water inlet area per linear foot, with hole diameters not less than three‑sixteenths inch, and testing according to AASHTO M 176M must show a minimum infiltration rate of ten gallons per minute per linear foot; pipe strength must also meet the ASTM D2412 requirement. For prefabricated pavement base drains, the core material is checked for the specified tensile strength, water absorption, crush strength and a flow capacity of at least fifteen gallons per minute per foot of width, while the open‑core area must be no less than six percent on the pavement‑side face and three percent on the shoulder‑side face and geotextile coverage must allow unobstructed inflow through at least fifty percent of the pavement‑side fabric and twelve percent of the shoulder‑side fabric. Pipe lengths are measured to within 0.2 % tolerance, handled without stretching, and each shipment is labeled with lot number, manufacturer information, product dimensions and geotextile designation as required by Section 610.2(g). These checks confirm compliance, constructability, performance and consistency with the project requirements.

For pervious asphalt pavement (Section 420), the contractor must construct two test sections on the prepared subgrade using the approved JMF and the same placement and finishing procedures intended for production; each section shall be at least 225 ft² and after compaction its thickness may not be more than ¼‑inch less than the design thickness. Both sections are then tested in accordance with ASTM C1701 and the average infiltration rate must exceed 200 in/hr, providing a direct compliance check on permeability. Constructability is confirmed by following the subgrade preparation, using smooth steel‑wheeled static rollers only and limiting compaction to no more than four passes so that the pavement remains free‑draining. Performance is further assured by maintaining placement temperatures above 50 °F and allowing the mix to cool to 100 °F before subsequent lifts. Consistency with the project specifications is demonstrated when all of these actions—test section size, JMF use, thickness tolerance, infiltration threshold, roller type, compaction pass limit and temperature constraints—are documented and accepted by the Representative.
