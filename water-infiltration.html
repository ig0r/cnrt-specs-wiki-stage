Water Infiltration

Part of: Subsurface Drainage is-subtopic-of
Water Infiltration is a topic under Subsurface Drainage.
water-infiltrationinfiltrationexcess-moisture
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Water infiltration denotes the movement of water, chlorides, and incompressible materials into a pavement system and the suite of engineered controls used to limit that movement [1][2]. Its chief purpose is to preserve material integrity and structural capacity by preventing excess moisture from accumulating in the pavement layers; this is accomplished through a functional drainage network, sealed joints, an undisturbed permeable subgrade, and specially designed pervious concrete or asphalt structures [1][2]. In practice the design requires pervious systems to achieve an average infiltration rate greater than 200 in/hr measured with ASTM C 1701 on two test sections, to maintain a void content of roughly 20 % ±5 % and a hardened density not exceeding 130 lb/ft³, and to limit surface compaction to no more than four static roller passes so the surface remains free‑draining [2]. Complementary measures—daily sealing of constructed or disturbed surfaces with a smooth‑drum steel‑wheel roller, shaping embankments and fills to drain, and specifying underdrain pipe perforations that provide at least 1.4 sq in inlet area per linear foot—ensure continuous positive drainage throughout construction and service life [2].

Purpose and design objectives

Water infiltration is addressed because excess water entering the pavement structure is detrimental to any pavement, leading to loss of material integrity and weakening of structural capacity. Accordingly, the engineering need is to prevent moisture‑related distress by ensuring that water, chlorides, and incompressible materials cannot enter the pavement system. For reconstruction projects such as full‑depth reclamation, this requires a stable underlying subgrade and a functional existing drainage system, with any current drainage problems identified and corrected before work begins. In pervious concrete pavement systems the design intent is to manage storm‑water by allowing it to pass through a permeable concrete layer into the underlying subsoil, thereby reducing runoff and providing temporary detention in an open‑graded angular stone layer. Pervious asphalt pavement systems employ a plant‑mixed asphalt concrete surface that is permeable to water and air together with an open‑graded angular stone layer so that storm‑water infiltrates into the subsoil, reducing runoff and associated distress. The construction provisions for pervious asphalt require that the average infiltration rate of two test sections, measured in accordance with ASTM C 1701, exceed 200 inches per hour; the subgrade is to be excavated and left as undisturbed and permeable as possible, and surface compaction is limited to no more than four static roller passes so that the material remains free‑draining and ponding is avoided. For embankments and fills, water infiltration is mitigated by shaping compacted layers to drain, fine‑grading to eliminate low spots, sealing the surface each workday with a smooth drum roller, and prohibiting edge furrows that would block drainage, all of which reduce water entry into the pavement structure. Subgrade construction further mandates sealing all constructed or disturbed subgrade surfaces at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage and minimize infiltration of surface water. [1][2]

The design must keep water from entering the pavement structure, preventing future infiltration of water, chlorides, and incompressible materials, and it must rely on a functional existing drainage system to achieve that goal. [1] In pervious‑concrete pavement systems the functional requirement is that the average infiltration measured on both test sections and on in‑place pavement shall exceed 200 inches per hour as required by ASTM C 1701, and any area failing to meet this minimum rate shall be removed and replaced. [2] Structurally the concrete must have a void structure of 20 % ±5 % measured according to ASTM C 1754 and an average hardened density within a tolerance of ± 5 pounds per cubic foot that does not exceed 130 pounds per cubic foot. [2] These parameters support the durability objective of maintaining long‑term permeability. [2] Construction shall be prohibited when air temperature falls below 40 °F or rain is forecast to protect safety by preventing loss of mortar that could impair drainage. [2] Serviceability is ensured by preserving the specified void content and density so the pavement continues to accept water without clogging. [2] For pervious‑asphalt systems the functional objective is that water infiltration must exceed 200 in/hr as shown by two test sections each at least 225 ft². [2] The compacted thickness may not be more than 1/4‑inch less than the design thickness, and the subgrade shall be left undisturbed and as permeable as possible, with any eroded or compacted zones scarified to a minimum depth of 6 in and repaired before geotextile placement. [2] Durability is achieved by limiting compaction to no more than four passes of a static steel‑wheeled roller so that the surface remains free draining and aggregate is not crushed. [2] Safety is supported by prohibiting heavy equipment traffic on the subgrade prior to base installation. [2] Serviceability is provided by ensuring the finished pavement retains its designed porosity throughout service. [2] Pipe underdrain systems must provide a minimum infiltration rate of 10 gallons per minute per linear foot of pipe as determined according to AASHTO M 176M, and they shall also provide at least 1.4 square inches water inlet area per linear foot through specified perforations. [2] The perforations are circular with nominal diameters not less than 3/16 inch or greater than 3/8 inch and are arranged in similar rows on both sides of the pipe’s vertical centreline with lower rows separated by an arc of at least 60 degrees to ensure functional water entry. [2] Structurally, pipes 4‑to‑10 inches in diameter must meet minimum stiffness values such as 30 pounds per square inch at 10 % deflection for corrugated polyethylene and 35 pounds per square inch at 5 % deflection for PVC. [2] Durability and serviceability objectives include a tensile strength of at least 1,200 psi, water absorption not exceeding 0.01 % after 24 hours, crush strength of at least 40 psi, a flow rate of at least 15 gallons per minute per foot of width, minimum open core areas of 6 % on the pavement side face and 3 % on the shoulder side face, and the ability to be flattened to 40 % (PVC) or 60 % (ABS) of the original dimension without splitting, cracking, or breaking. [2] For embankment and fill layers the functional role of water infiltration is limited by shaping the layers so they drain during construction and by fine‑grading to eliminate low spots, with each work day the surface sealed with a smooth drum roller to provide continuous positive drainage. [2] Structurally, edges may not contain furrows that block free drainage, and compaction moisture must stay within prescribed limits—soil within –3 % of optimum, Type 1 granular material within ±2 % of optimum, and Type 2 kept surface‑damp—to achieve a stable, non‑rutting condition. [2] These measures support durability by preventing water from entering the pavement structure, enhance safety through a compacted mass that does not rut or displace under equipment, and maintain serviceability by preserving the intended drainage and moisture conditions. [2]

Scope and applicability

Water infiltration is appropriate for rigid pervious concrete pavement systems when plant‑mixed pervious concrete is placed on a prepared surface that is designed to allow storm‑water to infiltrate into the subsoil and when an open‑graded angular stone layer is provided beneath the concrete to serve as temporary storm‑water detention. For these pavements Section 520 requires that two test sections, each having an area of at least 225 sq ft and whose compacted thickness is not more than ¼‑inch thinner than the design thickness, be constructed on a prepared subgrade and infiltrated in accordance with ASTM C 1701; the average infiltration rate for the pair must exceed 200 in/hr. The same minimum average infiltration rate of 200 in/hr applies to field testing performed for every 10 000 sq ft of installed pervious concrete, and any areas that do not meet or exceed this threshold must be removed and replaced. Water infiltration is also appropriate when a pervious asphalt pavement system is specified, meaning a plant‑mixed asphalt concrete surface installed on a prepared base that is designed to allow storm water to infiltrate into the subsoil, with the top layer forming a horizontal pervious plane that permits water and air to pass and an underlying open‑graded angular stone layer providing temporary detention of the infiltrated storm water. Under Section 420, water infiltration is considered appropriate for such systems when test sections—each at least 225 sq ft—are constructed on a prepared subgrade using the approved JMF and placement methods, compacted to a thickness that is not more than ¼‑inch less than the design thickness, and the average infiltration measured according to ASTM C 1701 exceeds 200 in/hr. The approach also requires that the subgrade be left as permeable as possible, that detention basins be protected from sediment and covered with a minimum 2‑in fine aggregate base, and that pervious mixtures not be placed when air or surface temperature is below 50 °F. During construction the mixture must be compacted with smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode for no more than four passes to avoid reducing surface porosity, and no tack coat may be used between lifts. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 299–302), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 409–411)

When excess moisture is present in the pavement structure, including a wet subgrade, or when drainage problems have not been corrected, the suitability of full‑depth reclamation diminishes and other rehabilitation strategies become more appropriate [1]. Likewise, if the observed distress is primarily surface‑related rather than requiring improvement of the support layers, an alternative design approach should be favored because it targets different failure mechanisms [1]. For pervious asphalt pavement systems, placement of the mixture shall not be done when the air or surface temperature is lower than 50 °F; under such cold conditions a design that does not rely on infiltration would be preferred [2]. Moreover, compaction must be limited to no more than four passes with a static steel‑wheeled roller so that the surface remains free‑draining, because exceeding this limit would reduce porosity and could also require an alternative pavement approach [2].

Design inputs and requirements

Water infiltration in portland cement concrete (PCC) pavements is controlled by the joint sealing requirements of Publication 408 Specifications, specifically Sections 512 and 521 which mandate cleaning and sealing of all joints and cracks prior to any patching work. The same specifications require that a sealant reservoir measuring 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch be constructed at each pavement‑to‑shoulder joint and that the reservoir be filled with a rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material. Transverse joints must incorporate reservoirs whose shape factor conforms to the definition provided in Section 521 and illustrated in Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards RC‑20M. For overlay projects, Section 469 of Publication 408 directs that severely spalled joints be cleaned and backfilled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material before the overlay is placed. Compliance with these joint‑sealing provisions, together with a stable subgrade and an operational drainage system, is required to prevent infiltration of water, chlorides, and incompressible materials into the pavement structure. In pervious concrete pavement systems, Section 520.3 of Publication 408/2020 specifies that infiltration testing shall be performed on two mandatory test sections in accordance with ASTM C 1701 and that the average infiltration rate of the two values must exceed 200 inches per hour. Additionally, for every 10,000 square feet of installed pervious concrete, three locations selected according to PTM No. 1 shall be tested by ASTM C 1701, and any concrete that does not achieve a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour shall be removed and replaced. Pervious asphalt pavement systems are governed by Section 420.3, which also requires an average infiltration rate greater than 200 inches per hour measured on two constructed test sections using ASTM C 1701; each test section must have an area of at least 225 square feet and the compacted thickness may not be more than one‑quarter inch less than the design thickness. The subgrade for these systems shall be excavated to undisturbed soil without compaction so that its permeability is retained, and placement activities are prohibited when ambient air or surface temperature falls below 50 °F. All constructed or disturbed subgrade surfaces must be sealed at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel‑wheel roller operated in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage and limit water infiltration. After subgrade acceptance, a Class 4, Type A geotextile separator shall be placed over the entire lane width of eight feet or greater before installation of the subbase; the geotextile must be laid continuously in the longitudinal direction with transverse limits extending between a minimum of six inches and a maximum of twelve inches from the interior edge of the pavement‑base drain, and any damage to the geotextile shall be repaired at no additional cost. Underdrain pipe systems are regulated by Section 610.2, which requires circular or slotted perforations that provide at least 1.4 square inches of inlet area per linear foot, and the assembled pipe must achieve a minimum infiltration capacity of 10 gallons per minute per linear foot as verified by AASHTO M 176M. Prefabricated pavement‑base drains must be capable of delivering a flow rate of at least 15 gallons per minute per foot of width in accordance with ASTM D4716, and they must incorporate minimum open‑core areas of six percent on the pavement side face and three percent on the shoulder side face. Embankment and fill layers shall be shaped to drain during construction; low spots must be fine‑graded and the surface sealed each workday with a smooth drum roller to maintain continuous positive drainage and reduce infiltration, while furrows at edges that would impede free surface‑water drainage are prohibited. The speed of compaction equipment on embankment or fill shall not exceed 1.5 feet per second, and moisture contents must remain within the specified limits—soil within three percent below optimum moisture and Type 1 granular material within plus or minus two percent of optimum—to achieve a stable, low‑permeability mass. The compaction of pervious mixes is limited to no more than four roller passes in order to preserve free‑draining porosity. [1][2]

Design methods and criteria

To limit future infiltration of water, chlorides, and incompressible materials into the pavement structure, the CPR guidelines prescribe a series of joint‑focused procedures that include joint rehabilitation to create new sealant reservoirs and reseal transverse or expansion joints; longitudinal joint cleaning and sealing on every CPR project; pavement‑shoulder joint sealing that constructs a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir and applies rubberized/asphalt sealant; transverse joint cleaning and sealing that must meet the shape‑factor requirements of Section 521; crack sealing in accordance with specifications for full‑depth or partial‑depth cracks; dowel bar retrofits that install epoxy‑coated dowels across cracks or joints to restore load transfer; and cross‑stitching that places epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle across longitudinal cracks or joints to achieve near‑complete load transfer. [1] Water infiltration performance for pervious concrete pavement is verified by constructing two test sections each having an area of at least 225 ft² with the approved mix, then conducting infiltration testing in accordance with ASTM C 1701 and requiring that the average of the two infiltration values exceed 200 inches per hour. [2] Additional infiltration tests are performed on three locations for every 10 000 ft² of placed concrete also following ASTM C 1701, and any area that does not meet or exceed a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour shall be removed and replaced. [2] Void content is measured by ASTM C 1754 with a target void structure of 20 % ±5 %, while hardened density is determined by the same standard with an acceptance tolerance of ±5 lb/ft³ and not to exceed 130 lb/ft³. [2] For pervious asphalt pavement, two test sections each at least 225 ft² are built with the approved jointed‑mortar‑facing (JMF) and placed according to production practices; infiltration testing using ASTM C 1701 must yield an average rate that exceeds 200 in/hr. [2] Subgrade preparation requires excavation to undisturbed soil, scarifying any eroded zones to a minimum depth of 6 inches and avoiding compaction before installing geotextile and stone beds, while detention basins are protected with a minimum 2‑inch layer of fine aggregate wrapped in non‑woven geotextile. [2] During placement the pervious asphalt must be compacted with a static steel‑wheeled roller limited to no more than four passes so that surface porosity is retained. [2] Under pipe underdrain specifications, each perforated or porous pipe must achieve at least a minimum infiltration rate of 10 gallons per minute per linear foot as measured in accordance with AASHTO M 176M. [2] The pipe must provide a minimum water inlet area of 1.4 square inches per linear foot, obtained by cutting circular perforations whose nominal diameter is not less than 3/16 inch and not greater than 3/8 inch arranged in rows on both sides of the vertical centreline with the lowermost rows spaced at least 60 degrees apart, or by using slotted perforations that do not exceed 1/8 inch width and whose length is limited to ten percent of the pipe’s average inside circumference for diameters between 4 inches and 8 inches (or 2½ inches for larger pipes). [2] Pipe material strength shall be verified with the parallel‑plate load test specified in ASTM D2412, while infiltration rate determination follows the AASHTO M 176M procedure. [2] Water infiltration in embankment or fill layers is managed by shaping each layer to drain, fine‑grading to remove low spots and sealing the surface at the end of each workday with a smooth drum roller to promote continuous positive drainage and reduce water infiltration; moisture control requires keeping soil within –3 % of optimum moisture content, Type 1 granular material within ±2 % of optimum moisture content (and surface damp for Type 2), while compaction must reach at least 97 % of maximum dry density (100 % in the top three feet) and compaction equipment shall not operate faster than 1.5 feet per second. [2] During subgrade construction water infiltration is controlled by sealing all constructed or disturbed surfaces at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage; after the completed subgrade has remained in place for more than 60 days or at least one winter season it shall be compacted and proof rolled again before installing a Class 4, Type A geotextile over the entire lane width of eight feet or greater. [2] The geotextile is installed continuously in the longitudinal direction with its transverse limits extending to within a minimum of 6 inches and a maximum of 12 inches from the interior edge of the pavement base drain, and any surface irregularities that exceed 1/2 inch are corrected by loosening, adding or removing material, then re‑compacting and rechecking before acceptance. [2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 149–167), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 301–302), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 410–411), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 506–509)

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, design must prevent future infiltration of water, chlorides and incompressible materials into the pavement structure by cleaning and sealing all joints and cracks when a PCC pavement is patched but not overlaid; no numeric thresholds or safety factors are prescribed for water infiltration in this guidance. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require that pervious concrete pavement systems demonstrate water‑infiltration performance by testing two mandatory test sections, each at least 225 ft², using ASTM C 1701, and the average infiltration value from those sections must exceed 200 inches per hour; for every 10 000 ft² of installed concrete the measured average infiltration rate must meet a minimum of 200 inches per hour or the pavement shall be removed and replaced. For pervious concrete, the mix void content is limited to 20 % ± 5 % to support the required permeability thresholds. Pervious asphalt pavement systems are also required to construct two test sections on the prepared subgrade and base using identical materials and methods as production; after curing each section shall be tested in accordance with ASTM C 1701 and the average of the two infiltration measurements shall exceed 200 inches per hour, and the compacted thickness may not be more than ¼‑inch less than the design thickness. The subgrade for pervious asphalt must be left undisturbed and as permeable as possible; any eroded or fine‑material‑filled areas shall be scarified to a minimum depth of 6 inches before placement of geotextile and stone bed. Section 210 requires that all constructed or disturbed subgrade surfaces be sealed at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel‑wheel roller in static mode to maintain continuous positive drainage and limit water infiltration; surface irregularities greater than ½ inch must be corrected before geotextile placement, and during acceptance testing no more than 10 % of random checks may exceed the ½‑inch threshold, otherwise additional verification measures are triggered. Embankments and fills shall be designed with drainage features that limit water infiltration; layers shall be shaped to drain during construction, low spots must be fine‑graded out, and the surface shall be sealed each workday by rolling with a smooth drum roller to provide continuous positive drainage, while edges may not have furrows that block free surface‑water flow, and moisture content limits require soil to be placed within –3 % of optimum moisture at compaction. Pipe underdrain systems must provide a minimum water inlet area of 1.4 square inches per linear foot of pipe; perforations shall give at least a 10 gallons‑per‑minute infiltration rate per linear foot as measured by AASHTO M 176M, circular holes shall have nominal diameters not less than 3/16 inch and not greater than 3/8 inch with lower rows spaced at least 60° apart, slotted perforations may not exceed 1/8‑inch width, the core of prefabricated pavement‑base drains must maintain a minimum of 6 % open area on the pavement side face and at least 50 % unobstructed inflow through the geotextile face, and a minimum of 3 % open area is required on the shoulder side face. [1][2]

Structural section and dimensions

The Pavement Policy Manual provides only a single explicit geometric control aimed at limiting water infiltration, namely a sealant reservoir that must be sized at 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch and placed at the pavement‑shoulder joint. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania specifications require each pervious concrete or pervious asphalt test section to occupy a minimum plan area of 225 square feet, and the installed slab thickness may not be more than a quarter‑inch less than the design thickness. For subgrade preparation, the same specification mandates that the underlying soil be scarified to at least a depth of 6 inches before placement of the pavement system. At the bottom of any detention basin associated with the pervious pavement, a fine‑aggregate layer must be placed having a minimum thickness of 2 inches. The coarse‑aggregate portion of the base may be installed in lifts whose individual thickness does not exceed 8 inches. In addition, when constructing pervious asphalt, the compacted lifts of the mixture shall be limited to a maximum of 4 inches per lift. Edge forms that define the lateral support for the pavement are required to be rounded with a radius no less than a quarter‑inch. The construction joints within the slab are to be cut to approximately one‑quarter of the slab thickness, providing a controlled path for joint sealing. [1][2]

Materials and mixture design

The pavement design shall call for sealing all joints and cracks with rubberized joint‑sealing material as required by Publication 408 Sections 512 and 521 to prevent water infiltration. For badly spalled joints that are to be overlaid, the voids must be filled with 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material (or an equivalent) in accordance with Publication 408 Section 469. At pavement‑shoulder interfaces a sealant reservoir sized at 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch shall be constructed and the joint sealed with rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material, while transverse‑joint reservoirs must satisfy the shape‑factor criteria set out in Publication 408 Section 521.

For pervious concrete pavement systems water infiltration specifications require an approved mix design and test sections placed to a thickness that cannot be more than 1/4‑inch less than the design thickness; the hardened concrete must achieve a void structure of 20 % ± 5 % and a hardened density that does not exceed 130 lb/ft³ with a tolerance of ± 5 lb/ft³, and infiltration performance must be demonstrated by testing each test section according to ASTM C 1701 with an average infiltration value exceeding 200 inches per hour. The detention basin beneath the pavement must contain at least 2 inches of fine aggregate under a coarse‑aggregate base placed in lifts not greater than 8 inches, and the subgrade shall be left as permeable as possible without compaction.

For pipe underdrain and pavement base drain systems the selected pipe type and size shall provide a circular pipe with a minimum inside diameter of 4 inches sized in increments of not less than 1 inch (or a semicircular pipe with an average inside diameter of 4 5/8 inches); perforations must furnish a minimum water inlet area of 1.4 square inches per linear foot, with circular holes having a nominal diameter not less than 3/16‑inch nor greater than 3/8‑inch and arranged on both sides of the pipe’s vertical centerline with the lowermost rows separated by an arc of at least 60 degrees, while slotted perforations may not exceed 1/8‑inch width. The pipe material shall meet stiffness requirements such that for pipes 4 inches through 10 inches the minimum pipe stiffness is 30 pounds per square inch at 10 % deflection for polyethylene or 35 pounds per square inch at 5 % deflection for PVC, and the infiltration rate for perforated or porous pipe shall be a minimum of 10 gallons per minute per linear foot as determined by AASHTO M 176M.

For prefabricated pavement base drains the core material must have a tensile strength of at least 1,200 pounds per square inch, water absorption not greater than 0.01 % after 24 hours, exhibit no fungal growth, possess a crush strength of at least 40 pounds per square inch, and provide a flow rate of at least 15 gallons per minute per foot of width; the associated geotextile fabric shall be non‑woven and needle punched in accordance with Table A.

For pervious asphalt pavement systems the detention basin shall be covered with a minimum of 2 inches of fine aggregate and its sides wrapped in a non‑woven geotextile fabric, while coarse aggregate must be placed in lifts not exceeding 8 inches. The pervious mixture used in test sections must follow the approved Joint Mix Formula (JMF) and be compacted to within 1/4‑inch of the design thickness; placement is prohibited when air or surface temperature is below 50 °F, the material must be cooled to 100 °F before additional layers are placed, and compaction shall be limited to no more than four roller passes to preserve free drainage. [1][2]

In pervious concrete pavement systems, the mix that will control water infiltration is required to be approved and then used to construct two test sections of at least 225 ft² each; the thickness of those sections may not be more than a quarter‑inch less than the design thickness. The resulting void content shall be 20 % ±5 %, the average hardened density shall not exceed 130 lb/ft³ (±5 lb/ft³), and infiltration testing in accordance with ASTM C1701 must show an average rate that exceeds 200 in/hr. The mix must be placed within 90 minutes after the final introduction of any mix water, and the performance of the test sections becomes the acceptance basis for the remainder of the project. For embankments or fills intended to limit water infiltration, the material is required to come from a single borrow source and be placed in uniform horizontal layers. The compacted thickness of each lift shall not exceed six inches for soil and Type 1 granular material and eight inches for Type 2 granular material. Each lift must be compacted with equipment appropriate to the material classification while keeping roller speed below 1.5 ft/s (1 mph) and maintaining target moisture within –3 % of optimum for soil, ±2 % of optimum for Type 1 granules, or surface‑damp for Type 2; the compaction shall achieve at least 97 % of maximum dry density, with 100 % in the top three feet. The placed layers are to be shaped and fine‑graded to promote drainage during construction, and the surface is sealed each workday with a smooth‑drum roller to provide continuous positive drainage and reduce water infiltration. By adhering to these layer thicknesses, moisture limits, density targets, equipment selection, and drainage shaping, the resulting mixture satisfies the required structural strength, long‑term durability, ease of placement, and performance against water ingress. Structural details and interfaces

To control water infiltration in Portland cement concrete (PCC) pavements, all transverse, longitudinal, and pavement‑shoulder joints as well as full‑depth cracks must be cleaned and sealed with rubberized joint sealant or asphalt wearing‑course material when an overlay is planned; the sealing includes constructing a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir for pavement/shoulder joints and ensuring that the transverse joint sealant reservoir meets the shape‑factor requirements of Specification Section 521 [1]. Joint rehabilitation should be undertaken on joints that are just beginning to show distress, using Type 1 procedures for sawcut joints or Type 2 procedures for metal plate joints, while dowel bar retrofits and cross‑stitching may be employed to restore load transfer when faulting or poor load transfer is observed, provided the existing drainage remains functional [1]. When spalling extends beyond the top half of a slab, partial‑depth spall repairs in accordance with Specification Section 525 or full‑depth longitudinal joint repairs as required by Specification Section 519 are mandated [1].

For pervious concrete pavement systems, construction joints must be placed at locations specified either by tooling into the fresh concrete to approximately one‑quarter of the slab thickness immediately after final finishing with a cross‑rolling tool or by saw cutting once the concrete has attained sufficient compressive strength; any fines generated by sawing must be removed by vacuum, and no reinforcement, load‑transfer systems, transitions, or other connections are prescribed for these joints [2].

Pipe underdrain and pavement base drain components shall consist of prefabricated drains whose inert plastic core is completely wrapped in non‑woven geotextile with the seam running along the length on the shoulder side face, preferably near the midpoint, and fabricated with a non water‑soluble adhesive; standard splice connections and fittings must be provided and be strong enough to withstand construction handling and permanent loading [2]. Perforated pipe used for underdrain shall provide at least a minimum water inlet area of 1.4 square inches per linear foot, with circular holes nominally between 3/16 inch and 3/8 inch in diameter and rows separated by an arc of at least 60 degrees, and the pipe must achieve a minimum infiltration rate of 10 gallons per minute per linear foot [2].

When rock is changed to another fill type in an embankment or fill section, the rock surface shall be choked with select granular material to completely fill all voids, followed by a second lift of select granular material compacted to a thickness of six inches; only one such transition may occur in an embankment or fill section, and each work day the surface should be fine‑graded and sealed by rolling with a smooth drum roller to maintain continuous positive drainage and reduce water infiltration [2].

The subgrade shall be sealed each day with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage and minimize surface water entry, and a Class 4, Type A geotextile separator shall be installed continuously in the longitudinal direction over the finished subgrade before the subbase, with its transverse edges lying within a minimum of six inches and a maximum of twelve inches from the interior edge of the pavement base drain [2].

To limit water infiltration at pavement interfaces, all joints and cracks should be cleaned and sealed with the specified rubberized joint sealing material before any repair that does not involve overlaying; a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir is required for each pavement/shoulder joint and the transverse joint sealant reservoir must meet the shape‑factor criteria set out in the relevant specification, while ensuring the existing drainage system remains functional and the subgrade stays stable [1]. When designing the interfaces for a full‑depth reclamation pavement, the system must be constructed to provide effective drainage so that water does not accumulate at any layer or the subgrade; any existing drainage problems should be identified and corrected before the reclaimed layer is placed, and wet subgrade locations should be located and provided with appropriate drainage measures [1]. For pervious concrete pavements, the subgrade is excavated to undisturbed soil and left un‑compacted so that it remains as permeable as possible; any eroded fine material must be removed and the underlying soil scarified to at least 6 inches depth [2]. The detention basin adjoining the pavement is protected by placing a minimum of 2 inches of fine aggregate on its bottom and wrapping the sides with non‑woven geotextile before installing coarse aggregate lifts, thereby preventing soil migration into the storage area [2]. Construction joints between concrete slabs are formed either by tooling to approximately one‑quarter slab thickness or by saw cutting after sufficient compressive strength is achieved, and any fines generated by sawing must be removed by vacuum to avoid clogging the void system [2]. All test sections and in‑place pavement must achieve an average infiltration rate that exceeds 200 inches per hour as verified by ASTM C 1701 testing [2]. To control water infiltration at the interface between the pavement surface and the underlying structure, the design shall incorporate perforated or porous drainage pipes that meet the Section 610.2 material requirements; a circular pipe must have a minimum inside diameter of 4 inches, be sized in increments of at least 1 inch, and provide a water inlet area of at least 1.4 square inches per linear foot together with an infiltration rate of not less than 10 gallons per minute per linear foot [2]. Perforations must be clean, either circular (diameter between 3/16 in and greater than 3/8 in) or slotted as limited in width and length [2]. Prefabricated pavement base drains shall consist of an inert plastic core wrapped completely with a non‑woven needle‑punched geotextile, offering a minimum open core area of 6 % on the pavement side face and 3 % on the shoulder side face, and must allow unobstructed inflow through at least 50 % of the geotextile area on the pavement side [2]. The surrounding backfill shall be fine aggregate Type A sand as specified in Section 703.1 to ensure proper drainage continuity [2]. Interfaces between pavement layers, fill materials, structures and adjacent features should be shaped to drain during construction, fine‑graded to eliminate low spots and sealed each work day with a smooth drum roller to promote continuous positive drainage and reduce water infiltration, and any edges must be free of furrows that would block surface‑water runoff [2]. In the pervious asphalt system, interfaces are protected by applying a thin, uniform coating of an approved emulsified asphalt to any existing vertical surfaces such as curbs, structures, gutters, or adjacent pavements that will contact the new mixture; this coating is placed in two or more applications [2]. Between lifts of the pervious asphalt no tack coat is used, and the subgrade is allowed to remain as permeable as possible by avoiding compaction before placement of geotextile and stone bed while repairing any damaged areas to the Representative’s satisfaction [2]. The detention basin base is wrapped with non‑woven geotextile and covered with a minimum of 2 inches of fine aggregate to prevent soil migration, and coarse aggregate is installed in lifts not exceeding 8 inches before compaction as specified [2]. Compaction of the pervious asphalt is limited to no more than four passes with a 10‑ton steel‑wheeled static roller to preserve surface porosity [2]. To limit water infiltration at layer interfaces, subgrade surfaces that have been constructed or disturbed must be sealed each workday using a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller in static mode to create continuous positive drainage; after the subgrade has cured for more than 60 days or one winter season it is compacted and proof rolled before a Class 4, Type A geotextile separator is placed over the entire lane width (8 ft or greater) prior to the subbase [2]. The geotextile must be installed continuously in the longitudinal direction with its transverse edges extending between 6 inches and 12 inches from the interior edge of the pavement base drain, and it must not be damaged during base‑drain installation [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

In the Pavement Policy Manual, section 4.3 JOINTS, controlling water infiltration begins with verifying that the subgrade is stable and that the existing drainage system is functional, which prevents moisture from entering the pavement structure [1]. The manual further requires that all joints and cracks to be patched but not overlaid be cleaned and sealed with the specified rubberized joint sealing material, creating effective barriers against water, chlorides, and incompressible materials [1]. For pavement‑shoulder interfaces the seal includes a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir filled with rubberized or asphalt sealant, further limiting infiltration under varying environmental conditions [1]. In section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES > B of the Pavement Policy Manual, any existing drainage problems must be identified and corrected before constructing the reclaimed pavement layer, and wet subgrade locations must be identified and effective drainage installed prior to full‑depth reclamation [1]. The same section also directs that other water‑related damage within the existing pavement layers be evaluated to determine its source and a corrective solution applied before reclamation because excess moisture in the structure, including the subgrade, is detrimental to material integrity and structural capacity [1]. In the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania DEPARTMENT OF TRANSPORTATION Publication 408/2020 SPECIFICATIONS, Section 520 (PERVIOUS CONCRETE PAVEMENT SYSTEM) requires excavation of the subgrade to undisturbed soil without compaction, removal of fine material accumulation, and scarification of any compacted zones at least six inches deep, followed by wetting the subgrade or subbase so that it is saturated but without standing water before concrete placement [2]. A detention basin consisting of a fine‑aggregate base, geotextile wrap, and properly placed coarse aggregate lifts provides sediment control and storage, and fresh pavement must be covered immediately with polyethylene sheeting secured against wind for a minimum of seven days; curing must begin within twenty minutes of discharge [2]. Placement of pervious concrete is prohibited when air temperatures are projected to fall below 40 °F or when rain is forecast within the twenty‑four hour period after placement, and any rainfall that does occur requires work to stop, formation of a construction joint, and re‑application of the curing sheet [2]. In Section 420 (PERVIOUS ASPHALT PAVEMENT SYSTEM), an undisturbed, minimally compacted subgrade is prepared in the same manner with scarification at least six inches where needed, and a detention basin is protected by a minimum of two inches of fine aggregate on the base and sides wrapped in non‑woven geotextile [2]. Pervious asphalt mixtures may not be placed when air or surface temperature is lower than 50 °F, and compaction is limited to no more than four passes with a ten‑ton steel‑wheeled static roller on a cool enough surface so that the pavement remains free draining [2]. Section 210 (SUBGRADE) requires sealing all constructed or disturbed surfaces each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage and limit surface‑water entry, and surface irregularities exceeding one‑half inch are corrected before further layers to preserve proper cross‑slope for runoff [2]. After the subgrade has remained in place for more than sixty days or at least one winter season, it is re‑compacted and proof rolled prior to installing a Class 4, Type A geotextile separator, which adds an additional barrier against moisture migration [2]. Section 206 (EMBANKMENT AND FILL) mandates that each layer be shaped and compacted so that it drains during construction; low spots are removed by fine grading and the surface is sealed at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum roller, and furrows that would block free drainage are prohibited [2]. Moisture conditions in the same specification are controlled by keeping soil within –3 % of its optimum moisture content, maintaining Type 1 granular material within ±2 % of optimum, placing Type 2 granular material in a surface‑damp condition but not wet, and ensuring that wet or frozen material is not compacted while any excess moisture is removed before compaction; where the foundation is saturated or unstable, rock fill is required to achieve a stable, well‑drained base [2]. Pipe underdrain design in Section 610 specifies circular pipe with a minimum inside diameter of four inches sized in increments of at least one inch, perforated to provide a minimum water inlet area of 1.4 square inches per linear foot using holes nominally not less than three‑sixteenths inch and not greater than three‑eighths inch, delivering a minimum infiltration rate of ten gallons per minute per linear foot as measured by AASHTO M 176M [2]. Prefabricated pavement base drains feature an inert plastic core with water absorption of 0.01 % after twenty‑four hours (ASTM D570), crush strength of at least forty pounds per square inch, flow rate of at least fifteen gallons per minute per foot of width, and minimum open‑core areas of six percent on the pavement side face and three percent on the shoulder side face; geotextile fabric permits unobstructed inflow through at least fifty percent of its area on the pavement side and twelve percent on the shoulder side, with fine aggregate Type A used for backfill [2].

The Pavement Policy Manual indicates that water infiltration control in Portland cement concrete pavement is achieved by cleaning and sealing joints, specifically by constructing a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir at the pavement‑shoulder joint and providing transverse joint reservoirs that satisfy the shape‑factor criteria of Publication 408 Section 521; it does not prescribe any accessibility, safety, or user requirements for water infiltration [1]. Verification and expected performance

To verify that the completed water‑infiltration system meets the project’s compliance, constructability, performance, and consistency requirements, separate checks are applied for each component of the pavement. For pervious concrete (Section 520), two test sections of at least 225 sq ft each are constructed using the approved mix and placement procedures; infiltration testing on both sections is performed in accordance with ASTM C 1701 and the average of the two values must exceed 200 in/hr. The test sections also must satisfy thickness limits of no more than a ¼‑inch reduction from design, a void content of 20 % ± 5 %, and a hardened density within ±5 lb/ft³ and not exceeding 130 lb/ft³, which together confirm constructability and consistency with the specifications. After the full pavement is placed, infiltration testing is carried out on three locations per every 10 000 sq ft of pervious concrete using ASTM C 1701; any area that does not meet a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 in/hr must be removed and replaced, and all test results are documented for acceptance. For pervious asphalt (Section 420), two test sections covering at least 225 ft² each are built on a prepared subgrade and base with the approved joint‑mix formula, placement, and finishing operations; infiltration testing in accordance with ASTM C 1701 must produce an average rate exceeding 200 in/hr, and the compacted thickness cannot be more than a ¼‑inch less than the design thickness, thereby confirming compliance with infiltration, dimensional, constructability, and performance criteria. For pipe underdrain and pavement base drain systems (Section 610), each installed circular pipe must have a minimum inside diameter of 4 inches (or be sized in not less than 1‑inch increments) and its perforations must provide at least 1.4 sq in of water inlet area per linear foot with hole diameters no smaller than 3/16 in and no larger than 3/8 in, the lower rows spaced at least 60° apart (or slotted openings not exceeding 1/8 in width). The tested infiltration rate for each pipe must be a minimum of 10 gallons per minute per linear foot. Pipe lengths are verified against the allowed maximums—flexible plastic pipe may be supplied in coils up to 4 000 ft and rigid pipe not exceeding 20 ft—and measured with a tolerance of 0.2 % while stress‑free on a flat surface. For prefabricated pavement base drains, verification includes confirming at least a minimum of 6 % open core area on the pavement side face, at least 50 % unobstructed geotextile area on that face, and a flow capacity not less than 15 gallons per minute per foot of width. Documentation of material labeling, certification, and use of fine‑aggregate Type II sand as backfill completes the constructability and performance check to ensure consistency with the project requirements.
