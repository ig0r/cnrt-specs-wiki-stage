Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement is a topic under Rigid Pavement.

Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-30

Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement (JPCP) is a rigid Portland‑cement concrete roadway system designed to provide a durable, smooth riding surface while accommodating the inevitable shrinkage, thermal expansion and moisture loss of individual slabs through a network of closely spaced transverse contraction joints—typically fifteen feet apart and cut at least one‑third slab depth—and load‑transfer devices such as coated dowel bars that maintain structural continuity across those joints[1][2]. The design limits panel dimensions to an overall size no greater than eighteen times the slab thickness with aspect ratios near unity, restricts vertical differentials to a quarter‑inch and edge slumping to one‑eighth inch, requires a minimum concrete compressive strength of about three thousand psi before traffic opening, and mandates prompt sealing of joints in 0.75 in × 0.75 in reservoirs to prevent water infiltration and preserve load transfer[1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement is designed to meet the fundamental need for a durable, smooth riding surface on long‑length rigid concrete roadways while counteracting the inevitable movements of individual slabs caused by shrinkage, thermal expansion and contraction, and moisture loss. [1][2] Uncontrolled cracking, edge slumping, excessive vertical differentials, and joint deterioration would otherwise degrade ride quality, structural continuity and durability, leading to premature pavement failure. [2] To direct where fractures occur, the guides prescribe relatively close transverse joints—typically spaced about fifteen feet apart and cut to a depth of at least one‑third the slab thickness—with joints laid perpendicular to traffic flow and limited longitudinal joints placed away from wheel paths. [1][2] Panel dimensions are limited so that aspect ratios approach unity, not exceeding an overall size of roughly eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness, thereby reducing the propensity for random or premature cracking. [2] The design also limits the finished vertical differential across any joint to a quarter‑inch and restricts edge slump between adjacent lanes to no more than one‑eighth inch, controls concrete temperature at placement to below eighty degrees Fahrenheit, and requires a minimum compressive strength of three thousand psi before traffic opening, all to ensure that slabs can sustain loads without loss of smoothness or structural integrity. [2] Load‑transfer devices such as coated dowel bars positioned within one inch of the joint centreline provide continuous load paths while permitting the controlled movement required by the joints. [1][2] Effective sealing of the transverse joints limits water infiltration and preserves load transfer capability, with joint rehabilitation most cost‑effective when performed at the first sign of minor distress. [1] When heavy, channeled loads compromise load transfer, dowel‑bar retrofits or cross‑stitching are prescribed to restore joint performance. [1] For reconstruction projects extending five hundred feet or more in Portland cement concrete, the pavement must be rebuilt in accordance with the relevant construction standards that reinforce these joint requirements and mandate proper sealing. [1] The outer edge of the pavement is protected by tied concrete shoulders, which increase overall stiffness, reduce differential heaving, settlement and horizontal separation, thereby lowering deflection magnitudes, extending service life, reducing maintenance demands and enhancing safety relative to alternative shoulder types. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–166); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–474)

Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement must provide a smooth riding surface while allowing effective drainage, and this is achieved by promptly resealing joints at the first sign of distress and by incorporating sealant reservoirs sized to 0.75 in × 0.75 in to keep water, chlorides and incompressible materials from entering the slab structure [1][2]. Structurally the design must guarantee adequate load transfer across both transverse and longitudinal joints, with coated dowel bars fully bonded and anchored using epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders and joint spacing prescribed at fifteen feet with joints placed perpendicular to traffic; when faulting or poor load transfer is observed, the underlying support must be restored or additional dowels installed [1]. Panel dimensions are limited so that no panel exceeds eighteen times the overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter side ratio does not exceed 1.5, with a preference for an aspect ratio close to unity [2]. Transverse joints must be saw‑cut to at least one‑third of slab depth (one‑quarter depth away from edges) and have a finished width between one‑quarter and one‑half inch; longitudinal joints are kept out of wheel paths and shall not exceed three‑quarters inch in width, with all joint locations placed within ±1/8 in of the design line [2]. Dowel bars are installed with bond‑breaker lubricant applied at least an hour before concrete placement, tie bars must satisfy pull‑out resistance criteria, and any ungrouted joints are shimmed and sealed prior to traffic [2]. Concrete strength requirements vary with mix type but the structural design calls for a minimum compressive strength of 4 925 psi and a flexural strength of 650 psi; slabs may not be opened to traffic until the slab reaches at least 1 000 psi (accelerated mixes require a minimum of 1 200 psi, standard mixes are typically specified for 3 000 psi or higher) [2]. Grout used around dowels must achieve at least 2 500 psi before traffic is permitted [2]. Durability requirements call for avoidance of high early‑strength concrete mixes because they tend to produce excessive shrinkage cracking; instead a mix that limits early shrinkage should be used together with appropriate joint sealing, and curing must begin within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates using curing compounds or insulating blankets when ambient temperatures are low, while concrete temperature is limited to below 160 °F and hourly temperature change is restricted to forty degrees Fahrenheit [1][2]. Safety is enhanced by maintaining the integrity of joints and shoulders to prevent slab migration or loss of traction, employing tied concrete shoulders that reduce differential heave, settlement and horizontal separation, limiting saw cuts to full‑depth openings no wider than one‑half inch, sealing all joints before traffic, constructing ramps for vehicle entry and exit, taking positive action to protect the pavement surface from rain, debris or equipment damage, and providing a macro‑texture depth of at least 0.040 in for skid resistance [1][2]. Serviceability is preserved through sufficient slab stiffness to keep deflections low, edge slumping limited to one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes and one‑quarter inch between lane and shoulder, vertical differentials across any joint not exceeding one‑quarter inch, surface variation not more than three‑sixteenth inch when measured with a straightedge both longitudinally and transversely, correction of high or low spots by grinding, and the reduced edge stresses and lower maintenance needs resulting from the stiff, well‑tied system further support safe traffic operations [1][2]. Together these functional, structural, durability, safety and serviceability objectives ensure that Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement performs as intended throughout its design life.

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–166); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–474)

Scope and applicability

Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement (JPCP) is suitable for reconstruction projects of Portland cement concrete where at least five hundred feet of pavement is being rebuilt, whether the work involves a full‑width rebuild of all lanes or a single‑lane replacement. For full‑width reconstructions the design follows RC‑27M, requiring fifteen‑foot joint spacing, perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals; for a one‑lane project an intermediate joint must be added when existing joint spacing exceeds twenty feet so that the same RC‑27M criteria are met. The district may elect JPCP in place of jointed reinforced concrete pavement because reinforcement does not add strength.

JPCP is also appropriate for widening projects when the existing pavement being expanded is a JPCP. Partial lane widenings less than ten feet must match the original pavement type and slab depth, using plain cement concrete; full‑lane additions can likewise use JPCP provided the original pavement is JPCP and traffic loading does not require a heavier design, with thickness matched to the existing slab.

In addition, JPCP is the preferred material for plain‑cement‑concrete shoulders that are tied to a concrete mainline pavement, especially in Long‑Life Concrete Pavement schemes where durability and reduced maintenance are required. Urban interstate or expressway projects call for full‑depth PCC shoulders with dowelled transverse joints, while rural interstates that do not need shoulder support for work‑zone traffic may employ tied concrete shoulders using undowelled transverse joints constructed as JPCP. When the two‑way average daily traffic is under ten thousand vehicles, widened slabs with asphalt or reinforced cement concrete shoulders can be considered as alternatives. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–96), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–166)

When the deteriorated segment extends roughly five hundred feet and the remaining intact slab between patches would be ten feet or less, repairing with intermittent jointed plain concrete slabs is ineffective; a full‑width reconstruction of the entire length becomes the preferred solution. For reconstructions that span five hundred feet or more, designers may elect either jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) or precast concrete paving systems (PCPS) instead of standard JPCP when traffic volumes are high, congestion severe, or rapid reopening is required, because these alternatives can be installed faster and provide enhanced safety. However, the higher unit cost of PCPS limits its use to situations where detour routes are unavailable or there are significant maintenance‑of‑traffic (MPT) concerns. In addition, when early traffic opening is a priority, accelerated concrete should replace high‑early‑strength concrete, which is prone to shrinkage cracking.

Separate from the slab pavement, the choice of shoulder type also dictates when JPCP is not optimal. If there is a strong likelihood that the shoulder will be needed for incident management on a high‑volume corridor or for future work‑zone traffic control, a full‑depth cement concrete (PCC) shoulder with dowelled transverse joints is required rather than a standard tied JPCP shoulder. Urban interstates and expressways must employ such full‑depth PCC shoulders, whereas rural routes without anticipated incident‑management use may retain tied JPCP shoulders with undowelled joints. Finally, on roadways where the two‑way average daily traffic falls below ten thousand vehicles, widened concrete slabs supported by asphalt or roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) shoulders become an acceptable alternative to a full JPCP shoulder system. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–166)

Design inputs and requirements

Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement design must incorporate site‑specific temperature and exposure data to size panels and locate expansion joints, material specifications that meet the required minimum compressive (4,925 psi) and flexural (650 psi) strengths, geotechnical information on subbase condition requiring removal of soft or wet material and placement of compacted No. 2A aggregate, and detailed existing‑condition surveys to establish surface elevations and slab dimensions based on longitudinal joint locations. Traffic considerations are limited to construction sequencing such as direction of repair work and the requirement that slabs only be opened to traffic after grout achieves its specified strength. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement must be designed and constructed in accordance with the state roadway construction standards, specifically the sections that govern JPCP, and the rigid‑pavement specifications that prescribe the detailed technical requirements. Joint spacing is limited to fifteen feet, with transverse joints cut perpendicular to traffic flow, and new work must align its transverse joints with existing ones; when original joint spacings exceed twenty feet the new spacing is constrained between ten and seventeen feet to maintain panel dimensions within acceptable limits. The slab length may not exceed fifteen feet, extending to twenty feet only for prestressed sections, while the minimum slab length is six feet and panel dimensions are restricted to no more than eighteen times the slab thickness with an aspect‑ratio ceiling of 1.5. Transverse contraction joints are sawed to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness, with a width not less than one‑quarter inch and not greater than one‑half inch, and longitudinal joint widths may not exceed three‑quarters inch; all joint faces must be free of curing compound before sealant is applied. Load transfer across joints relies on coated dowel bars that are anchored with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders, installed with vertical or horizontal tilt limited to 0.25 inch, translation limited to one inch, seated on non‑metallic chairs providing at least a half‑inch clearance, and surrounded by peripheral voids no larger than half an inch; tie bars are required to pass pull‑out testing at a concrete strength of 3,500 psi, resisting either twelve thousand pounds or a slip of one‑thirty‑second inch. Joint sealing uses rubberized joint‑sealing material applied into reservoirs sized 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch, and all joints must be sealed before traffic is permitted unless an approved protective approach is used. When an overlay is planned, spalled joints are cleaned and filled with a 4.75 mm asphalt wearing course as prescribed for the overlay preparation. The concrete mix must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a flexural strength of 650 psi, with traffic opening allowed only after the slab reaches at least 3,000 psi (or 1,000 psi when verified by maturity testing). The cement factor for Class AA slip‑form concrete is required to lie between 530 and 600 lb/yd³, the water‑cement ratio may not exceed 0.42 (targeted at 0.40 and never below 0.38), and air content at placement is limited to 7 % ± 1.5 %. Acceptable coarse aggregates are Type A stone or gravel, while reactive aggregates may be used only if their 14‑day expansion falls between 0.10 % and 0.30 % and they are incorporated in a cement‑supplementary material blend meeting the prescribed cement content. Placement temperatures must remain within fifty to ninety degrees Fahrenheit; concreting is halted when ambient temperature drops below forty degrees (or forty‑five for accelerated mixes), and a temperature‑control plan is required for placements above eighty degrees. Evaporation rates may not exceed 0.20 lb/ft²·hr unless mitigation measures are employed, and curing must begin within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates, with insulating blankets applied when air temperature is forty‑five degrees or lower and surface temperature changes limited to forty degrees per hour. Slabs thicker than six inches require application of a white PAMS liquid membrane at a rate of at least one gallon per 150 ft². Surface quality tolerances are tightly controlled: edge slump may not exceed one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes, overall surface deviation shall not exceed three‑sixteenths of an inch measured with a straightedge parallel to the centreline, and macrotexture must achieve a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 inch for tight‑radius applications. At least fifty‑five percent of the concrete must meet the specified compressive strength or plastic air‑content limits; any pavement exhibiting depressions, high points, honeycombing, rain damage, improper joint construction, curing defects, or other unrepairable surface deficiencies is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced. Project constraints require a pre‑paving quality‑control plan approved at least two weeks before work, plant‑mixed concrete unless an exemption is granted, verification that each truckload meets aggregate gradation limits, and documentation of any engineering‑judgment exemptions for deviations from the prescribed criteria. Excavated patch areas may not remain unpatched for more than forty‑eight hours, traffic on newly saw‑cut patches is permitted only after seventy‑two hours, slots between cuts must be cleaned within twenty‑four hours, and final cleaning must be completed no later than thirty minutes before concrete placement; ramps are required for vehicles entering or leaving the slab, and all joints must be sealed prior to opening unless an approved protective method is used. Widening or lane additions shall use plain cement concrete constructed in accordance with Class AA cement concrete policies, matching original joint spacing and slab thickness where feasible, and any deviation from these criteria requires documented engineering justification. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–96), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–166); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 360–474)

Design methods and criteria

The design of jointed plain concrete pavement begins with a traffic analysis in which the average initial truck factor values tabulated for rigid pavements are selected and applied to compute equivalent single axle loads that represent the anticipated future traffic volume[1]. Those ESAL figures are then entered into the DARWin software, whose secondary screening module reproduces the rigid‑pavement design workflow and draws thickness recommendations from guidance tables that specify the overlay depth required for the calculated traffic levels[1]. In parallel, geometric limits derived from empirical studies constrain panel dimensions to no more than eighteen times the slab thickness and require the longer‑to‑shorter side ratio to be ≤1.5, with a preference for near‑unity aspect ratios[2]. Joint layout follows approved plans that limit nominal joint width to a maximum of ½ inch (minimum ¼ inch) and typically target an actual cut width of about 1/8 inch; transverse joints are saw‑cut to at least one‑third the slab thickness within 12 inches of an edge and to at least one‑quarter thickness elsewhere, while longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths[2]. The concrete mix is trial‑tested to achieve a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a flexural strength of 650 psi, with slabs cast a quarter inch thicker than the nominal design thickness and placed at fresh‑mix temperatures between 50 °F and 90 °F; evaporation rates are limited to 0.20 lb/ft²·h and placement is prohibited when ambient temperature falls below 40 °F (45 °F for accelerated‑strength concrete)[2]. Placement is performed with a fully energized, self‑propelled slip‑form paver equipped with side forms to prevent edge slumping, allowing the concrete to be laid in one or more fully consolidated layers[2]. Reinforcement consists of clean steel dowel bars positioned so that transverse saw cuts fall within ±1 inch of the dowel centreline, with misalignment, tilt and skew each limited to 0.25 inch and a longitudinal clearance of at least 0.5 inch from the slab surface; tie bars are selected from the first day’s placement and tested for pull‑out resistance after the concrete reaches at least 3,500 psi compressive strength[2]. Surface smoothness is verified with a 12‑foot straightedge and edge slump between adjacent lanes is limited to 1/8 inch prior to curing completion, while curing proceeds under the conditions specified in the quality‑control plan and all joints must be sealed before traffic is permitted[2]. The pavement may be opened to traffic only after the concrete attains a minimum compressive strength of 3,000 psi, verified either by standard cylinder testing or by the maturity method prescribed in AASHTO T‑413, with an initial inspection occurring at 1,000 psi; grout used to fill dowel slots must reach at least 2,500 psi before loading[2]. Sealant application is restricted to ambient temperatures between 40 °F and 90 °F, requires heating to the manufacturer’s specified pouring temperature, and the sealant reservoir—typically 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch for pavement‑shoulder joints or one inch deep by one‑half inch wide for relief joints—is filled leaving a small opening below the surface[1][2]. Maintenance procedures include joint rehabilitation that restores transverse contraction capability by forming new sealant reservoirs and resealing the joints, partial‑depth repairs when spalling is confined to the upper half of the slab and full‑depth repairs for deeper distress, as well as dowel‑bar retrofits using epoxy‑coated smooth dowels and cross‑stitching with deformed tie bars to achieve near‑complete load transfer across cracks or joints[1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 10 (p. 181); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 360–474)

Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement (JPCP) must satisfy a comprehensive set of geometric, material, construction and quality‑control requirements that together provide the assumed durability, safety and reliability for service. Transverse joints are required to be spaced at fifteen‑foot intervals, perpendicular to traffic flow, and any existing spacing exceeding twenty feet must be reduced by adding an intermediate joint so that effective spacing remains between ten and seventeen feet; widening projects also constrain new joint spacing within this range [1]. Coated dowel bars fully bonded along their embedded length are mandated at each transverse joint to provide load transfer, with end misalignment limited to a quarter‑inch from the bar midpoint and longitudinal or horizontal translation not exceeding one inch [1][2]. Joint and crack sealing must be performed at the first sign of distress using rubberized sealant placed in reservoirs sized 0.75 in × 0.75 in (or, where specified, a one‑inch deep by one‑half‑inch wide reservoir filled to within one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the surface) and applied only when ambient temperature is between forty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit [1][2]. The subgrade must be stable and adequately drained to prevent water, chlorides or incompressible materials from entering the concrete structure [1]. Full‑width reconstructions of five hundred feet or more are governed by Publication 72M RC‑27M, which may require increased slab thickness after a pavement design analysis to accommodate longer rebuilds [1]. For lane additions ten feet wide or greater the original slab thickness is retained unless heavier traffic justifies redesign; partial widenings under ten feet must duplicate the existing pavement type and depth, aligning new joints with the original layout [1]. Concrete shoulders tied to the mainline are mandatory for Long‑Life Concrete Pavement, with urban interstates requiring full‑depth PCC shoulders with dowelled transverse joints, while rural routes may use undowelled JPCP shoulders or dowelled shoulders depending on incident‑management needs, and corridors with average daily traffic below ten thousand vehicles may employ asphalt or RCC shoulders [1]. Any asphalt concrete overlay must be designed using the AC Overlay of JPCP method, applying average initial truck factors for ESAL calculations and determining future‑traffic pavement thickness through the DARWin secondary screen guided by the appropriate tables [1]. The concrete mix shall achieve a minimum compressive strength of 4 925 psi and a flexural strength of 650 psi, with each slab poured a quarter‑inch thicker than the nominal design thickness to provide a sacrificial layer; cement content is limited to between 530 and 600 lb/yd³, the water‑cement ratio may not exceed 0.42 (target 0.40, lower bound 0.38), and plastic air content must be 7 % ± 1.5 % [2]. When supplementary cementitious material is used, at least 400 lb/yd³ of Portland cement must remain in the mix and slag may not exceed thirty percent of the total cementitious weight; aggregate expansion limits and gradation tolerances are also prescribed [2]. Panel dimensions are limited so that the longest side does not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness, and the aspect ratio (longer side divided by shorter side) shall be no greater than 1.5, preferably close to unity [2]. Transverse joint width must be between one‑quarter and one‑half inch, longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters inch, and the finished vertical differential across any joint is limited to a quarter inch; saw cuts defining joints must penetrate at least one‑third of slab depth within twelve inches of an edge and at least one‑fourth depth elsewhere, with full‑depth longitudinal cuts restricted to a half‑inch maximum width [2]. The finished surface may not vary more than three‑sixteenths inch when measured with a straightedge parallel to the centreline, and macrotexture shall achieve a mean depth of at least 0.040 inch [2]. Slots for dowels are cut at mid‑depth, cleaned within thirty minutes before concrete placement and sealed with air pressure of at least one hundred psi; lubricants must be applied at least one hour prior, chairs provide a minimum half‑inch clearance and expansion caps leave a quarter‑inch gap [2]. Embedded tie bars must develop a pull‑out resistance of twelve thousand pounds or limit slip to 1/32 inch once the concrete reaches 3 500 psi, as verified by center‑pull testing [2]. Concrete temperature at placement must be maintained between fifty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit and may not exceed eighty degrees without a temperature‑control plan; ambient air temperature must not fall below forty degrees (forty‑five for accelerated mixes), evaporation rates may not exceed 0.20 lb/ft²·h unless mitigated, and surface temperature changes during curing are restricted to no more than forty degrees per hour [2]. Formwork deflection under a 1 700‑pound center load over a four‑foot span may not exceed one hundredth of an inch, and edge slumping is limited to one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and one‑quarter inch between lane and shoulder [2]. A formal quality‑control plan must monitor mixing, curing, joint sawing, sealing and testing; for daily placements exceeding seven hundred square yards the plan must include nondestructive verification of longitudinal‑transverse unit alignment for at least the first five transverse joints across the pavement width and one joint per 2 640 linear feet [2]. Acceptance requires that no more than a half‑inch deviation exist below design thickness, that at least fifty‑five percent of cores meet the specified compressive strength and air‑content limits, and that surface defects such as rain damage, honeycombing or excessive edge slumping be absent; failure to meet any threshold mandates repair or replacement [2]. The pavement may not be opened to traffic until it attains a minimum compressive strength of three thousand psi (or one thousand psi under controlled early opening) and all joints have been sealed, with the project Representative declaring readiness [2]. Saw cutting for relief‑joint interfaces must occur within seven days after joint installation and only after the asphalt wearing course has cooled below one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit; cuts are limited to a tolerance of plus or minus one‑quarter inch [2]. These criteria embody the assumed safety margins, thresholds, factors of safety and reliability requirements that Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement must satisfy.

Structural section and dimensions

Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement designs call for transverse joints spaced at fifteen feet, placed perpendicular to traffic flow, and the inclusion of coated dowel bars with appropriate seals at each joint [1]. When existing joint spacing exceeds twenty feet and a widening is performed, an intermediate joint must be introduced so that the effective spacing conforms to the fifteen‑foot requirement, while new slabs laid in such projects are limited to lengths between ten and seventeen feet; if the original spacing is twenty feet or less, the new joints follow the pre‑existing pattern [1]. In addition, the guides permit concrete slabs to be fabricated with a minimum length of six feet and a maximum of fifteen feet, extending to twenty feet where prestressed concrete is used, and they encourage slab aspect ratios close to unity, not exceeding one‑and‑a‑half, with overall panel dimensions limited to eighteen times the overlay thickness [2]. The slab thickness is required to be cast with an additional quarter‑inch of sacrificial concrete over the nominal design thickness, and for full‑lane widenings ten feet wide or more the slab thickness must match that of the adjoining pavement, whereas partial widenings narrower than ten feet must use the same concrete type and depth as the original structure [1]. Transverse joint widths are limited to a minimum of one‑quarter inch and a maximum of one‑half inch, longitudinal joints may be up to three‑quarters inch wide, and vertical differential across any joint shall not exceed one‑quarter inch [2]. Saw cuts intended for crack control must penetrate at least one‑third of the slab depth when located within twelve inches of an edge and at least one‑quarter of the depth elsewhere [2]. The subbase beneath the concrete slabs is specified to consist of No. 2A coarse aggregate, placed and compacted to a uniform grade that does not vary more than plus or minus half an inch from the design elevation; no explicit dimensional requirements are given for base layers, treated‑soil layers, shoulders, edge supports, or additional reinforcement [2][1]. Reinforcement beyond the coated dowel bars is not prescribed in the guidelines, and no separate dimensions are provided for base, subbase, treated‑soil, shoulder, or edge‑support elements [1][2].

Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement slabs are fabricated slightly thicker than the nominal design by adding a ¼‑inch sacrificial layer, and their lengths are limited to 15 ft (20 ft when prestressed) with a minimum of 6 ft so that transverse joint locations can align with existing joints. Joint gaps are controlled to a minimum of ¼ in and a maximum of ½ in for transverse joints and not more than ¾ in for longitudinal joints, while tie bars are installed along longitudinal joints when the continuous paving length exceeds about 65 ft. The bedding layer is graded with a laser‑controlled screed that can be set to the required cross‑slope and tolerance, ensuring the slab sits within ±⅛ in of the theoretical plane; transitions such as temporary asphalt wedges are provided where vertical drops exceed 2 in. These provisions together satisfy the design criteria for thickness, joint spacing, slope, and tie‑ins. [2]

Materials and mixture design

Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement is placed using Portland‑cement concrete that conforms to the specifications for Class AA cement concrete as defined in Publication 408 Section 704 [1][2]. The mix design calls for a cement factor between 530 and 600 lb per cubic yard, a water‑cement ratio not to exceed 0.42 (target 0.40 and never below 0.38), and an air content of 7 % ± 1.5 % measured at placement [2]. Aggregates must be durable washed stone or gravel meeting No. 57, No. 67, or No. 8 specifications and arranged in an optimized gradation; daily verification by sieve analysis against the Combined Gradation Percent Retained Chart of PTM No. 529 is required, with deviations up to five percent permissible when authorized [2][1]. Any adjustment to aggregate proportions that would alter cement or water content triggers the need for a new mix design and trial batch [2]. Concrete must be plant‑mixed, and use of volumetric or mobile mixers is prohibited unless a written waiver is obtained [2]. The pavement must achieve a minimum compressive strength at least as high as the value required for accelerated concrete in Publication 408 Section 501, which is commonly specified as 4,925 psi for standard placement and may be reduced to 3,000 psi for overlay work when confirmed by cylinder testing or the maturity method [1][2]. Flexural strength must be at least 650 psi, with a minimum of fifty‑five percent of tested samples meeting both compressive and flexural limits [2]. Grout used for dowel and joint bedding must reach a compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic loading [2]. Reinforcement steel shall be clean and free of rust, dirt, oil, grease or other contaminants; only light surface rust is permissible [2]. Load‑transfer dowels of Type B receive a bond‑breaker lubricant applied at least one hour before concrete placement, while Type A dowels require no coating [2]. Joint repair materials consist of a quarter‑inch thick preformed cellular polystyrene filler cut to slot dimensions, non‑metallic expansion caps 1½ inches thick leaving a ¼‑inch clearance from the dowel end, and plastic or non‑metallic chairs providing at least a ½‑inch clearance around the cap [2]. Curing shall be accomplished with an approved method such as white polyethylene sheets, monomolecular film, or a liquid curing compound applied at not less than one gallon per 150 ft² (or one gallon per 125 ft² for overlays) to obtain uniform coverage [2]. Concrete temperature at placement must be maintained between 50 °F and 90 °F, and a temperature‑control plan is required when temperatures exceed 80 °F [2]. Slab thickness may not fall more than half an inch below the design depth, and each slab is cast with a sacrificial quarter‑inch over‑thickness to accommodate finishing [2]. For widening or lane‑addition projects the same Class AA plain cement concrete is required, with new slabs matching the existing uniform depth and original transverse‑joint pattern [1].

Structural details and interfaces

Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement is constructed using a systematic network of transverse contraction joints that are laid perpendicular to the direction of traffic and typically spaced at fifteen‑foot intervals on the original pavement, while the overall panel dimensions are limited to no more than eighteen times the slab thickness and an aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5 : 1 [1][2]. When a lane is added or the pavement is widened, the new transverse joints must be aligned with the existing joint grid; if the original spacing does not exceed twenty feet the added slabs retain the same length as the original ones, whereas for spacings greater than twenty feet the design limits the new slab lengths to between ten and seventeen feet and inserts intermediate joints so that slab lengths remain roughly equal [1]. All transverse joints are created by saw‑cutting across the full width of the slab after the concrete has achieved sufficient strength; the cut must intersect the asphalt wearing course within ± ¼ inch, be made only when surface temperature is below 140 °F, and extend to a depth of at least one‑third of the slab thickness within twelve inches of the edge and at least one‑quarter thickness elsewhere [2]. The finished joint width shall not exceed one‑half inch and shall be no less than one‑quarter inch along its entire length, with a minimum saw‑cut width of one‑eighth inch; longitudinal joints, when required, are limited to three‑quarters inch in width, are avoided in wheel paths, and may be provided as Type L joints for simultaneous lane patches or with a quarter‑inch polystyrene board bond breaker for single‑lane repairs [2]. Six epoxy‑coated dowel bars are placed in each transverse joint, their entire embedded length being bonded with epoxy, polyester, or vinyl‑ester binders to secure them to the surrounding concrete and provide effective load transfer across the joint; the dowels are smooth, lubricated over at least half their length with a bond‑breaker, seated on plastic chairs that maintain a minimum ½‑inch clearance from slot surfaces, and capped with 1½‑inch non‑metallic expansion caps leaving a ¼‑inch gap to the cap bottom [1][2]. Dowel slots are cut to within ± ⅛ inch of the specified locations, three per wheel path (six saw cuts), and holes may be no more than one‑eighth inch larger in diameter than the coated dowel bars; the ends of each dowel must not be tilted or skewed more than 0.25 inch and may not translate longitudinally or horizontally by more than one inch [2]. Where higher transfer efficiency is required across longitudinal cracks or lane‑to‑shoulder joints, cross‑stitching is employed by installing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle across the crack, achieving near‑complete load transfer; continuous tie bars longer than sixty‑five feet are placed according to shop drawings, kept at least twenty‑four inches from any dowelled contraction joint, and omitted adjacent to cracks larger than one‑eighth inch [1][2]. Load‑transfer units, whether dowels or narrow‑mouth surface slots, are secured in place before paving operations, and concrete must be placed over these assemblies within approximately thirty minutes of placement; the vertical and horizontal positions of all load‑transfer units are verified within eighteen hours after each day’s work by an approved nondestructive method [2]. Sealant reservoirs measuring three‑quarters of an inch by three‑quarters of an inch (or a saw‑cut reservoir one inch deep by half an inch wide) are provided at transverse and longitudinal joints, cleaned, dried, and then filled with rubberized or asphalt joint sealants; sealing is performed only when ambient temperature is between forty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit, with the top of the sealant placed ⅛ to ¼ inch below the riding surface and a self‑adhesive tape bond breaker applied over the sealant with an overhang not exceeding one‑eighth inch [1][2]. The pavement shoulders are constructed of plain cement concrete that matches the main slab material; on high‑volume corridors full‑depth concrete shoulders are tied to the main slab through dowelled transverse joints in accordance with Publication 408, while on lower‑traffic rural routes undowelled shoulder joints may be used [1]. At appurtenances such as manholes, inlets, valve boxes, bridges, culverts, or railway‑track interfaces, premolded expansion‑joint filler of at least one‑half inch thickness (three‑quarters inch where slabs meet rigid elements) is installed around the outer sides of structures, with mortar fills and expansion fillers ranging from one‑quarter to three‑quarters inch for railway interfaces [2]. Transitions are required wherever the pavement encounters a vertical drop greater than two inches, using temporary asphalt wedges; at the limits of concrete overlays over existing treated base or concrete pavement, a geotextile debonding layer is placed as specified [2]. All connections and reinforcement details follow the specified standards to ensure continuity of load transfer and structural integrity throughout the pavement system, and a written quality‑control plan approved by the department must define joint curing, protection from moisture loss, the type, quantity and locations of load‑transfer units, and periodic verification procedures [2][1].

The design of every interface in Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement is governed by a coordinated layout that specifies joint spacing, dimensions, load‑transfer devices and material continuity [1][2]. Transverse joints are normally placed at fifteen‑foot intervals perpendicular to traffic, but when a lane or widening of five hundred feet or more is added the new joints must be aligned with the existing pattern; if the original spacing does not exceed twenty feet the same slab length is retained, whereas spacings greater than twenty feet are limited to a minimum of ten feet and a maximum of seventeen feet [1][2]. Longitudinal joints are positioned parallel to the final lane markings and must lie within twelve inches of those markings while avoiding wheel paths [2]. The width of transverse joints is limited to a minimum of one‑quarter inch and a maximum of one‑half inch, whereas longitudinal joint widths may not exceed three‑quarters inch [1][2]. Slab panels are proportioned so that the longer side does not exceed eighteen times the pavement thickness and the length‑to‑width ratio is kept at or below one point five, preferably near unity, to control cracking [2]. The subbase is finished to a firm, even grade within plus or minus one‑half inch of the specified contour and laser‑controlled screeding limits variation over any slab length to no more than one‑quarter inch before concrete placement [2]. Where a new concrete overlay rests on an existing treated base course or concrete pavement, a geotextile debonding layer is placed across the transition to isolate the layers [2]. Load‑transfer units consist of epoxy‑coated dowel bars that extend through the full embedment depth and are bonded in place with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders; the dowels are positioned before concrete placement so that each transverse saw cut falls directly over the bar centre [1][2]. Dowel bars rest on non‑metallic chairs that provide at least a half‑inch clearance from all slot surfaces, and a bond‑breaker coating is applied to at least one‑half of the dowel length unless the coating was applied in the shop; vertical tilt or horizontal skew may not exceed a quarter inch at either end and overall translation is limited to one inch [1][2]. When deficiencies in load transfer are identified, epoxy‑coated smooth dowels are retrofitted across wheel paths and longitudinal cracks may be cross‑stitched with deformed tie bars to approach full transfer [1]. Expansion‑joint fillers are placed through the full slab depth; a premolded filler of at least one‑half inch thickness is used around utilities, while a minimum three‑quarter inch thick filler is required adjacent to rigid structures such as bridges, culverts or rail crossings, with mortar filling any gaps in rail webs and flanges before the filler is installed [1][2]. If a new or patched expansion joint abuts an existing one, a three‑quarters‑inch expansion‑joint material is placed in the adjacent joint [2]. The interface between pavement and shoulder is treated as a tied concrete element; urban interstates require full‑depth plain cement concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints, whereas rural routes may use undowelled joints unless traffic volumes or work‑zone conditions dictate otherwise, and for two‑way average daily traffic below ten thousand widened slabs with asphalt or reinforced cement concrete shoulders may be considered, provided any full‑depth concrete shoulder matches the mainline material gradations from subgrade to surface [1]. Prior to any patching or overlay all joints and cracks are cleaned and sealed; a sealant reservoir measuring 0.75 in by 0.75 in is formed at pavement‑to‑shoulder connections and filled with rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material, while transverse joints receive reservoirs shaped according to the required shape factor [1][2]. Joint sealing follows a sequence of placing a backing material, applying a self‑adhesive tape whose width matches the reservoir, and pouring sealant so that its surface lies between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the finished pavement level; placement is permitted only when ambient temperature is between forty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit unless an exception is authorized [1][2]. Saw cuts that create transverse joints are made to at least one‑third of slab thickness within twelve inches of an edge and to at least one‑quarter thickness elsewhere, ensuring the cut aligns with dowel centres; for interfaces with asphalt relief joints the cut is placed directly over the concrete‑asphalt boundary within plus or minus a quarter inch after the wearing course has cooled below 140 °F and no later than seven days, forming a reservoir one inch deep by half an inch wide [1][2]. After curing, all joint faces are cleaned, backfilled with oversized backer rod and sealed; surface tolerances are verified with a straightedge to three‑sixteenths inch before traffic is permitted [1][2]. Quality‑control procedures require verification of load‑transfer unit vertical and horizontal alignment within eighteen hours after each day’s placement for projects larger than seven hundred square yards, checking at least five transverse joints across the width and one joint per 2 640 linear feet; any dowel bars that fail to meet the specified tolerances are addressed through an action plan before traffic is allowed [1][2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Jointed plain concrete pavement is designed to rest on a stable subgrade while ensuring that the pre‑existing drainage system remains fully operational, thereby preventing water, chlorides or other incompressible materials from infiltrating the pavement structure and removing any soft, wet or unsuitable subbase material that could cause moisture‑related distress. The slabs incorporate blockouts that align with existing utility castings and any planned drainage structures. Concrete mix design is based on the site’s average daily temperature and exposure conditions so that panel dimensions and expansion‑joint spacing correspond to the local thermal regime. During construction the base course is kept moist and its surface temperature maintained below 115 °F by sprinkling water as needed, and concrete placement is prohibited when the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·h unless windbreaks or fog‑misting are employed to mitigate rapid drying. In hot weather the temperature of plastic concrete must be kept between 50 °F and 90 °F, with mixing water or aggregates cooled as required, and placement is halted if ambient temperatures rise above 90 °F; conversely, when air temperature falls below 50 °F high‑low thermometers are placed on the slab surface and, if readings drop into the 40–35 °F range, the cure period is extended by an additional day, with work considered defective below 35 °F. Cold‑weather concreting ceases when ambient temperature reaches 40 °F (45 °F for accelerated‑strength concrete) and resumes only after temperatures rise above those limits. After placement the slab temperature may not exceed 160 °F during curing, and rain protection must be provided before the concrete sets, with edges and surfaces covered immediately when precipitation threatens. All joints are cleaned, dried and sealed after sawing using a rubberized joint‑sealing material placed in sealant reservoirs sized 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch, thereby preventing water intrusion and providing proper load transfer. For overlay work the existing surface must be completely dry—any standing water after milling is drained immediately—and the underlying asphalt is cooled to below 120 °F before concrete placement; fog‑misting or protective covers may be used to achieve these conditions without leaving moisture on the pavement. When ambient temperatures are 45 °F or lower insulating blankets or heating blankets are applied to thin slabs, and they may be removed only after air temperature exceeds 45 °F or the concrete attains a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi, with removal performed so that surface‑temperature change does not exceed 40 °F in any one‑hour period. Curing compounds are applied uniformly within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation, and fog‑misting or protective covers may be employed to maintain the required moisture conditions without leaving excess water on the pavement. By maintaining effective drainage, preventing infiltration, using appropriate sealants, and following these temperature and moisture controls, the pavement is better able to resist climate‑induced distress and achieve a longer service life. The pavement must provide a smooth, safe travel surface while preserving strict geometric continuity with any existing rigid pavement it adjoins. All transverse joints are required to be spaced at fifteen‑foot intervals, cut perpendicular to traffic flow, equipped with coated dowel bars and sealed in accordance with the RC‑27M standard. When a single lane is reconstructed for lengths of five hundred feet or more, the new joints must line up with the existing joint pattern; if the original spacing exceeds twenty feet an intermediate joint must be inserted to keep spacing within the prescribed limit. The use of high‑early‑strength concrete is avoided because its tendency to shrink can produce cracking that would undermine safety and ride quality. Longitudinal joints are required to run parallel to the final lane markings and lie within twelve inches of those markings so that they never intersect a driving lane. Edge slippage between adjacent lanes or ramps is limited to one‑eighth inch, and between a lane and its shoulder to one‑quarter inch. The finished surface must achieve a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 inch to provide adequate skid resistance, and the surface may not deviate more than three‑sixteenths of an inch when measured with a straightedge parallel to the centreline (or one‑quarter inch across longitudinal joints where cross‑slope changes). Concrete slabs are cast with lengths no greater than fifteen feet, or twenty feet when prestressed, and not shorter than six feet; their widths follow the longitudinal joint layout shown on the shop drawings. The longer side of any panel may not exceed eighteen times the slab thickness, and the aspect ratio of length to width shall not be greater than 1.5, with a shape as close to square as practicable. Transverse saw cuts are limited to a width between one‑quarter and one‑half inch and must penetrate at least one‑third of the slab thickness within twelve inches of any edge and at least one‑fourth of the thickness elsewhere; longitudinal joint cuts may not exceed three‑quarters inch in width. All cuts are required to be full‑depth, made with a power‑driven self‑propelled saw that does not cause spalling, and the resulting joints must be cleaned of debris and sealed with an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant applied only when ambient temperature is between forty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit unless otherwise authorized. Dowel bars are positioned within ±1⁄8 inch of the drawing locations, each end not deviating more than 0.25 inch from the midpoint, and longitudinal or horizontal translation is limited to one inch; they are fully bonded with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders to achieve proper load transfer. The vertical differential across any joint may not exceed one‑quarter inch, although a differential up to one‑half inch is permissible if the slab will be diamond‑ground later. Concrete must attain a minimum compressive strength of three thousand psi before traffic is permitted, while dowel and bedding grouts must reach at least two thousand five hundred psi; patches may be accepted at one thousand psi. Safety provisions require protection of traffic from drop‑off hazards, the provision of access ramps whenever equipment moves on or off the pavement, sequencing that avoids simultaneous work in both lanes, and a one‑lane‑at‑a‑time patching approach when necessary; protective mats may be placed only after sweeping the surface clean of debris, and temporary openings prior to grout cure require shims and any vertical drops greater than two inches must be bridged with an asphalt wedge transition. Continuous tied plain‑cement concrete shoulders are mandated because they reduce edge stresses, limit differential heave and enhance vehicle stability; on urban interstates and expressways these shoulders are full‑depth cement concrete with dowelled transverse joints, while on rural routes with average daily traffic below ten thousand the widened slabs may be paired with asphalt or roller‑compacted concrete shoulders, and in all cases shoulder materials must mirror those of the mainline pavement from subgrade through to surface. The guides do not prescribe additional accessibility features beyond the required access ramps; all other user‑related requirements are satisfied through the geometric tolerances, texture depth, smoothness limits, joint sealing and controlled opening of the pavement to traffic. Verification and expected performance

The completed Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement is accepted only after a comprehensive quality‑control plan, submitted at least two weeks before paving begins, has been reviewed and approved. The plan must assign responsibilities, define sampling frequency, and specify the methods that will be used to verify strength, thickness, grade, joint construction, curing, and traffic opening. During placement the contractor continuously monitors aggregate gradations against PTM No. 529, implements a concrete‑temperature control plan whenever temperatures exceed 80 °F, and subjects the first truck of each lot to a plastic‑air test that must fall within 7 % ± 1.5 %. The mix must satisfy Class AA limits: cement factor between 530 and 600 lb/yd³, water‑cement ratio not exceeding 0.42 with a target of 0.40 but never below 0.38, air content 7 % ± 1.5 %, and aggregate reactivity limited to a 14‑day expansion of 0.10–0.30 %. Field verification of the water‑cement ratio uses batch tickets together with AASHTO T 318. Form tolerances are maintained, grade and depth are controlled, and edge slumping is measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge while the concrete is still plastic; any slump greater than one‑eighth inch between lanes or one‑quarter inch between lane and shoulder triggers corrective action. After hardening straightedge tests must show surface deviations no more than three‑sixteenths of an inch in either direction, and macrotexture is confirmed by ASTM E965 to achieve a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 inch for radii under 50 ft. Joints are cut with green‑cut saws to a minimum width of one‑eighth inch, to a depth of at least one‑third the slab thickness within twelve inches of an edge and at least one‑fourth elsewhere, spaced as shown on the approved plans while avoiding longitudinal joints in wheel paths. Saw cuts at relief‑joint interfaces must fall within a quarter‑inch of the required line and form reservoirs exactly one inch deep by one‑half inch wide; tolerances are defined in Section 105.12. Joint wipe tests, sealing with appropriate backing and sealant material, and application of curing compound occur within thirty minutes after bleed‑water dissipation. Insulating blankets are used when air temperature is 45 °F or lower, and temperature change must be limited to forty degrees per hour; concrete experiencing a higher rate of temperature change is considered defective. After placement the as‑built slabs are cross‑checked against shop drawings: each slab is cast a quarter inch thicker than nominal design, compressive strength must reach at least 4,925 psi (or the project‑specified minimum of 3,000 psi) and flexural strength at least 650 psi. Core drilling or approved nondestructive methods verify slab thickness, allowing a deficiency of no more than one‑quarter inch; any measurement more than half an inch short is deemed defective. Vertical differentials across joints may not exceed one‑quarter inch, transverse joint gaps must be between one‑quarter and one‑half inch (longitudinal gaps not exceeding three‑quarters inch), and dowel slots, tie bars and grout ports must be located within ±1/8 inch of their drawing positions. Dowel and bedding grouts must achieve a compressive strength of at least 2,500 psi before traffic is permitted, and dowel‑bar anchoring cores confirm peripheral voids less than 0.5 inch. Percent‑within‑limits values for compressive strength and plastic‑air content must be at least 55 %; the lot is defective if these fall below that threshold or if surface defects such as rain damage, improper joint construction, curing errors, poor finish, cracking, honeycombing, depressions or high points exceeding Section 506.3(o) tolerances cannot be satisfactorily repaired. Panel aspect ratios are kept as close to 1.0 as feasible and shall not exceed 1.5, with panel dimensions limited to eighteen times the overlay thickness. For pours larger than 700 square yards, longitudinal‑transverse alignment is verified by an approved nondestructive method within eighteen hours of placement. Constructability checks also require a ramp for entry and exit, sweeping the surface clean of debris, and installing protective mats where needed. Any observed slab or joint damage triggers immediate suspension of work and corrective action approved by the Representative. Only after all dimensional, strength, texture, joint, visual, and constructability criteria are satisfied does the Representative approve opening to traffic, thereby confirming compliance, constructability, performance, and consistency with the project requirements. The Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement is expected to keep edge geometry within the allowable slump limits, maintain a riding surface flat enough that a straight‑edge shows no more than three‑sixteenths inch deviation, provide a minimum mean texture depth of 0.040 in. for skid resistance, deliver sufficient load transfer through dowel and tie‑bar pull‑out resistance, achieve the required compressive strength before traffic is permitted, and retain slab thickness within quarter‑inch tolerances; edge slumping is measured on fresh concrete with a 12‑foot straightedge (max 1/8 in. between lanes, 1/4 in. to shoulders), surface flatness is rechecked after hardening with the same straightedge, macrotexture is verified by ASTM E965 at four lane locations and must average at least 0.040 in., tie‑bar pull‑out is tested in place using a hydraulic jack to either 12,000 lb or 1/32 in. slip, compressive strength is confirmed by cylinder testing or maturity methods to meet the minimum 1,200 psi for accelerated mixes (or the Table D value for normal mixes), and slab depth is validated by core drilling or approved nondestructive methods with a maximum deficit of 1/4 in.; any deficiency triggers corrective replacement at no extra cost.
