3R Project

Part of: Rehabilitation is-subtopic-of
3R Project is a topic under Rehabilitation.
3r-projects3r3r-workresurfacing-restoration-and-rehabilitation
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

The 3R Project is a PennDOT‑defined rehabilitation strategy used when an existing interstate, arterial, collector or local road requires preservation and performance enhancement, allowing the pavement to be returned to structural and functional adequacy through resurfacing, restoration or other rehabilitative treatments without undertaking major capacity upgrades, realignments or geometric reconstruction[1]. Its design is governed by the same cross‑slope criteria as new construction—requiring the finished surface to meet the minimum cross‑slope of Publication 13 and, if that cannot be achieved, a design‑exception request must be prepared—and by traffic‑volume thresholds (ADT > 5 000 or ADTT > 500) that dictate whether the widening structure extends two feet or one foot into the shoulder, with concrete or Type 6/7 shoulders selected according to excavation or cleaning needs; reclaimed asphalt concrete may be used for base or surface courses under prescribed minimum depths[1]. Critical implementation controls include prohibiting traffic on any milled surface for more than six calendar days and requiring placement of the first overlay within seven calendar days, with failure to meet the seven‑day window triggering liquidated damages—timing and safety requirements that dominate project decision‑making[2].

Purpose and decision context

The 3R Project is meant to support the decision to apply a resurfacing, restoration or rehabilitation treatment to an existing roadway in order to extend its service life and/or improve its structural and functional capacity without undertaking major capacity upgrades, realignments or geometric changes; it also supports the decision of whether a design exception request must be prepared when the required minimum cross‑slope cannot be achieved, requiring that the finished roadway surface provide at least the minimum cross slope prescribed in Publication 13 and, if this criterion cannot be satisfied, that a design exception request be prepared as outlined in Publication 10X Appendix P; additionally it guides the decision on shoulder treatment for 3R work, specifying that when the current ADT exceeds 5 000 or the ADTT exceeds 500 the widening structure shall be extended two feet into the shoulder, whereas when ADT is 5 000 or less and ADTT is 500 or less the widening structure shall be extended one foot, and it determines that concrete shoulders of Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or 6‑SP shall be provided where excavation or scarifying of the existing shoulder is necessary, Type 7 where only cleaning and patching are required, and reclaimed asphalt concrete material is acceptable for shoulder base courses; finally it supports the decision of whether a 2A Subbase may be considered as the base layer in rigid‑pavement 3R projects when the original pavement was constructed on an open graded subbase, asphalt‑treated permeable base or cement‑treated permeable base, with the choice to be made in consultation with HDTD. [1]

[1] — Ch. 1 (p. 51), Ch. 2 (pp. 60–62), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162)

The 3R Project is used when an existing road needs preservation and performance enhancement, aiming to extend the facility’s service life and improve its structural and functional capacity without undertaking major capacity upgrades or geometric changes. It applies to all functional classifications—from Interstates to local roads—and focuses on resurfacing, restoration, or rehabilitation actions that can add structural capacity where needed. The project also addresses the need to preserve pavement quality during construction by requiring that traffic must not be allowed on any milled surface for more than six calendar days and that the first overlaying asphalt course be placed within seven calendar days of the start of milling. These timing constraints are intended to prevent surface deterioration, satisfy contractual traffic‑control requirements, and avoid work‑zone liquidated damages. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 1 (p. 51), Ch. 2 (pp. 60–62), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 348)

Scope and applicability

The 3R Project is appropriate when the work involves improving an existing roadway—interstate, arterial, collector or local road—to extend its service life or enhance its structural and functional capacity without undertaking major capacity upgrades, realignment, or geometric reconstruction, and it applies to resurfacing, restoration or rehabilitation activities such as pavement overlays that may add structural capacity. It is used when the existing pavement can be returned to a condition of structural or functional adequacy through partial recycling, placement of additional surface materials, or similar treatments, i.e., when the pavement has not yet failed or become functionally obsolete. For cross‑slope design the 3R Project is treated the same as new‑construction and reconstruction work; the finished roadway surface must provide the minimum cross slope specified in Publication 13, and if those criteria cannot be satisfied a design‑exception request must be prepared. The 3R approach is appropriate for both rigid‑pavement rehabilitation and flexible‑pavement resurfacing where shoulders are part of the treatment; it may involve providing concrete or Type 6/7 shoulders on Interstates, limited‑access freeways or arterials that require excavation, scarifying, cleaning or patching of an existing shoulder, or, when widening the pavement, extending the new structure into the shoulder by two feet if the current ADT is greater than 5000 or the ADTT is greater than 500, otherwise by one foot; reclaimed asphalt concrete may also be used for base or surface courses provided recycled‑material guidelines are followed. When the existing pavement was originally constructed on an open‑graded subbase, an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base or a Cement Treated Permeable Base, the design may consider using a 2A Subbase as the base layer for a 3R Project, provided the selection is made in consultation with HDTD and the effective k‑value calculation is adjusted so that the ratio of the subbase to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed 4. [1]

[1] — Ch. 1 (p. 51), Ch. 2 (pp. 60–62), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162)

When a project’s objectives include increasing traffic capacity, executing a major realignment, or substantially upgrading geometric features, the 3R scope—limited to extending service life and modestly improving structural or functional capacity—is not appropriate; such work should be classified under New Construction or Reconstruction (CIRT or NoCIRT) which address those larger changes. Conversely, if the goal is only to maintain existing pavement without adding structural capacity, a Pavement Preservation approach is preferred. When the required minimum cross‑slope for the finished surface cannot be achieved, a 3R project becomes unsuitable; in such cases the guidelines mandate that a design exception request must be prepared and an alternate treatment should be considered. For situations where preserving existing pavement is more appropriate, the guidance directs users to follow the pavement preservation procedures outlined elsewhere. When the existing pavement has failed or become functionally obsolete, a 3R Project—defined as rehabilitation work that includes resurfacing, restoration, or structural overlays—is not suitable; in those conditions reconstruction is required, which replaces the entire pavement structure with an equivalent or increased structure and is therefore preferred over 3R treatments. [1]

[1] — Ch. 1 (p. 51), Ch. 2 (p. 60), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139)

Information needs

The 3R Project must obtain existing pavement cross‑slope measurements and superelevation data so that the finished surface can be verified to provide at least the minimum cross slope required by Publication 13. If those design criteria cannot be met, a design exception request must be prepared and the team must evaluate whether additional leveling material or removal of surface material is needed. The project also needs current average daily traffic (ADT) and the ADT multiplied by the percentage of trucks, buses, and recreational vehicles (ADTT); when the current ADT is greater than 5 000 and/or the ADTT is greater than 500, the widening structure shall be extended two feet into the shoulder, while when the current ADT is 5 000 or less and/or the ADTT is 500 or less, the widening structure shall be extended one foot. Knowledge of the existing shoulder type is required to select among Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S, 6‑SP, Type 7, or concrete shoulders, and the joint spacing of concrete shoulders shall not exceed 20 ft. If reclaimed asphalt is used, the recycled base course depth shall be at least five inches on arterials or four inches on collectors and local roads. The roadbed soil resilient modulus (including any seasonal adjustments) and detailed subbase information—type, thickness, and loss‑of‑support factor for each layer—must be obtained to calculate an effective k‑value, and the depth to a rigid foundation must be recorded when bedrock lies within ten feet of the subgrade surface. The base elastic modulus used in DARWin is set at 15 000 psi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30 000 psi for normal (summer/fall) conditions, and 50 000 psi for frozen conditions, but these may be adjusted so that the ratio of the subbase to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed four. When a mixed base such as CTPB over a 2A Subbase is considered, the combined thickness is used in the weighted loss‑of‑support calculation, and any use of a 2A Subbase for a 3R or CPR project requires consultation with HDTD. The 3R Project must collect detailed condition and site data that include the locations of all cores to be taken from existing pavement, theoretical surface elevations and joint dimensions derived from accurate surveys, and ambient average daily temperatures together with exposure conditions for thermal‑expansion calculations in the concrete mix design. It also requires submittals of concrete mix designs with trial‑batch test results showing a required compressive strength of 4 925 psi and flexural strength of 650 psi, slab layout drawings, grout‑reservoir placement plans, and installation drawings that specify the bedding layer and any supplemental aggregate for subbase or subgrade remediation. The milling portion of the project requires that the finished surface be free from gouges, grooves and ridges and meet the tolerance specified in Section 413.3(l). Site information must further include the exact locations of utility facilities within the work area and the designated stockpiling location together with its approximate distance in miles from the project site and the name and telephone number of the contact person listed in the special provision. Traffic control data are needed to ensure that no vehicles travel on any milled surface for more than 6 calendar days and that the first asphalt overlay be placed within 7 calendar days, with appropriate ROUGH ROAD or BUMP signage installed if the overlay is delayed; liquidated damages are assessed when the 7‑day overlay deadline is missed. Stakeholder input is obtained through a series of mandated meetings—including a precast pavement planning meeting, a pre‑fabrication meeting and a pre‑placement meeting—where the Representative, inspection personnel, CQAD and SMS representatives, project superintendent, foreman, surveyor, grout installers, sawcutters and the technical representative of the PCPS system review surveys, slab sizes, schedules, quality‑control plans and field data collection; stakeholder input also includes satisfying the utility owner for any repaired facilities and providing advance notification of material deliveries to the designated department contact. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 60–62), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 348), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Alternatives

In a 3R project the feasible alternatives for shoulder construction include providing a concrete shoulder or using asphalt‑type shoulders such as Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S, 6‑SP or Type 7, each selected according to whether excavation, scarifying, or only cleaning and patching of the existing paved shoulder is required [1]. When widening the pavement the design shall compare extending the structure into the shoulder by two feet for corridors with a current ADT greater than 5,000 or an ADTT greater than 500 against a one‑foot extension where ADT is 5,000 or less and ADTT is 500 or less, with the option to eliminate the extension if an appropriate shoulder type is used [1]. The use of reclaimed asphalt concrete material in a recycled base course for Type 6/6‑F/6‑S shoulders or in a surface course for Type 7 shoulders may be compared with the use of new material, and the minimum depth shall be five inches on Arterials and four inches on Collectors and Local Roads [1]. Rehabilitation alternatives that must be compared include crack‑and‑seat with structural overlays, rubblization with structural overlays, structural asphalt overlays, concrete overlays, partial recycling of the existing pavement, or placement of additional surface materials to restore functional adequacy [1]. The base layer options that require comparison are an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base (ATPB) versus a Cement Treated Permeable Base (CTPB), and different subbase thicknesses may be evaluated as part of the design trade‑off [1]. Loss‑of‑support factors for each subbase layer shall be used to correct the effective k‑value, and the depth to any rigid foundation within ten feet that could affect the effective k‑value should also be considered [1]. Seasonal values of the base elastic modulus—15,000 psi for spring thaw, 30,000 psi for normal conditions, and 50,000 psi for frozen conditions—are presented as alternative inputs for design analysis [1]. For transverse joint load‑transfer the alternatives to be compared are bottom‑slot/embedded dowels, conventional slot/embedded dowels, or narrow‑mouth surface slots, each required to meet joint‑width limits of not exceeding ½ inch transversely and ¾ inch longitudinally and vertical differentials within ¼ inch (or up to ½ inch before grinding) [2]. Slab‑leveling techniques that must be evaluated include using four symmetrically placed setting bolts, injecting polyurethane under the slab through grout holes, or fastening the slab to strongback beams [2]. The tie‑bar installation approaches to be compared are drilling and installing ties in existing pavement versus employing a cross‑stitching technique [2]. For milling of an asphalt pavement surface the two permissible dispositions of milled material that must be compared are retaining the material with the contractor for immediate disposal or retaining it with the Department for delivery to a designated stockpile, with the contract required to specify which condition applies and, when retained by the Department, to identify the stockpile site, approximate distance from the project, and advance notification of scheduled delivery [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 60–62), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 348), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Decision criteria

The source makes clear that the overriding controls for a 3R project are the timing requirements and traffic‑safety restrictions: milling must be followed by placement of the first overlay within seven calendar days, and traffic may not use the milled surface for more than six days; failure to meet the seven‑day deadline triggers liquidated damages. Consequently, schedule (the seven‑day window) and safety (traffic prohibition and required signage) are the primary criteria that must drive the decision. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 348)

In 3R projects the prioritization of competing criteria begins with traffic demand and existing shoulder condition; when the current average daily traffic exceeds 5 000 vehicles or the adjusted daily truck‑traffic factor exceeds 500, the widening structure shall be extended into the shoulder area by two feet, whereas when the current ADT is 5 000 or less and the ADTT is 500 or less the extension shall be one foot. After the width decision, the selection of shoulder type follows the pavement classification and distress level: concrete shoulders may be provided for rigid pavements provided that joint spacing does not exceed 20 ft; Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S, or 6‑SP shoulders are required on interstates and other limited‑access freeways and arterials where excavation or scarifying of the existing shoulder is necessary, a minimum three‑foot width of those types may be used when only the first few feet adjacent to the pavement are distressed, and Type 7 shoulders are specified where only cleaning and patching is necessary; for flexible pavements on collectors and local roads the shoulder material should match the pavement structure. Recycled asphalt material may be used in base or surface courses according to the recycled‑shoulder provisions, with a minimum depth of five inches on arterials and four inches on collectors and local roads. When determining the effective modulus of subgrade reaction for a 3R project, each competing factor—subbase type, thickness, loss‑of‑support factor and depth to rigid foundation—is incorporated through a depth‑weighted average; the loss‑of‑support factors for each layer are multiplied by that layer’s depth and divided by the total depth of all layers (LS1·D1/(D1+D2)+ LS2·D2/(D1+D2)). This weighted ratio is then used together with the appropriate base elastic modulus—15 000 psi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30 000 psi for normal conditions, or 50 000 psi for frozen conditions—to compute the effective k‑value. The resulting design must also satisfy the constraint that the ratio of the subbase to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed 4, preventing an artificial condition. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162)

Analysis methods and tools

The 3R rehabilitation activities are evaluated through life‑cycle cost analysis when a PennDOT Oversight design calls for it, and Federal Oversight designs may be approved or denied based on the FHWA’s risk determination; the source does not mention condition ratings, decision trees, or benefit‑cost analysis as part of the 3R evaluation. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139)

The 3R Project design assumes that for new construction, reconstruction, and 3R projects the finished roadway surface should provide the minimum cross‑slope prescribed in Publication 13, Design Manual Part 2; if the existing geometry does not satisfy this requirement, superelevations must be checked and corrected, which introduces uncertainty about how much leveling or removal material will be needed, and when the required cross‑slope cannot be achieved the project must prepare a design exception request, adding further uncertainty regarding approval. The design also assumes that the current average daily traffic (ADT) and derived ADTT are known, so that when the current ADT is greater than 5000 and/or the ADTT exceeds 500 the widening structure shall be extended two feet into the shoulder, whereas when the current ADT is 5000 or less and/or the ADTT is 500 or less the widening structure shall be extended one foot; it further assumes that concrete shoulders shall have joint spacing that does not exceed 20 ft and that reclaimed asphalt concrete may be used for base or surface courses on specified shoulder types, but uncertainties arise from the accuracy of traffic counts, truck‑percentage estimates, and variability in the quality and performance of reclaimed material when placed to the minimum depth of five inches on arterials or four inches on collectors and local roads. The effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k) used in a 3R pavement design is assumed to depend on the selected subbase type, its estimated thickness, the loss‑of‑support factor applied to each layer, any shallow rigid foundation within ten feet of the surface, and seasonal variations in base elastic modulus, with the additional constraint that the ratio of the combined subbase modulus to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed four; because these inputs are often estimated—such as choosing a subbase type, estimating its thickness, applying LS values for erosion or pumping, and selecting seasonal E_SB values of 15,000 psi for spring thaw, 30,000 psi for normal conditions, or 50,000 psi for frozen conditions—the resulting k‑value carries uncertainty that propagates to pavement thickness and performance predictions. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 60–62), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162)

Stakeholders and constraints

In 3R projects that involve shoulder work, the District Executive may approve paving the entire shoulder with the same material and design as a widening at his or her discretion; no other participants are identified for input or decision‑making in that context. For rehabilitation work classified as a 3R project, certified PME/PMs provide the technical input and submit a recommendation on Form D‑4332, while the District Engineer makes the final decision to approve the PennDOT Oversight pavement design; any required waivers or deviations must be reviewed and approved by BODD, and designs under Federal Oversight also require FHWA review and approval. When a 2A Subbase is considered for the base layer in a 3R project, consultation with HDTD is required, meaning HDTD must provide input and effectively approve that subbase selection. In Section 529—Full‑Depth Precast/Prestressed Concrete Pavement Slabs, input for a precast‑pavement (3R) effort is gathered from all parties attending the planning, pre‑fabrication and pre‑placement meetings; these include the Representative together with inspection personnel, CQAD and SMS representatives, the project superintendent, foreman, surveyor, grout installers, sawcutters, the technical representative of the PCPS system, the slab fabricator and any other subcontractors. Submittals such as shop drawings, working drawings and quality‑control plans must be provided to the Representative at least 21 calendar days before the pre‑production meeting, and the Representative then has a further 21 calendar days to review them and issue a notice of acceptance or rejection, which constitutes the final decision and approval for that project. Under Section 491—Milling of Asphalt Pavement Surface, repairs or replacements of utility facilities must be made to the satisfaction of the utility owner, giving that party a key input role, and the Department’s Representative is the authority whose acceptance of the milling and overlay methods constitutes the decision and approval for the project. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 348), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

The selection of a 3R project is limited to activities that extend service life or improve structural and functional capacity and does not permit major capacity upgrades, realignment, or significant geometric changes. All 3R designs must satisfy the roadway design criteria defined in Publication 13 Design Manual Part 2 Contextual Roadway Design, with Chapter 4 providing the pavement type definitions and geometric requirements that govern any treatment. The finished surface must achieve the minimum cross‑slope prescribed in Chapters 3 and 4 of Publication 13, and superelevations on curves must be verified and corrected where necessary; shoulder reconstruction must meet the same cross‑slope standards, and a design exception request is required when these criteria cannot be met, per Publication 10X Appendix P. Concrete joint spacing in shoulders may not exceed 20 ft. When widening a pavement, if the current average daily traffic (ADT) exceeds 5,000 vehicles per day or the ADT‑truck‑traffic factor (ADTT) exceeds 500, the widening structure shall be extended into the shoulder by 2 feet; if both values are 5,000 and 500 or less, the extension shall be 1 foot, with optional full‑width paving at the District Executive’s discretion. Recycled asphalt concrete may be used for base or surface courses, but a minimum depth of 5 inches is required on arterials and 4 inches on collectors and local roads. Rehabilitation projects must conform to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242) and require certification using Form D‑4332; any deviation must be submitted as a waiver to the Bureau of Design Development (BODD) for review and approval. For PennDOT Oversight projects, pavement designs must be submitted to BODD and undergo annual QA visits, and when the work involves less than 30,000 square yards of mainline pavement surface a formal type determination is not required provided documented justification is included in the project file. Federal‑Oversight projects require FHWA approval of the complete pavement design package unless FHWA determines otherwise based on risk. All Interstate and Expressway rigid‑pavement designs for 3R work must include a base consisting of an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) over a 2A Subbase layer, with ATPB and CTPB bid as alternates and the use of Open Graded Subbase prohibited for any concrete pavement. When calculating the effective subgrade reaction k‑value, the subbase type, thickness and loss‑of‑support factors must be incorporated and the ratio of subbase to roadbed soil resilient modulus may not exceed 4; seasonal base elastic moduli are prescribed at 15,000 psi for spring thaw, 30,000 psi for normal conditions and 50,000 psi for frozen conditions. The choice of a full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete slab for a 3R project is constrained by Section 529.3, which requires that the manufacturer’s PCPS system be listed in Bulletin 15 and that mix designs be submitted at least twenty‑eight days before production. The concrete slabs must achieve a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a minimum flexural strength of 650 psi, and each slab shall be cast one‑quarter inch thicker than the nominal design thickness. Dimensional tolerances must follow Table A, load‑transfer dowels must be placed within ±1⁄8 inch together with grout ports and lifting devices per Section 1105, and joint geometry limits require transverse joints not to exceed one‑half inch in width (with a minimum of one‑quarter inch along the entire length), longitudinal joints not to exceed three‑quarters inch, and vertical differential not to exceed one‑quarter inch. Subbase preparation must comply with Section 703.2, thermal‑expansion considerations shall use AASHTO ME guide values, and traffic may be opened onto the new slab only after dowel and bedding grout attain at least 2,500 psi compressive strength. Milling‑based 3R interventions are constrained by Section 491.3, which mandates the use of a milling machine equipped with automatic grade and slope control, prohibits traffic on any milled surface for more than six calendar days, requires placement of the first overlaying asphalt course within seven calendar days from the start of milling, and subjects failure to meet the seven‑day overlay requirement to liquidated damages as specified in Section 108.07(c). The contract also prescribes the use of ROUGH ROAD (W8‑8) signs in accordance with Publications 212, 213 and the MUTCD, and specifies whether milled material is retained by the contractor or delivered to a Department stockpile. [1][2]

Timing and implementation

The Pavement Policy Manual specifies that for any 3R project—whether new construction, reconstruction or rehabilitation—if the designer determines that the required minimum cross slope described in Publication 13 cannot be achieved, a design‑exception request must be prepared [1]. The same manual requires that superelevations in curved sections found to be out of tolerance shall be checked and corrected, which may involve adding leveling material or removing surface material [1]. For 3R widening projects the manual sets traffic‑volume thresholds that dictate shoulder extension: when the current average daily traffic exceeds 5 000 vehicles per day or the truck‑bus‑recreational vehicle component exceeds 500, the widening structure shall be extended two feet into the shoulder; when both ADT is 5 000 or less and ADTT is 500 or less the extension may be limited to one foot [1]. In rehabilitation of rigid pavements without widening, concrete shoulders may be provided but their joint spacing shall not exceed twenty feet, establishing a dimensional limit that must be observed [1]. When recycled‑asphalt base material is specified, the manual imposes minimum thickness thresholds—five inches on arterials and four inches on collectors and local roads—that trigger compliance with the material requirement [1].

The Department of Transportation specifications for 3R projects trigger several pre‑construction actions for precast pavement components. The contractor must submit concrete mix designs to the Representative at least twenty‑eight days before slab production begins, and must provide shop drawings, working drawings and the manufacturer’s quality‑control plan at least twenty‑one calendar days prior to the pre‑production meeting; after receipt the Representative has a further twenty‑one‑day review period [2]. A pre‑fabrication meeting is required no later than fourteen calendar days before slab fabrication starts, and a pre‑placement meeting must occur ten to fourteen calendar days before installation of the slabs [2]. Traffic may not be opened onto the installed slab until dowel and bedding grout have reached a minimum compressive strength of 2 500 psi and all joint tolerances are satisfied—transverse joints shall not exceed one‑half inch, longitudinal joints shall not exceed three‑quarters inch, and vertical differentials shall not be greater than one‑quarter inch [2].

For the milling portion of a 3R project, the specifications define two timing thresholds that trigger contractual action. If a milled surface remains open to traffic for more than six calendar days, the condition constitutes non‑compliance; likewise, if the first overlaying asphalt course is not placed within seven calendar days from the start of milling, liquidated damages are assessed under the contract [2]. When the first overlay cannot be placed on the same day as milling, rough‑road (W8‑8) signs must be installed half a mile in advance and at regular intervals, and vertical edges around utilities must be eliminated each day or a bump sign placed to warn motorists [2].

To carry out the selected 3R option that employs full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, the contractor must secure a qualified manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15 and submit approved mix designs together with shop and working drawings and the fabricator’s quality‑control plan at least 21 calendar days before the pre‑production meeting; all required hardware such as lifting devices, foam gaskets and slab‑setting bolt components must also be provided. For the option that involves milling of an asphalt pavement surface, the milling phase requires a dedicated milling machine that is designed and built for milling existing asphalt pavements, includes an automatic grade and slope control system and is capable of milling concrete; the milled material must be either disposed of immediately by the contractor or delivered to a department‑designated stockpile as specified in the contract. Traffic may not be allowed on any milled surface for more than 6 calendar days, and the first overlaying asphalt course shall be placed within 7 calendar days from the start of the milling operation, with work stoppages caused by the Department, utilities or documented weather delays excluded from this 7‑day count. Outcomes and follow-up

Success for the 3R Project is realized when the finished roadway surface provides at least the minimum cross‑slope prescribed in Publication 13, Design Manual Part 2, and superelevations on curved sections are inspected and corrected if necessary; shoulder reconstruction must also meet those same cross‑slope criteria without requiring a design‑exception request. Compliance is measured by checking that the as‑built surface satisfies the minimum cross‑slope requirement, confirming any leveling or material removal has been applied, verifying superelevation corrections, and documenting whether a design‑exception request was prepared. For shoulders, success requires concrete joint spacing of no more than 20 ft, selection of the appropriate Type 6/6‑F/6‑S/6‑SP or Type 7 shoulder based on roadway class, and widening into the shoulder limited to 2 ft when average daily traffic exceeds 5,000 vehicles or annual daylight truck traffic exceeds 500 trucks, otherwise 1 ft; reclaimed asphalt concrete must be placed with a minimum depth of 5 in on arterials or 4 in on collectors and local roads. These elements are verified by inspecting the constructed shoulder to confirm joint spacing, material type, widening width, and reclaimed‑asphalt depth. Success for the precast concrete slab portion of a 3R Project is achieved when each slab attains a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a minimum flexural strength of 650 psi, conforms to the dimensional tolerances shown on the authorized shop drawings—including dowel placement within ±1/8 in—has a sacrificial thickness of 1/4 in, is installed so that transverse joint widths do not exceed 1/2 in (with a minimum of 1/4 in) and longitudinal joints do not exceed 3/4 in, exhibits vertical differentials across any joint of no more than 1/4 in, rests on a sub‑base surface within ±1/2 in of grade, and all dowel and bedding grouts reach at least 2,500 psi before traffic is allowed. Compliance is documented through laboratory test results for concrete strength, field inspections using straight‑edge or depth‑gauge checks to confirm joint gaps and vertical differentials, verification of sub‑base grading, and recorded grout‑strength values prior to opening. Success for the milling component of a 3R Project occurs when the milled surface is free from gouges, grooves, and ridges and meets the tolerance requirements of Section 413.3(l); the removed material must be disposed of or delivered as specified; traffic may not be permitted on the milled surface for more than six calendar days; and the first overlaying asphalt course must be placed within seven calendar days of the start of milling to avoid liquidated damages. These conditions are measured by visual inspection of surface quality, documented records of closure duration, timestamps showing that the overlay was applied within the seven‑day window, and verification of proper disposition of the milled material.
