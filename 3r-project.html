3R Project

Part of: Rehabilitation is-subtopic-of
3R Project is a topic under Rehabilitation.
3r-projects3r3r-workresurfacing-restoration-and-rehabilitation
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

The 3R Project is a PennDOT‑directed rehabilitation approach applied to existing roadways—interstate, arterial, collector or local—to extend service life and improve structural and functional capacity without undertaking major capacity upgrades, realignments or geometric changes [1]. It operates through a performance‑based decision process that requires up‑to‑date distress surveys, cross‑slope verification per Publication 13, and traffic‑threshold shoulder extensions (two‑foot extension when ADT > 5 000 or ADTT > 500, otherwise one foot), with remedial actions triggered when the five‑year survey shows at least 30 % of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses or when combined deviations exceed 20 % of the surface area [2]. The project’s defining characteristics include mandatory use of polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing under prescribed temperature and seasonal limits, optional concrete or Type 6/7 shoulder treatments, and the option to employ a 2A subbase after consultation with HDTD, all governed by the 60‑month warranty distress limits and design‑exception procedures when minimum cross‑slope cannot be achieved [1][2].

Purpose and decision context

The 3R Project is meant to support the decision to apply a resurfacing, restoration or rehabilitation treatment to an existing roadway in order to extend its service life and/or improve its structural and functional capacity without undertaking major capacity upgrades, realignments or geometric changes; it also supports the decision of whether a design exception request must be prepared when the required minimum cross‑slope cannot be achieved, requiring that the finished roadway surface provide at least the minimum cross slope prescribed in Publication 13 and, if this criterion cannot be satisfied, that a design exception request be prepared as outlined in Publication 10X Appendix P; additionally it guides the decision on shoulder treatment for 3R work, specifying that when the current ADT exceeds 5 000 or the ADTT exceeds 500 the widening structure shall be extended two feet into the shoulder, whereas when ADT is 5 000 or less and ADTT is 500 or less the widening structure shall be extended one foot, and it determines that concrete shoulders of Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or 6‑SP shall be provided where excavation or scarifying of the existing shoulder is necessary, Type 7 where only cleaning and patching are required, and reclaimed asphalt concrete material is acceptable for shoulder base courses; finally it supports the decision of whether a 2A Subbase may be considered as the base layer in rigid‑pavement 3R projects when the original pavement was constructed on an open graded subbase, asphalt‑treated permeable base or cement‑treated permeable base, with the choice to be made in consultation with HDTD. [1]

[1] — Ch. 1 (p. 51), Ch. 2 (pp. 60–62), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162)

The 3R Project is employed when an existing road requires preservation and performance enhancement, with the objective of extending the facility’s service life and improving its structural and functional capacity while avoiding major capacity upgrades or geometric changes; it is applicable to all functional classifications from Interstates to local roads. [1] It also fulfills the warranty‑driven performance goal that pavement segments remain within prescribed distress limits over a 60‑month period, thereby addressing the problem of deteriorating pavements that could become unsafe or fail contractual criteria. [2] If the year‑5 performance survey determines that at least thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action shall be provided by microsurfacing or another treatment approved by the Department. [2] Should combined deviations exceed the threshold levels such that they cover more than twenty percent of the surface area of any segment, removal and replacement of the entire segment is required. [2] The project targets pavement sections exhibiting fatigue or excessive rutting by applying a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing treatment under controlled construction conditions as described in Section 483. [2] Placement of the micro‑surfacing mixture may occur only when the surface temperature is at least 50 °F for the entire placement and no rain is imminent nor freezing temperatures are expected within twenty‑four hours after application; applications are prohibited from September 16 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0, 2‑0, 3‑0, 4‑0, 10‑0, 5‑0 and from October 1 to April 30 in Districts 6‑0, 8‑0, 9‑0, 11‑0, 12‑0, 5‑0. [2] For new construction, reconstruction, and 3R projects the finished roadway surface shall provide a minimum cross‑slope as indicated in Publication 13, Design Manual Part 2 so that drainage and vehicle stability are maintained; superelevations in curved sections shall be checked and corrected if necessary, and when the design criteria for cross slopes cannot be met a design exception request must be prepared. [1] In the shoulder context of 3R work, concrete shoulders may be provided to supply lateral support or a low‑maintenance surface, but at no time shall the joint spacing of those concrete shoulders exceed 20 ft; when the current ADT is greater than 5 000 and/or the ADTT is greater than 500, the widening structure shall be extended into the shoulder area by two feet, otherwise a one‑foot extension shall be provided, and reclaimed asphalt concrete material may be used for shoulder base courses. [1]

[1] — Ch. 1 (p. 51), Ch. 2 (pp. 60–62), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 331–351)

Scope and applicability

The 3R Project is appropriate when the work involves improving an existing roadway—interstate, arterial, collector or local road—to extend its service life or enhance its structural and functional capacity without undertaking major capacity upgrades, realignment, or geometric reconstruction, and it applies to resurfacing, restoration or rehabilitation activities such as pavement overlays that may add structural capacity. It is used when the existing pavement can be returned to a condition of structural or functional adequacy through partial recycling, placement of additional surface materials, or similar treatments, i.e., when the pavement has not yet failed or become functionally obsolete. For cross‑slope design the 3R Project is treated the same as new‑construction and reconstruction work; the finished roadway surface must provide the minimum cross slope specified in Publication 13, and if those criteria cannot be satisfied a design‑exception request must be prepared. The 3R approach is appropriate for both rigid‑pavement rehabilitation and flexible‑pavement resurfacing where shoulders are part of the treatment; it may involve providing concrete or Type 6/7 shoulders on Interstates, limited‑access freeways or arterials that require excavation, scarifying, cleaning or patching of an existing shoulder, or, when widening the pavement, extending the new structure into the shoulder by two feet if the current ADT is greater than 5000 or the ADTT is greater than 500, otherwise by one foot; reclaimed asphalt concrete may also be used for base or surface courses provided recycled‑material guidelines are followed. When the existing pavement was originally constructed on an open‑graded subbase, an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base or a Cement Treated Permeable Base, the design may consider using a 2A Subbase as the base layer for a 3R Project, provided the selection is made in consultation with HDTD and the effective k‑value calculation is adjusted so that the ratio of the subbase to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed 4. [1]

[1] — Ch. 1 (p. 51), Ch. 2 (pp. 60–62), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162)

When project objectives include increasing traffic capacity, executing a major realignment, or substantially upgrading geometric features, the 3R scope—limited to extending service life and modestly improving structural or functional capacity—is not appropriate; such work should be classified under New Construction or Reconstruction (CIRT or NoCIRT) which address those larger changes. If the goal is only to maintain existing pavement without adding structural capacity, a Pavement Preservation approach is preferred. When the required minimum cross‑slope for the finished surface cannot be achieved, a 3R project becomes unsuitable and the guidelines mandate that a design exception request must be prepared and an alternate treatment should be considered. When the existing pavement has failed or become functionally obsolete, reconstruction is required and therefore preferred over 3R treatments. The micro‑surfacing element of a 3R Project should not be used when the atmospheric temperature is less than 50 °F for any part of the placement, and placement must be halted if rain is imminent or freezing temperatures are expected within 24 hours after application. In Districts 1‑0 through 5‑0 (Monroe, Carbon, Schuylkill) micro‑surfacing may not be applied from September 16 to April 30, and in Districts 6‑0, 8‑0, 9‑0, 11‑0, 12‑0 and 5‑0 (Berks, Lehigh, Northampton) it may not be applied from October 1 to April 30; when these seasonal limits are exceeded a different pavement treatment such as a conventional overlay or reconstruction should be considered. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 1 (p. 51), Ch. 2 (p. 60), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 331–332)

Information needs

The 3R Project must obtain existing pavement cross‑slope measurements and superelevation data for new construction, reconstruction, and 3R projects so the finished surface can be verified to provide at least the minimum cross slope required by Publication 13, Design Manual Part 2; if those design criteria cannot be met, a design exception request must be prepared according to Publication 10X, Appendix P and the team must evaluate whether additional leveling material or removal of surface material is needed. The project also needs current average daily traffic (ADT) and the ADT multiplied by the percentage of trucks, buses, and recreational vehicles (ADTT); when the current ADT is greater than 5000 and/or the ADTT is greater than 500, the widening structure shall be extended two feet into the shoulder, while when the current ADT is 5000 or less and/or the ADTT is 500 or less, the widening structure shall be extended one foot. Knowledge of the existing shoulder type is required to select Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S, 6‑SP, Type 7, or concrete shoulders, and the joint spacing of concrete shoulders shall not exceed 20 ft; if reclaimed asphalt is used, the recycled base course depth shall be at least five inches on arterials or four inches on collectors and local roads. In addition, the roadbed soil resilient modulus (including any seasonal adjustments) and detailed subbase information—type, thickness, and loss‑of‑support factor for each layer—must be obtained to calculate an effective k‑value, and the depth to a rigid foundation must be recorded when bedrock lies within ten feet of the subgrade surface. The base elastic modulus used in DARWin is set at 15 000 psi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30 000 psi for normal (summer/fall) conditions, and 50 000 psi for frozen conditions, but these may be adjusted so that the ratio of the subbase to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed four. When a mixed base such as CTPB over a 2A Subbase is considered, the combined thickness is used in the weighted loss‑of‑support calculation, and any use of a 2A Subbase for a 3R or CPR project requires consultation with HDTD. Under the 60‑month warranty for asphalt concrete pavement, the 3R Project must obtain detailed pavement‑condition data through the automated and manual distress surveys required by the warranty, applying Publication 336 methods to each half‑mile segment defined in the Department’s Location Reference System. The project must also track traffic data via annual classification counts to verify that cumulative equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) stay within the projected contract values, with any exceedance of 100 % of the design‑life ESALs shifting responsibility for rutting or base failures. When fatigue cracking or other base‑condition distress is identified, core samples are required and tested for voids, asphalt content and gradation to determine if the base condition is beyond the contractor’s control. Stakeholder input consists of written Department notifications of performance survey results, required remedial actions—including thresholds such as 30 % segment area with two or more low‑severity distresses triggering microsurfacing and a remedial‑action area greater than 20 % of a segment requiring full replacement—approvals for any alternate repair plans, and participation in the Conflict Resolution Team for dispute resolution. Under the polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt paving system specifications, the 3R Project must gather pavement condition data such as any fatigue or excessive rutting identified during a pre‑placement meeting with contractor and Penn DOT staff. It must also collect site weather information ensuring that the surface temperature is at least 50 °F, that rain is not imminent and no freezing temperatures are expected within 24 hours, and it must adhere to district‑specific seasonal restrictions on application dates. An approved Aggregate Delivery Plan detailing the number of trucks needed for continuous material flow is also required before work begins, providing logistical site information. Stakeholder input is captured through the pre‑placement meeting where specifications, quality‑control plan and supply lists are reviewed. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 60–62), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 331–351)

The information supporting a 3R Project must come from the automated (or manual when required) surveys prescribed in Publication 336 and be updated at least annually by the Department or its designee; each survey must identify, measure, and report all distresses for every segment so that the performance‑criteria limits in Table A can be applied. The results are to be communicated to the Contractor in writing within 14 calendar days of survey completion, allowing the Contractor to dispute the findings within 30 calendar days. Because remedial action is triggered when a year‑5 survey shows that 30% of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, the data must be complete enough to determine that threshold and current enough to reflect the most recent annual condition. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351)

Alternatives

In a 3R project the finished roadway surface shall provide a minimum cross‑slope, and this requirement may be satisfied by adding additional leveling material or, where appropriate, by removing existing surface material; if neither corrective action meets the design criteria a design exception request must be prepared. For shoulder treatment the feasible alternatives that must be compared include providing a concrete shoulder or using asphalt‑type shoulders such as Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S, 6‑SP, or Type 7, each selected according to whether excavation, scarifying, or only cleaning and patching of the existing paved shoulder is required. When widening a pavement the design shall compare extending the structure into the shoulder by two feet for corridors whose current average daily traffic exceeds 5 000 vehicles or whose ADT‑truck‑bus‑recreational vehicle product (ADTT) exceeds 500, against a one‑foot extension where the current ADT is 5 000 or less and ADTT is 500 or less, with the option to eliminate the extension if an appropriate shoulder type is used. The choice between using reclaimed asphalt concrete material in a recycled base course for Type 6/6‑F/6‑S shoulders or in a surface course for Type 7 shoulders versus new material is permissible, and the minimum depth shall be five inches on arterials and four inches on collectors and local roads; joint spacing for concrete shoulders may not exceed 20 ft. Rehabilitation alternatives that should be compared comprise crack‑and‑seat with structural overlays, rubblization with structural overlays, structural asphalt overlays, concrete overlays, partial recycling of the existing pavement, or placement of additional surface materials to restore functional adequacy. The base layer options that must be evaluated include selecting either an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base or a Cement Treated Permeable Base, varying subbase thicknesses, applying alternative loss‑of‑support factors for each subbase layer to correct the effective k‑value, and considering the depth to any rigid foundation within ten feet that could affect the effective k‑value; seasonal values of the base elastic modulus—15 000 psi for spring thaw, 30 000 psi for normal conditions, and 50 000 psi for frozen conditions—are also available as alternatives. Under the warranty specification the feasible remediation alternatives that must be compared are microsurfacing (or any other Department‑approved treatment) when low‑severity distresses occupy at least 30 % of a segment’s area, full removal and replacement of the segment when combined deviations exceed 20 % of the segment surface, and either separate square or rectangular patches versus one continuous repair when the distance between repair areas is less than 100 feet; each option must satisfy the performance‑criteria limits in Table A and the required surface tolerances. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 60–62), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351)

Decision criteria

The decision for a 3R Project is governed primarily by the performance criteria established in the warranty, which trigger remedial action when distress limits are exceeded; safety considerations also dominate because the Department may order immediate emergency repairs if the pavement is deemed potentially harmful or unsafe, and schedule constraints require that any mandated remediation be completed within sixty calendar days of notification, with penalties applied after the sixty‑first day. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351)

In 3R projects the first priority is traffic demand and existing shoulder condition; when the current average daily traffic exceeds 5 000 vehicles or the adjusted daily truck‑traffic factor exceeds 500, the widening structure shall be extended into the shoulder area by two feet. When the current ADT is 5 000 or less and the ADTT is 500 or less the extension shall be one foot. After the width decision, shoulder type selection follows pavement classification and distress level; concrete shoulders may be provided for rigid pavements provided that joint spacing does not exceed 20 ft. Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S, or 6‑SP shoulders are required on interstates and other limited‑access freeways and arterials where excavation or scarifying of the existing shoulder is necessary, a minimum three‑foot width of those types may be used when only the first few feet adjacent to the pavement are distressed, and Type 7 shoulders are specified where only cleaning and patching is necessary. For flexible pavements on collectors and local roads the shoulder material should match the pavement structure. Recycled asphalt material may be used in base or surface courses according to the recycled‑shoulder provisions, with a minimum depth of five inches on arterials and four inches on collectors and local roads. When determining the effective modulus of subgrade reaction for a 3R project each competing factor—subbase type, thickness, loss‑of‑support factor and depth to rigid foundation—is incorporated through a depth‑weighted average; the loss‑of‑support factors for each layer are multiplied by that layer’s depth and divided by the total depth of all layers (LS1·D1/(D1+D2)+ LS2·D2/(D1+D2)). This weighted ratio is then used together with the appropriate base elastic modulus—15 000 psi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30 000 psi for normal conditions, or 50 000 psi for frozen conditions—to compute the effective k‑value, and the resulting design must also satisfy the constraint that the ratio of the subbase to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed 4. The warranty specification resolves competing performance criteria by applying quantitative distress limits; when a segment survey shows that 30 % of its area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment must be performed. If combined deviations would affect more than 20 % of a segment’s surface area the entire segment must be removed and replaced. Repair zones spaced less than 100 feet apart are merged into one continuous repair. Any emergency condition triggers immediate action with repairs required to start within 48 hours of notification after a 24‑hour review period. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351)

Analysis methods and tools

The 3R Project’s rehabilitation alternatives are assessed through life‑cycle cost analysis whenever a PennDOT Oversight design requires such an evaluation, with the design submission routed to BODD for review and approval [1]. For projects under Federal Oversight, the FHWA applies its risk determination process to decide whether a design receives approval or is denied, serving as the primary evaluative mechanism in that context [1]. In addition to these cost‑based and risk‑based tools, the Project relies on systematic pavement condition surveys; automated survey equipment supplies the principal performance data while manual inspections are employed for flushing and pothole identification, both following the rating procedures and extent‑estimation methods prescribed in Publication 336 [2]. An annual performance analysis compares the measured distress levels against the limits defined in Table A, and this analysis is reinforced by routine traffic classification counts that verify the projected Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) values remain within contractual bounds [2]. When further investigation of base condition or material properties is required, nondestructive testing or core drilling accompanied by laboratory analyses such as PTM 715 and AASHTO T‑209 are utilized to provide additional assessment data [2].

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351)

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), under Section 2.5 CROSS‑SLOPES, the 3R Project design assumes that for new construction, reconstruction, and 3R projects the finished roadway surface shall provide the minimum cross‑slope prescribed in Publication 13; if the existing geometry does not satisfy this requirement, superelevations must be checked and corrected, which creates uncertainty about how much leveling or removal material will be needed, and when the required cross‑slope cannot be achieved a design exception request must be prepared, adding further uncertainty regarding approval [1]. Still in the Pavement Policy Manual, under Section 2.6 SHOULDERS, the analysis assumes that current average daily traffic (ADT) and derived annual daylight traffic (ADTT) are known, so that when ADT exceeds 5 000 vehicles per day or ADTT exceeds 500, the widening structure shall be extended two feet into the shoulder, whereas when ADT is 5 000 or less and/or ADTT is 500 or less the widening structure shall be extended one foot; uncertainty arises from the accuracy of traffic counts, truck‑percentage estimates, and variability in these inputs [1]. The same manual, also within Section 2.6 SHOULDERS, assumes that concrete shoulders shall have joint spacing not to exceed 20 ft and that reclaimed asphalt concrete may be used for base or surface courses on specified shoulder types, with a minimum depth of five inches on arterials and four inches on collectors and local roads; uncertainty is introduced by the quality and performance variability of reclaimed material when placed to these minimum depths [1]. In Section 2.5 CROSS‑SLOPES, the manual further assumes that the effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k) depends on the selected subbase type, its estimated thickness, the loss‑of‑support factor applied to each layer, any shallow rigid foundation within ten feet of the surface, and seasonal variations in base elastic modulus, with the additional constraint that the ratio of combined subbase modulus to roadbed soil resilient modulus shall not exceed four; because these inputs are often estimated—such as choosing a subbase type, estimating its thickness, applying LS values for erosion or pumping, and selecting seasonal E_SB values of 15 000 psi for spring thaw, 30 000 psi for normal conditions, or 50 000 psi for frozen conditions—the resulting k‑value carries uncertainty that propagates to pavement thickness and performance predictions [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408 (2020) specifications, under Section 496.3 CONSTRUCTION (c) Warranty for asphalt‑concrete pavement with a 60‑month warranty, assume that performance data are obtained from automated surveys conducted in accordance with Publication 336, with manual surveys used only for flushing and potholes, and that the Department’s annual traffic counts accurately represent equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) within the projected values; uncertainty stems from potential inaccuracies in these traffic counts and survey methods [2]. The same warranty section assumes that the distress limits shown in Table A—specifically that a segment area of 30 % containing two or more low‑severity distresses triggers microsurfacing—are appropriate thresholds for remediation, but when remedial‑action areas greater than 20 % of a segment’s surface occur the specification requires removal and replacement of the entire segment, introducing uncertainty about whether the project will meet these limits [2]. Further, the warranty provisions introduce uncertainty through the requirement that repair zones spaced less than 100 ft apart be combined into one continuous repair and by factors beyond the contractor’s control, including unexpected base‑condition failures, traffic loadings that exceed 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design‑life ESALs, routine Department maintenance, destructive procedures, natural disasters, or accident‑related spills, any of which can alter responsibility for remediation and add variability to the final results [2]. In Section 483.3 CONSTRUCTION of the same specifications, the polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing results assume that the existing surface will be fully cleaned of vegetation, loose material, dirt, mud, and other objectionable items, that all pavement markings and legends are completely removed, and that a tack coat is applied when the underlying layer is concrete; uncertainty arises if any fatigue or excessive rutting identified at the pre‑placement meeting is deemed unacceptable for treatment [2]. The micro‑surfacing section also assumes that placement may only occur when atmospheric temperature remains at least 50 °F for the entire operation, rain is not imminent, and no freezing temperatures are expected within 24 hours after application; deviation from these thresholds introduces uncertainty that can invalidate the results [2]. Finally, the specifications prohibit micro‑surfacing work from September 16 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0, 2‑0, 3‑0, 4‑0, 10‑0 and 5‑0 (Monroe, Carbon, and Schuylkill Counties) and from October 1 to April 30 in Districts 6‑0, 8‑0, 9‑0, 11‑0, 12‑0 and 5‑0 (Berks, Lehigh, and Northampton Counties); any deviation from these seasonal windows adds further uncertainty to project outcomes [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 60–62), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 331–351)

Stakeholders and constraints

In 3R projects that involve shoulder work, the District Executive may approve paving the entire shoulder with the same material and design as a widening at his or her discretion; no other participants are identified for input or decision‑making in that context. For rehabilitation work classified as a 3R project, certified PME/PMs provide technical input and submit a recommendation on Form D‑4332, while the District Engineer makes the final decision to approve the PennDOT Oversight pavement design. Any required waivers or deviations from the Manual for such rehabilitation work must be reviewed and approved by BODD. When a 2A Subbase is considered for the base layer in a 3R project, consultation with HDTD is required, meaning HDTD must provide input and effectively approve that subbase selection. For micro‑surfacing (3R) projects covered under Section 483, a preplacement meeting that includes the Representative, the Contractor and PennDOT personnel is required to provide input on pavement condition and specifications. The Contractor must submit an Aggregate Delivery Plan to the DME/DMM for approval before work begins. Any waiver of weather limitations can only be issued in writing by the District Executive. Under the 60‑month warranty provision of Section 496, the Department, through its Representative or the District Executive, provides primary input by conducting performance surveys and issuing written notifications that determine whether remediation is required, and it must review and approve any alternative testing, measurement, or remedial‑action plan submitted by the Contractor within five calendar days. The Contractor supplies input by proposing alternate methods or elective work and participates in a Conflict Resolution Team composed of two Department representatives, two Contractor representatives and a mutually agreed fifth member; each team member has an equal vote and the majority decision is final for disputes over remediation. The selection of a 3R project must conform to the roadway design criteria defined in Publication 13 Design Manual Part 2 Contextual Roadway Design, where Chapter 4 supplies the definitions and geometric design criteria that govern any treatment [1]. All finished surfaces shall achieve the minimum cross‑slope prescribed in Publication 13 Chapters 3 and 4, and superelevations on curves shall be verified and corrected as necessary [1]. Any shoulder reconstruction associated with a 3R project shall meet the same cross‑slope requirements of Publication 13 Chapters 3 and 4, and concrete joint spacing in shoulders may not exceed 20 ft [1]. When widening a pavement, if the current average daily traffic exceeds 5,000 vehicles per day or the ADTT exceeds 500, the widening structure shall be extended into the shoulder by 2 feet; if the ADT is 5,000 or less and the ADTT is 500 or less, the extension shall be 1 foot, with optional full‑width paving at the District Executive’s discretion [1]. For recycled asphalt concrete base or surface courses in a 3R project, a minimum depth of 5 inches is required on arterials and 4 inches on collectors and local roads [1]. All 3R work must conform to Publication 242 Pavement Policy Manual, and any deviation from that manual shall be submitted as a waiver to the Bureau of Design Development (BODD) for review and approval [1]. Pavement designs for PennDOT Oversight projects shall be submitted to BODD and are subject to annual quality‑assurance visits; when the work involves less than 30,000 square yards of mainline pavement surface a formal type determination is not required provided that documented justification for the selected treatment is included in the project file [1]. Federal‑Oversight projects require FHWA approval of the complete pavement design package unless FHWA determines otherwise based on risk [1]. For rigid‑pavement 3R work on Interstates and Expressways, the base shall consist of an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) over a 2A Subbase layer, with ATPB and CTPB bid as alternates, and the use of Open Graded Subbase is prohibited [1]. When calculating the effective subgrade reaction k‑value for a 3R project, the ratio of subbase to roadbed soil resilient modulus may not exceed 4, and seasonal base elastic moduli are prescribed at 15,000 psi for spring thaw, 30,000 psi for normal conditions and 50,000 psi for frozen conditions [1]. Under the 60‑month warranty for asphalt concrete pavement, if a year‑5 performance survey shows that thirty percent of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action shall be taken using microsurfacing or another treatment approved by the Department [2]. If deviations beyond the threshold levels in Table A combine to affect more than twenty percent of the surface area of any segment, the entire segment must be removed and replaced [2]. All repairs performed as part of a 3R project shall meet the surface tolerance specified in Section 413.3(l), and when the distance between repair areas is less than one hundred feet the work shall be executed as one continuous repair [2]. Remediation actions must commence within sixty calendar days after written notice, shall use plant‑mixed asphalt concrete unless an alternate material is expressly approved, and traffic control shall conform to Publications 212, 213 and the MUTCD [2]. For polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing treatments, a pre‑placement meeting with the Representative must be held and an Aggregate Delivery Plan submitted for approval before work begins [2]. Placement of the polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt may occur only when the atmospheric temperature remains at least fifty degrees Fahrenheit for the entire placement, and it is prohibited during district‑specific seasonal windows, for example no work from September 16 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0 through 5‑0 and from October 1 to April 30 in Districts 6‑0, 8‑0, 9‑0, 11‑0, 12‑0 and 5‑0 [2]. Equipment used for the micro‑surfacing shall include screening plants limited to three‑eighth‑inch openings for Type A aggregates, a continuous‑flow twin‑shafted pug mill at least fifty inches long, and a self‑propelled mixing machine equipped with the prescribed conveyor and pump systems [2].

Timing and implementation

The decision to initiate a 3R Project should be made after the Department’s performance survey shows that the distress criteria have been exceeded; specifically, if the year‑5 survey finds that thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action such as microsurfacing must be selected. The Department then notifies the Contractor in writing within fourteen calendar days of the survey results, at which point the decision to proceed with the approved treatment is triggered. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351)

In the Pavement Policy Manual, if the required minimum cross slope for a new construction, reconstruction or 3R project cannot be achieved according to Publication 13, the designer must prepare a design‑exception request [1]. The same manual also requires that superelevations in curved sections found to be out of tolerance be checked and corrected, which may involve adding leveling material or removing surface material [1]. For a 3R widening, the Pavement Policy Manual specifies that when the current average daily traffic exceeds 5 000 vehicles per day or the truck‑bus‑recreational vehicle component exceeds 500, the widening structure shall be extended two feet into the shoulder; when both ADT is 5 000 or less and ADTT is 500 or less the extension may be limited to one foot [1]. Under Penn DOT’s 60‑month warranty, action is triggered when the five‑year performance survey determines that thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, requiring microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment [2]. The warranty also triggers remedial action if deviations beyond the Table A limits in combination affect more than twenty percent of the surface area of any segment—medium‑severity cracks are counted as six inches on each side—and in that case the entire segment must be removed and replaced [2]. Once such a determination is made, the Department must issue written notice of required remediation within five calendar days, and the contractor must complete the work within sixty calendar days of receiving that notice, after which liquidated damages may begin to accrue [2]. An emergency repair is triggered when conditions are potentially harmful or unsafe; the contractor has twenty‑four hours to acknowledge the Department’s immediate notification and must begin repairs within forty‑eight hours, otherwise the Department may perform the emergency repair itself [2]. Before any micro‑surfacing material is placed, a preplacement meeting with the contractor and Penn DOT personnel must be held and an Aggregate Delivery Plan submitted for approval; placement may proceed only when atmospheric temperature remains at least fifty degrees Fahrenheit throughout placement, rain is not imminent, freezing temperatures are not expected within twenty‑four hours after application, and seasonal bans prohibit micro‑surfacing from September 16 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0, 2‑0, 3‑0, 4‑0, 10‑0, 5‑0 or from October 1 to April 30 in Districts 6‑0, 8‑0, 9‑0, 11‑0, 12‑0, 5‑0 [2].

The contractor must furnish all materials, equipment, labor and traffic‑control services required for warranty remediation at no cost to the Department, using plant‑mixed asphalt concrete unless other material is writtenly approved and employing temporary repair methods only when weather prevents permanent work. After the Department issues a written notice that remedial action is needed, the contractor must notify the District Executive of a tentative start date and submit a detailed schedule within five calendar days, then complete the required remediation within sixty calendar days; failure to begin by day 61 triggers liquidated damages. For emergency repairs the contractor has twenty‑four hours to acknowledge the request and, if it accepts, must commence work within forty‑eight hours of the Department’s initial notification. To implement a micro‑surfacing option under the 3R Project, the contractor must first hold a preplacement meeting with PennDOT personnel before any material is placed and submit an approved Aggregate Delivery Plan that details the number of trucks delivering screened aggregates. The work requires a self‑propelled continuous‑loading mixing machine equipped with a chain‑dragged conveyor, a twin‑shafted pug mill at least 50 inches long, a spray bar, calibrated material control devices, and a mechanical squeegee spreading box with flexible seals, as well as screening plants fitted with a 3/8‑inch (Type A) or 1/2‑inch (Types B/RF) scalping screen. Placement may only occur when the surface is clean, tack‑coated where required and dampened, and it must be done under weather constraints that mandate a minimum atmospheric temperature of 50°F for the entire placement, prohibit work from September 16 to April 30 in certain districts (or October 1 to April 30 in other specified districts), and forbid application if rain is imminent or freezing temperatures are expected within 24 hours. For projects less than 20,000 square yards a truck‑mounted unit may be used, but limited‑access highways and municipal projects require the full equipment set. [2]

Outcomes and follow-up

Success for a 3R Project is realized when the finished roadway surface provides the minimum cross‑slope specified in Publication 13, Design Manual Part 2, and superelevations on curved sections are inspected and corrected if necessary, with no design‑exception request required; compliance is measured by verifying that the as‑built surface meets the minimum cross‑slope, confirming any needed leveling or material removal, checking superelevation corrections, and documenting the absence of a design exception [1]. For shoulders, success requires full conformity with the shoulder design and recycling specifications in Section 2.6 B: concrete shoulders must be placed so that joint spacing does not exceed 20 ft, the appropriate Type 6/6‑F/6‑S/6‑SP or Type 7 shoulder must be provided according to roadway class and whether only cleaning/patching is needed, widening into the shoulder shall follow traffic thresholds—2 ft extension when ADT > 5000 or ADTT > 500, otherwise 1 ft—and reclaimed asphalt concrete shall be used with a minimum depth of 5 inches on arterials or 4 inches on collectors and local roads; measurement is performed by inspecting the constructed shoulder to verify each quantitative limit and material selection [1]. Additionally, success under the 60‑month warranty is achieved when each pavement segment remains within the distress limits set out in Table A throughout the warranty period, as confirmed by the Department’s annual automated (and where needed manual) performance surveys conducted according to Publication 336; a segment is considered successful at the year‑5 survey if less than 30% of its area contains two or more low‑severity distresses and total deviations do not exceed greater than 20% of the segment’s surface area, otherwise full‑depth removal and replacement would be required [2]. The measurement for warranty performance consists of those survey results and any written Department notifications indicating whether distress limits have been met; if limits are exceeded, the contractor must complete the prescribed remediation work within 60 calendar days to satisfy the final warranty inspection requirements [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 60–62); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351)

The Department monitors 3R Project results by conducting annual automated surveys of each half‑mile segment and supplementing them with manual surveys for flushing, potholes and any other required checks in accordance with Publication 336; performance is judged against Table A limits such as the 30% threshold for low‑severity distresses. Survey outcomes are documented in written reports delivered within fourteen calendar days that name the segment, the specific distress criterion, and whether the limit is met or exceeded, and the contractor receives full access to all performance surveys and traffic counts; if a threshold is crossed, the Department notifies the contractor in writing within five days that remediation is required. These documented results are used to trigger prescribed remedial actions—microsurfacing when the 30% low‑severity distress limit is reached or full segment replacement when deviations total greater than 20% of the surface area of any segment—and to justify additional surveys or adjustments to maintenance plans based on observed performance trends.
