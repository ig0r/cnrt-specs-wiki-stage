Modulus of Rupture

Part of: Portland Cement Concrete is-subtopic-of
Modulus of Rupture is a topic under Portland Cement Concrete.

Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

The Modulus of Rupture (MOR) is the extreme‑fiber tensile stress that a concrete slab can sustain at the point of fracture, serving as the direct measure of its flexural strength and the key strength parameter governing rigid pavement design [1]. It is calculated from compressive strength using S′c = K·(f′c)^0.5 with K = 9, which for Class AA concrete in Pennsylvania yields a prescribed value of 631 psi that must be used unless local experience dictates otherwise [1]. In fiber‑reinforced overlays the plain‑concrete MOR provides the baseline flexural strength and is augmented by the residual fiber strength (f150) to produce an effective flexural strength feff = MOR + f150, a value that drives mix design, slab thickness, and durability considerations [1].

Material purpose and applicability

The concrete modulus of rupture is the extreme‑fiber tensile stress that a concrete slab can sustain at the point of fracture, providing a direct measure of its flexural strength. In the 28‑day mean PCC modulus of rupture calculation (section 8.4) it is obtained from S′c = K (f′c)^0.5 with K equal to 9, and for Class AA concrete in Pennsylvania this yields a value of 631 psi; that 631 psi figure is prescribed for use in all rigid pavement designs unless district experience or data indicate otherwise, thereby serving as the key strength parameter that governs the design of rigid pavements. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays (section 10.10) the modulus of rupture represents the flexural strength of the plain concrete and provides the baseline strength term that, when combined with the residual fiber strength f150, produces an elevated effective flexural strength feff = MOR + f150, which is entered into structural design programs to size and evaluate concrete overlays for durability and serviceability. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 160–161), Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

Composition and characteristics

The concrete modulus of rupture is defined as the extreme fiber tensile stress that develops at the breaking bond of a flexural specimen. It is calculated by multiplying a constant K, which equals 9, by the square root of the concrete’s compressive strength (f’c). For Class AA concrete in Pennsylvania the resulting mean value is 631 psi and this figure is to be used in all rigid pavement designs unless district experience suggests otherwise. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 160–161)

The guide provides this relationship for the modulus of rupture:

Sc′=K(fc′)0.5

This formula computes the concrete modulus of rupture, defined as a measure of extreme fiber tensile stress under a breaking bond. It uses the constant K and the PCC compressive strength f'c. As K grows, the result rises. As f'c grows, the result also rises.

Adding macrofibers to a concrete overlay raises its modulus of rupture because the residual strength contributed by the fibers augments the plain‑concrete flexural strength, giving an effective flexural strength equal to MOR plus f150. The size of this increase varies with the fiber composition—different synthetic or steel types, geometries, and surface treatments change the residual strength that can be achieved for a given mix. Because distinct fibers require different dosages, the proportioning of fibers typically falls between 0.2% and 0.5% by volume to meet the specified residual strength, and higher fiber content generally yields greater MOR improvement. However, increasing fiber type or amount also reduces workability, with slump potentially dropping up to 4 in., a loss that can be mitigated by admixtures. [1]

Key material properties

The modulus of rupture of a pavement overlay is governed primarily by its flexural strength, which can be increased by the residual strength contributed by macrofibers (f150) so that an effective flexural strength (feff = MOR + f150) is obtained; mechanical performance depends on fiber material properties such as stiffness, geometry, aspect ratio and surface texture because these control the residual strength the fibers provide; physical mix characteristics like workability also matter since adding macrofibers can reduce slump by up to 4 in. and may require adjustments of water‑reducing or air‑entraining admixtures; chemical durability is reflected in the ability of fiber‑reinforced concrete to retain significant residual strength after many freeze/thaw cycles, enhancing flexural fatigue resistance and post‑cracking toughness even though compressive strength is not improved. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

In the 28‑day mean PCC modulus of rupture subsection (section 8.4), the modulus of rupture is defined as the extreme fiber tensile stress under a breaking bond and is calculated by S′c = K (f′c)^0.5 with K set to 9; applying the average compressive strength for Class AA concrete in Pennsylvania (4,925 psi) yields a modulus of rupture of 631 psi, which should be used in all rigid pavement designs unless district experience and data indicate otherwise. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays (section 10.10), the modulus of rupture is measured as the flexural strength of the plain concrete and this MOR value is combined with the fiber‑induced residual strength f150—determined by ASTM C1609—to obtain an effective flexural strength (feff) used in overlay design, and fibers are classified primarily by the residual strength they can provide, which typically ranges between 100 and 225 psi, rather than by volume fraction or geometry. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 160–161), Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

Behavior and mechanisms

Fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays exhibit a higher flexural (modulus of rupture) capacity under load, with macrofibers raising both the flexural and ultimate load capacity compared with plain concrete slabs. The fibers also improve fatigue performance and post‑cracking strength while retaining significant residual strength after many freeze/thaw cycles, indicating good resistance to temperature cycling. The source does not address moisture or chemical exposure effects on modulus of rupture. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

In fiber-reinforced concrete overlays, the Modulus of Rupture is governed primarily by mechanical mechanisms such as crack-bridging provided by macrofibers, which add a residual strength (f150) to the plain‑concrete flexural strength and thus raise the effective flexural strength used in design; this increase can depend on the fiber type and content. The effectiveness of a macrofiber is controlled by its material properties, geometry, surface enhancements, and interaction with the concrete matrix, so fibers with appropriate stiffness, aspect ratio and surface texture enhance load transfer efficiency and reduce crack widths, thereby improving post‑cracking strength and toughness. Although the fibers improve flexural fatigue performance and post‑cracking behavior, they do not improve the inherent compressive and flexural strengths of the uncracked concrete. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

Durability and deterioration

In pavement overlays the modulus of rupture can be compromised by crack formation and widening—including plastic shrinkage cracking—and by repeated freeze/thaw cycling, which erode flexural strength; fiber‑reinforced concrete mitigates these durability concerns by limiting crack widths, reducing the number of cracks, and preserving residual strength even after many freeze/thaw cycles. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

Adding synthetic or steel macrofibers to the concrete mix reduces the susceptibility of the material’s modulus of rupture to deterioration by raising effective flexural strength and limiting crack growth; typical fiber contents range from 0.2% to 0.5% by volume and provide residual strength that remains significant even after many freeze‑thaw cycles. Tighter joint and crack openings of less than 0.5 to 1.0 mm improve durability, while wider cracks (>1.0 mm) do not confer the same improvement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

Material interactions and compatibility

The Modulus of Rupture (MOR) of the plain concrete forms the baseline flexural strength that is augmented by the residual strength contributed by macrofibers, yielding an effective flexural strength (feff) used in overlay design. Because the effectiveness of a macrofiber depends on its material properties, geometry, surface enhancements, and interaction with the concrete matrix, the fiber‑matrix bond directly influences how much additional strength is obtained. Adding macrofibers also reduces mix workability, lowering slump by up to 4 in. (100 mm), so water‑reducing or air‑entraining admixtures are often adjusted to meet fresh‑property specifications. For FRC overlays the typical fiber content may range from 0.2% to 0.5% by volume. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

Influence of production and placement

In fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, the mix design influences the attainable modulus of rupture because macrofibers can reduce slump by up to 4 in; however, the addition of water‑reducing admixtures or other mixture modifications can compensate for the slump loss and improve finishability, helping the concrete achieve its specified flexural strength (MOR) when placed. Trial batches are always recommended to confirm that the FRC mixture can meet all fresh property specifications before placement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

Construction‑related variations such as slab movement, slab misalignment, plastic shrinkage cracking and crack widening are identified as factors that can reduce the long‑term flexural capacity reflected by the Modulus of Rupture. In addition, if the timing or depth of sawcut contraction joints is not proper, premature cracking and dominant joints may develop, further compromising MOR performance. The presence of stripping in the bottom layers of the existing asphalt when a bonded overlay is placed on a milled surface also threatens long‑term strength, while excessive macrofiber content can lower workability, with slump reductions of up to 4 in., potentially affecting the uniformity and thus the MOR of the cured slab. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

Selection, specification, and improvement

The modulus of rupture for rigid pavement designs is calculated from the average compressive strength of Class AA concrete in Pennsylvania (4,925 psi) using the relationship S′c = K·(f′c)^0.5 with K set to 9, which produces a modulus of rupture of 631 psi; this 631‑psi figure should be used for all rigid pavement designs unless district experience and data indicate otherwise, making local performance information the overriding criterion. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays the pavement engineer must specify a residual strength (f150) that must be achieved so that, when added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture, the resulting effective flexural strength meets the design objectives for slab flexural capacity, service life, allowable crack widths and joint load‑transfer efficiency under the expected traffic loads and support conditions; residual strength values for concrete overlay applications typically range between 100 psi and 225 psi, and the required amount of macrofibers, generally 0.2% to 0.5% by volume, is chosen based on those technical factors and cost considerations. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 160–161), Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

Adding synthetic or steel macrofibers to the concrete mix is an effective way to raise the effective flexural strength, because the residual strength (f150) contributed by the fibers is added to the plain‑concrete Modulus of Rupture (MOR) to give feff = MOR + f150. Selecting a fiber dosage that yields a residual strength in the range of 100 to 225 psi—typically achieved with 0.2% to 0.5% by volume of fibers 1–2.5 in long and aspect ratio 30–100—provides higher load‑transfer efficiency, narrower cracks, and better fatigue performance. Because macrofibers reduce workability, water‑reducing admixtures can be used to offset slump loss of up to 4 in (100 mm), and air‑entraining admixture may be adjusted during trial batches to meet fresh‑property specifications; such trial mixes serve as quality control to verify the required residual strength before placement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

Performance evaluation and implications

Evaluation of the Modulus of Rupture for pavement concrete relies on laboratory slab testing that measures flexural and ultimate load capacity, and on the ASTM C1609 test which provides the residual strength (f150) used together with MOR to compute an effective flexural strength. Field evidence is obtained from comparative performance studies that show FRC overlays outperform plain‑concrete overlays, and documented projects compiled in the National Concrete Overlay Explorer serve as long‑term performance records. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)

In the 1993 AASHTO rigid pavement design procedure described in Chapter 8, the 28‑day mean PCC Modulus of Rupture is listed among the required input variables, meaning its measured value must be supplied when using the DARWin software to calculate slab properties. The 28‑day mean PCC modulus of rupture is expressed as S′c = K (f′c)^0.5 with K set to 9, and for Class AA concrete in Pennsylvania an average compressive strength of 4,925 psi with a standard deviation of 432 psi yields a prescribed S′c of 631 psi; this value is to be used in all rigid pavement designs unless District experience and data indicates otherwise, directly governing the selection of concrete mixes and the predicted flexural capacity of the pavement structure. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, the effective flexural strength is obtained by adding the material’s Modulus of Rupture to the residual strength contributed by macrofibers (f150), and this higher feff allows designers to reduce slab thickness or increase span while still meeting load‑transfer and durability requirements; fibers raise both the flexural capacity and the load‑transfer efficiency by as much as 30 % compared with plain concrete, limit crack widths to less than about one millimeter, and thereby produce slower crack propagation, better joint performance, and longer service life under heavy repeated loading. The increased MOR‑based strength also influences construction specifications such that the required fiber content typically ranges from 0.2 % to 0.5 % by volume and fresh‑mix workability adjustments are needed to achieve the target residual strength. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (p. 160), Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188)
