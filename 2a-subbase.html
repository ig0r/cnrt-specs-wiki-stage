2A Subbase

Part of: Subbase is-subtopic-of
2A Subbase is a topic under Subbase.
2a-subbase2a-subbase-material2-a-subbase-material
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

The 2A Subbase is a No. 2A aggregate layer designated as the sub‑base material in pavement construction, placed directly on a prepared subgrade (or over a Class 4 Type A geotextile) and compacted to at least three inches in lifts not exceeding eight inches (four inches when granulated slag is used) until a non‑movement condition is achieved[1][2]. Its primary purpose is to provide a uniformly compacted, well‑moistened foundation that isolates and supports the subgrade, creating a stable platform for overlying base courses such as Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base while contributing to the effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k) through a weighted‑average loss‑of‑support calculation[2][1]. The finished surface must be shaped to a firm, even plane within ±½ inch of grade and contour, and if the layer is exposed for more than sixty calendar days or used as a haul road it must be retested and any deficiencies corrected to preserve its drainage and load‑bearing performance[2][1].

Material purpose and applicability

The 2A Subbase is a No. 2A aggregate layer designated as the sub‑base material in pavement construction [1][2]. In rigid pavement designs the 2A Subbase is placed directly beneath a base course that is typically an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) [1]. For flexible pavements the prepared 2A Subbase serves as the foundation on which the required asphalt base course is placed [2]. For rigid pavement structures the same 2A Subbase provides the foundation on which reinforced or plain cement concrete is installed [2]. The layer must be placed directly on the prepared subgrade—or over a specified geotextile—compacted to a minimum depth of three inches and to a non‑movement condition as required by Section 206.3(b) [2]. After placement the subbase surface must be shaped into a firm, even plane within a tolerance of plus or minus one‑half inch of grade and contour [2]. Its function is to provide a uniformly compacted foundation that isolates and supports the subgrade, creating a stable platform for the overlying base course and pavement [2]. When used to backfill beneath a fractured concrete slab, Subbase No. 2A supplies a stable fill and support beneath the repaired pavement structure [2]. In structural analysis the 2A Subbase contributes to the effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k‑value) because its strength or modulus is combined with that of the overlying base in a weighted‑average loss‑of‑support calculation, ensuring adequate support and drainage for the pavement system [1]. The combined base‑subbase assembly also provides drainage for the pavement system [1]. If a 2A Subbase is used as a haul road or remains exposed to weather for more than sixty calendar days, it must be retested for surface irregularities and depth and any deficiencies corrected before final paving [2].

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 226–227), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–435)

2A Subbase is appropriate as the base layer beneath an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) for all Interstate and Expressway rigid pavement designs, and it may also be used as the base layer on 3R and other CPR projects when the existing pavement was built on OGS, ATPB, or CTPB after consultation with HDTD. In design calculations the subbase is treated together with the base, and the effective k‑value uses a Base Elastic Modulus of 15,000 psi for spring thaw, 30,000 psi for normal (summer/fall), and 50,000 psi for frozen conditions, reflecting climate influences. When a drainage layer is required, Open Graded Subbase is prohibited and only ATPB or CTPB should be specified instead of 2A Subbase. Thus another material should be selected when a dedicated drainage layer is needed or when project specifications prohibit the use of 2A Subbase. [1]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162)

Composition and characteristics

The 2A Subbase is defined as a course of No. 2A aggregate that, where required, is placed on a prepared subgrade together with a Class 4, Type A geotextile and must be laid in a well‑moistened condition so that segregation is prevented and the moisture content is sufficient to limit additional water during compaction; each layer is spread uniformly without abrupt turns or fabric damage and compacted to a non‑movement condition. For both rigid and flexible pavements the aggregate must be placed to a minimum compacted depth of 3 inches, and the overall subbase must be built in layers not more than 8 inches in compacted depth, although when granulated slag is used each layer must be limited to not more than 4 inches in compacted depth. In the full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slab system the same No. 2A coarse aggregate, as referenced in Sections 703.2 and 350, is placed and compacted to provide a firm, even surface that must be finished within a tolerance of plus or minus ½ inch of grade and contour before slab placement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 226–227), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Variations in the composition or source of the 2A subbase are limited by the mixing requirements: a blend of materials from different sources may be used only if it is permitted, and the material must contain enough moisture to prevent segregation during stockpiling, hauling, and placing, thereby reducing the amount of water that must be added during compaction. Keeping the subbase well‑moistened from before placement through compaction helps achieve the non‑movement density condition required by Section 206.3(b). [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 226–227)

Key material properties

The effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k) governs the mechanical stiffness and support characteristics of a 2A Subbase, and it depends on the subbase type’s strength or modulus values, its thickness, and the loss‑of‑support factor used to adjust k for erosion or pumping. Temperature conditions also affect the base elastic modulus applied in design, with 15,000 psi specified for spring thaw conditions, 30,000 psi for normal (summer and fall) conditions, and 50,000 psi for frozen conditions. The ratio of the subbase to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed 4. Performance relies on keeping the aggregate sufficiently moist so that segregation does not occur during stockpiling, hauling, or placement and the material remains well‑moistened through compaction. The material must then be compacted to a condition of non‑movement as specified in Section 206.3(b). Each No. 2A layer shall be placed to at least a 3‑inch compacted depth. Subbase layers shall not exceed 8 inches in compacted depth, and when granulated slag is used the limit is reduced to 4 inches per layer. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 226–227)

The effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k) is used to characterize the mechanical performance of a 2A Subbase; this k‑value is obtained by combining the base and subbase layers and applying a weighted average that reflects each layer’s thickness and its loss‑of‑support factor [1]. The resulting k‑value incorporates a Base Elastic Modulus for the subbase that is specified as 15,000 psi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30,000 psi for normal (summer/fall) conditions, and 50,000 psi for frozen conditions [1]. In addition, the calculated k‑value must be adjusted so that the ratio of the subbase resilient modulus to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed 4 [1]. For construction classification, a No. 2A aggregate subbase is required to be placed in lifts with a minimum compacted depth of 3 inches [2]. The thickness of any individual compacted lift shall not exceed 8 inches, except when granulated slag is used, where the maximum lift thickness is limited to 4 inches [2]. Compaction quality is verified by achieving the condition of non‑movement defined in Section 206.3(b) and by conducting density tests at designated locations; after each test a hole is excavated through the full subbase thickness and the Representative measures the finished depth, with any deficiency of ½ inch or more rendering the section defective [2]. If the 2A Subbase serves as a haul road or remains exposed to weather for more than 60 calendar days, it must be retested for surface irregularities and depth, and any deficiencies must be corrected in accordance with Sections 210.3(c) and 350.3(g) [2]. Prior to placing either flexible or rigid pavement, stability is further evaluated by performing a proof roll as specified in Section 206.3(a)1; areas that exhibit instability require removal of the subbase and repair of the underlying subgrade [2].

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 226–227)

Behavior and mechanisms

The 2A Subbase contributes to the pavement’s load‑bearing capacity through its inherent strength or modulus, which is expressed as an effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k) used in design [1]. Moisture‑related deterioration is accommodated by applying a loss‑of‑support factor that adjusts the effective k‑value to reflect potential erosion or pumping of the subbase material [1]. Temperature conditions modify the elastic modulus applied to the 2A Subbase, with a value of 15,000 psi specified for “spring thaw” conditions, 30,000 psi for “normal” summer and fall conditions, and 50,000 psi for frozen winter conditions [1]. When a No. 2A subbase is exposed to weather for more than 60 calendar days, the specification requires retesting for surface irregularities and depth, with any deficiencies corrected before further construction activities [2]. If the 2A subbase is employed as a haul road, it must also undergo this post‑exposure retest prior to continued use [2]. The material shall not be left exposed during winter shutdowns; if it remains in place over winter without an asphalt or concrete protective layer, proof rolling is required before placement of the overlying pavement and any zones showing instability must be removed and repaired at no additional cost to the Department [2].

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 227)

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (section 8.6 MEAN EFFECTIVE K‑VALUE), the mechanical response of a 2A Subbase is governed primarily by its material type and thickness, which determine the subbase strength or modulus and thereby affect the effective k‑value used in rigid pavement design [1]. The same source specifies that a loss‑of‑support factor must be applied to correct the effective k‑value for potential erosion or pumping of the subbase material, reflecting a mechanical mechanism related to loss of support [1]. It also requires consideration of bedrock located within 10 feet of the subgrade surface because such proximity can alter the subgrade reaction, another mechanical influence on behavior [1]. Temperature conditions are represented by prescribed base elastic modulus values—15,000 psi for spring thaw, 30,000 psi for normal conditions, and 50,000 psi for frozen conditions—and these values should be adjusted so that the ratio of the subbase to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed 4, linking thermal effects to mechanical stiffness [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications (section 350.3 CONSTRUCTION) state that performance is governed primarily by moisture content, which must be sufficient to keep the material well‑moistened from placement through compaction in order to prevent segregation and to limit additional water needed during compaction, a physical mechanism controlling behavior [2]. Under the same specification each No. 2A aggregate layer must be placed to a minimum compacted depth of 3 inches and built up in layers not exceeding 8 inches in compacted depth (or not more than 4 inches when granulated slag is used), establishing mechanical thickness limits for the subbase [2]. Compaction must continue until the subbase reaches a condition of non‑movement as required by Section 206.3(b), providing a mechanical criterion for achieving sufficient density and stability [2].

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 226–227)

Durability and deterioration

The durability of a 2A Subbase is chiefly threatened by loss of support, which may occur through erosion or pumping of the material and thereby reduces its effective modulus; this is accounted for with a loss‑of‑support factor in k‑value calculations. Designers must ensure that the ratio of the subbase modulus to the underlying soil resilient modulus does not exceed four, otherwise an artificial condition could be introduced. Placement of 2A Subbase on soft, muddy, or frozen ground is prohibited because such conditions can produce a defective layer. Insufficient moisture that allows segregation during stockpiling, hauling, or placing may threaten the stability of the subbase. During installation of the overlying subbase the Class 4, Type A geotextile shall not be disturbed or damaged; any pulling, folding, or tensioning requires repair at no additional cost. The subbase must achieve the non‑movement compaction condition and retain the specified thickness; a depth deficiency of 1/2 inch or more renders the subbase defective and calls for scarification, blending of additional material, and recompaction. Construction layers shall not exceed 8 inches in compacted depth, and when granulated slag is used each layer shall be limited to not more than 4 inches in compacted depth. The durability of a No. 2A subbase is compromised when it is exposed to weather for more than 60 calendar days or when it is used as a haul road, because such conditions require retesting for surface irregularities and depth and correcting any deficiencies. The specification forbids leaving the subbase uncovered during winter shutdowns; if the subbase remains over winter without an asphalt base course or cement concrete protective layer, it must be proof‑rolled before placement of flexible or rigid pavement, and any areas showing instability require removal of the subbase and repair or replacement of the underlying subgrade. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 226–227)

The susceptibility of a 2A Subbase to deterioration is influenced by its material type and thickness; thinner sections provide less support and therefore increase the risk of degradation [1]. The loss‑of‑support (LS) factor applied for erosion or pumping corrects the effective k‑value and further reduces support, raising the likelihood of deterioration [1]. Seasonal exposure conditions change the base elastic modulus, with spring‑thaw conditions yielding a modulus of about 15,000 psi that is less stiff than frozen conditions where the modulus reaches roughly 50,000 psi, thereby increasing susceptibility during thaw periods [1]. The design ratio of subbase to roadbed soil resilient modulus should not exceed 4; exceeding this limit would artificially overstress the subbase and increase its deterioration potential [1]. Exposure to traffic, including construction traffic, increases the likelihood that a No. 2A aggregate subbase will deteriorate [2]. When the subbase remains exposed to weather for more than 60 calendar days, susceptibility to deterioration also rises, and the specification requires retesting for surface irregularities and depth and correction of any deficiencies [2]. Providing permanent cover—such as placing the required asphalt base course over all prepared subbase on flexible pavements or installing reinforced or plain cement concrete over rigid pavements before winter shutdowns—prevents exposure and therefore reduces the risk of deterioration [2].

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 227)

Material interactions and compatibility

During construction the 2A Subbase must be placed on a prepared subgrade that has been corrected for any soft, muddy or frozen conditions, ensuring the aggregate layer rests directly on stable soil rather than an unsuitable surface. A Class 4, Type A geotextile is installed beneath the subbase and must remain undamaged while the material is back‑dumped, spread and compacted, so the subbase’s load is transferred through the fabric to the subgrade. The aggregate is mixed with enough moisture before delivery to keep it well‑moistened, which prevents segregation during stockpiling, hauling and placement and reduces the amount of water that must be added during compaction. Each 2A layer is compacted to a minimum depth of three inches (or four inches for No. OGS aggregate) in lifts not exceeding eight inches, providing a dense, non‑moving mass that interacts mechanically with the underlying soil and overlying pavement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 226–227)

Influence of production and placement

Proper mixing of the 2A subbase material must retain sufficient moisture to prevent segregation during stockpiling, hauling and placement, thereby reducing the amount of water that needs to be added at compaction and helping to achieve a uniform density. During placement the material is confined by berms, spread in a manner that does not cause segregation to a uniform depth and must reach a minimum compacted depth of 3 inches for No. 2A aggregate; it is placed over an intact Class 4 Type A geotextile, abrupt turns with tracked equipment are avoided and the fabric must not be damaged, which together prevent uneven support or loss of drainage function. Compaction is performed from the sides toward the centre with overlapping passes until a non‑movement condition is reached, as required by Section 206.3(b), securing the intended load‑bearing capacity and resistance to settlement. Depth checks require that any deficiency of 1/2 inch or more be corrected by scarifying to 3 inches, blending in additional material and recompacting, ensuring the finished subbase meets the specified elevation and performance criteria. After placement and compacting, the surface must be shaped so that it is firm, even and within a tolerance of plus or minus ½ inch of grade and contour, providing the intended support and drainage for the overlying slab. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 226–227), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Variations in the subbase type and its installed thickness are factors that influence the effective modulus of reaction governing long‑term performance, while any loss of support caused by erosion or pumping of the 2A Subbase material is especially critical because it reduces the k‑value used in design. In addition, if bedrock lies within 10 feet of the subgrade surface for a significant length of the project, its proximity should be considered when determining the effective k‑value. Using prohibited Open Graded Subbase instead of an approved ATPB or CTPB drainage layer would also compromise the intended behavior. The long‑term behavior of a 2A Subbase is most affected by construction variations that disturb its designed thickness, moisture balance, compaction quality and the integrity of the underlying geotextile. Placing aggregate on soft, muddy or frozen subgrade, or failing to correct subgrade deficiencies before placement, creates defects that must be remedied because they compromise support and drainage. Using material with insufficient moisture that can segregate, or damaging the Class 4 Type A geotextile during back‑dumping, also degrades performance. Exceeding the permitted compacted layer thickness—more than 8 inches, or more than 4 inches when granulated slag is used—and allowing a depth shortfall of ½ inch or greater are identified as defects that must be corrected. Each No. 2A layer must be placed to at least a compacted depth of 3 inches and compacted to a condition of non‑movement with overlapping passes from the sides toward the centre to prevent loss of stability. Use of a No. 2A subbase as a haul road or exposure to weather for more than sixty calendar days triggers retesting for surface irregularities and depth, with any deficiencies required to be corrected. If the subbase remains uncovered during winter shutdowns, proof rolling must be performed before placing the final pavement, and any indication of instability mandates removal of the affected subbase, repair or replacement of the underlying subgrade, and reinstalling new subbase material. Inadequate placement or compaction of No. 2A coarse aggregate, as referenced in Section 703.2 and Section 350, is identified as a defect that directly affects durability, and the final surface must be shaped to a firm, even plane within a tolerance of plus or minus ½ inch of grade and contour; any excess creates uneven support that can degrade long‑term behavior. During seating operations for slab fracturing, failure to eliminate unsuitable or unstable material before backfilling compromises performance because all such material must be removed and the void beneath the fractured slab filled to its bottom with Subbase No. 2A; leaving inappropriate material in place or not achieving full‑depth backfill therefore represents a construction defect that most directly influences long‑term behavior. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 226–227), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–435)

Selection, specification, and improvement

The selection of a No. 2A subbase is driven by several design factors that affect the effective modulus of subgrade reaction, requiring engineers to consider the subbase type because different types provide different strengths or modulus values [1]. They must also evaluate the required subbase thickness to achieve an economical design while accounting for loss‑of‑support factors that correct the k‑value for possible erosion or pumping [1]. When bedrock lies within 10 feet of the subgrade surface over a significant length, its influence must be included in the k‑value calculation [1]. Any adjustment of elastic modulus values must keep the ratio of the subbase to the roadbed soil resilient modulus at or below 4 [1]. For Interstate and Expressway rigid pavements the design mandates that a base consisting of an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) layer be placed over the 2A subbase [1]. The intended traffic and exposure conditions further guide specification, such that if the No. 2A subbase is used on haul roads or will be exposed to weather for more than 60 calendar days, the material must be retested for surface irregularities and depth and any deficiencies corrected in accordance with the specified sections [2]. The subbase should not be left uncovered during winter shutdowns; instead a protective asphalt base course for flexible pavements or cement concrete for rigid pavements shall be placed before the shutdown [2]. If the subbase remains over winter without that protective layer, proof rolling as prescribed must be performed before placement of the flexible or rigid pavement, and any areas showing instability must be removed and the subgrade and subbase repaired or replaced; removal and replacement of the entire subbase is required when the majority of the material exhibits instability during proof rolling [2]. Where existing concrete slabs are being replaced, any soft, wet, or otherwise unsuitable subbase and subgrade material must be eliminated before the new base is constructed [2]. The replacement subbase shall consist of No. 2A coarse aggregate placed and compacted as required, and the finished surface must be firm and even within a tolerance of plus or minus 1/2 inch of grade and contour [2].

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 227), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

For concrete pavements, designers should pair a 2A Subbase with an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) rather than using an Open Graded Subbase, which is prohibited [1]. The effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k‑value) calculation must include the appropriate loss‑of‑support factor for each layer—e.g., a 0.5 LS for a TPBC layer—and use the combined thickness of base and subbase to obtain a weighted average, helping control erosion or pumping [1]. Seasonal adjustments to the base elastic modulus are required, using 15,000 psi for spring thaw, 30,000 psi for normal conditions, and 50,000 psi for frozen conditions [1]. The ratio of subbase to roadbed soil resilient modulus shall not exceed 4 to avoid artificial design conditions [1]. Moisture content must be kept sufficient throughout stockpiling, hauling and placement so that segregation is prevented and the amount of water added during compaction is minimized [2]. Each layer of No. 2A aggregate shall be placed to achieve at least a three‑inch compacted depth, with individual layers not exceeding eight inches in compacted thickness; when granulated slag is used the layer thickness shall be limited to no more than four inches, although the contractor may place a maximum compacted layer of six inches provided the full layer depth is compacted as required by Section 206.3(b) [2]. Proper compaction shall be performed from the sides toward the centre with overlapping passes until a non‑movement condition is attained [2]. If post‑placement testing reveals a depth deficiency of one‑half inch or more, the affected area must be scarified to a three‑inch depth, blended with additional material and re‑compacted, establishing an on‑site quality‑control loop [2]. When the subbase serves as a haul road or remains exposed to weather for more than sixty calendar days, it shall be retested for surface irregularities and depth and any deficiencies must be corrected in accordance with Sections 210.3(c) and 350.3(g) [2]. If the subbase is left over winter without an asphalt or concrete protective layer, a proof roll required by Section 206.3(a)1 must be performed before placement of the final flexible or rigid pavement; zones that exhibit instability shall require removal of the subbase, repair or replacement of the underlying subgrade and reinstatement of new subbase material, after which the subbase shall be replaced [2].

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 226–227)

Performance evaluation and implications

Evaluation of a 2A Subbase under this specification is based on field performance checks; laboratory testing or long‑term monitoring is not prescribed. After each density test the crew must excavate a test hole to the full subbase depth, and the Representative measures that depth. If the measured depth is deficient by 1/2 inch or more from the specified depth the subbase is deemed defective and must be scarified, blended with additional material and recompacted until compaction achieves a condition of non‑movement as referenced in Section 206.3(b). When the placed subbase has been exposed to weather for more than 60 calendar days it is retested for surface irregularities and depth, and any deficiencies are corrected in accordance with the specification. Prior to placement of flexible or rigid pavement a proof roll required by Section 206.3(a)1 is performed; if instability is observed during that rolling—when the majority of or all subbase exhibits instability—the subbase must be removed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 226–227)

The effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k) employed in rigid‑pavement design is directly influenced by the 2A Subbase because its type determines a specific strength or modulus, and its thickness alters the calculated design thickness and overall economy. A loss‑of‑support factor is applied to the 2A layer to correct the k‑value for possible erosion or pumping, thereby affecting durability and maintenance considerations. When the 2A Subbase is combined with an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base, their depths are weighted to obtain a single effective k‑value, and the subbase modulus must be limited so that its ratio to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed 4, preventing an artificial design condition. In construction, each No. 2A aggregate layer must achieve at least a 3‑inch minimum compacted depth and no single layer may exceed 8 inches (or 4 inches when granulated slag is used), which limits settlement and supports pavement performance. The subbase must be placed in uniform layers that are compacted to a non‑movement condition, and the material must be kept well‑moistened during mixing, stockpiling, hauling and placement to prevent segregation and reduce the need for additional water during compaction, promoting consistent density and durability. The subgrade shall be prepared free of soft, muddy or frozen areas before subbase installation, and any depth deficiency of ½ inch or more is deemed defective and must be scarified, blended with new material and recompacted, preventing uneven support that could lead to premature maintenance. If a 2A subbase is used as a haul road or is exposed to weather for more than 60 calendar days, the layer must be retested for surface irregularities and depth and any deficiencies corrected; it may not remain uncovered during winter shutdowns, and a protective asphalt base course for flexible pavements or reinforced/plain cement concrete for rigid pavements must be placed before the end of the construction season. When a subbase is left over winter without such protection, a proof roll as specified in Section 206.3(a)1 is required before placement of the final pavement and any zones showing instability must have the subbase removed and the subgrade repaired or replaced. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, No. 2A coarse aggregate is placed and compacted as the subbase material and shaped to a firm, even plane within a tolerance of plus or minus 1/2 inch of grade and contour, ensuring adequate support and precise grade control that influences construction accuracy and long‑term performance.
