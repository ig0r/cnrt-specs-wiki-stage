2A Subbase

Part of: Subbase is-subtopic-of
2A Subbase is a topic under Subbase.
2a-subbase2a-subbase-material2-a-subbase-material
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

The 2A Subbase is a layer of No. 2A aggregate placed directly on a prepared subgrade, often over a Class 4 Type A geotextile, that forms the structural sub‑base beneath an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base in rigid Interstate and Expressway pavements [1] [2]. It is spread to a uniform thickness, kept sufficiently moist to prevent segregation, and compacted to at least three inches (or up to eight inches per lift, four inches when granulated slag is used) until a non‑movement condition is achieved, thereby creating a firm, even bearing plane within ±½ inch of the design grade [2]. The aggregate must consist of hard, durable particles free of clay, silt, vegetation and deleterious minerals, with moisture absorption limited to 3 % (Type A) or 3.5 % (Type B), so that its stiffness can be expressed through an effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k‑value) that is weighted with the overlying base and adjusted for temperature and loss‑of‑support factors [1] [2].

Material purpose and applicability

The 2A Subbase is a course of No. 2A aggregate that serves as the subbase layer in pavement construction [2]. In rigid pavement designs for Interstate and Expressway facilities, the 2A Subbase is placed beneath an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) and therefore forms part of the structural system supporting the overlying base course [1][2]. The material is spread to a uniform thickness and compacted to at least a minimum compacted depth of 3 inches directly on the prepared subgrade before any base or pavement courses are placed, creating a firm foundation for the subsequent layers [2]. When a precast concrete slab is installed, No. 2A aggregate is similarly placed and compacted beneath the slab to produce an even surface whose final grade must be within plus or minus 1/2 inch of the specified contour, thereby providing a stable bearing plane for the slab [2]. During rubblizing operations, No. 2A aggregate backfills all excavated areas after concrete breakage; it is placed in lifts not exceeding six inches and compacted by rolling until no movement occurs, which supplies a stable fill beneath the seated pavement [2]. Because different subbase types exhibit varying strengths or modulus values, the 2A Subbase’s properties are incorporated together with the overlying base when calculating the effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k‑value), using a weighted average to reflect the combined support provided by the two layers [1]. This combined effect ensures that the pavement system receives adequate structural support and drainage as required for long‑term performance [1].

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 231–232), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 430–447)

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, 2A Subbase is appropriate as the base layer beneath an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) for all Interstate and Expressway rigid pavement designs, and it may also be used as the base layer on 3R and other CPR projects when the existing pavement was built on OGS, ATPB, or CTPB after consultation with HDTD. The manual indicates that in design calculations the subbase is treated together with the base and that the effective k‑value uses a Base Elastic Modulus of 15,000 psi for spring thaw, 30,000 psi for normal (summer/fall), and 50,000 psi for frozen conditions, reflecting climate influences. It further states that when a drainage layer is required an Open Graded Subbase is prohibited and only ATPB or CTPB should be specified instead of 2A Subbase, so another material must be selected in those cases. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications call for No. 2A aggregate to be placed as a subgrade layer beneath both rigid and flexible pavements—including shoulders—at a minimum compacted depth of three inches directly on the prepared subgrade or over a Class 4, Type A geotextile before the base course. The specifications also permit the use of a 2A Subbase as a haul‑road surface, but require retesting for surface irregularities and depth if the layer remains exposed to weather in excess of 60 calendar days or is left uncovered during winter shutdowns, indicating that another material or protective cover should be selected for such prolonged exposure. Additionally, the specifications prohibit placement of the subbase material on soft, muddy, or frozen areas, so any subgrade that cannot meet those conditions also calls for an alternative material. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 231–232)

Composition and characteristics

According to Section 350—Subbase, the 2A Subbase consists of No. 2A aggregate placed directly on a prepared subgrade and overlain by a Class 4, Type A geotextile. The material must contain enough moisture to prevent segregation during handling and must be kept well‑moistened from mixing through placement, after which it is compacted to the non‑movement condition specified for density. Each layer shall be placed to a minimum compacted depth of three inches; no individual layer may exceed eight inches in compacted thickness, and when granulated slag is used each layer shall not exceed four inches. The finished subbase must meet depth tolerances within a half‑inch of the design elevation. In the context of full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529—Construction), the same No. 2A coarse aggregate is placed and compacted in accordance with Sections 703.2 and 350, after which the surface is shaped to a firm, even layer whose grade and contour must be within plus or minus ½ inch. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 231–232), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Variations in the aggregate makeup are permitted only if allowed; otherwise an unapproved blend can cause segregation that undermines uniform compaction. The mixing step must provide material with enough moisture to prevent segregation during stockpiling, hauling and placing and to keep added water during compaction to a minimum, because moisture directly influences the density and stability of the 2A subbase. Additionally, each lift must be placed in layers not more than 8 inches in compacted depth, or not more than 4 inches when granulated slag is used, to maintain proper stiffness and avoid excessive deformation. Variations in the makeup of the coarse aggregate used for a 2A subbase directly influence its durability and performance because the specification requires hard, tough, durable particles that are free from clay, silt, vegetation, and deleterious substances such as reactive chert or gypsum; any excess of these materials can cause inconsistent performance. For gravel used in an open‑graded subbase at least 75 % must be crushed with three fracture faces. Type A applications require a minimum of 85 % crushed particles with two fracture faces. The maximum allowable absorption is limited to 3.0 % (or 3.5 % for Type B). Materials such as steel slag are only acceptable if total expansion after testing is less than 0.50 %; otherwise curing procedures must be applied, and steel slag may not be used as subbase at all. Likewise, granulated slag must have a dry rodded density not exceeding 80 lb/ft³ and no more than 20 % passing the 150 µm sieve, limits that control its strength and drainage characteristics. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 231–232), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–655)

Key material properties

The mechanical stiffness and support characteristics of a 2A Subbase are expressed through its effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k), which varies with the subbase type’s strength or modulus values, its thickness, and the loss‑of‑support factor used to adjust k for erosion or pumping [1]. Temperature conditions also affect the base elastic modulus applied in design, being specified as 15,000 psi for “spring thaw” conditions, 30,000 psi for “normal” (summer and fall) conditions, and 50,000 psi for “frozen” conditions [1]. The ratio of the subbase resilient modulus to the roadbed soil resilient modulus is limited so that it does not exceed 4 [1]. In addition, the depth to a rigid foundation within ten feet of the surface is considered when determining the effective k‑value [1]. Proper performance also requires that the aggregate contain sufficient moisture to prevent segregation during stockpiling, hauling and placement, thereby minimizing additional water needed for compaction [2]. The material must be placed on a prepared subgrade that is free of soft, muddy or frozen zones [2]. Each No 2A layer shall be placed to at least three inches of compacted depth, and overall layers shall not exceed eight inches unless granulated slag is used; lifts shall not exceed six inches [2]. The coarse aggregate must consist of hard, tough and durable uncoated inert particles that are reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation and deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide [2]. Mechanically the aggregate is required to have low water absorption, with a maximum allowable absorption of 3.0 % for all Type A use as determined by AASHTO T 85 [2]. Abrasion loss must be limited in accordance with AASHTO T 96, and soundness shall be acceptable as measured by percentage loss after five sodium‑sulfate cycles [2]. Freeze‑thaw resistance must satisfy that expansion is less than 0.50 % and total freeze‑thaw loss does not exceed the maximum of 25 % set in PTM No. 525 [2]. Chemically the aggregate must meet limits on glassy particles, metallic iron and other reactive constituents, while bulk density and specific gravity are controlled by AASHTO T 19 and T 85 to ensure proper compaction and void structure [2]. The required stiffness and resistance to deformation are verified by compacting the material to a non‑movement condition, rolling until no shoving or wave motion is observed under the roller [2].

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 231–232), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 430–431), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–655)

The properties of a 2A Subbase are characterized through the effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k), which is obtained by combining the base and subbase layers and applying a weighted average based on each layer’s thickness and loss‑of‑support factor; the resulting k‑value also incorporates the Base Elastic Modulus for the subbase, specified as 15,000 psi for “spring thaw” conditions, 30,000 psi for normal (summer/fall) conditions, and 50,000 psi for frozen conditions, and must be adjusted so that the ratio of the subbase to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed 4. [1] In the construction specification, the finished 2A subbase is first evaluated by density testing and then a test hole is excavated at each location so that the Representative can measure the full depth of the layer, with any depth shortfall of ½ inch or more deemed defective. [2] Compaction quality is verified by achieving the non‑movement condition required in Section 206.3(b) with overlapping roller passes, and moisture is controlled during mixing to prevent segregation. [2] The subbase is classified by placing No. 2A aggregate in layers that must be compacted to a minimum compacted depth of 3 inches, and the overall layer thickness is limited to not more than 8 inches (or 4 inches when granulated slag is used). [2] For rubblizing work, No. 2A subbase is used as backfill and its performance is judged by placement in lifts that do not exceed six inches, with each lift compacted by rolling until non‑movement is achieved; any shoving or wave motion under the roller is prohibited. [2]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 231–232), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 430–431)

Behavior and mechanisms

The 2A Subbase contributes to the pavement’s load‑bearing capacity through its inherent strength or modulus, which varies with subbase type, and this stiffness is reflected in the effective k‑value used for design. Moisture‑related deterioration of the 2A Subbase is accounted for by applying a loss of support factor that corrects the effective k‑value for potential erosion or pumping of the material. Temperature conditions alter the elastic modulus applied to the 2A Subbase, with a value of 15,000 psi specified for “spring thaw” conditions. The same temperature provision assigns an elastic modulus of 30,000 psi for “normal” conditions (summer and fall). For “frozen” conditions in winter the prescribed elastic modulus is 50,000 psi. The 2A subbase must be kept sufficiently moist during stockpiling, hauling and placement to prevent segregation and to limit additional water needed for compaction. It may not be placed on soft, muddy or frozen areas. For Type A applications the maximum allowable absorption is limited to 3.0 %, while Type B applications permit up to 3.5 % absorption. Aggregates with an absorption less than 2 % are considered freeze‑thaw resistant and are exempt from further testing, indicating good performance under wetting–drying cycles. Traffic is prohibited on a No. OGS aggregate subbase, but a No. 2A aggregate subbase may serve as a haul road provided that after exposure to weather in excess of 60 calendar days the surface and depth are retested and any deficiencies corrected. When winter shutdown leaves the subbase exposed without an overlying asphalt or concrete layer, proof‑rolling is required before pavement placement and any observed instability mandates removal and repair of the subgrade and replacement of the subbase. The material shall be composed of hard, tough, durable particles that are free of clay, silt, vegetation and deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide, which gives it inherent resistance to chemical attack. Soundness is verified by measuring the percentage loss after five immersion‑drying cycles in a sodium sulfate solution. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 231–232), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–655)

The mechanical response of a 2A Subbase is governed primarily by its material type and thickness, which determine subbase strength or modulus and thus affect the effective k‑value used in rigid pavement design. In addition, a loss‑of‑support factor is applied to correct the effective k‑value for potential erosion or pumping of the subbase material. The proximity of bedrock within 10 feet of the surface of the subgrade is considered because it can alter the subgrade reaction. Temperature conditions are reflected through prescribed base elastic modulus values—15,000 psi for spring thaw, 30,000 psi for normal, and 50,000 psi for frozen—and these should be adjusted so that the ratio of the subbase to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed 4. In Section 350—Subbase, the performance of a No. 2A subbase is governed primarily by moisture content sufficient to prevent segregation during stockpiling, hauling and placing and to minimize water added during compaction, while the subgrade must be free of soft, muddy or frozen areas before placement. Each layer must be compacted to a condition of non‑movement as specified in Section 206.3(b) and placed at a minimum compacted depth of 3 inches; the compacted depth may not exceed 8 inches, or 4 inches when granulated slag is used, limits that control the mechanical stability of the layer. The presence of a Class 4, Type A geotextile beneath the subbase further influences load distribution and drainage, providing additional physical support. Traffic restrictions together with proof‑rolling requirements act as mechanical checks on the subbase’s integrity after installation. In Section 529—Full Depth Precast/Prestressed Concrete Pavement Slabs, the behavior of the 2A subbase is governed primarily by physical placement of the specified coarse aggregate and mechanical compaction that creates a firm, even surface, and the final surface must be shaped to meet a grade and contour tolerance of plus or minus 1/2 inch. Section 703—Aggregate specifies that the performance of a 2A subbase is governed primarily by the physical durability and hardness of its coarse‑aggregate particles, which must be hard, tough and free of deleterious minerals such as reactive chert or gypsum because these substances can cause inconsistent performance. For Type A use the maximum allowable absorption is 3.0% and for Type B use it is 3.5%, as determined according to AASHTO T 85, controlling moisture susceptibility of the aggregate. The total chemical expansion must be less than 0.50% as measured by PTM No. 130. Freeze‑thaw durability is assessed through soundness testing with a maximum percentage loss after five cycles in sodium sulfate solution defined by PTM No. 510, and the freeze‑thaw resistance of aggregate may not exceed 25% as required by PTM No. 525. Abrasion resistance, determined according to AASHTO T 96, controls the wear characteristics of the subbase under traffic loading. In Section 526—Rubblizing of Concrete Pavements, the behavior of a No. 2A subbase is controlled mainly by physical and mechanical actions during placement; it must be placed in lifts not exceeding six inches and compacted by rolling until non‑movement is achieved, while vertical deflection under the roller must stay below three‑quarters of an inch to achieve a stable surface. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 231–232), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 430–447), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–655)

Durability and deterioration

The primary durability threat to a 2A Subbase is loss of support caused by erosion or pumping, which reduces the effective modulus and is accounted for with a loss‑of‑support factor in k‑value calculations [1]. Because subbase types exhibit different strengths or moduli and thickness influences weighted support, designers must verify that the ratio of the subbase modulus to the underlying soil resilient modulus does not exceed four, lest an artificial condition be introduced [1]. Moisture‑related loss of support must also be prevented, and the material should be protected from traffic and prolonged exposure to preserve durability [2]. Subbase material shall not be placed on soft, muddy, or frozen areas [2]. Any depth deficiency of one‑half inch or more must be corrected before proceeding [2]. Each layer must be compacted to a non‑movement condition as specified in the construction requirements [2]. The thickness of the subbase shall not exceed eight inches, and when granulated slag is used the maximum thickness is limited to four inches [2]. If a 2A aggregate subbase is employed as a haul road or remains exposed to weather for more than sixty calendar days, surface irregularities and depth must be retested and any deficiencies corrected in accordance with the specified procedures [2]. During winter shutdowns the subbase must be covered with an asphalt base course for flexible pavements or cement concrete for rigid pavements; otherwise proof rolling is required and observed instability mandates removal of the subbase and repair of the subgrade before replacement [2]. Traffic, including construction traffic, shall not be allowed on a subbase constructed of No. OGS aggregate, and any damage to the underlying geotextile must be repaired at no additional cost [2]. The presence of deleterious materials limits durability, so coarse aggregates must be free of clay, silt, vegetation, and substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica, and hydrated iron oxide because these can cause inconsistent performance [2]. Soundness is evaluated by a sodium‑sulfate immersion‑drying test; aggregates that fail this test may be rejected unless a satisfactory service record exists [2]. Natural aggregates with an absorption less than two percent are considered freeze‑thaw resistant, whereas those with absorption exceeding two percent will be regarded as freeze‑thaw resistant only if either their sodium sulfate soundness level is less than sixteen percent or their AASHTO T 103 freeze‑thaw loss after twenty‑five cycles (coarse fraction) does not exceed seven point zero percent; otherwise they are vulnerable to freeze‑thaw damage [2]. Steel‑slag stockpiles must demonstrate total expansion below zero point five percent to avoid expansive cracking [2].

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 231–232), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–655)

The susceptibility of a 2A Subbase to deterioration is influenced by the type of subbase material and its thickness, with thinner sections providing less support, and by the loss‑of‑support (LS) factor applied for erosion or pumping; a higher LS factor reduces effective support. Seasonal exposure conditions that change the base elastic modulus also affect susceptibility, because spring‑thaw conditions with an effective modulus of 15,000 psi provide less stiffness than frozen conditions with 50,000 psi. The design ratio of subbase to roadbed soil resilient modulus should not exceed 4 to avoid artificial overstressing. Under SECTION 350—SUBBASE, 350.3 CONSTRUCTION, susceptibility increases when the No. 2A subbase is placed on soft, muddy, or frozen subgrade, when the material lacks sufficient moisture to remain well‑moistened during stockpiling and compaction (which can cause segregation), when the 2A aggregate is used as a haul road or left exposed to weather for more than 60 calendar days, and when it remains uncovered during winter shutdowns. Conversely, preparing the subgrade as required, maintaining proper moisture, compacting to a non‑movement condition, protecting the geotextile from damage, and covering the finished subbase with the prescribed asphalt base for flexible pavements or cement concrete for rigid pavements before winter all reduce the likelihood of instability and the need for corrective retesting. Within SECTION 703—AGGREGATE, 703.2 COARSE AGGREGATE, susceptibility rises when the coarse aggregate contains higher amounts of deleterious substances such as clay, silt, vegetation, reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide, or when its absorption exceeds 2 percent because such aggregates are not automatically freeze‑thaw resistant. If the absorption is above 2 percent, the material can still be protected provided its sodium sulfate soundness is less than 16 percent or its AASHTO T 103 loss after 25 cycles does not exceed 7.0 percent, and limiting glassy particles to no more than 10 percent helps prevent weakening of the subbase. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 231–232), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–655)

Material interactions and compatibility

According to the coarse‑aggregate specifications, 2A subbase material must be free from deleterious substances such as clay, silt, vegetation and reactive minerals (reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica, hydrated iron oxide) because their presence can cause inconsistent performance. When aggregate absorption exceeds 2 % the specification requires a sodium‑sulfate soundness level less than 16 % (or freeze‑thaw loss under 7.0 %) to ensure resistance to sulfate‑induced deterioration. Contamination limits are also imposed; for example, more than 5 % steel‑slag contamination causes the stockpile to be classified as steel slag and subject to additional expansion testing, while glassy particles and metallic iron are limited to 10 % and waived only for subbase use. These provisions address compatibility risks related to sulfates, clays, reactive aggregates and certain contaminants, although the source does not mention organics, dissimilar materials, corrosion, shrinkage restraint or broader chemical attack. [2]

[2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–655)

Influence of production and placement

Mixing of No. 2A aggregate must be performed with acceptable methods and sufficient moisture to prevent segregation during stockpiling, hauling and placing, and to keep the material well‑moistened from before placement through compaction so that extra water is not required during compaction (Section 350—Subbase > 350.3 Construction). Placement requires constructing berms, verifying the Class 4, Type A geotextile, and spreading the No. 2A aggregate uniformly without segregation while controlling elevation and depth; each layer must be placed to a minimum compacted depth of 3 inches (Section 350—Subbase > 350.3 Construction). Layers may not exceed 8 inches in compacted depth, or not more than 4 inches when granulated slag is used, and abrupt turns with tracked equipment that could pull or damage the fabric must be avoided (Section 350—Subbase > 350.3 Construction). In rubblizing work the subbase must be placed in lifts that do not exceed six inches, and each lift must be compacted by rolling until no movement occurs; any shoving or wave motion under the roller is prohibited (Section 526—Rubblizing of Concrete Pavements > 526.3 Construction). Compaction must achieve a condition of non‑movement as specified in Section 206.3(b) by working from the sides toward the center with each pass uniformly overlapping the previous pass, thereby attaining the required density and stability (Section 350—Subbase > 350.3 Construction). After compaction the depth is checked, and if the finished subbase is deficient by one‑half inch or more, the layer is scarified to a depth of three inches, additional material blended in, and re‑compacted to meet the tolerance (Section 350—Subbase > 350.3 Construction). The finished subbase surface must be shaped to a firm, even plane within a tolerance of plus or minus ½ inch of grade and contour, providing the required support and drainage performance for the pavement structure (Section 529—Full Depth Precast/Prestressed Concrete Pavement Slabs > 529.3 Construction). [2]

Variations in subbase type and installed thickness affect the effective modulus of reaction that governs long‑term performance of a 2A Subbase. Loss of support caused by erosion or pumping of the 2A Subbase material is especially critical because it reduces the k‑value used in design. When bedrock lies within 10 feet of the subgrade surface for a significant length of the project, its proximity should be considered in determining the effective k‑value. Using a prohibited Open Graded Subbase instead of an approved ATPB or CTPB drainage layer would also compromise the intended behavior. Placing the No. 2A subbase material on soft, muddy or frozen subgrade compromises support and uniformity and is identified as a construction variation that most strongly influences long‑term performance. Delivering the material with insufficient moisture, which leads to segregation during stockpiling, hauling and placement, is another defect that can degrade future behavior. Damage to the Class 4 Type A geotextile during placement must be repaired because such damage undermines the subbase’s long‑term stability. If the compacted depth is deficient by at least ½ inch from the specified elevation, the subbase is considered defective and its durability is adversely affected. Failure to achieve a non‑movement compaction condition or allowing traffic on the fresh subbase also jeopardizes long‑term performance. Exposing a No. 2A haul‑road subbase to weather for more than 60 calendar days creates surface irregularities that must be retested and corrected, with repair, scarifying, re‑placement or removal required before pavement placement. The presence of deleterious coarse‑aggregate constituents such as clay, silt, vegetation, reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide in the placed material is a construction variation that most strongly affects long‑term behavior. Absorption exceeding the limits of 3.0 % for Type A use or 3.5 % for Type B use increases moisture susceptibility and reduces strength, thereby influencing durability. Soundness loss measured after five wet‑dry cycles with a sodium sulfate solution (PTM No. 510) and abrasion loss per AASHTO T 96 are indicators of aggregate stability that, when unsatisfactory, compromise long‑term performance. Excessive fines finer than the 75 µm sieve (determined by PTM No. 100) hinder proper coating of particles and can degrade subbase behavior over time. Additional defects such as thin or elongated particles with a length‑to‑thickness ratio greater than 5:1, friable particles, deleterious shale, glassy particles above allowable percentages, and metallic iron create weak zones that accelerate deformation and cracking. Placing backfill in lifts thicker than the maximum 6‑inch limit during rubblizing work violates the specification and can lead to insufficient compaction. Rolling that does not achieve a condition of non‑movement, as well as any shoving or wave motion observed under the roller, must be avoided because they compromise uniformity and support of the subbase. Selection, specification, and improvement

The selection of a 2A Subbase is driven by several design factors that affect the effective modulus of subgrade reaction. Engineers must consider the subbase type because different types provide different strengths or modulus values, and they evaluate the required subbase thickness to achieve an economical design while accounting for loss‑of‑support factors that correct the k‑value for possible erosion or pumping. The depth to a rigid foundation is examined; if bedrock lies within 10 feet of the subgrade surface over a significant length, its influence must be included in the k‑value calculation. Any adjustment of elastic modulus values must keep the ratio of the subbase to the roadbed soil resilient modulus at or below 4. For Interstate and Expressway rigid pavements the design mandates an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) layer over the 2A Subbase. The selection and specification of a 2A subbase are governed by the requirements set out in the applicable sections of the report. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, the specification calls for using No. 2A coarse aggregate as defined in Section 703.2 (and reiterated in Section 350) whenever a subbase is required, after any soft, wet or otherwise unsuitable material has been removed; the placed and compacted layer must be shaped to a firm, even surface that stays within plus or minus 1/2 inch of the intended grade and contour. The coarse‑aggregate requirements call for hard, tough, durable, uncoated inert particles reasonably free from clay, silt, vegetation and deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica and hydrated iron oxide, and must meet the physical property limits set out in Tables B‑D for the chosen type. For gravel‑based subbase the maximum allowable absorption is 3.0% for Type A use and 3.5% for Type B use, and at least 75% of the particles must be crushed with three fracture faces when used as open‑graded subbase. For rubblizing projects the specification calls for using No. 2A subbase as the backfill material in all excavated sections, and it must be placed in lifts that do not exceed a maximum of 6 inches while being compacted by rolling until nonmovement is achieved; shoving or wave motion under the roller is not permitted. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 430–447), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–655)

For concrete pavements the manual advises that a 2A Subbase should be overlaid with an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) rather than an Open Graded Subbase, which is prohibited for such projects [1]. The effective modulus of subgrade reaction calculation shall incorporate the appropriate loss‑of‑support factor for each layer—for example a factor of 0.5 for a TPBC layer—and use the combined thickness of the base and subbase to obtain a weighted average, a practice that helps control erosion or pumping [1]. Seasonal adjustments to the base elastic modulus are required, using 15,000 psi for spring thaw conditions, 30,000 psi for normal conditions, and 50,000 psi for frozen conditions, while the ratio of subbase to roadbed soil resilient modulus shall not exceed 4 in order to avoid artificial design conditions [1]. The specifications require that moisture be maintained in the 2A Subbase material sufficient to prevent segregation during stockpiling, hauling and placing, and also to minimize additional water needed for compaction [2]. The mix shall follow acceptable methods and subgrade conditions must be verified before placement of each lift [2]. Each lift shall be compacted from the sides toward the centre until a non‑movement condition is achieved, and depth shall be checked with test holes; any area that is deficient by ½ inch or more shall be scarified to a depth of 3 inches, blended with additional material and re‑compacted [2]. When lifts exceed 1,500 tons a Subbase Delivery Plan must be submitted in accordance with the specification [2]. If granulated slag is used as an alternative aggregate, the lift thickness shall not exceed 4 inches, although it may be placed to a maximum compacted depth of 6 inches provided the full layer depth is compacted per Section 206.3(b) [2]. Selecting durable stone or gravel that meets the required crushed‑particle percentages and keeping absorption below the limits set for Type A (3.0 %) and Type B (3.5 %) aggregates further improves performance [2]. Approved alternative aggregates may be employed, including blast‑furnace slag with a minimum unit weight of 70 lb/ft³, granulated slag limited to no more than 20 % non‑slag material and a maximum size of 50 mm, or lightweight aggregate whose absorption factor does not exceed 2.5 % [2]. Strict quality controls shall be enforced, comprising petrographic analysis, soundness testing (sodium sulfate loss), abrasion testing, fines limits, deleterious shale determination, and for steel slag an expansion test ensuring total expansion is less than 0.50 % with moisture curing of the stockpile for at least six months if the limit is exceeded [2].

Performance evaluation and implications

The evaluation of a No 2A subbase incorporates both field performance checks prescribed in Section 350—Subbase (specifically 350.3 Construction) and laboratory and production testing of the coarse aggregate required by Section 703—Aggregate (703.2 Coarse Aggregate). In the field, after each density test the contractor is required to dig a full‑depth test hole at the test location; the Representative then measures the finished depth and will declare the layer defective when the depth is short by ½ inch or more. When the subbase has been used as a haul road or has been exposed to weather for more than 60 calendar days, the specification calls for retesting of surface irregularities and depth, and any identified deficiencies must be corrected. If the subbase remains over winter without an asphalt or concrete cover, proof rolling is required before placement of flexible or rigid pavement, and observed instability mandates removal of the subbase and repair or replacement of the subgrade. Laboratory evaluation of the aggregate intended for a 2A subbase is performed by the LTS. The LTS shall conduct petrographic analysis in accordance with PTM No. 518 and shall also require any additional tests needed to demonstrate acceptable durability, including soundness testing according to PTM No. 510, abrasion loss measured by AASHTO T 96, specific gravity and absorption per AASHTO T 85, and bulk density per AASHTO T 19. For steel‑slag stockpiles the LTS shall perform an expansion test in accordance with PTM No. 130 and will accept the material only when total expansion is less than 0.50 %. Production testing must be carried out at the source by a PennDOT Certified Aggregate Technician following Bulletin 14, with all results documented and made available to the Department. [2]
