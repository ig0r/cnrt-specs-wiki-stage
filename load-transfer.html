Load Transfer

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Load Transfer is a topic under Rigid Pavement.
load-transfer
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-29

Load transfer is the engineered mechanism that restores functional continuity across transverse and longitudinal joints in Portland‑cement concrete pavements, preventing the loss of structural integrity caused by cracks or joint openings[1][2]. It works by installing fully embedded, epoxy‑coated dowel bars (or equivalent load‑transfer units) that span the joint, are securely anchored with approved binders, and are placed within strict geometric limits—joint gaps of one‑quarter to one‑half inch, vertical differentials not exceeding one‑quarter inch, and bar dimensions of 1.25–1.5 in diameter and 18 in length[1][2]. The system’s performance is verified by limiting the differential deflection between loaded and unloaded slab faces to no more than 7.5 mil after one million load cycles (with a maximum increase of 2.5 mil thereafter), thereby ensuring near‑complete load sharing, uniform support, and long‑term pavement serviceability[1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Load Transfer” also include the following subtopics: Aggregate Interlock.

Aggregate Interlock serves as a slab-fracture rehabilitation strategy that preserves load transfer by maintaining continuous full-depth cracks with interlocked aggregate pieces under traffic loads. [2] The method utilizes variable-force impact equipment and seating with a minimum 50-ton pneumatic-tired roller to prevent substrate movement while ensuring the fractured slab meets prescribed elastic modulus limits for load-transfer reliability. [2]

Aggregate Interlock

Purpose and design objectives

Load transfer is required because transverse cracks or joints in Portland‑cement concrete slabs interrupt structural continuity, causing wheel loads to be applied only to isolated slab sections and resulting in elevated stresses, joint deterioration such as spalling or cracking, faulting, slab migration, loss of aggregate interlock, and ultimately reduced pavement performance and service life. The engineering need is therefore to provide a mechanism that redistributes wheel loads from one slab to the adjacent slab so that the slabs behave as a single structural unit and maintain uniform support beneath the pavement. Across the guides this mechanism is most commonly achieved with dowel bars that are fully embedded and securely anchored across the crack or joint, and which may be coated, galvanized, stainless‑steel or other approved types installed in slots at slab edges or on the opposite side. The specifications require joint widths to be maintained between one‑quarter and one‑half inch along their entire length and finished vertical differentials across any joint not to exceed one‑quarter inch, thereby limiting edge deflection and joint opening. Moreover, the dowel system must demonstrate that the absolute difference in deflection between the loaded and unloaded sides of the joint does not exceed 7.5 mil after one million load cycles, with only minimal increase thereafter, confirming near‑complete load sharing and lane‑to‑lane ties. By satisfying these geometric and performance criteria the load‑transfer units prevent excessive differential movement, moisture intrusion, loss of uniform support, and the associated deterioration that would otherwise compromise the pavement’s integrity and service life. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–447), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

The load‑transfer system must create uninterrupted functional continuity across all transverse and longitudinal joints so that traffic loads are passed from one slab to the next without interruption or loss of capacity [1][2]. To achieve this, dowel bars are required to have a true‑circular, burr‑free end that engages the concrete cleanly and prevents joint deflection as well as moisture intrusion [2]. Structurally the joints must be sized within prescribed limits, with transverse gaps kept between one‑quarter and one‑half inch, longitudinal gaps not exceeding three‑quarters inch, and the finished vertical differential across any joint limited to a quarter inch [2]. The assembled load‑transfer assembly must limit slab differential deflection measured by LVDT devices to no more than 7.5 mils after one million loading cycles and may increase only an additional 2.5 mils after ten million cycles, thereby preserving ride quality [2]. Durability is ensured by fully bonding each dowel bar along its entire embedded length with approved epoxy or polymer binders and by coating the bars with a fusion‑bonded epoxy of at least eight mils non‑abraded thickness, allowing no more than two holidays per linear foot [1][2]. The surrounding grout must attain a compressive strength of at least 2 500 psi before traffic is permitted (with a minimum of 1 000 psi for repaired sections), and voids around each dowel may not exceed half an inch in any core, while bond‑breaker lubricant must be applied at least one hour prior to concrete placement [2]. Dowel bars must be stored on site no longer than thirty days unless protected, and they must remain free of corrosion, uncoated spots, or other contaminants to prevent loss of transfer over the pavement’s service life [2]. Safety is addressed by requiring that the load‑transfer units transmit loads without causing slab uplift, edge differential, or excessive joint movement, thereby avoiding sudden slab motion that could endanger vehicles and preventing premature cracking [1][2]. Serviceability objectives are met by delivering a uniformly supported pavement surface that maintains smooth ride quality, accommodates future overlays, and sustains the structural integrity of the concrete slab system over time [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 408–448), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

Scope and applicability

Load Transfer is appropriate for rigid pavement systems that employ jointed Portland‑cement concrete, whether the slabs are cast in place or supplied as precast units [1][2]. It is required whenever transverse or longitudinal joints must convey traffic loads across slab edges, such as in new multilane slip‑form installations, widening of existing sections, construction of contraction or expansion joints, and both intermittent and continuous patching operations [2]. The approach is also used for retrofit situations that involve dowel‑bar retrofits, full‑depth patching, or reconstruction of jointed PCC slabs extending roughly five hundred feet or more [1]. For precast concrete pavement slab (PCPS) applications, the slabs are fabricated with dowel slots at one transverse edge and embedded dowels at the opposite edge, and longitudinal tie bars are installed to provide lane‑to‑lane ties for continuous sections longer than about sixty‑five feet [2]. Load‑transfer devices are employed when joint spacings of fifteen or thirty feet with perpendicular joints are specified, and when aggregate interlock still exists, such as under heavily channeled traffic conditions or where hairline cracking is observed [1]. The method is suitable for projects where the subgrade and drainage are stable enough to permit a retrofit rather than an overlay, and where base repairs beneath an asphalt overlay on a concrete‑based pavement do not require load transfer [1]. Dowel bars used as load‑transfer units must have diameters of 1.25 to 1.5 in (tolerance +0.25 in/–0.001 in) and lengths of 18 in (±½ in) [2]. They must be provided with a corrosion‑protective coating, such as a minimum non‑abraded 8‑mil epoxy Type B coating or at least 3.4‑mil galvanizing, and must meet the bond‑breaker requirements of Type A or B per AASHTO M254 [2]. High‑performance dowel bars listed in Bulletin 15 or approved under PTM No. 642 may be used for Long Life Concrete projects, provided only one dowel type is employed unless a special waiver is obtained and the bars are stored no longer than thirty days without protective covering [2]. When load‑transfer units are used, a sample containing the prescribed minimum number of dowels (six for non‑Bulletin 15 bars, three for Bulletin 15 bars) with pre‑applied bond‑breaker lubricant must be submitted for verification at least forty‑five days before paving [2]. In full‑depth patch work that involves single‑course repairs of normal‑strength or accelerated‑strength concrete at least one lane wide, full‑depth saw cuts are made at the interface, dowel bars are installed in the transverse faces of the existing slab, and a load‑transfer unit together with a sealant reservoir is provided to restore joint continuity [1][2]. Load transfer devices are not required for simple base repairs beneath an asphalt overlay on a concrete‑based pavement [1].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–448), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

When a concrete slab rests on a Portland‑cement concrete base and is subsequently covered with an asphalt overlay, the joint at the interface does not require dowel‑type load‑transfer devices; designers therefore omit them and treat the patch joint as a simple overlay connection. In situations where high early‑strength (HES) concrete would be prone to excessive shrinkage cracking or when traffic must be reopened quickly, conventional load‑transfer detailing becomes less attractive and alternatives such as accelerated‑setting concrete or precast concrete pavement are preferred because they avoid the crack risk and meet the rapid‑opening schedule. Likewise, if joint performance is compromised by faulting or inadequate load transfer, the primary remedy shifts from adding dowels to first confirming that the subgrade is stable and that drainage functions properly; when either the support is unstable or drainage is deficient, slab stabilization or jacking must be undertaken before any dowel‑bar retrofit can be justified. Even with adequate drainage, a dowel‑bar retrofit is only appropriate when the underlying support remains sound and the joint distress is limited to hairline cracks that still benefit from aggregate interlock; more extensive cracking or widespread damage calls for replacing the joint with a full‑depth concrete patch rather than relying on load‑transfer devices. For longitudinal cracks that remain in reasonably good condition, cross‑stitching offers an alternative means of achieving near‑complete load transfer without installing conventional dowel bars. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90)

Design inputs and requirements

Load transfer in jointed Portland‑cement‑concrete pavements is governed by state roadway construction standards and detailed specifications that prescribe the design, materials, installation, and performance of dowel bars, tie bars, and other load‑transfer units. [1][2] The primary documents are the Roadway Construction Standards (Publication 72M) together with the Specification Manual (Publication 408), which define the required joint spacing, coating, sealing, and maintenance procedures for both reinforced‑concrete and plain‑concrete pavements. [1] For new construction reinforced‑concrete slabs the standards call for transverse patch joints spaced at 30 feet, perpendicular orientation, epoxy‑coated smooth dowel bars, and proper sealant reservoirs; plain‑concrete slabs use a 15‑foot joint spacing but retain the same coating and sealing requirements. [1] All dowel bars and tie bars must be supplied by manufacturers listed in Bulletin 15 and must meet the material criteria of Section 106, including an epoxy coating that conforms to ASTM A775 (or Type B fusion‑bonded epoxy per AASHTO M 254) with a minimum non‑abraded thickness of eight mils and no more than two holidays per linear foot, repaired if damage exceeds two percent of the bar surface. [2] The coating must be applied either in the shop or, when not pre‑lubricated, a bond‑breaker lubricant is required on at least half of each dowel length before placement. [2] Placement tolerances are strict: dowels and tie bars are to be cast square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface within plus or minus one eighth inch of the locations shown on approved shop drawings, and transverse joints must be cut over the centre of each dowel. [2][1] Joint geometry is limited to a width between one quarter and one half inch for transverse joints, while longitudinal joints may not exceed three quarters inch; when replacing an existing expansion joint the material thickness shall be three quarters inch (or one half inch for corner repairs). [2] The load‑transfer method must follow the system approved for the precast concrete pavement slab, whether bottom‑edge dowel slots with embedded dowels, conventional surface slots, or narrow‑mouth slots, and alignment of load‑transfer units before paving is limited to a maximum end tilt or skew of 0.25 inch and longitudinal or horizontal translation not greater than one inch. [2] Performance of the dowels is verified by testing under PTM No. 642, requiring that differential deflection measured by LVDT not exceed 7.5 mils after one million load cycles and increase no more than an additional 2.5 mils after ten million cycles. [2] High‑performance dowel bars not listed in Bulletin 15 must be accompanied by full test reports, shop drawings, and a sample load‑transfer unit containing at least six pre‑lubricated dowels submitted for approval at least forty‑five days before paving; only one type of dowel may be used on a project unless a deviation is approved. [2] Grout placed in dowel slots, tie‑bar slots, or lifting inserts must achieve a compressive strength of at least 2 500 psi before traffic is permitted, with the bedding grout reaching 1 000 psi prior to opening and the same 2 500 psi within twelve hours after placement. [2] Storage of dowel bars on site is limited to thirty days without special permission, and anchoring verification may be performed by core sampling that limits voids around each dowel perimeter to less than half an inch. [2][1] Joint‑maintenance provisions require regular cleaning, sealing, and the construction of sealant reservoirs shaped as specified in RC‑20M to preserve drainage and load‑transfer capability, with sections of Publication 408 (e.g., Sections 512, 513, 516, 519, 521, 525, 590) governing these activities. [1] For patching joints full‑depth saw cuts are required at the pavement‑patch interface, a sealant reservoir is constructed and sealed after placement, and the joint is sealed in accordance with Section 705.4. [2][1] All steel splices must use coupling devices listed in Bulletin 15 that develop at least 125 percent of the specified yield strength. [2] The overall design and construction process must adhere to the combined requirements of the Roadway Construction Standards, Specification Manual, product approval bulletins, ASTM material standards, geometric tolerances, performance testing criteria, grout strength limits, storage constraints, and joint‑maintenance provisions to ensure adequate load transfer across joints throughout the pavement’s service life. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–448), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

Design methods and criteria

Load transfer in jointed concrete pavements is achieved through a set of prescribed retrofit and construction procedures rather than explicit analytical equations or software models, with the central mechanism being the installation of load‑transfer units such as coated dowel bars, tie bars, and expansion‑joint material [1][2]. For transverse joints and working cracks the standard practice calls for a dowel‑bar retrofit in which smooth epoxy‑coated dowels are placed across every joint or crack, bonded along their entire embedded length with an epoxy, polyester, or vinylester binder, and spaced according to joint‑spacing criteria of 30 feet for reinforced concrete slabs and 15 feet for plain concrete slabs; high‑performance dowel bars are recommended when the pavement segment is expected to remain in service for more than twenty years [1]. The geometry of these dowels follows standard drawings and ASTM/AASHTO specifications, typically using diameters of 1.5 inches or 1.25 inches with tolerances of +0.25 inch and –0.001 inch and lengths of 18 inches, while the drilled holes are limited to no more than one‑eighth inch larger than the dowel diameter [2]. Alignment requirements limit vertical tilt or horizontal skew to a maximum displacement of 0.25 inch at each end, longitudinal translation to one inch, and horizontal translation also to one inch, ensuring proper positioning before concrete placement [2]. The dowels receive a Type B fusion‑bonded epoxy coating with a minimum non‑abraded thickness of eight mils, and a bond‑breaker lubricant is applied to at least half of the dowel length unless it has already been treated in the shop, to prevent bonding to the surrounding concrete [2]. Anchorage quality is verified by coring, accepting only cores where the summation of voids around the dowel perimeter is less than 0.50 inch on any one core, thereby confirming adequate bond performance [2]. When longitudinal cracks or joints are in reasonable condition, cross‑stitching is employed by installing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle across the crack to provide near‑complete load transfer, while severely deteriorated slab support may be addressed through slab stabilization or jacketing prior to reinstalling dowel bars [1]. Structural adequacy of each load‑transfer unit is demonstrated through PTM No. 642 testing, which limits Linear Variable Differential Transformer (LVDT) differential deflection to no more than 7.5 mil after one million load cycles and permits an additional increase of only 2.5 mil after ten million cycles [2]. Test reports containing representative samples—at least three dowels for standard bars or six for high‑performance bars pre‑lubricated with bond breaker—must be submitted at least forty‑five days before paving, and placement may not commence until a qualified representative verifies passing results [2]. Joint rehabilitation forms part of the load‑transfer strategy by cleaning the joint, constructing a sealant reservoir typically sized at three‑quarters of an inch by three‑quarters of an inch, and resealing with rubberized or asphalt‑based joint sealant, as prescribed in the standard references Publication 72M and Publication 408 which serve as design tools guiding these measures [1]. Additional handling requirements dictate that dowel bars may remain on the jobsite for no more than thirty days unless protected by an opaque cover, and any damaged bars observed after installation must be replaced before paving proceeds [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–448), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

Load transfer across transverse and longitudinal joints in concrete pavements must satisfy a comprehensive set of design criteria, assumptions, thresholds, safety factors and reliability requirements articulated by the guides. At every transverse joint the load‑transfer mechanism is achieved by installing dowel bars that are fully bonded along their entire embedded length with approved anchoring binders such as epoxy, polyester or vinylester, and the bonding must extend over the whole bar to ensure continuity of stress transfer. When a repaired or new pavement is expected to remain in service for more than twenty years before a programmed replacement, the designer is required to specify high‑performance dowel bars that meet the higher durability standards set by the guides. Joint spacing and alignment must conform to the applicable state (e.g., PennDOT) standards, with transverse joint gaps controlled between one‑quarter inch and one‑half inch along their full length and longitudinal joints limited to three‑quarters inch, while finished vertical differentials across any joint may not exceed one‑quarter inch. Dowel slots must be provided with prescribed mouth widths—approximately 2½ inches for wide‑mouth slots or about one inch for narrow‑mouth slots—and the dowel bars placed in those slots are required to receive a bond‑breaker lubricant on at least half of their length unless the shop has already applied it. Alignment tolerances are strict; each end of a dowel may not be tilted or skewed more than 0.25 inch from the intended plane, longitudinal and horizontal translations from the designed joint location must not exceed one inch, and the bar centreline must remain within ±1 inch of the transverse‑joint centreline. The anchoring material that secures each dowel is required to achieve its initial set within five minutes, and core‑drilling verification must show that total voids around a dowel’s perimeter are less than 0.5 inch in any sampled core. All reinforcement steel, dowel bars and longitudinal tie bars shall be coated with fusion‑bonded epoxy in accordance with ASTM A775, with a non‑abraded thickness of at least eight mils; the coating quality is limited to no more than two holidays per linear foot as detected by a 67.5‑volt holiday detector, and any repaired surface may not exceed two percent of the bar area, while patching of damaged areas is further restricted to five percent of the total bar surface. Mechanical performance of each load‑transfer unit must be demonstrated through PTM No. 642 testing, whereby differential deflection between loaded and unloaded sides of a joint cannot exceed 7.5 mils after one million load cycles and may not increase by more than an additional 2.5 mils after ten million cycles, providing the required safety margin for long‑term service. Galvanized dowels must possess a minimum coating thickness of 3.4 mils, and all dimensions—including diameter, length and spacing—must conform to the referenced ASTM and AASHTO specifications, ensuring dimensional reliability. The design assumes a stable subgrade, functional drainage and sufficient support beneath the pavement structure; if joint performance deteriorates—as indicated by faulting or inadequate transfer—the design must restore load‑transfer capability through appropriate remedial actions. For sections subjected to heavily channeled wheel loads, dowel‑bar retrofits using epoxy‑coated smooth bars placed in the wheel paths are prescribed to improve transfer across transverse joints or cracks. Where a longitudinal crack or joint remains in reasonably good condition with aggregate interlock and only hairline cracking, cross‑stitching may be applied to achieve nearly 100 percent load transfer, providing an alternative reliability measure. Concrete placed for the pavement must attain a compressive strength of at least 1,000 psi before traffic is permitted, as verified by cylinder testing, and grout used in dowel slots, tie‑bar slots and lifting ports must achieve a minimum of 2,500 psi prior to opening. Cement‑treated bedding material beneath the slabs must reach 500 psi at opening to traffic, while cementitious bedding grout must attain 1,000 psi at opening and increase to 2,500 psi within twelve hours; trial mixes are required to be tested at least twenty‑eight days in advance with independent laboratory results submitted for verification. Documentation requirements reinforce these technical criteria: a complete load‑transfer unit sample containing a minimum of six high‑performance dowels (or three standard dowels) with pre‑applied bond‑breaker lubricant, together with shop drawings and PTM No. 642 test reports, must be submitted at least forty‑five days before paving begins; only one type of dowel bar may be used on a long‑life concrete project unless an exception is granted, and dowels stored on the jobsite for more than thirty days require special approval and protective covering to maintain coating integrity. Collectively these thresholds, tolerances, material specifications, performance tests and documentation steps constitute the design criteria, assumptions, safety factors and reliability requirements that load transfer must satisfy in jointed concrete pavements. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–448), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

Structural section and dimensions

Load‑transfer design for jointed concrete pavements dictates that transverse joints be spaced according to the type of concrete, with reinforced concrete pavement requiring a maximum spacing of thirty feet and plain concrete pavement limited to fifteen feet, and an additional intermediate joint is introduced whenever existing spacing exceeds twenty feet. The slab thickness may not be reduced below eight inches regardless of other considerations. Slabs are required to be fabricated with lengths ranging from six feet up to fifteen feet, extending to twenty feet when prestressed units are used, and each slab is provided with a sacrificial topping that adds one‑quarter inch to the nominal design thickness. Joint geometry is tightly controlled, requiring transverse joint gaps no larger than one‑half inch and not less than one‑quarter inch along their entire length, while longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters inch and vertical differentials across any joint are limited to one‑quarter inch. Dowel and tie‑bar slots must be positioned within plus or minus one‑eighth inch of the locations shown on the shop drawings. The prepared subbase surface must be graded to within plus or minus one‑half inch of the design grade, and the bedding layer placed beneath the slab may not vary more than one‑eighth inch over the length of a slab. No additional dimensional requirements are prescribed for the underlying base, treated‑soil layers, shoulders, edge supports, or reinforcement beyond the joint spacing and slab thickness already noted. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Load‑transfer design begins by ensuring that every transverse joint in a patch is dowelled and the dowels are fully bonded along their embedded length, typically with epoxy or similar binders. For full‑width reconstructions longer than 500 ft the joint spacing follows the standard manuals: 30 ft spacing for jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) and 15 ft spacing for jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP), both using perpendicular joints, coated dowels and proper seals; when only one lane is rebuilt the new joints must line up with existing ones and an intermediate joint is added if the existing spacing exceeds about 20 ft. Thicknesses are increased only after a pavement design analysis indicates a need for a deeper slab on longer sections, while cross‑slope and profile are restored by diamond grinding after installation. Longitudinal tie bars or bolts are specified only for patches longer than 65 ft, providing the required tie‑in to adjacent lanes. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89)

Materials and mixture design

The load‑transfer requirement calls for a steel dowel bar system crossing every transverse joint or crack, the bars being supplied as smooth, epoxy‑coated units that satisfy the applicable steel standards such as ASTM A513 and AASHTO T 253, with optional mechanical galvanizing of at least 3.4 mil thickness, stainless‑steel options per ASTM A955/A955M, or uncoated corrosion‑resistant grades defined in AASHTO M 334 Level 3 [1][2]. All dowels must receive a fusion‑bonded epoxy Type B coating having a non‑abraded thickness of not less than eight mils, or may be provided with a two‑layer epoxy system whose total thickness ranges from twenty to sixty mils and is free of holidays [2]. The nominal diameter is 1.5 inches, with an alternative size of 1.25 inches permitted within a tolerance of plus or minus 0.25 inch, and the length is specified as 18 inches ± ½ inch, with coating applied over at least half the dowel length; high‑performance units may be supplied with bond‑breaker lubricant pre‑applied [2]. The dowels are to be anchored along their full embedded length using a binder selected from epoxy, polyester, or vinylester systems that secures the bar in place, and the anchoring material must achieve its initial set within five minutes as documented on its performance data sheet [1][2]. The slots cut for dowel installation are backfilled with a concrete patching material that is placed and cured in situ, without any additional prescribed mixture proportions beyond the requirement to achieve adequate strength [1]. The voids surrounding the dowels are filled with an epoxy‑based rapid‑setting grout that must develop a compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is permitted; where cementitious or polyurethane bedding grouts are used to fill larger voids they must reach at least 1,000 psi initially and increase to 2,500 psi within twelve hours, with verification by trial batches in accordance with AASHTO T 106 or PTM No. 521 [2]. Joint sealing is achieved with a rubberized sealant installed in sealant reservoirs typically sized 0.75 in by 0.75 in, providing an effective seal around each dowel bar [1]. The primary transverse joint is filled with a three‑quarter‑inch joint filler material and corner repairs employ a half‑inch filler as specified [2]. Some guides further require that the concrete forming the precast slabs attain a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a flexural strength of 650 psi, with aggregate gradation limited so that coarse aggregate retained on a 3/8‑inch sieve does not exceed twenty percent and conforms to a 3 : 1 shape ratio per ASTM D4791 Method B; the use of high‑early‑strength cement concrete is prohibited [2]. The bedding layer beneath the slabs must be a cement‑treated material composed of Portland cement and Type A sand that reaches at least 500 psi compressive strength before opening to traffic [2]. While one set of specifications does not prescribe concrete mixture proportions for the load‑transfer elements, another set explicitly defines slab and bedding concrete strengths and aggregate limits [1][2].

Across the guides, material and mixture selection for load‑transfer systems in jointed concrete pavements is governed by structural, durability, constructability and performance criteria. All transverse joints are required to contain dowel bars that extend through the full embedded length of the joint and are secured with a reliable anchoring binder such as epoxy, polyester or vinylester, providing the stiffness needed for effective load transfer. The same guides stipulate that every dowel bar, longitudinal tie bar and reinforcement steel be coated with an epoxy system in accordance with ASTM A775 and pre‑lubricated with a bond‑breaker to preserve long‑term transfer efficiency, while only one approved high‑performance dowel type may be used on a project unless a specific exception is granted. Concrete for the pavement slabs must be proportioned to achieve at least 4,925 psi compressive strength and 650 psi flexural strength, with trial batches prepared under anticipated site conditions no later than twenty‑eight days before slab production to verify these properties. Both sources advise against the use of high‑early‑strength cement in long‑life concrete pavements because of its propensity for excessive shrinkage cracking that can compromise durability. When rapid opening to traffic is required, an accelerated rapid‑setting mix capable of reaching traffic‑allowable strength within four to seven hours may be employed; otherwise a standard mix with optimized aggregate gradation attains the necessary strength in twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours. The optimized aggregate gradation includes limits on thin, elongated coarse particles, restricting them to no more than twenty percent passing the 3/8‑inch sieve and requiring a shape ratio of three‑to‑one to mitigate durability concerns. For projects anticipating higher loads or longer service life, a high‑strength precast concrete pavement classified as Class AA can be selected despite its greater cost, providing additional stiffness for load transfer. Grout placed in dowel slots and lifting inserts must be pumpable, conform to supplier guidance, and achieve a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before the slab is opened to traffic, with verification performed using AASHTO T106 or PTM No. 521 procedures. Cement‑treated bedding material may be used up to two inches thick but must also reach at least 500 psi compressive strength prior to traffic loading, while fine‑grained bedding is limited to a half‑inch thickness. Constructability requirements include preparing concrete and grout trial batches twenty‑eight days in advance, limiting on‑site storage of coated dowel bars to thirty days without protection, inspecting all coated steel for coating defects before installation, and submitting an LTU sample containing at least six dowels with pre‑applied bond‑breaker lubricant for approval prior to paving. Joint gaps are restrained to half an inch transversely and three‑quarters of an inch longitudinally, and a sacrificial quarter‑inch surface layer is provided so that post‑placement grinding does not impair joint function. Structural details and interfaces

Load transfer in rigid concrete pavements is achieved through a coordinated system of joints, reinforcement, load‑transfer units, transitions, connections, and sealing details. Transverse joints are cut full depth perpendicular to traffic flow and must be spaced so that the gap between adjacent slabs does not fall below one‑quarter inch nor exceed one‑half inch along their entire length; joint spacing is further governed by pavement type, with 30 ft center‑to‑center specified for joint‑reinforced concrete pavements and 15 ft for jointed plain concrete pavements [1][2]. Longitudinal joints are limited to a maximum opening of three‑quarters inch and, when present in long slabs, are reinforced by epoxy‑coated tie bars placed at an angle across the crack (cross‑stitching) to provide near‑complete load transfer, with tie bars required for slab lengths exceeding roughly 65 ft [1][2]. Load‑transfer dowel bars installed across each transverse joint typically number six per joint and are smooth, epoxy‑coated steel members whose diameter ranges from 1.25 to 1.5 in (±0.25 in) and length is about 18 in (±½ in); the coating must be a fusion‑bonded epoxy Type B of at least eight mils non‑abraded thickness or, for galvanized steel, a zinc coat of minimum 3.4 mil, with no more than two holidays per linear foot and any damage repaired so that the affected area does not exceed two percent of the bar surface [2][1]. The dowel bars are anchored in the concrete using an epoxy, polyester, or vinylester binder, and at least half of the length of Type B‑coated bars receives a bond‑breaker lubricant applied at least one hour before concrete placement; the dowel slots may be bottom‑edge, conventional surface slots approximately 2½ in wide, or narrow‑mouth surface slots about one inch wide at the face tapering to three inches below mid‑depth, and holes drilled for the bars may not exceed one‑eighth inch beyond the bar diameter, with tilt or skew limited to 0.25 in at either end and longitudinal translation limited to one inch [2][1]. Tie bars, epoxy‑coated per ASTM A775, run along longitudinal joints to furnish lane‑to‑lane continuity; they are required for slabs longer than 65 ft, must be placed at least 24 in from any dowelled contraction joint, and are grouted with a material that attains an initial set within five minutes and reaches a compressive strength of at least 2,500 psi before traffic is permitted [2][1]. The load‑transfer unit (LTU) system, comprising the dowel bars installed adjacent to existing joints, must be demonstrated with a sample containing a minimum of six high‑performance dowels (or three standard dowels when listed), stored protected for no more than thirty days, and must satisfy performance testing that limits differential joint deflection to 7.5 mil at one million load cycles and restricts additional deflection to no more than 2.5 mil after ten million cycles [2][1]. Where a vertical drop greater than two inches exists, a temporary asphalt wedge transition is provided to bridge the elevation change, an open‑cell polyester foam gasket is placed beneath the slab to accommodate compression under traffic loads, and expansion joint filler of three‑quarter inch (or one‑half inch for corner repairs) is installed in the joint nearest a remaining expansion joint; longitudinal joints of Type A and B patch work also require a full‑depth ¼‑inch polystyrene board bond breaker [2]. All joints are sealed with hot or cold applied joint sealer, and sealant reservoirs—typically sized 0.75 in by 0.75 in—are constructed where required to maintain continuity across the pavement structure; routine joint maintenance including spall repair, cleaning, and resealing in accordance with specified sections of Publication 408 is essential to preserve load‑transfer capability [1][2]. When subgrade support or drainage is deficient, slab stabilization or jacking may be necessary to restore the structural conditions that enable effective load transfer [1].

The design of every interface in a rigid concrete pavement—between adjacent slabs, between slab and subgrade, at the shoulder connection, and where the pavement meets other structures—is intended to provide continuous transfer of wheel loads across those boundaries. Primary load‑transfer devices are epoxy‑coated dowel bars placed in each transverse joint and deformed tie bars installed along longitudinal joints, with the dowels bonded over their full embedded length using epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders to create a continuous connection. In precast slabs the dowels and ties are cast directly into the concrete according to shop drawings, positioned square to slab edges and parallel to the top surface within ±1⁄8 in of the indicated locations, and their free ends must be true circles without burrs. The epoxy coating applied to the dowels must be a fusion‑bonded layer at least 8 mil thick that meets AASHTO M 254, with Type A used when low bond strength is desired and Type B when a separate bond‑breaker lubricant is required; the coating may contain no more than two holidays per linear foot. For Type B dowels a bond‑breaker lubricant is applied to at least one‑half of the bar length at least an hour before concrete placement unless the shop has already provided a bond‑breaker. Joint geometry is tightly controlled: transverse joint gaps are limited to a minimum of ¼ in and a maximum of ½ in, longitudinal gaps may be as large as ¾ in, and vertical differentials across any joint must not exceed ¼ in. For new construction the transverse joint layout follows prescribed spacings—reinforced concrete slabs receive perpendicular joints at 30‑ft intervals while plain concrete slabs are spaced at 15 ft; if a calculated spacing would exceed 20 ft an intermediate joint is introduced to keep transfer distances within acceptable limits, and in single‑lane reconstruction projects new joints are aligned with existing ones. Full‑depth saw cuts are made at every existing pavement‑to‑patch interface, the transverse faces of the old slab receive dowel bars that align with these cuts, and a sealant reservoir measuring 0.75 in by 0.75 in is formed around each dowel assembly and filled with a rubberized or asphalt joint sealant to maintain continuity of load transfer between slabs, shoulders, and adjoining features. When an expansion‑joint replacement is required, a ¾‑in expansion‑joint filler is placed in the adjacent joint and the Type B dowels are lubricated as described; the dowel ends remain bondless and are anchored with a rapid‑set material that must achieve initial set within five minutes, with anchor‑hole tolerances not exceeding ⅛ in larger than the bar diameter and voids smaller than ½ in. The interface between slab and subgrade is prepared with a bedding layer whose type and thickness are prescribed: fine‑grained granular material not exceeding ½ in, cement‑treated material up to 2 in, or grout placed to achieve at least 1 000 psi compressive strength (2 500 psi within 12 h) before traffic; cement‑treated bedding must reach 500 psi at opening. All reinforcement steel, including dowels and ties, is coated with epoxy in accordance with ASTM A775, and any coupling devices must satisfy the strength and coating criteria of Bulletin 15. Prior to paving a load‑transfer unit (LTU) sample containing at least six high‑performance dowels (or three for certain bulletins) is submitted together with PTM No. 642 test reports, shop drawings and other documentation for approval; placement may not begin until the representative verifies passing results. Installation tolerances require that vertical tilt of each dowel end relative to the slab midpoint does not exceed ¼ in and longitudinal translation does not exceed 1 in, ensuring proper alignment before concrete placement. Performance of installed dowels is verified by measuring differential deflection with LVDT probes, which must be limited to 7.5 mil after one million load cycles and may increase no more than 2.5 mil after ten million cycles. In cases where faulting or inadequate transfer is observed, the design calls for restoring sub‑grade support and adding appropriate load‑transfer devices—dowels for heavily channeled loads, additional sealant reservoirs at pavement‑shoulder connections, or deformed tie bars placed in a cross‑stitch pattern across longitudinal cracks—to reestablish structural continuity across all interfaces. By adhering to these dimensional tolerances, coating requirements, joint configurations, bedding specifications, and verification procedures, the interfaces between pavement layers, materials, structures, and adjacent features are designed to transfer wheel loads efficiently while preserving overall structural integrity. [1][2]

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Effective load‑transfer design for jointed concrete pavements begins with verification that the existing drainage system functions properly and that the subgrade provides a stable foundation, thereby preventing water, chlorides, or other incompressible materials from infiltrating the pavement structure. Both guides require that every longitudinal and transverse joint be sealed to block moisture intrusion that could degrade transfer efficiency. Joint and crack sealing, rehabilitation of sealant reservoirs, and thorough cleaning of joints before any bond‑breaker or sealing material is applied are mandated to remove contaminants and ensure a dry interface. Dowel bars and other load‑transfer devices are installed with a bond‑breaker coating and anchored so that voids do not exceed the prescribed maximum, limiting pathways for water to reach the reinforcement. When repair patches remain open during rain, an outlet is excavated at the lowest point on the shoulder to convey water away, and cooling water, slurry, and saw‑cut dust are kept out of any lane opened to traffic. Throughout placement and early curing, the pavement must be protected from precipitation by having sufficient protective material on site to cover edges and surfaces until the concrete reaches its initial set. If forecast temperatures during curing are expected to drop below 50 °F, insulation or comparable protection is applied to maintain a minimum concrete temperature of 40 °F. Joints that have been widened by second‑stage saw cuts are sealed before any equipment or traffic enters the slab, preserving joint integrity under varying environmental conditions. These combined drainage verification, moisture‑control, and climate‑protection measures ensure that dowel bars and other load‑transfer devices remain effective throughout service, safeguarding structural performance despite environmental challenges. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–412)

Load‑transfer design for jointed concrete pavements is required to follow strict geometric criteria that include transverse joints placed perpendicular to traffic and spaced according to the pavement type—30 ft for jointed reinforced concrete and 15 ft for jointed plain concrete—while longitudinal joints must be aligned parallel to final pavement markings and located within twelve inches of those markings so they never cross a driving lane [1][2]. The transverse joints are executed as full‑depth saw cuts whose width does not exceed one‑half inch and whose opening is maintained at a minimum of one‑quarter inch along the entire length, and longitudinal joints are limited to three‑quarters inch in width, with the finished vertical differential across any joint constrained to one‑quarter inch [2]. Dowel bars installed in these joints must be true‑circular, burr‑free, and sized at either 1.5 inches or 1.25 inches in diameter within a tolerance of +0.25 inch to –0.001 inch, with lengths of 18 inches ± ½ inch, and they must be placed square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface with placement accuracy of plus or minus one‑eighth inch [2]. All dowel ends are required to receive a fusion‑bonded epoxy coating of at least eight mils non‑abraded thickness, limited to no more than two holidays per linear foot, and any repair to the coating may not affect more than two percent of the bar surface area; rust or visible defects must be removed and recoated before installation [2]. The dowels are to be fully embedded in a bedding grout that achieves a minimum compressive strength of 2 500 psi prior to opening the repair area, and the anchoring material used to set the dowels must reach its initial set within five minutes with voids no larger than one‑half inch [2]. Where load‑transfer units are employed, at least two incompressible shims shall be placed in ungrouted joints before traffic is allowed, and expansion joint material of three‑quarters inch (or one‑half inch for corner repairs) must be installed when patches replace existing expansion joints [2]. To preserve a uniform supporting surface for overlays, reconstruction lanes longer than about 500 ft should have new joints aligned with the existing joint pattern, thereby maintaining ride quality for users [1]. Safety and accessibility considerations mandate that construction traffic protection measures prevent drop‑off hazards, keep cooling water, slurry and dust out of open lanes, and ensure that any unpatched area is not left exposed for more than forty‑eight hours, thus minimizing work‑zone impacts on the traveling public [1][2]. When rapid opening of a lane is required for public or worker safety, precast concrete paving systems may be selected as an alternative to accelerated concrete patching, reducing the duration of traffic disruption and associated liquidated damages [1]. User requirements limit patch lengths to six‑to‑twenty feet for Type A patches, 20.1‑to‑65 feet for Type B, and 65.1‑to‑500 feet for Type C, and no patch may be narrower than a single lane width, ensuring that vehicle maneuverability is not compromised [2]. An LTU sample containing the required number of dowels—at least six for high‑performance bars or three for standard bars—with pre‑applied bond‑breaker lubricant must be submitted at least forty‑five days before concrete placement, and paving cannot commence until verification testing of that sample is approved [2]. Dowels may not remain on site for more than thirty days without protective covering; if stored longer they must be wrapped in opaque polyethylene to prevent corrosion, thereby maintaining the integrity of the load‑transfer system throughout construction [2].

Verification and expected performance

The completed load‑transfer system is verified through a sequence of documented checks that address design compliance, material acceptance, constructability, and performance. First, the contractor must demonstrate that all dowel bars and tie bars have been cast in the concrete slab at the locations shown on the approved shop drawings, with positional tolerances not exceeding one‑eighth inch. The dimensions of each bar—diameter of either 1.5 in or 1.25 in within +0.25 in/–0.001 in and length of 18 in ±½ in—are inspected to confirm true‑circular, burr‑free free ends. Each bar is required to carry a fusion‑bonded epoxy coating of at least eight mils thickness, with no more than two holidays per linear foot as detected by a 67.5‑V detector, and the coating type must meet the Type B specification used in the project’s bond‑breaker system. The bond‑breaker material, whether shop‑applied or pre‑lubricated, must satisfy the pull‑out load criteria of AASHTO M 254. Joint geometry is checked on site to ensure that transverse joints are between one‑quarter and one‑half inch wide along their entire length, longitudinal joints do not exceed three‑quarters inch, and vertical differentials across any joint are limited to a quarter inch. After concrete placement, the grout placed in dowel slots and bedding chambers must achieve a compressive strength of at least 2 500 psi before traffic is permitted. Performance of each load‑transfer unit (LTU) is confirmed by submitting full PTM No. 642 test reports, shop drawings, and an LTU sample containing at least six dowels with pre‑applied bond‑breaker lubricant to the Representative at least forty‑five days prior to paving. The PTM tests must show that the maximum LVDT differential deflection for both approach and leave sides does not exceed 7.5 mil at one million load cycles, and that the increase in deflection after ten million cycles is no greater than 2.5 mil over the one‑million‑cycle value. Only one dowel type may be used unless a project‑specific approval is obtained. Prior to concrete placement, LTU assemblies are secured at the prescribed locations on the standard drawings, and their alignment is verified so that vertical tilt or horizontal skew of each end does not exceed a quarter inch relative to the midpoint, and longitudinal translation from the intended joint line is limited to one inch. The contractor must demonstrate the fastening method to the Representative for approval before paving begins. During construction, daily anchoring checks are performed by the Representative. On the first day of each season’s dowel‑anchoring work, three anchored dowels are randomly selected; a four‑inch core is drilled at least three inches from the joint face and the void space around the dowel is measured. Acceptance requires that the summed voids around the perimeter be less than half an inch; any failure triggers placement of a new patch joint two feet away at no additional cost. All dowel bars must be stored protected from the elements, with on‑site storage not exceeding thirty days unless otherwise approved. These coordinated inspections, testing, and documentation ensure that the load‑transfer system complies with the project specifications, can be constructed as designed, will perform under traffic loading, and remains consistent with all contractual requirements. The load‑transfer design is intended to preserve the service life of jointed concrete pavements by limiting joint movement, faulting, and ride‑quality degradation. To verify that the joint deflects only a very small amount under repeated traffic, structural adequacy testing per PTM No. 642 will be performed using Linear Variable Differential Transformers (LVDTs) to record the differential deflection between the loaded and unloaded sides of the joint. The test must demonstrate that after one million load cycles the differential movement does not exceed 7.5 mil, and that the increase in differential movement by ten million cycles is no greater than an additional 2.5 mil. In parallel, the effectiveness of the dowel‑bar system will be confirmed through core sampling to ensure that voids around each dowel perimeter are less than 0.50 inch, and compressive strength testing of concrete cylinders per PTM No. 604 will verify that the repaired slab reaches at least 1,000 psi before traffic is permitted. Surface flatness will also be inspected with a straightedge to confirm tolerances. Together these measurements directly assess whether load transfer maintains structural continuity across joints, prevents excessive joint deflection and moisture intrusion, and thereby sustains the intended distress resistance and smooth riding surface throughout the pavement’s design life.
