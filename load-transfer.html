Load Transfer

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Load Transfer is a topic under Rigid Pavement.
load-transfer
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-29

Load transfer is a design approach used in jointed Portland‑cement concrete pavements to restore structural continuity that is lost when transverse cracks or joints interrupt the slab, by providing a mechanism—most commonly fully embedded dowel bars anchored across the crack—to redistribute wheel loads from one slab to the adjacent slab and thereby maintain near‑complete load sharing, uniform support, and long‑term pavement integrity[1][2]. The system must limit vertical differential movement at a joint to no more than 7.5 mil after one million load cycles and permit an increase of no more than 2.5 mil after ten million cycles, criteria that are verified with linear variable differential transformers[1][2]. Effective load transfer therefore depends on precise placement, full‑length bonding with epoxy‑type binders, strict dimensional tolerances, and controlled joint gaps, all of which are prescribed in the governing state and AASHTO specifications[1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Load Transfer” also include the following subtopics: Aggregate Interlock.

Aggregate Interlock serves as a mechanical method for load transfer by maintaining structural continuity through the physical locking of coarse aggregates within fractured concrete slabs. [2] This mechanism relies on controlled cracking and reseating procedures to ensure that the broken fragments remain engaged, preserving the pavement’s ability to redistribute wheel loads across joints without relying solely on dowel bars. [2]

Purpose and design objectives

Load transfer is required because transverse cracks or joints in Portland‑cement concrete slabs interrupt structural continuity, causing traffic loads to be applied only to isolated slab sections and resulting in elevated stresses, joint spalling, cracking, faulting, slab migration, loss of aggregate interlock, and ultimately reduced pavement performance and service life. The engineering need is therefore to provide a mechanism—most commonly dowel bars that are fully embedded and securely anchored across the crack or joint—to redistribute wheel loads from one slab to the adjacent slab, restore near‑complete load sharing, maintain uniform support, and preserve long‑term structural integrity of the pavement. In precast concrete pavements the same requirement is expressed as keeping adjacent slabs acting as a single structural unit when subjected to traffic loads by creating lane‑to‑lane ties through dowel bars at transverse joints and tie bars along longitudinal joints, thereby providing uniform support beneath the slabs. Effective load transfer limits the vertical differential movement between the loaded and unloaded faces of a joint to no more than 7.5 mil after one million load cycles and permits an increase of no more than 2.5 mil by ten million cycles, criteria that are verified with linear variable differential transformers. Proper dowel installation requires each bar to be placed in a precisely drilled hole, anchored with fast‑setting material, and inspected so that the total void around its perimeter is less than 0.50 inch on any core. Where slabs are placed directly on bedding, they must not be opened to traffic for more than three days unless effective load transfer and slab undersealing are provided. Cast dowel bars and tie bars as shown on shop drawings complete the required lane‑to‑lane ties, ensuring structural integrity and ride quality over millions of repetitions. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 399–435), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–673)

Load transfer in jointed concrete pavements must provide continuous functional continuity across transverse joints so that applied traffic wheel loads are distributed into the surrounding slab, preserving structural integrity and preventing premature joint failure[1][2]. To achieve this function the design requires that every transverse or patch joint be equipped with dowel bars that are securely anchored and fully bonded along their entire embedded length using approved epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders[1][2]. The dowels must be placed in precisely cut slots so that the transverse saw cuts fall within one inch of the bar centreline, with each end allowed no more than a quarter‑inch deviation in tilt or skew and longitudinal and horizontal translations limited to one inch[2]. Structural dimensions are prescribed as a diameter of 1.25 to 1.50 inches with an allowable tolerance of +0.25/‑0.001 in and a length of 18 ± ½ in, and the free ends must be true circles without burrs to avoid stress concentrations[2]. All steel components, including dowel bars, tie bars and longitudinal joint ties, shall receive a Type B fusion‑bonded epoxy coating of at least eight mils non‑abraded thickness, with no more than two holidays per linear foot as detected by a 67.5‑V holiday detector, in accordance with ASTM A775[2]. The joint gap after finishing shall be limited to between one‑quarter and one‑half inch and the vertical differential across any joint may not exceed a quarter inch, thereby limiting slab movement that could compromise safety[2]. Durability of the load‑transfer system is ensured by requiring the bedding and grout surrounding the dowels to achieve defined compressive strengths before traffic is permitted, namely at least 500 psi for cement‑treated bedding, a minimum of 1 000 psi (rising to 2 500 psi within twelve hours) for cementitious bedding grout, and a minimum of 2 500 psi for the dowel grout[2]. Bedding thickness is limited to one‑half inch when fine‑grained material is used and two inches for cement‑treated bedding, controls joint movement and supports long‑term performance[2]. A bond‑breaker lubricant must be applied to at least half the length of each Type B coated dowel bar at least one hour prior to concrete placement, and shims are employed to restrain slab movement until the grout reaches the required strength, thereby preventing premature loading that could damage the transfer system and avoiding sudden slab movement that could endanger vehicles[1][2]. Traffic may not be allowed onto the pavement until the specified compressive strengths are attained, a restriction that protects the integrity of the load‑transfer assembly and thus enhances safety[2]. Serviceability is verified by monitoring joint deflection with Linear Variable Differential Transformers; the differential deflection shall not exceed 7.5 mil after one million load cycles and may increase no more than an additional 2.5 mil after ten million cycles, limits that preserve ride quality and a uniformly supported surface for overlays[2]. By meeting these functional, structural, durability, safety and serviceability objectives the load‑transfer system contributes to a smooth riding experience, prevents moisture intrusion and corrosion, and maintains the long‑term performance of jointed concrete pavements[1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–435), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–673)

Scope and applicability

Load transfer is a design approach applied to rigid‑concrete pavements, including Portland‑cement concrete slabs, jointed reinforced or plain cement‑concrete slabs, prestressed concrete slabs, long‑life concrete pavement sections, and precast concrete pavement slab systems used for both intermittent patching and continuous installations. The technique is called for whenever the structure contains transverse or longitudinal joints, working cracks, deteriorated joint areas, or when new or replacement joints are created during full‑lane patching or reconstruction activities. It is especially appropriate for projects that involve dowel‑bar retrofits, full‑depth patching, or reconstruction of jointed slabs where the work extends roughly five hundred feet or more and where design joint spacings of fifteen to thirty feet with perpendicular joints are specified. In addition, load transfer is required whenever a pavement section exceeds about sixty‑five feet in length, when new joints are placed adjacent to existing joints during full‑lane patching, or when a new joint is formed within a slab. The method is also suitable for heavily channeled traffic conditions on pavements that retain aggregate interlock, for hairline cracking, and where the subgrade and drainage conditions are stable enough to permit retrofit rather than overlay. For single‑course full‑depth patches—whether normal‑strength or accelerated‑strength—the patch length may range from six feet to five hundred feet, must extend at least one lane width, and requires saw‑cut faces that receive dowel bars in the transverse faces before concrete placement. Steel dowels are installed across cracks or joints according to standard drawings, coated and bonded along their entire embedded length with epoxy, polyester, or vinylester binders, and sealed appropriately; acceptable dowel bar materials include steel‑coated bars meeting ASTM A513 and AASHTO T 253, mechanically galvanized bars per ASTM B695/B696 with a minimum coating of 3.4 mil, stainless‑steel bars per ASTM A955/A955M, uncoated corrosion‑resistant bars satisfying AASHTO M 334 Level 3, and high‑performance dowels sized 1.25–1.5 in in diameter and 18 in long that are epoxy‑ or GFRP‑coated with a Type B epoxy coating at least eight mil thick. Dowel bars are installed with lubricated ends, drilled holes no larger than one‑eighth inch beyond the coated dowel, and a bond‑breaker lubricant applied to at least half of the length of Type B coated dowels; tie bars are used in longitudinal joints for sections longer than sixty‑five feet or for Type C patches. Load‑transfer units must be placed and anchored according to shop or contract drawings, staked before paving so that vertical tilt or horizontal skew at each end does not exceed 0.25 in and longitudinal translation is limited to one inch, and all dowel assemblies must pass structural adequacy testing under PTM No. 642 with differential LVDT deflection no greater than 7.5 mil after one million load cycles and an additional increase of no more than 2.5 mil after ten million cycles. For long‑life concrete pavement projects only a single type of high‑performance dowel bar may be used unless a waiver is granted, and a test‑verified LTU sample containing at least six dowels (or three for Bulletin 15 bars) with pre‑applied lubricant must be approved before paving; dowel bars must also be stored protected from the elements for no more than thirty days and high early‑strength concrete is prohibited. Bedding beneath the slabs may consist of fine‑grained granular material not exceeding one‑half inch in thickness or cement‑treated bedding up to two inches thick that must achieve at least five hundred psi compressive strength before traffic is allowed, and grout placed in dowel and tie‑bar slots must reach a minimum compressive strength of 2 500 psi, with epoxy, cementitious, or polyurethane grouts permissible when they meet manufacturer specifications. Load‑transfer devices are not required for simple base repairs beneath an asphalt overlay on a concrete‑based pavement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–436), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–673)

When a concrete slab rests on a Portland‑cement concrete base and is subsequently covered with an asphalt overlay, the joint at the interface does not require dowel‑type load‑transfer devices; designers therefore omit them and treat the patch joint as a simple overlay connection. In situations where high early‑strength (HES) concrete would be prone to excessive shrinkage cracking or when traffic must be reopened quickly, conventional load‑transfer detailing becomes less attractive and alternatives such as accelerated‑setting concrete or precast concrete pavement are preferred because they avoid the crack risk and meet the rapid‑opening schedule. Likewise, if joint performance is compromised by faulting or inadequate load transfer, the primary remedy shifts from adding dowels to first confirming that the subgrade is stable and that drainage functions properly; when either the support is unstable or drainage is deficient, slab stabilization or jacking must be undertaken before any dowel‑bar retrofit can be justified. Even with adequate drainage, a dowel‑bar retrofit is only appropriate when the underlying support remains sound and the joint distress is limited to hairline cracks that still benefit from aggregate interlock; more extensive cracking or widespread damage calls for replacing the joint with a full‑depth concrete patch rather than relying on load‑transfer devices. For longitudinal cracks that remain in reasonably good condition, cross‑stitching offers an alternative means of achieving near‑complete load transfer without installing conventional dowel bars. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90)

Design inputs and requirements

Load transfer in jointed Portland‑cement‑concrete pavements is regulated by a combination of state roadway construction standards and national specifications that prescribe the design, materials, installation and performance testing of dowel bars and related load‑transfer units. [1][2] The primary documents are the PennDOT Roadway Construction Standards (Publication 72M) together with the Department’s Specification Manual (Publication 408), which define the required joint spacing—30 ft for reinforced concrete sections and 15 ft for plain‑concrete sections—and mandate that every transverse patch joint be provided with smooth, epoxy‑coated dowel bars fully bonded along their entire embedded length with an epoxy, polyester or vinyl‑ester binder. [1] Complementary requirements appear in the AASHTO‑based specifications, which call for dowel bars to meet Section 709.1(a)2, ASTM A513 and AASHTO T 253, to receive a Type B epoxy coating of at least eight mils non‑abraded thickness, and to have a bond‑breaker lubricant applied to at least one‑half of the bar length unless already treated in the shop. [2] Both guides require that dowel bars be installed according to approved design drawings—RC‑26M for retrofits or the shop drawings of a listed precast concrete pavement system (Bulletin 15)—with location tolerances of plus or minus one‑eighth inch and alignment square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface. [1][2] The joint width limits are also specified, with transverse joints permitted to be between one‑quarter inch and one‑half inch wide and longitudinal joints not exceeding three‑quarters inch. [2] The dowel‑transfer assembly must achieve a compressive strength of at least 2,500 psi for the dowel and bedding grout before traffic opening, and performance testing under PTM No. 642 requires that linear variable differential transformer (LVDT) deflection be limited to no more than 7.5 mils after one million load cycles and not increase by more than 2.5 mils after ten million cycles. [2] Void content around each dowel after placement is limited to a summed perimeter void of less than 0.50 in, while misalignment tolerances restrict vertical tilt or horizontal skew at each end to no more than 0.25 in and longitudinal translation from the intended joint location to one inch. [2] Anchoring material used to bond the dowels must set within five minutes, and the total voids around the perimeter of any core taken after installation must not exceed the specified limit, providing a direct check on proper bonding as required by both the PennDOT specifications (Sections 527, 516) and the AASHTO provisions. [1][2] Project constraints include the requirement that the precast concrete pavement system be an approved manufacturer listed in Bulletin 15, that only one type of dowel bar be used on a given project unless an exception is granted, and that on‑site storage of dowel bars not exceed thirty days without special approval. [2] For high‑performance dowels not listed in Bulletin 15, the guides demand submission of full test reports, shop drawings and LTU samples to the representative at least forty‑five days before paving, whereas listed dowels require a reduced documentation set. [2] Installation procedures call for staking load‑transfer units at their designated locations prior to concrete placement, performing full‑depth saw cuts at any pavement‑patch interface, installing transverse dowels across the joint, constructing and sealing a sealant reservoir in accordance with RC‑20M, and applying cross‑stitching of longitudinal cracks as described in Specification 408 Section 528. [1][2] Additional maintenance provisions such as joint cleaning, proper sealant shape, drainage control and slab jacking to restore support of settled slabs are governed by the PennDOT sections on joint maintenance (Sections 512, 513, 516, 519, 521, 525, 590, 681). [1] Together these standards, specifications, performance thresholds and project constraints constitute the governing framework for ensuring effective load transfer across joints in jointed concrete pavements. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–436), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–673)

Design methods and criteria

Load transfer in jointed concrete pavements is addressed through a set of prescribed retrofit, repair, construction, and testing procedures rather than through explicit analytical equations or software models. [1][2] The primary retrofit technique involves installing smooth epoxy‑coated dowel bars across every working transverse crack or joint, with the dowels bonded along their entire embedded length using an epoxy, polyester, or vinylester binder; spacing criteria of 30 feet for reinforced slabs and 15 feet for plain slabs guide where retrofits are required. [1][2] When the anticipated service life of a pavement segment exceeds twenty years, high‑performance dowel bars are recommended to ensure long‑term load‑transfer capability. [1] For longitudinal cracks or joints that remain in reasonable condition, cross‑stitching is employed by placing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle across the crack, providing near‑complete transfer of loads. [1][2] In cases where slab support has deteriorated, slab stabilization or jacketing is performed prior to reinstalling dowel bars so that both support and load‑transfer functions are restored. [1] The PCPS system specifies three allowable dowel‑slot configurations at transverse joints—bottom‑edge slots paired with embedded dowels on the opposite edge, conventional slots approximately 2½ inches wide with embedded dowels, and narrow‑mouth surface slots that taper from about 1 inch at the face to roughly 3 inches below and receive dowel bars during placement—all of which must follow the fabricator’s approved shop drawings. [2] Tie bars are required along longitudinal joints when slab lengths exceed 65 feet, also in accordance with the approved shop drawings. [2] All dowel and tie bars must receive an epoxy coating that complies with ASTM A775 or, for coated dowels, ASTM A513/AASHTO T 253, and a bond‑breaker lubricant must be applied to at least one‑half of the bar length; Type B coatings demand a minimum non‑abraded thickness of 8 mil applied at least one hour before concrete placement, whereas Type A dowels do not require lubricant. [2] Prior to concrete placement, load‑transfer units are staked in their exact locations and anchored so that vertical tilt or horizontal skew does not exceed 0.25 inch at each end and longitudinal or horizontal translation is limited to one inch. [2] Installation tolerances require drilling holes no larger than one‑eighth inch beyond the bar diameter, injecting anchoring material into the rear of the hole before inserting the dowel, rotating the bar three to five times to purge air, and allowing the anchoring material to set within five minutes. [2] Acceptance criteria stipulate that the summed perimeter voids around any core taken from an installed dowel must be less than 0.50 inch, and random pull‑out tests on tie bars are conducted with a hydraulic jack to either 12 000 lb or until 1/32 inch slip occurs, with the average resistance divided by bar spacing compared against prescribed table values. [2] Load‑transfer performance is further validated through structural adequacy testing per PTM No. 642, where differential deflection measured by linear variable differential transformers across a joint must not exceed 7.5 mil after one million load cycles and may increase no more than 2.5 mil after ten million cycles. [2] Full test reports, shop drawings, and LTU samples containing at least six dowels with pre‑applied bond‑breaker lubricant are submitted to the Representative at least forty‑five days before paving, and dowel bars may not remain on site for more than thirty days without protective covering. [2] For new pavement joints a load‑transfer unit is provided, a sealant reservoir sized three‑quarters of an inch by three‑quarters of an inch is constructed, and the joint is sealed with rubberized or asphalt‑based sealant; for patching joints full‑depth saw cuts are made at the interface, dowels are installed in the transverse faces of the existing concrete, and the same sealant reservoir and sealing steps are followed. [1][2] All bedding, grout, and cementitious mixes used in the retrofit must be prepared according to manufacturer recommendations, with trial batches tested for compressive strength using AASHTO T 106 for concrete cubes or PTM No. 521 for grout cylinders before traffic opening. [2] The procedural guidance, dimensional requirements, material specifications, and performance‑based testing criteria are documented in the standard references Publication 72M (Roadway Construction Standards) and Publication 408 (Specifications), which serve as the primary design tools for implementing load‑transfer measures. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–436), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–673)

Load transfer at every transverse joint of concrete pavements is required to be provided by dowel bars that are fully bonded along their entire embedded length using approved anchoring binders such as epoxy, polyester or vinylester. When the repaired pavement is expected to remain in service for more than twenty years before a programmed replacement, high‑performance dowels must be specified. Joint spacing must conform to applicable state standards and transverse joint openings are limited to no more than one‑half inch with a minimum gap of one‑quarter inch maintained along the full length of the joint. Finished vertical differential across any joint may not exceed one‑quarter inch. Prior to allowing traffic, the grout or bedding material surrounding the dowel must achieve a compressive strength of at least 2,500 psi. The dowel bars are required to be isolated from the surrounding concrete with a bond‑breaker and to receive a fusion‑bonded epoxy coating of Type B having a minimum non‑abraded thickness of eight mils; holiday detection is limited to no more than two holidays per linear foot, and repaired surface area may not exceed two percent of the bar. Dimensional tolerances for high‑performance dowels are prescribed at diameters of either 1.25 in or 1.50 in with an allowable variation of plus or minus 0.25 in, and lengths of 18 in ± ½ in. Installation must ensure that the sum of voids surrounding each dowel bar does not exceed a half‑inch perimeter when verified by core drilling on the first day of anchoring. Alignment tolerances require that vertical tilt or horizontal skew at either end of a dowel relative to its midpoint be limited to 0.25 in, and longitudinal as well as horizontal translation of the bar be limited to one inch each. A test assembly containing at least six dowel bars (or three when high‑performance bars are used) with bond‑breaker lubricant pre‑applied must be submitted for review at least forty‑five days before concrete placement, and paving may not commence until the representative approves the documentation and verifies that all prescribed standards have been met. Only a single type of dowel bar is permitted on long‑life concrete projects unless an explicit exception is granted; bars stored on site for more than thirty days require special approval, and any bar exhibiting corrosion, coating defects, or damage must be replaced prior to placement. If joint performance deteriorates, evidenced by faulting or inadequate transfer, the design must restore load‑transfer capability assuming a stable subgrade, functional drainage, and sufficient support beneath the pavement structure. For sections subjected to heavily channeled wheel loads, dowel‑bar retrofits employing epoxy‑coated smooth bars placed in the wheel paths are prescribed to improve transfer across transverse joints or cracks. Where a longitudinal crack or joint remains in reasonably good condition with aggregate interlock and only hairline cracking, cross‑stitching may be applied to achieve nearly 100 percent load transfer. Laboratory testing of load‑transfer units must demonstrate that differential deflection measured by an LVDT does not exceed 7.5 mil after one million loading cycles and increases no more than an additional 2.5 mil after ten million cycles, establishing a reliability baseline for long‑term service. These combined criteria define the assumptions, thresholds, factors of safety, and reliability requirements that must be satisfied to ensure effective load transfer in concrete pavements. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–436), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 671–673)

Structural section and dimensions

Load transfer in jointed concrete pavements is governed by specific slab length and thickness requirements that appear across the guides; slabs are fabricated with lengths ranging from six to fifteen feet, extending to twenty feet when prestressed units are used, and a sacrificial layer of one‑quarter inch is added to the nominal design thickness [2][1]. The minimum slab thickness may not be reduced below eight inches [1]. Joint spacing for transverse joints varies with pavement type, with reinforced concrete pavements calling for a nominal joint spacing of thirty feet and plain concrete pavements requiring fifteen feet, and an intermediate joint must be provided when existing spacing exceeds twenty feet [1]. The width of the transverse joint gap is required to lie between one‑quarter and one‑half inch, while longitudinal joints are limited to a maximum opening of three‑quarters inch [2]. Dowel slots employed for load transfer are sized according to the chosen method; conventional slots have surface openings of approximately two‑and‑a‑half inches, whereas narrow‑mouth slots start at about one inch wide at the surface and taper to roughly three inches deeper [2]. No dimensional requirements are prescribed for the base, subbase, treated‑soil layers, shoulders, edge supports, or reinforcement beyond those implicit in the slab and joint specifications [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Load‑transfer design begins by ensuring that every transverse joint in a concrete patch is equipped with dowel bars whose embedded length is fully bonded, most commonly using epoxy or comparable binders to secure the load path across joints. The slabs are cast slightly over‑thickened, typically a quarter‑inch thicker than the nominal structural thickness determined from the pavement design analysis, providing additional stiffness and continuity for load transfer [1][2]. Subbase preparation and bedding placement must achieve a firm, even surface within a half‑inch grade tolerance before slab installation, and the bedding material is fine‑graded and placed with a laser‑controlled screed that can establish the required cross‑slope while limiting surface variation to less than one‑eighth inch over each slab length [2]. For full‑width reconstructions exceeding 500 ft, joint spacing follows standard manual recommendations: 30 ft for jointed reinforced concrete pavement and 15 ft for jointed plain concrete pavement, both employing perpendicular joints, coated dowels, and appropriate seals; when only a single lane is rebuilt, new joints are aligned with existing ones and an intermediate joint is inserted if the existing spacing exceeds roughly 20 ft [1]. Slab lengths are limited to a minimum of six feet and a maximum of fifteen feet (twenty feet when prestressed) so that transverse joint openings can be controlled within one‑quarter to one‑half inch and longitudinal joints do not exceed three‑quarters inch, reinforcing the overall joint geometry required for effective load transfer [2]. Cross‑slope and profile are restored after slab placement by diamond grinding, which fine‑tunes the surface to meet design grades while preserving the integrity of the dowel system [1]. Any vertical drop greater than two inches between the new concrete and the existing pavement must be accommodated with a temporary asphalt wedge transition, whereas permanent vertical differentials are limited to a quarter inch to avoid excessive stress concentrations at joints [2]. Longitudinal tie bars or bolts are specified only for patches longer than sixty‑five feet, providing the necessary tie‑in to adjacent lanes and ensuring continuity across longitudinal joints in continuous sections exceeding that length [1][2]. When analysis indicates that a deeper slab is required on longer sections, thicknesses are increased accordingly, but such adjustments follow the initial design assessment rather than being applied arbitrarily [1]. Together, these coordinated selections of slab thickness, layer configuration, joint spacing, cross‑slope control, transition treatment, and tie‑in detailing satisfy the design criteria for reliable load transfer across concrete pavement structures.

Materials and mixture design

Load‑transfer design for jointed concrete pavements calls for an epoxy‑coated smooth steel dowel bar system that crosses each transverse joint or crack, with the coating applied as a fusion‑bonded Type B epoxy of at least eight mils non‑abraded thickness and an overall minimum thickness of 3.4 mil [1][2]. The dowels must be installed with full‑length anchorage using a binder selected from epoxy, polyester, or vinylester, which secures the bar along its embedded length [1]. All reinforcement steel, including dowel bars, tie bars, and longitudinal joint ties, is required to receive an epoxy coating in accordance with ASTM A775 [2]. Dowel diameters are limited to either 1.5 in or 1.25 in with a tolerance of +0.25 – 0.001 in, and lengths must be 18 in ±½ in, placed within ±1/8 in of the locations shown on shop drawings [2]. The portion of each dowel embedded in the slab is to be treated with a bond‑breaker lubricant applied to at least one‑half of its length, and the drilled holes are to be only slightly larger than the coated bars [2]. Slots cut for the dowels are backfilled with a concrete patching material that is placed and cured in place, while rapid‑setting grout used to fill the dowel slots and bedding chambers must achieve a compressive strength of at least 2 500 psi before traffic is permitted [1][2]. Joint sealing around the dowels is achieved with a rubberized sealant placed in reservoirs typically sized 0.75 in by 0.75 in, providing an appropriate seal for each joint [1]. Expansion joints adjacent to the load‑transfer units are filled with material of ¾ in thickness for new joints and ½ in thickness for corner repairs, and an anchoring cement that sets within five minutes must be supplied together with its full material data sheet [2]. The bedding beneath the dowels may consist of a fine‑grained granular material meeting Section 703.1 not exceeding a half‑inch thickness, or a cement‑treated mix of Portland cement and concrete sand that attains at least 500 psi compressive strength at opening to traffic and does not exceed two inches in thickness [2]. For the concrete slab itself, one guide requires a minimum compressive strength of 4 925 psi and a flexural strength of 650 psi as indicated on shop drawings, while another guide does not prescribe concrete mixture proportions for load‑transfer purposes [2][1]. When mixture specifications are provided, the coarse aggregate retained on a 3/8‑in sieve must be limited to no more than twenty percent with a shape ratio of three‑to‑one, cement content is capped at 611 lb per cubic yard, and the water‑to‑cement ratio is restricted to 0.42 for slip‑form paving or 0.45 for fixed‑form paving; high‑early‑strength cement is expressly prohibited [2]. Repairs or new joints require a single‑course full‑depth cement‑concrete patch of normal or accelerated strength, into which the load‑transfer dowels are installed in the transverse faces of the existing pavement and an appropriate load‑transfer unit provided [2].

Load‑transfer design begins by selecting dowel bars that satisfy structural deflection limits and durability requirements, with diameters limited to 1.5 in or 1.25 in (tolerance +0.25 in/–0.001 in) and an overall length of 18 in ±½ in, and each bar must pass PTM‑642 testing demonstrating a maximum LVDT differential deflection not greater than 7.5 mil after one million load cycles and no more than a 2.5 mil increase after ten million cycles [2]. To protect the bars from corrosion and to aid installation, they are coated either with a fusion‑bonded epoxy Type B having at least eight mils of non‑abraded thickness or, when galvanized, with a minimum coating thickness of 3.4 mil (2 oz/ft²), and all reinforcement steel, dowel bars and longitudinal joint ties receive an epoxy coating in accordance with ASTM A775 [2]. The selected dowels are anchored across transverse joints using a reliable binder such as epoxy, polyester or vinylester, which is applied over the full embedded length to ensure continuous load transfer [1]. Prior to placement, bars are lubricated with a bond‑breaker, inspected visually for coating defects or corrosion, and field‑verified by testing three to six bars depending on type; storage on site is limited to thirty days unless protected, after which visual inspection is required again [2]. The concrete mix that surrounds the load‑transfer system is proportioned to meet both structural capacity and long‑term durability, employing an optimized aggregate gradation in which coarse aggregate retained on a 3/8‑in sieve does not exceed twenty percent of the total aggregate and conforms to a shape ratio of three to one per ASTM D4791 Method B [2]. Cement content is capped at 611 lb per cubic yard for both slip‑form and fixed‑form paving, with water‑to‑cement ratios limited to 0.42 for slip‑form mixes and 0.45 for fixed‑form mixes, and high‑early‑strength cement concrete is avoided in long‑life pavements to prevent shrinkage cracking [1][2]. When rapid opening to traffic is required, an accelerated rapid‑setting mix may be used, achieving the requisite strength within four to seven hours; otherwise a standard mix with the optimized gradation reaches opening strength in twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours while satisfying the same cement and w/c limits [1]. For projects anticipating higher loads or requiring greater stiffness, a high‑strength precast concrete pavement (Class AA) may be selected despite its higher cost, providing enhanced load‑transfer performance [1]. The bedding material that secures dowels and longitudinal ties is mixed to achieve at least 500 psi compressive strength at traffic opening, with a maximum thickness of two inches for cement‑treated bedding and a half‑inch limit for fine‑grained granular bedding [2]. Grout placed in the joint slots must be pumpable, attain a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before slab opening, develop an early strength of 1,000 psi (and reach 2,500 psi within twelve hours), and the same trial mix that demonstrated the required 28‑day strength is used for production batches [2]. By adhering to these material selections and proportioning criteria, load‑transfer systems achieve the desired structural integrity, durability, constructability, and performance throughout the pavement’s service life [1][2].

Structural details and interfaces

Load transfer in rigid concrete pavements is achieved through a coordinated system of transverse and longitudinal joints that are precisely detailed, reinforced, sealed, and connected to provide a continuous path for traffic loads [1][2]. Transverse joints are cut perpendicular to traffic flow and must be maintained with a width between one‑quarter and one‑half inch along their entire length; the guides also require that these joints be kept clean, sealed, and provided with sealant reservoirs typically sized 0.75 in by 0.75 in to ensure adequate fill [1][2]. Longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters inch in width and are reinforced when slab lengths exceed roughly sixty‑five feet [1][2]. The spacing of transverse joints is governed by pavement type, with joint‑reinforced concrete pavements specifying a center‑to‑center distance of thirty feet and jointed plain concrete pavements specifying fifteen feet [1]. When a transverse joint abuts an existing expansion joint, a three‑quarter inch expansion‑joint filler is placed in the joint to accommodate movement [2]. If vertical drops greater than two inches occur at a joint during construction, a temporary asphalt wedge transition is installed to maintain smooth vehicle passage [2]. Across each transverse joint a set of epoxy‑coated dowel bars provides the primary load‑transfer mechanism; typically six smooth dowels are installed per joint, although high‑performance dowel kits may contain three larger bars [1][2]. The dowel bars are round steel with a fusion‑bonded epoxy Type B coating of at least eight mils (or mechanically galvanized to a minimum thickness of 3.4 mils), and the coating must be lubricated on at least half of its length before concrete placement [2][1]. High‑performance dowels are permitted with diameters of 1.5 inches or 1.25 inches ±0.25 inch and a length of 18 ± 0.5 inch, and they must be provided with end caps for tubular types [2]. The dowel holes are drilled no more than one‑eighth inch larger than the bar diameter and the bars are anchored in place with a rapid‑set material such as epoxy, polyester, or vinylester that reaches initial set within five minutes [1][2]. Alignment of each dowel is controlled so that vertical tilt or horizontal skew does not exceed 0.25 inch at either end, longitudinal translation relative to the intended joint location is limited to one inch, and overall displacement of each end from the bar midpoint remains within these limits [2]. The dowels are installed as part of load‑transfer units (LTUs) that are pre‑assembled with bond‑breaker lubricant already applied; a representative LTU sample must be submitted for PTM No. 642 testing demonstrating differential deflection not greater than 7.5 mil at one million load cycles and an increase of no more than 2.5 mil after ten million cycles, with test reports provided at least forty‑five days before placement [2]. Only a single dowel type may be used on a project unless an exception is granted, and bars may not remain on the jobsite for more than thirty days without special permission [2]. The grout placed in dowel slots, tie‑bar slots, and lifting inserts must achieve at least 2 500 psi compressive strength before traffic opening, and a hot or cold joint sealer is applied to each joint after placement; foam gasket material may be installed beneath the slab to accommodate minor movements [2]. Longitudinal reinforcement in the form of epoxy‑coated tie bars is required for continuous sections longer than sixty‑five feet and for Type C patches, with tie bars spaced at least twenty‑four inches from any dowelled contraction joint [1][2]. The tie bars are coated per ASTM A775 and may be installed as deformed bars placed at an angle across longitudinal cracks (cross‑stitching) to provide near‑complete load transfer when the crack condition permits [1][2]. Cross‑stitching involves placing epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars diagonally across a longitudinal joint, and this technique is intended to achieve nearly 100 % load transfer across cracks that are in reasonably good condition [1][2]. Effective load transfer also depends on routine joint maintenance, including spall repair, cleaning, resealing according to the specifications of Publication 408 Sections 512, 513, and 521, and rehabilitation of deteriorated joints [1]. Where subgrade support or drainage is deficient, slab stabilization or jacking may be required to restore the structural conditions that enable proper load transfer [1]. Together these joint dimensions, reinforcement details, connection treatments, transition provisions, and ongoing maintenance practices constitute the structural system needed for reliable load transfer in concrete pavements [1][2].

Interface design ensures loads applied to any slab are efficiently transferred to neighboring slabs, underlying layers and adjoining features [1][2]. The design begins with a fine‑graded sub‑base and a granular bedding not exceeding one‑half inch in thickness, or a cement‑treated bedding up to two inches that must reach at least 500 psi compressive strength before traffic is permitted [2]. Reinforced concrete slabs receive transverse joints at 30‑foot intervals while plain concrete slabs use 15‑foot spacing; when the calculated spacing would exceed 20 feet an intermediate joint is introduced, and in single‑lane reconstruction projects new joints are aligned with existing ones [1]. At each transverse joint dowel bars are placed through the joint, coated with a Type B fusion‑bonded epoxy of minimum eight mil non‑abraded thickness, diameters of 1.5 in or 1.25 in (tolerance +0.25 in, –0.001 in), lengths of 18 in ±0.5 in, and anchored with full‑length bonding using epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders; the bars are positioned square to the slab edge within ±1/8 inch, with vertical tilt or horizontal skew limited to 0.25 inch at each end and longitudinal translation not exceeding one inch [1][2]. For Type B coated dowels a bond‑breaker lubricant is applied to at least half the length unless the shop coating already provides sufficiently low bond strength, while Type A bars require no lubricant [2]. Joint gaps are controlled so that transverse joints range from a minimum of one‑quarter inch to a maximum of one‑half inch and longitudinal joints may be as wide as three‑quarters inch; at pavement‑shoulder connections a sealant reservoir measuring 0.75 in by 0.75 in is formed and filled with rubberized or asphalt joint sealant to complement the mechanical dowels [1][2]. Longitudinal cracks or joints in good condition receive deformed tie bars placed in a cross‑stitch pattern, providing near‑complete load transfer across the crack; these tie bars are installed according to shop drawings and coated per ASTM A775 [1][2]. Dowel slots are drilled no larger than one‑eighth inch beyond bar diameter, lubricated with bond‑breaker, and filled with a rapid‑set anchoring grout that attains initial set within five minutes and reaches at least 2 500 psi compressive strength before opening to traffic, with voids around the perimeter limited to less than 0.5 inch [2]. When patches intersect existing pavement, full‑depth saw cuts are made, dowel bars installed in the transverse faces of the existing slab, sealant reservoirs constructed and sealed; if an adjacent expansion joint remains, a 3/4‑inch (or 1/2‑inch for corner repairs) filler is placed [1][2]. Dynamic testing must demonstrate that differential deflection across a joint does not exceed 7.5 mil after one million load cycles and increases no more than 2.5 mil by ten million cycles, confirming the effectiveness of the designed interfaces; if faulting or inadequate transfer is observed, sub‑grade support is restored and appropriate load‑transfer devices such as dowel retrofits or additional sealant reservoirs are added [2][1]. Only one type of dowel bar may be used on a project unless special approval is obtained; high‑performance dowels require PTM No. 642 test reports, shop drawings and LTU samples submitted 45 days before paving, and dowel bars may not remain on site more than thirty days without protection [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

In designing load‑transfer systems for jointed concrete pavements the drainage network must be confirmed as operational and the subgrade must possess sufficient stability so that water, chlorides or other incompressible materials are excluded from entering the pavement structure [1]. This protection is achieved through continuous joint and crack sealing, rehabilitation of sealant reservoirs, and diligent cleaning of joints to guard against moisture‑related deterioration and to sustain the performance of dowel bars, cross‑stitching or other retrofit devices [1]. Dowel bars are required to be firmly anchored so that voids around them are minimized, a condition that not only secures effective load transfer across joints but also serves as a barrier to moisture intrusion [2]. When construction activities expose a patch area to rain, an outlet is excavated at the lowest point of the patch through the shoulder and any damaged shoulder is repaired to provide a path for runoff and prevent water accumulation within the joint zone [2]. Additionally, cooling water, saw‑cutting slurry and dust generated during construction are kept out of traffic lanes to avoid introducing moisture into the pavement surface or joints [2].

Load‑transfer design must satisfy geometric criteria that prescribe the orientation and spacing of transverse joints, requiring them to be perpendicular to traffic flow and spaced at 30 ft for jointed reinforced concrete pavements and 15 ft for jointed plain concrete pavements. The width of each transverse joint shall be maintained between one‑quarter and one‑half inch along its entire length, while longitudinal joints are prohibited from crossing traffic lanes and must run parallel to and within twelve inches of the final pavement markings. Finished vertical differentials across any joint are limited to a quarter inch, with an interim allowance of half an inch before grinding operations, and full‑depth saw cuts used for joint preparation may not exceed one‑half inch in width. Where an expansion joint abuts an existing joint, a three‑quarter‑inch expansion‑joint material shall be installed adjacent to the remaining joint. Load‑transfer dowel bars are required to be round members of nominal diameter 1.25 in or 1.5 in (tolerance +0.25 in/–0.001 in) and length 18 in ±½ in, with each end true‑circular, free of burrs, and positioned so that tilt or skew does not exceed a quarter inch from the dowel midpoint and lateral translation is limited to one inch. The dowels must be fully embedded in the pavement and bonded along their entire length using epoxy, polyester, or vinyl‑ester binders, and they shall receive a fusion‑bonded epoxy coating of at least eight mils non‑abraded thickness (Type B) with bond‑breaker lubricant applied to at least half of the coated length unless already treated in the shop. Performance limits require that differential deflection across an assembled joint not exceed 7.5 mil after one million load cycles and increase no more than an additional 2.5 mil after ten million cycles. Accessibility and safety provisions mandate protection of traffic from drop‑off conditions; any temporary vertical offset greater than two inches between a new precast slab and the existing pavement must be bridged with a temporary asphalt wedge transition, and all such drop‑offs are to be protected in accordance with Section 901.3(j). Uncured dowel or bedding grout may not be exposed to traffic for more than three days, excavations must be sealed within forty‑eight hours, and newly saw‑cut patches may remain open to traffic for no longer than seventy‑two hours until required strength criteria are satisfied. When public or worker safety dictates a minimum of work‑zone impacts, the use of precast concrete paving systems is preferred as an alternative to accelerated concrete patching, and reconstruction of lanes longer than about five hundred feet shall align new joints with existing ones to preserve a uniform supporting surface for overlays and maintain ride quality. User‑related requirements include limiting edge slumping to one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes or ramps and to one‑quarter inch between lane edges and shoulders, thereby preserving vehicle stability and ride comfort. Repair patches must extend the full lane width, with lengths limited to six to twenty feet for standard repairs (and up to sixty‑five feet for larger categories), and they shall incorporate a sealant reservoir and joint sealing to prevent ingress of water, slurry, or dust. Verification and expected performance

The completed load‑transfer system is verified through a coordinated series of inspections, material testing, and performance monitoring that together demonstrate compliance with the contract documents, constructability of the installed units, functional performance under traffic loading, and alignment with overall project requirements. First, dowel bars and longitudinal tie bars are placed exactly as shown on the approved shop drawings; each bar is set square to the slab edge and parallel to the top surface within a tolerance of plus or minus one‑eighth inch. The transverse joint gaps are checked to ensure they remain between a quarter‑inch minimum and a half‑inch maximum along their entire length, while longitudinal joints are limited to three‑quarters of an inch and no finished vertical differential across any joint exceeds a quarter‑inch. All dowel bars must have true‑circular, burr‑free free ends, the specified epoxy coating per ASTM A775 applied at the required thickness, and no more than two holidays per linear foot as detected with a 67.5‑volt holiday detector; any visible rust or coating defects are repaired before concrete placement. A trial batch of the fine‑grained or cement‑treated bedding material is mixed at least twenty‑eight days prior to slab installation, tested in accordance with AASHTO T 106 and documented by an independent laboratory to meet the required compressive strength (500 psi for cement‑treated bedding), and the same mix is used on site within the prescribed thickness limits. Likewise, a trial batch of dowel grout is prepared under identical conditions, cylinders are tested per PTM No. 521, and the grout must achieve at least 2,500 psi compressive strength before traffic is permitted; this requirement is also confirmed by laboratory reports submitted to the Representative. On the first day of dowel‑bar anchoring each construction season, three random cores are taken at least three inches from the joint face; the voids around each dowel perimeter must be less than half an inch, and any failure triggers additional anchoring evaluations and corrective patching. Structural adequacy is further demonstrated by full test reports prepared according to PTM No. 642, including Linear Variable Differential Transformer (LVDT) measurements that limit differential deflection to no more than 7.5 mils after one million load cycles and restrict the increase in deflection to no more than 2.5 mils by ten million cycles. An LTU sample containing a minimum of six dowel bars pre‑lubricated with bond‑breaker is submitted, together with the complete test reports and shop drawings, at least forty‑five days before paving; concrete placement does not commence until the Representative signs off on these documents and confirms that all test results meet the specified criteria. Only one approved type of dowel bar may be used unless an exception is granted, and storage of dowels follows Section 1002.3 with a maximum unprotected onsite exposure of thirty days. Collectively, these inspections, material verifications, core‑sampling checks, and performance tests ensure that the load‑transfer installation complies with design intent, can be constructed as detailed, will perform under service loads, and remains consistent with all project specifications.
