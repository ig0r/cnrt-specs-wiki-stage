Joint

A joint in concrete or asphalt pavement is a purpose‑designed discontinuity that accommodates slab movement, provides load transfer (often via dowel bars or tie bars), ensures drainage, and is constructed with specified dimensions, sealants, bond‑breakers, expansion‑joint fillers, and other materials as defined by the applicable specifications.

Purpose and design objectives

Joints are incorporated in pavement structures to accommodate movements that would otherwise cause cracking, loss of bond, or structural discontinuity, thereby preserving load transfer and protecting durability. In concrete‑pavement patch design transverse joints must be spaced at 30 feet for JRCP and 15 feet for JPCP to meet current joint spacing requirements. For partial‑depth repairs joints are provided so that slab expansion and contraction can be accommodated without imposing compressive or point‑loading stresses on repair patches; when joint performance is poor the adjacent slab may push against a patch causing compression failure, spalling at transverse joints, or point‑loading where a crack propagates toward the patch instead of vertically, therefore the design calls for installing a rigid polystyrene board for longitudinal joints and premolded expansion‑joint material for transverse joints, each equal in width to the existing joint, so that the patch does not contact the adjacent slabs and sufficient space for slab movement is maintained. Joint locations are intended to mitigate loss of slab support caused by pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement that can create voids beneath the slab; without addressing this, excessive slab deflections and stresses would produce joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and eventual breakup of the slab, compromising structural integrity. Joints provide the lateral support needed at slab corners to reduce corner stresses and allow load to be transferred effectively across slab edges; when transverse joints are dowelled or tied concrete shoulders are present this support improves and lowers the load‑transfer coefficient J, whereas inadequate lateral support raises J, indicating a need for better joint performance. Slip‑form concrete pavements incorporate joints to accommodate inevitable shrinkage and temperature‑induced movements of the slab, thereby preventing uncontrolled or random cracking; transverse and longitudinal contraction joints are sawed at the locations and depths prescribed in Section 501.3(i)2 to provide a controlled crack plane and allow load‑transfer devices to function as intended. Full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs require the joint system to control slab movement and maintain structural continuity by limiting transverse joint width to a maximum of 1/2 inch and at least 1/4 inch, restricting longitudinal joints to no more than 3/4 inch, and keeping the finished vertical differential across any joint within 1/4 inch; load‑transfer methods are provided at transverse joints and open joints are shimmed when slabs are opened before grout placement to prevent drift of the slabs under traffic. Roller‑compacted concrete pavements use fresh vertical joints to assure a continuous bond between newly placed and previously placed lanes, while control joints are saw‑cut to one‑quarter depth to prevent random cracking and cold joints are identified for appropriate treatment, thereby addressing separation, cracking, and loss of bond at lane edges and lift interfaces. Concrete pavement patches incorporate joints to accommodate movement and prevent damage by providing expansion‑joint material that is 3/4 inch when a patch replaces an existing expansion joint adjacent to a remaining joint and 1/2 inch for corner repairs, and full‑depth cuts along existing joints relieve dowel or tie‑bar restraints. Unbonded concrete overlays install joints chiefly to accommodate slab movement before sawing and to control cracking; saw cuts of at least D/3 depth near edges are required, any panel that cracks outside the sawed joints is deemed defective, joint spacing and panel dimensions are limited to avoid premature cracking, and longitudinal joints are kept out of wheel paths to preserve structural performance. Bonded concrete overlays require a joint system primarily to prevent debonding between the new slab and the existing pavement and to accommodate movements that could otherwise cause cracking or loss of load transfer; transverse joints are saw‑cut directly over all existing joints with a width at least equal to the underlying joint or crack, depth equal to the full overlay thickness plus ½ inch, and intermediate joints are placed so that no panel exceeds 15 feet in length. Slab stabilization provisions call for joints or cracks exhibiting a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more to be stabilized when joint efficiency is at least 65 percent, or patched and stabilized when efficiency is less than 65 percent, and upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 inch during grout injection is not allowed, thereby controlling excessive vertical movement that can cause cracking and reduced performance. For pervious asphalt pavement systems joints preserve continuity of the pervious course when construction stops or lanes meet by overlapping the new course 1 inch to 2 inches over the previously placed edge and maintaining a uniform uncompacted depth adjacent to the compacted lane, while at the end of each day’s work or whenever an interruption exceeds 30 minutes a temporary vertical bulkhead—or a full‑depth saw cut—must be installed to create a straight transverse construction joint that extends through the entire depth and width of the pervious course, thereby controlling surface irregularities and maintaining structural continuity across breaks. Asphalt‑treated permeable base courses apply the same principle, requiring longitudinal joints to overlap adjacent lanes by 1 inch to 2 inches and mandating transverse construction joints whenever daily work ends or an interruption exceeds a 30‑minute period, using a temporary vertical bulkhead or saw cuts to form a full‑depth straight joint, thus controlling surface irregularities and preserving structural continuity. Joint rehabilitation restores the sealing function of transverse contraction, construction, or expansion joints that have become compromised by constructing a sealant reservoir and applying sealant, thereby preventing water ingress and maintaining durability despite movements caused by temperature changes and loads. Plain cement concrete gutters employ contraction joints formed or sawed to the dimensions shown on the Standard Drawing and specified in Section 501.3(i), with a 3/4‑inch premolded expansion joint material cut to the gutter’s cross‑section and installed at structures and at each day’s end, and all joints are prepared and sealed or caulked according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, to control cracking caused by concrete contraction and accommodate movement from thermal expansion. Long‑life concrete pavement incorporates joints to prevent moisture loss from concrete at joint locations during curing and after joint sawing, a condition that could compromise durability; consequently the quality‑control plan must specify a method to cure and protect joints during the curing period so that moisture loss in and adjacent to joints is prevented, and all joints must be sealed before any traffic is allowed unless an approved method for protecting the joint from damage and preventing material intrusion is used. Precast concrete pavement slabs rely on the joint system to supply load‑transfer capability and lane‑to‑lane ties needed to keep adjacent slabs acting together and maintain uniform support under the slab, thereby addressing differential movement and related distress; joints with faulting exceeding 1/4 inch are milled when a geotextile interlayer is used, and for an asphalt interlayer joints with faulting over 1/2 inch must be scratched or milled to reduce the faulting before overlay placement. Load‑transfer units such as dowel bars are required so that the vertical movement between the approach and leave sides of a concrete pavement joint stays within strict limits under repeated traffic loading; specifically, the differential deflection must not exceed 7.5 mils at one million load cycles and may not increase by more than 2.5 mils when the cycle count reaches ten million. The joint must provide effective load transfer by dowelling all transverse joints and securely anchoring the dowels throughout their embedded length with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders. High‑performance dowel bars should be considered when the repaired pavement is expected to remain in service for more than 20 years. If existing joint spacing exceeds 20 feet, an intermediate joint shall be placed to satisfy current spacing requirements of 30 feet for reinforced concrete (RC‑21M) and 15 feet for plain concrete (RC‑27M). In slip‑form cement concrete pavements the joint must be located perpendicular to the centreline across the full pavement width, with longitudinal joints placed parallel to and within 12 inches of final pavement markings unless otherwise detailed. Dowel bars in slip‑form construction shall be installed so that vertical tilt or horizontal skew does not exceed a displacement of 0.25 inch at each end, longitudinal translation is limited to 1 inch, and horizontal translation is limited to 1 inch. Transverse contraction joints shall be sawed to a depth of D/3 and a width of 1/8 inch with the cut centred within ±1 inch over dowel centres. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slabs the transverse gap shall not exceed ½ inch and must be at least ¼ inch along its entire length, while longitudinal gaps may not exceed ¾ inch and the finished vertical differential across any joint shall not exceed ¼ inch to preserve ride quality and safety. Panel dimensions in unbonded concrete overlays shall not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio shall not exceed 1.5, thereby providing functional slab movement while meeting structural limits on panel size. In bonded concrete overlays a backing material shall be installed within ±¼ inch of the existing pavement surface so that it cannot be dislodged during paving, and transverse joints shall be saw‑cut to the full overlay thickness plus ½ inch with a width at least as wide as the underlying joint. All longitudinal and transverse joints shall be sealed after cleaning any deleterious material in order to protect durability against moisture intrusion. Durability of joints is achieved by resealing at the first sign of distress, using rubberized sealant and, for pavement‑shoulder interfaces, a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir. Joint faces shall be cleaned, media‑blasted, and subjected to QC wipe tests before sealant placement, and an oversized backer rod whose width equals the joint width plus 50 percent shall be installed to enhance durability. Joint material shall meet movement capability and adhesion requirements of ASTM C719, attain a side‑wall bearing pressure of at least 4 psi, resist ozone and ultraviolet exposure per ASTM C793, and function over a temperature span from –20°F to +140°F. Traffic may not be opened onto repaired concrete until the cement grout has cured for at least 12 hours or, for hot‑asphalt repairs, until the asphalt has cooled or polyurethane has expanded and hardened for a minimum of 30 minutes. In concrete pavement patching the joint shall not be opened to traffic until the concrete attains a compressive strength of at least 1,000 psi. Surface variation across joints in slip‑form pavements shall not exceed 3/16 inch and a minimum mean texture depth of 0.040 inches shall be provided to satisfy safety and serviceability requirements. Upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch shall not be allowed, and joint faulting exceeding 0.5 inch shall trigger grinding or placement of a scratch course to maintain safety. Serviceability is maintained by limiting slab deflections, preventing further cracking, and preserving a smooth riding surface with surface irregularities not exceeding 3/16 inch. Joint rehabilitation techniques such as dowel bar retrofits, cross‑stitching, or resealing shall be employed when faulting or poor load transfer is observed to restore functional performance. For long‑life concrete pavement joints the alignment of the first five transverse joint locations across the full width and one joint per 2,640 linear feet shall be verified within eighteen hours after each day’s placement to ensure structural continuity. Stabilization of joints with a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more and a joint efficiency of at least 65 percent may be required when approved by the representative, contributing to durability and safety. All sealants shall be placed only when the joint faces are clean, dry, and free of oil, moisture, or other contaminants, and ambient temperature must be between 40°F and 90°F unless otherwise permitted. The side‑wall bearing pressure requirement of at least 4 psi for joint material also addresses safety by ensuring adequate support under traffic loads. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

Joint design is appropriate for Portland cement concrete (PCC) pavements that are being reconstructed or fully‑depth patched, because load transfer at transverse joints must be provided by dowels [1]. When reconstruction involves all adjacent traffic lanes over a length of 500 feet or more the work shall follow Publication 72M RC‑21M for jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) or RC‑27M for jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP); RC‑21M requires 30‑foot joint spacing, perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, while RC‑27M requires 15‑foot joint spacing with the same joint characteristics [1]. If only one lane is reconstructed for 500 feet or more, the new joints must be aligned with existing joints and an intermediate joint shall be added when the existing spacing exceeds 20 feet to satisfy the RC‑27M spacing requirement [1]. Full‑depth patching of deteriorated PCC slabs also requires specification of new pavement joints, whether skewed or non‑skewed, and dowel‑bar anchorage throughout their embedded length [1]. Joint rehabilitation, cleaning and sealing are required when a PCC structure is being restored rather than overlaid; sealing of joints and cracks is mandated for patches that are not to be overlaid but is not recommended if an overlay will be placed [1]. Dowel‑bar retrofits and cross‑stitching may be employed on heavily loaded concrete roads where improved load transfer across transverse joints or longitudinal cracks is needed, provided the subgrade and drainage are stable [1]. Partial‑depth repairs of PCC are appropriate for transverse or longitudinal joints, cracks, or interior slab spalls whose repair depth does not exceed one half the slab thickness; such repairs may be classified as Type 1 spot repairs (15 inches to 6 feet long) or Type 2 extended repairs (>6 feet) [1]. The material selected for these joint‑related patches depends on the available time before traffic reopening, ambient temperature and cost, with Class AA Cement Concrete Modified specified for regular curing schedules and accelerated repair material used when a faster opening is required [1]. To preserve original joint function in partial‑depth repairs a rigid polystyrene board should be placed between the patch and adjacent slab for longitudinal joints and a premolded expansion‑joint material for transverse joints, providing adequate space for slab movement during temperature cycles [1]. For plain or reinforced cement‑concrete (rigid) pavements covered by Section 501, transverse contraction joints must extend across the full width and be located over dowel‑bar centers whenever a concreting interruption exceeds 30 minutes; such joints may not be placed within 10 feet of another transverse joint, while longitudinal joints shall be parallel to and within 12 inches of the final pavement markings unless the drawings specify otherwise, with load‑transfer units or dowel bars secured in advance of paving [2]. In full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs described in Section 529, joints are required when a continuous prestressed precast paving system is employed; the mix design must consider ambient average daily temperatures and exposure conditions to determine panel size and expansion‑joint locations, transverse joint width shall not exceed 1/2 inch and shall be at least 1/4 inch along its entire length, longitudinal joints may not exceed 3/4 inch, and a minimum concrete compressive strength of 4,925 psi and flexural strength of 650 psi are required [2]. Roller‑compacted concrete pavements described in Section 502 use joints to connect lanes and lifts; a joint placed within 60 minutes of the preceding placement is considered fresh and must be kept moist with loose material removed before the next lift, whereas any joint exceeding that interval becomes a cold joint that must be cut full depth at least 6 inches from the exposed edge, cleaned of debris and moistened immediately prior to placement of the adjacent lane; control joints are saw‑cut to one‑quarter depth after rolling and no later than 18 hours after placement, and joints may be sealed when indicated in the plans [2]. Concrete pavement patching under Section 516 requires joint detailing; if a patch replaces an existing expansion joint while the adjacent lane’s joint remains, a 3/4‑inch expansion‑joint material shall be installed in the nearest joint, corner repairs shall use 1/2‑inch expansion‑joint material to reestablish the original transverse joint location, and one‑lane‑at‑a‑time or single‑lane patches of Type A and B shall provide a full‑depth 1/4‑inch polystyrene board bond breaker in the longitudinal joint while Type C patches omit the bond breaker but require tie bars; all constructed joints are to be sealed as required [2]. Unbonded concrete overlays on existing concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 548) are appropriate when panel dimensions do not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter side aspect ratio does not exceed 1.5; joint spacing follows the project plans or an approved layout, longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths, and saw cuts that form transverse and longitudinal joints must be at least D/3 within 12 inches of pavement edges and at least D/4 elsewhere, made as soon as the concrete is hard enough to prevent excessive raveling while preventing random cracking [2]. Bonded concrete overlays on existing concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545) require joints wherever the existing pavement has transverse or longitudinal joints, full‑depth repairs are performed, or panel lengths would exceed 15 feet; joint backing or an approved filling material shall be installed to within ±1/4 inch of the surface in all existing transverse joints before placement, after paving transverse joints are saw‑cut directly over each existing joint with a depth equal to the full thickness of the overlay plus 1/2 inch, intermediate transverse joints are cut so that no panel exceeds 15 feet, longitudinal joints receive a 1/4‑inch wide sealant reservoir cut to the same depth, and only terminal joints and those from full‑depth repairs are matched on existing continuously reinforced concrete pavement [2]. For flexible (asphalt) pavements, Section 405 specifies that a longitudinal joint is created when a newly placed wearing course abuts a previously placed wearing course, including joints formed by tandem pavers; joints involving existing pavement, within one foot of construction obstructions, or occurring in small areas such as intersections or notches may be excluded, the density of each joint is verified by core sampling with a lower specification limit of 91 %, and a longitudinal‑joint lot consists of 12,500 ft composed of five 2,500‑ft sublots [2]. Bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 540) are appropriate when the overlay thickness and panel layout meet the limits set in Section 540.3(i); panels shall not exceed a maximum dimension of 18 times the concrete overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter side ratio shall not exceed 1.5, joint spacing follows the plans or an approved layout that avoids longitudinal joints in wheel paths, and saw cutting is the only acceptable method for creating transverse joints, using a green‑cut saw to a depth of D/3 and a minimum width of 1/8 inch once the concrete has hardened enough to prevent excessive raveling [2]. Notched wedge joints may be used in flexible‑pavement layers when the nominal maximum aggregate size of the hot‑mix does not exceed 19.0 mm; if the joint is adjacent to opposing traffic, the abutting asphalt must be placed within one working day, and if next to traffic moving in the same direction it may be placed within two working days; longitudinal joints should be offset approximately six inches from the joint in the layer below, located near the pavement centerline on two‑lane roads or within twelve inches of lane lines on roadways with more than two lanes, the joint face must be painted with a uniform coating of asphalt material before placing abutting lanes, and the joint is sealed on surface courses with hot PG 64S‑22 binder applied at 265 °F to 320 °F in a band four inches wide (±1 inch) and one‑sixteenth inch thick (±1/32 inch); sealant may not be placed when air temperature is below forty degrees Fahrenheit [2]. Ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses form longitudinal butt joints that are compacted with rollers and then sealed with hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement kept between 265 °F and 320 °F; the sealant band shall be four inches wide (±1 inch) and one‑sixteenth inch thick (±1/32 inch) centered within one inch of the joint, and sealant may not be placed when air temperature is below forty degrees Fahrenheit unless the Representative permits it [2]. In pervious asphalt pavement systems (Section 420), longitudinal joints are formed by overlapping the previously placed lane edge by 1–2 inches and maintaining a uniform uncompacted depth adjacent to the compacted lane, while transverse joints are required at the end of each day’s work or whenever an interruption longer than 30 minutes occurs; such transverse joints may be created either by installing a temporary vertical bulkhead that forms a full‑depth, full‑width construction joint or by sawing a full‑depth joint before paving resumes [2]. Asphalt‑treated permeable base courses (Section 360) use the same longitudinal joint formation as pervious asphalt and create transverse construction joints at the end of each day’s work or after an interruption exceeding 30 minutes, using either a temporary vertical bulkhead or a saw cut [2]. Joint sealing material specified in Section 705 is appropriate for asphalt pavement joints when a rubberized joint‑sealing material that complies with ASTM D6690‑Type II or Type IV is used; for all other joint‑sealing applications ASTM D6690‑Type IV shall be used, silicone sealant may be employed where a low‑modulus, non‑sag sealant meeting the listed tensile, elongation, extrusion, hardness, shelf‑life, ozone and UV resistance criteria is required, premolded expansion‑joint filler is suitable where a compressive load of at least 5 psi is needed to achieve 25 % compression and not more than 30 psi for 85 % compression, and premolded polyurethane foam joint filler may be used when the dimensions are 8 feet length, width between 4 7/16 in. and 4 3/4 in., depth from 7 ¾ in. to 13 in., density 6.0–10.0 lb/cu in., compressive deflection pressures between 5 psi and 30 psi, recovery of at least 95 %, and water absorption not exceeding 30 % volume [2]. For plain or reinforced cement‑concrete pavements (Section 501) joints are required whenever transverse or longitudinal joint assemblies are needed, the pavement abuts structures such as manholes, inlets, valve boxes, bridges, culverts, or railway tracks; a quality‑control plan must include procedures for proper joint construction, timely sawing, sealing and joint wipe testing, and premolded expansion‑joint filler of at least 1/2 inch thickness shall be placed around the outer sides of structures, at least 3/4 inch thick adjacent to rigid structures, a minimum 3/4‑inch thick filler against mortar‑filled rails, and a minimum 1/4‑inch thick filler along each mortar‑filled rail [2]. Cross‑stitching joints (Section 528) are intended for rigid concrete pavements where an existing longitudinal joint or crack is present; they may not be applied to transverse joints, work may proceed only when ambient air temperature and pavement surface temperature are at least 40 °F after a full‑depth saw cut has been made along the entire slab, holes are placed with a hydraulic drill no more than 3/8 inch larger than the tie bar, epoxy bonding compound is injected and the tie bar inserted, and sealing is performed only after the temperature condition is met and the faces are clean and dry [2]. Premolded expansion‑joint filler (Section 705) is appropriate for transverse joints in reinforced or plain cement‑concrete pavement where the filler must be supplied in lengths equal to the lane, ramp or taper width with a minimum thickness of 1 inch; it is also suitable for longitudinal joints in concrete base courses and other structural joints, with pieces at least 8 feet long that are joined securely, and acceptable materials include cork, sponge rubber, fiber joint fillers, preformed polypropylene foam, and a composite of recycled automobile‑tire rubber bonded with polyurethane [2]. Pavement relief joints (Section 504) are used in rigid‑pavement projects that incorporate a sleeper slab over a subbase, polyethylene sheeting, asphalt base and wearing courses; the base‑course joint faces are coated with PG 64S‑22, transverse joints are provided at the interface of the relief joint and adjacent concrete pavement where saw cuts must be made within ±¼ inch after the wearing course has cooled below 140 °F, forming a reservoir 1 inch deep by ½ inch wide; sealing material may be placed only when the reservoir faces are clean and dry, air temperature is between 40 °F and 90 °F unless otherwise allowed, and the fill level is kept 1/8‑¼ inch below the pavement surface with a tape bond breaker not extending more than 1/8 inch up the face [2]. Full‑depth longitudinal concrete joint repair (Section 519) is appropriate for rigid pavement projects that involve repairing longitudinal joints; saw cuts parallel to the existing longitudinal joint must not exceed ½ inch in width when opened to traffic, and traffic may be permitted on the repaired area for a maximum of 72 hours after the cuts are made [2]. Pervious concrete pavements (Section 520) require construction joints at locations specified in the design; such joints may be formed immediately after final finishing to about one‑quarter slab thickness with a cross‑rolling tool or later saw‑cut once the concrete has achieved sufficient compressive strength to prevent aggregate loss, and any fines generated by saw cutting must be removed to avoid clogging [2]. Pressure‑relief joints (Section 511) require sawing a joint opening four inches wide through the full depth and width of the pavement, removing existing concrete material, cleaning all vertical faces, and coating the joint sides with either sealant or lubricant adhesive as recommended by the manufacturer [2]. Geofoam lightweight fill systems (Section 219) create a controlled joint system for pavement support where vertical joints between adjacent longitudinal rows of blocks must be offset at least 2 feet, any gap on a vertical joint may not exceed 0.1 foot, and the top of the Type 1 block is capped with a minimum 12‑inch‑thick layer of Type 2 blocks before being covered by a minimum of 4 feet but not more than 6 feet of overlying material [2].

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, if a concrete pavement repair segment approaches roughly ±500 feet and the remaining intact pavement between patches is ten feet or less, the guidance directs removal and full‑section reconstruction rather than relying on intermittent jointed patches. When rapid reopening of traffic is required, the manual advises avoiding high‑early‑strength concrete because of shrinkage risk; instead accelerated concrete may be considered, and when minimizing work‑zone impacts is a priority, precast concrete paving systems become the preferred alternative design. The same manual indicates that when joint performance problems such as faulting or poor load transfer are evident, alternatives such as slab stabilization, jacking, dowel‑bar retrofit, full‑depth concrete patching, or joint replacement should be used instead of resealing the joint. It further notes that joint rehabilitation is limited to situations where the existing concrete pavement is being restored rather than overlaid; therefore, if an unbonded Portland cement concrete or asphalt overlay is planned, sealing the joints is not recommended and another design approach must be employed. Extensive spalling beyond minor distress also makes full‑depth repairs preferable to simple joint sealing, as does any repair that would need to extend deeper than half the slab thickness, which requires a full‑depth concrete patch rather than a partial‑depth repair. For corner spalls that extend beyond mid‑slab depth, the manual specifies full‑depth corner repairs as the appropriate remedy, shifting away from reliance on joint treatment alone. When the existing pavement condition is only good to fair, a bonded jointed overlay may be used; however, if the pavement is moderately to significantly deteriorated, exhibits rocking or faulted slabs, has unstable support, or suffers drainage problems, an unbonded concrete overlay—or rubblizing of the existing slab—becomes the preferred design because it does not require matching the underlying joints and provides a new stable platform. The Pennsylvania specifications state that for an unbonded concrete overlay, if a crack is present at or within 1.5 feet of the planned joint location, sawing must be omitted and the condition addressed per Section 590, indicating that a conventional joint is not suitable and another design remedy should be used. They also require that panel dimensions shall not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and that an aspect‑ratio greater than 1.5 shall be avoided; when these limits would be exceeded, the Representative may adjust the joint layout or select alternative panel sizing, implying a different pavement design is preferable. For asphalt longitudinal joints, the specifications exclude joints adjacent to existing pavement not built under the contract, any joint within one foot longitudinally of an obstruction such as manholes or bridge structures, and small areas like intersections, gore zones or transitions where consistent longitudinal‑joint construction cannot be ensured; in these excluded categories a different treatment must be considered. In concrete partial‑depth repairs, when delamination runs through an existing pavement joint the repair shall maintain the joint as two separate minimum‑size areas only if the quality‑control plan contains a detailed procedure for re‑establishing the joint and protecting adjacent cracks; otherwise a full‑depth concrete patch is required, making the full‑depth design preferable. Similarly, if removal of unsound concrete or damage to dowel bars during a partial‑depth repair would cause the repaired zone to become a full‑depth patch, the specifications require constructing a full‑depth concrete pavement patch, thereby limiting the use of joint‑based partial‑depth repairs. The ultra‑thin bonded wearing course provisions limit longitudinal joints to butt‑type joints that must be sealed with hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement kept between 265°F and 320°F on a clean, dry surface, and they prohibit sealant placement when air temperature falls below 40°F unless the Representative permits it; transverse joints are required only if continuous forward paving can minimize them, otherwise paving must be stopped and an alternative design without such joints considered. Cross‑stitching of longitudinal joints is prohibited when ambient or pavement surface temperature is below 40 °F, and it may not be performed on transverse joints or cracks; consequently an alternative repair or design method must be used under those temperature conditions. The plain cement concrete base‑course specifications allow work to proceed without forms when a widening does not exceed six feet in width, and they permit tied joints for convenience of construction or omission of sawing in full‑width construction, making a non‑sawn or tied‑joint approach preferable to conventional jointed construction. Finally, because the section on sealing joints and cracks has been deleted from the specifications, no sealing treatment is prescribed, further reducing the need for conventional joint detailing. Joint design and construction for concrete pavements are governed by PennDOT Roadway Construction Standards (Publication 72M) and the Department Specification Manual (Publication 408) [1]. For full‑depth reconstruction of lanes longer than 500 ft, joints must follow RC‑21M for jointed reinforced concrete pavement, which calls for 30‑foot spacing, perpendicular orientation, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, or RC‑27M for jointed plain concrete pavement, which requires 15‑foot spacing with the same geometry and dowel treatment [1]. Designers also must specify New Pavement Joint items per RC‑26M, using skewed or non‑skewed baskets as required [1]. Joint rehabilitation and maintenance are prescribed in Publication 408 sections 512, 521, 513, 525, 527, 528, 529, 590, 1091, 527, 528, requiring cleaning and sealing joints with rubberized joint sealant, constructing a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir for pavement/shoulder joints, and using 4.75 mm asphalt wearing‑course material to fill badly spalled joints before overlay [1]. Dowel‑bar retrofits and cross‑stitching are controlled by Sections 527 and 528, specifying epoxy‑coated dowels or deformed tie bars to restore load transfer [1]. Partial‑depth joint repairs must comply with Section 525; the repair depth may not exceed half the slab thickness, and defects deeper than that require full‑depth patches per Sections 516 and 519. Partial‑depth work is classified as Type 1 for spots between 15 inches and 6 feet and Type 2 for lengths greater than 6 feet, and may be performed either under a CPR project or as routine maintenance [1]. Slab stabilization shall be executed in accordance with Publication 72M RC‑26M and Publication 408 Section 679; when no preliminary testing has been done, at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized, and traffic control during drilling follows Publication 213 Temporary Traffic Control Guidelines [1]. Joint placement and spacing for unbonded concrete overlays must follow RC‑20M using the joint‑spacing tables (Table 10.10) based on overlay type and thickness; separation between existing pavement and the new overlay is achieved with a fine‑graded 1‑inch minimum asphalt separation layer, or alternatively a non‑woven geotextile interlayer as allowed by Publication 408 Section 548 [1]. Additional overlay joint requirements are referenced in Publication 408 Sections 540, 541, 545 and slab fracturing procedures in Section 522 [1]. The design of transverse joints uses the AASHTO Rigid Pavement Design Procedures, which require a load‑transfer coefficient J; the applicable J ranges are given in Table 8.2 for all Department designs, with higher J values recommended for lower k‑values or higher truck percentages and lower J values used for higher k‑values or lower truck percentages [1]. For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 501) longitudinal joints shall be placed parallel to and within 12 inches of the final pavement markings unless construction drawings indicate otherwise; transverse joints in new multilane slabs shall run perpendicular to the centreline, dowels must be located as shown on the Standard Drawings with mis‑alignment limits of 0.25 inch tilt or skew and 1 inch longitudinal and horizontal translation, saw cuts shall be made to a depth of D/3 and a width of 1⁄8 inch, tie bars may not be installed within 24 inches of a dowelled contraction joint, and each selected tie bar shall be tested after the concrete reaches 3,500 psi compressive strength either to a pull‑out load of 12,000 lb or to 1⁄32 inch slippage [2]. Edge slumping during slip‑form paving is limited to 1⁄8 inch between adjacent lanes and 1⁄4 inch between lane and shoulder [2]. Full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529) require expansion‑joint locations be determined using the aggregate thermal‑expansion coefficient values from the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide; transverse joint gaps may not exceed ½ inch and must be at least ¼ inch along their entire length, longitudinal joint widths may not exceed ¾ inch, and all joints shall be sealed as specified in Section 501.3(n) [2]. Roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502) define a fresh joint as a vertical or horizontal joint where the adjacent RCC material is placed within 60 minutes of the previous lane or lift, although the Representative may adjust this limit based on retarders or weather; fresh vertical joints shall be cut vertically through the full depth in sound compacted material at least 6 inches from the exposed edge, cleaned and moistened before placement, while control joints shall be saw‑cut to one‑quarter depth of the compacted RCC pavement no later than 18 hours after placement [2]. Concrete pavement patching (Section 516) requires all full‑depth saw cuts used to form joints to be cut to full depth and may not exceed ½ inch in width when opened to traffic; when a patch replaces an existing expansion joint the joint shall be filled with ¾‑inch expansion‑joint material (or ½‑inch for corner repairs); Type B coated dowel bars shall receive bond‑breaker lubricant on at least one‑half of their length before concrete placement unless already lubricated in the shop; longitudinal joints in Type A and B patches shall incorporate a ¼‑inch full‑depth polystyrene board bond breaker, with traffic restored only after the concrete attains a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi [2]. Unbonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 548) place joints according to the spacing shown on the project plans or, if none are provided, a layout approved by the Department and laid out before concrete placement while avoiding longitudinal joints in wheel paths; panel dimensions shall have an aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5 and a maximum panel size of 18 times the overlay thickness; saw cuts must be at least D/3 within 12 inches of the pavement edges and at least D/4 elsewhere, shall be clean of deleterious material, and sealing shall occur after cutting; curing cover may be removed for up to a half‑hour before sawing and joint faces must be protected from curing compound until sealant installation [2]. Bonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545) require joint backing or other approved filling material to be installed to within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface in all existing transverse joints before overlay placement, and joint faces shall be protected from curing compound or cleaned prior to sealant installation; transverse joints shall be saw‑cut directly over all existing transverse joints with a depth equal to the full thickness of the overlay plus ½ inch, width at least that of the underlying joint or crack and a minimum of ½ inch when inlaying, after which a sealant reservoir is constructed as shown on the Standard Drawing for Cement Concrete Pavement Joints; longitudinal joints require a sealant reservoir ¼ inch wide cut to the full depth of the overlay plus ½ inch; locations of all transverse joints shall be accurately marked with approved devices and the proposed marking method submitted for approval at least 20 days before construction, and paving may not begin without that approval [2]. Bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 540) require joint locations to follow the spacing shown on the project plans or, if none are provided, a department‑approved joint layout plan must be prepared before concrete placement; longitudinal joints shall be avoided in wheel paths and panel dimensions may not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness nor have a length‑to‑width ratio greater than 1.5; sawing is the only acceptable technique for creating joints, using a green‑cut saw to a depth of one‑third slab thickness (D/3) and a minimum width of 1⁄8 inch, with timing of the cut determined using FHWA’s HIPERPAV or equivalent software approved by the Department; after cutting all transverse and longitudinal joints shall be sealed with an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations as required by Section 501.3(n) [2]. Joint material specifications (Section 705) require premolded expansion joint filler, load‑transfer units for reinforced and plain cement concrete pavements to meet ASTM A513, AASHTO T 253 and PTM No. 642 limits, dowel‑bar coating shall be a fusion‑bonded epoxy Type B with a minimum non‑abraded thickness of eight mils, and rubberized joint sealing material shall be ASTM D6690 Type II or Type IV for asphalt pavement joints and only ASTM D6690‑Type IV for all other applications, with optional use of ASTM D6690‑Type I when sourced from a Bulletin 15 manufacturer [2]. Joint cleaning and sealing (Section 521) requires removal of all existing sealing material, cleaning to a minimum depth of 1½ inches, air‑blasting with a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi free of oil, moisture or other contaminants; a backer rod sized 25–35 % larger in diameter than the joint opening shall be placed so that its top lies one to two times the width of the joint plus ¼ inch below the pavement surface, and sealant may be placed only when the joint is clean and dry, ambient air and pavement temperatures are 40 °F or greater, and the reservoir is filled to a level 1⁄8‑¼ inch below the pavement surface [2]. Joint sealing material must be supplied by manufacturers listed in Bulletin 15, meet physical requirements such as tensile stress at 150 % elongation per ASTM D412, specific gravity per ASTM D792, shore A durometer per ASTM D2240 after a seven‑day cure at 77 °F ± 3 °F and 45–55 % relative humidity, ozone/UV resistance per C793, flow per C639‑Type II, and bond to cement mortar test per AASHTO T 132 with a minimum tensile strength [2]. Design methods and criteria

Joint maintenance in Portland cement concrete (PCC) pavement begins with resealing as soon as joints exhibit distress and proceeds to joint rehabilitation, which is performed as type 1 for saw‑cut joints and type 2 for metal‑plate joints to create new sealant reservoirs and reseal the joint in accordance with Publication 72M and Publication 408 Section 513 [1]. Longitudinal, transverse, and pavement/shoulder joints are cleaned and sealed following the relevant sections of Publication 408, using a sealant reservoir sized 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch for pavement/shoulder joints; crack sealing is carried out per Sections 590 and 1091, dowel bar retrofits install epoxy‑coated dowels across cracks or transverse joints as described in Section 527, and cross‑stitching places epoxy‑coated deformed tie bars at an angle across longitudinal cracks or joints as outlined in Publication 72M and Section 528 [1]. For partial‑depth repairs of joints the prescribed procedure follows the construction repair area preparation, concrete removal, partial‑depth repair, and curing steps set out in Publication 408 Specifications Section 525.3; the repair depth must not exceed half the slab thickness and the patch must be isolated from adjacent slabs by a rigid polystyrene board for longitudinal joints or premolded expansion joint material for transverse joints to re‑establish the original joint clearance [1]. PennDOT classifies these repairs as Type 1 spot repairs when the affected length is between 15 inches and 6 feet, and as Type 2 extended repairs when the length exceeds 6 feet, with material choice (Class AA Cement Concrete Modified, accelerated material, or rapid‑set concrete) based on available time before traffic reopening, ambient temperature, cost, size, depth, and location of the repair [1]. Joint‑related slab stabilization is performed by drilling holes in the concrete at transverse and patch joints and filling the resulting voids with a grout material selected from cement‑based grout, hot asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane foam; material quantities are estimated as 1 cubic foot of cement grout per hole (≈0.25 bag of cement), 30–50 gallons of hot asphalt per hole, or 0.4 lb of polyurethane per square foot of slab [1]. When no preliminary testing has been done, at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints shall be stabilized, and joint and crack sealing together with functional subsurface drains are required to limit water ingress [1]. The load‑transfer coefficient J is incorporated into the AASHTO Rigid Pavement Design Procedures as a factor that represents lateral support at slab corners; its magnitude is adjusted according to measured joint performance and pavement stiffness, with values chosen from the ranges in Table 8.2, using lower‑end values when deflection testing of transverse joints shows good to normal load‑transfer efficiency and higher‑end values when efficiencies are low, and higher J‑values are applied when the subgrade modulus (k) is lower or the truck traffic percentage is higher, while lower J‑values are selected when k is higher or the truck percentage is lower [1]. In slip‑form paving the procedure requires that load‑transfer units and dowel bars be secured at their designated locations before concrete placement, that those positions be marked so transverse contraction joints can be sawed directly over the centre of the dowels, that the transverse joints be cut to a depth equal to one‑third of slab thickness (D/3) and a width of 1⁄8 inch, and that an oversized backer rod be placed in the joint; each selected tie bar is then tested for pull‑out resistance after the concrete has attained a compressive strength of 3,500 psi by loading it with a hydraulic jack to either 12,000 pounds or until 1⁄32 inch slip occurs and the average result is compared to Table B, and all joints widened by second‑stage sawing are sealed before any pavement section is opened to equipment or traffic [2]. The design of expansion joints for slip‑form work uses standard values for the aggregate expansion coefficient found in the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide, transverse joint gaps are limited to a minimum of 1⁄4 inch and not more than 1⁄2 inch along their entire length, longitudinal joints may not exceed 3⁄4 inch (any narrower joint must be saw‑cut to meet the minimum), and load transfer across transverse joints follows the method prescribed for the accepted PCPS system or as indicated in the fabricator shop drawings [2]. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs the mix design must incorporate ambient average daily temperatures and exposure conditions so that panel size and expansion‑joint locations are selected using the standard expansion‑coefficient values from the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide; transverse joint gaps are limited to at least 1⁄4 inch and not more than 1⁄2 inch, longitudinal joints may not exceed 3⁄4 inch, and load transfer is provided by the prescribed dowel‑slot or embedded‑dowel methods as shown on the accepted shop drawings [2]. In roller‑compacted concrete pavements a fresh vertical or horizontal joint must be placed within 60 minutes of the preceding lane or lift unless the Representative adjusts that limit, a cold joint requires full‑depth cutting at least six inches from the exposed edge and immediate moistening of the joint face before placing the adjacent lane, control joints are saw‑cut to one‑quarter depth of the compacted pavement as soon as possible but no later than 18 hours after placement, sealing is performed in accordance with Section 501.3(n), and field density at joints is evaluated using the equation X = (ρw/ρt)×100 where ρw is the recorded wet density and ρt is the computed RCC unit weight [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements transverse expansion joints are first identified and marked, then two full‑depth cuts are sawed on each side of the precut joint following the underlying joint edges; the saw‑cut depth must be no less than D/3 within 12 inches of the pavement edge and no less than D/4 elsewhere, with a minimum width of 1⁄4 inch when using a green‑cut saw, panel dimensions are limited so that the longer dimension does not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the aspect ratio shall not exceed 1.5, joint spacing follows project plans or a department‑approved layout that avoids longitudinal joints in wheel paths, and FHWA’s HIPERPAV or similar approved software may be used to determine a project‑specific sawing window with early‑entry saws recommended [2]. In bonded concrete overlays joint backing or an approved filling material is installed within ±1⁄4 inch of the existing pavement surface, each transverse joint is accurately marked before placement, and after the overlay has cured sufficiently to cut without raveling the transverse joints are saw‑cut directly over all existing transverse joints to a depth equal to the full thickness of the overlay plus ½ inch while ensuring no damage to dowel load‑transfer devices in the original pavement; a sealant reservoir is then constructed according to the standard drawing before sealing the joint [2]. Joint efficiency testing requires field‑QC documentation before performing deflection testing of each transverse joint or crack in accordance with ASTM D4695 when air temperature is between forty and seventy degrees Fahrenheit; the loaded slab corner deflection is compared to the unloaded condition using the joint‑efficiency equation JE = (unloaded slab corner)/(loaded slab corner) × 100, joints that exhibit a loaded slab corner deflection of at least 0.02 inch and a joint efficiency of at least 65 % are stabilized, whereas joints with the same deflection but a joint efficiency below 65 % are patched subject to Representative approval, after grouting retesting is performed after twenty‑four hours and any slab still moving 0.02 inch or more may be re‑grouted or replaced in accordance with Section 516 [2]. Load‑transfer unit design for reinforced and plain cement concrete pavements relies on structural adequacy testing according to PTM No. 642, where Linear Variable Differential Transformer (LVDT) differential deflection under dynamic and static loading must not exceed 7.5 mils at one million cycles and may increase no more than 2.5 mils by ten million cycles; dowel bars must conform to dimensional tolerances of a diameter of either 1.5 inches or 1.25 inches with an allowable plus 0.25 inch, minus 0.001 inch and a length of eighteen inches, they must be provided with a fusion‑bonded epoxy Type B coating having a minimum non‑abraded thickness of eight mils and no more than two holidays per linear foot as detected with a 67.5‑volt holiday detector, and compliance with ASTM A513, AASHTO T 253 Section 5 and the bond‑breaking criteria of AASHTO M 254 (Type A or Type B) is required together with material specifications such as galvanizing per ASTM B695/B696 or stainless steel per ASTM A955/A955M [2]. Joint sealing material specifications are evaluated by standardized test procedures rather than analytical models; silicone joint sealants are assessed for tensile stress at 150 % elongation according to ASTM D412 (Die C), extrusion rate in grams per minute from zero to one hundred degrees Fahrenheit using an air‑powered caulking gun at ninety pounds per square inch, specific gravity by ASTM D792 Method A, durometer hardness on the Shore “A” scale per ASTM D2240 after a seven‑day cure at 77 °F ± 3 °F and 45–55 % relative humidity, ozone and ultraviolet resistance per ASTM C793, flow per ASTM C639‑Type II, bond strength to cement mortar per AASHTO T132 with a minimum value, tack‑free time not exceeding the limit of ASTM C679, and movement capability and adhesion per ASTM C719; rubberized joint sealants are evaluated by ASTM D6690 (Types I, II, IV), neoprene compression seals and strip seals must meet the dimensional and movement‑range criteria of ASTM D2628 and D3542, lubricant adhesive must conform to ASTM D4070, preformed closed‑cell polyethylene filler is tested for compressive strength using ASTM D7174 with a minimum load to achieve 25 % compression of at least five pounds per square inch and a maximum load for 85 % compression not greater than thirty pounds per square inch, and polyurethane foam fillers are characterized by density, compressive deflection, pressure to compress 25 % and 65 %, recovery percentage and water absorption as defined in ASTM D7174; acceptance of each lot requires an eCAMMS‑generated laboratory report that states approval or rejection [2].

Rubberized sealants shall conform to ASTM D6690—Type II or Type IV for asphalt pavement joints and exclusively Type IV for all other joint applications; neoprene seals shall satisfy ASTM D2628 and D3542 movement‑range criteria, function from –20 °F to +140 °F, maintain dimensional tolerances of misalignment not greater than 1⁄8 inch, and provide at least 4 psi side‑wall bearing pressure at 85 % nominal width; the accompanying lubricant adhesive may not be used more than nine months after manufacture. Closed‑cell polyethylene fillers shall meet ASTM D7174 Type I compressive strength limits of a minimum 5 psi load to achieve 25 % compression and a maximum 30 psi load for 85 % compression, whereas polyurethane foam fillers shall be supplied in lengths ≥8 ft with widths between 4 7⁄16 in and 4 3⁄4 in and depths of 7¾‑13 in, possess a density of 6.0–10.0 lb/in³, meet the specified compressive loads for 25 % and 65 % deflection, recover at least 95 % after 60 seconds, and absorb no more than 30 % water by volume; asphalt‑rubber sealing compound shall comply with ASTM D5078. All seal or filler lots must be accompanied by an eCAMMS laboratory report confirming acceptance per Section 106.03(b)3 and must be marked or labeled as required. Rigid‑pavement joints shall use premolded expansion‑joint filler with minimum thicknesses of at least ½ inch for full depth around manholes, inlets, valve boxes or similar appurtenances, at least ¾ inch when the pavement adjoins other rigid structures such as bridges or culverts, and a minimum ¼‑inch thick filler along each mortar‑filled rail when constructing alongside railway tracks. For transverse joints in reinforced or plain cement concrete pavement the filler shall be provided in lengths equal to the traffic lane width with a minimum thickness of 1 inch, have a smooth top surface and include punched holes for dowel bars. In slab‑stabilization grout applications the specification must identify the dry grout constituents, require that they be measured by mass (or supplied in uniform‑volume sacks), add water based on metered or weighed daily consumption, limit time in the mixer or injection pump sump to one hour after mixing, and require flowability measurements at least twice each work shift; grout injection holes shall not exceed 1½ inch in diameter, injection pressure shall not exceed 200 psi unless otherwise directed, and upward pavement movement during injection may not be greater than 0.05 inch. Joint sealing material may only be placed when ambient or pavement surface temperature is not below 40 °F; the seal must be filled to a level of ⅛‑inch to ¼‑inch below the surface without spilling onto the slab. In concrete pavement partial‑depth repair the joint treatment is chosen based on the time available before opening to traffic, the expected ambient temperature, cost considerations, and the size, depth and location of the repair; regular patches are specified to use Class AA Cement Concrete Modified, whereas accelerated work requires an accelerated material and rapid‑set mixes may be employed only when the quantity makes delivery of conventional concrete uneconomical [1]. The repair depth is limited to no more than one‑half of the slab thickness, with Type 1 repairs applied to joints or spalls that are 15 inches to 6 feet long and Type 2 repairs used for lengths greater than 6 feet [1]. To preserve structural integrity and durability a rigid polystyrene board (for longitudinal joints) or a premolded expansion‑joint material (for transverse joints) having the same width as the existing joint is placed between the patch and the adjacent slab so that the new concrete does not contact the surrounding slabs [1]. For slab stabilization the joint material may be cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane; a cement grout consists of cement, water, supplementary cementitious material and possibly admixtures with an estimated consumption of one cubic foot of grout (equivalent to 0.25 bag of cement) per hole, hot‑asphalt undersealing typically requires between 30 and 50 gallons per hole, and the polyurethane system uses an estimate of 0.4 pounds per square foot of slab to form a lightweight rigid foam, with grout cure time about 12 hours and hot asphalt or polyurethane requiring a minimum of 30 minutes before traffic may be reopened [1].

For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement the joint material selection begins with a concrete mix design submitted at least 28 days before production that achieves a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a minimum flexural strength of 650 psi, incorporates the thermal‑expansion coefficient values from the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide, and is cast with a sacrificial thickness of 1/4 inch above the nominal design thickness; transverse joint gaps are limited to a maximum of 1/2 inch but not less than 1/4 inch and longitudinal joints may not exceed 3/4 inch, while dowel‑bar and bedding grouts must reach at least 2,500 psi compressive strength before traffic is allowed [2]. For partial‑depth concrete repairs the mix shall be produced with volumetric mixers as called out in Section 704.3 or hand‑batched using a power‑driven revolving drum or paddle mixer that can thoroughly blend the material, and any batch size used with drum or paddle mixers must remain within the mixer’s recommended minimum and maximum limits; when the repair meets an existing transverse joint the joint is to be filled with 1/2‑inch expansion joint material and when it abuts a longitudinal joint a 1/4‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene filler, both extending at least 1/2 inch below the bottom of the repair whenever possible [2]. Joint stabilization for slabs requires that the cement grout mixture be proportioned by accurately weighing all dry ingredients and batching water to the total daily consumption, mixed immediately, not retained in the mixer or injection pump sump for more than one hour, with flowability measured at least twice each shift; holes for injection are limited to a maximum diameter of 1 ½ inch and must be drilled vertical to a depth that penetrates any stabilized base, grout pressure may not exceed 200 psi, and upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited [2].

In an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course the sealant shall be hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement heated and maintained between 265 °F and 320 °F, shall not be placed when air temperature is below 40 °F unless the Representative allows it, and shall be applied to clean, dry joints using a pressure applicator that produces a straight band four inches wide plus or minus one inch and a thickness of one‑sixteenth inch plus or minus one‑thirty‑second inch centered within one inch of the joint [2]. Joint filler material must be selected from an approved manufacturer list and be one of the types—cork, sponge rubber, fiber joint filler, or polypropylene foam—that conforms to the applicable test standards (AASHTO M 153 for cork or preformed rubber, AASHTO M 213 for fiber, ASTM D8139 for closed‑cell polypropylene foam); transverse joints in reinforced or plain cement concrete pavement require filler pieces with a length equal to the traffic lane width and a minimum thickness of 1 inch with a smooth top surface and punched holes for dowel bars, while longitudinal joints in concrete base courses require pieces at least 8 feet long joined securely [2]. For pervious asphalt pavement joints the mix is acceptable only when the percent of coated aggregate particles is not less than 95 %, the percent passing the No. 200 sieve does not exceed 4 %, infiltration performance meets a minimum average rate of 200 inches per hour, surface irregularities greater than 3/8‑inch are corrected, and any depth deficiency of 1/2‑inch or more is removed and replaced [2]. Joint sealant material must be obtained from the approved lists in Bulletin 15 or Bulletin 41, heated only within the safe heating range and manufacturer‑specified pouring temperature shown on the container label, not maintained at that temperature for more than 6 hours, and reheated according to the manufacturer’s guidance; before sealing cracks and joints are cleaned, dried and any loose material removed, a reservoir may be routed about ½ inch deep when required, and the surrounding pavement is cleared 4–6 inches on each side with a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi or a hot air lance, while sealant placement is prohibited when ambient temperature is below 40 °F or above 90 °F unless written departmental approval is obtained; joints from ¼ inch to 1 inch wide are filled with asphalt sealant applied level with the surface and wiped to leave a thin film roughly 1/32–1/16 inch thick, and openings wider than 1 inch are filled with an Asphalt Wearing Course of 4.75 mm [2].

Long‑life concrete pavement joints shall use plant‑mixed concrete unless the Representative approves otherwise, and the mix is controlled by daily sieve analysis of the stockpiled aggregate to verify that the combined percent retained gradation remains within the limits defined in PTM No. 529; if the gradation deviates, aggregate proportions are adjusted on‑site, but any adjustment that also changes cementitious material or water content requires a new mix design and trial batches, each truckload is tested for plastic air content and only trucks meeting 7.0 % ± 1.5 % may be incorporated, and a concrete temperature control plan is required to keep placement temperatures below 80 °F [2].

Pervious concrete pavement systems joint and mixture selection follows the approved pervious‑concrete mix design verified by two test sections; those sections must achieve a void structure of 20 % ± 5 %, an average hardened density not to exceed 130 lb/ft³ with a tolerance of ± 5 lb/ft³, and an infiltration rate whose average exceeds 200 in/hr, while slab thickness for joints may be no more than ¼‑inch less than the design thickness and construction joints are cut or rolled to approximately one‑quarter of the slab thickness after final finishing [2].

Plain or reinforced cement concrete pavement joint material selection follows the specific items listed in Section 501.2; the bond‑breaking lubricant shall have a flash point of not less than 149 °F and may not be graphite based, adhesive anchoring material must be approved per Bulletin 15 and produce an average unbonded area of ≤10 % for three test assemblies, and load‑transfer units, dowel bars with shop‑applied bondbreaker, and premolded expansion joint fillers are to be used only where indicated or directed [2].

For unbonded concrete overlays the separation layer must meet structural and durability limits: a geotextile interlayer is placed after milling joints that show greater than ¼‑inch faulting, laid flat with edges overlapped 8 inches ± 2 inches, limited to three layers, extended at least 4 inches beyond the pavement edge and kept with fabric surface temperature ≤120 °F; alternatively a nominal depth of 1 inch of the Superpave mix design specified in Section 410.3 may be used as an asphalt interlayer, and if joint faulting exceeds ½‑inch a scratch course or milling must be performed before placement, with surface temperature also not exceeding 120 °F and controlled by misting or approved cooling methods [2].

Structural details and interfaces

All concrete pavement projects require the provision of both transverse and longitudinal joints, with transverse contraction joints cut across the full width so that load‑transfer devices are centered in the joint and longitudinal joints provided where slab lengths exceed standard limits [1][2]. For jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) the design calls for 30‑foot joint spacing while jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) uses 15‑foot spacing, both with perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals; reinforcement is specified for JRCP but does not add additional strength [1]. Load transfer at transverse joints must be achieved by dowelling every patch joint and securely anchoring the dowels to the existing pavement with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders, ensuring full‑length bond of the dowels, and when load‑transfer efficiency is deficient a dowel‑bar retrofit installs six epoxy‑coated smooth dowels across each wheel path [1]. In slip‑form construction the transverse joints are cut full width and LTUs (dowel bars or other units) lie at the joint centre; LTUs must be secured before concrete placement, with allowable vertical tilt or horizontal skew of 0.25 inch per end and longitudinal translation not to exceed 1 inch, and Type B coated dowels require bond‑breaker lubricant on at least half their length [2]. Longitudinal joints use tie bars; for non‑standard slab lengths no tie bar may be within 24 inches of a dowelled contraction joint and additional tie bars must not be installed within 30 inches of any transverse joint, each tie bar having to pass a pull‑out test of 12,000 lb or 1/32 inch slippage before construction proceeds [2]. When full‑depth precast or prestressed slabs are placed, expansion‑joint locations are derived from thermal‑expansion calculations and longitudinal joints must match shop drawings as well as existing transverse joints; dowel bars and tie bars are cast in the slab with bond‑breaker coating on the dowels, foam gaskets or slab‑setting bolt hardware may be installed, and tie bars are required for continuous sections longer than 65 feet; joint geometry is tightly controlled with transverse gaps not exceeding ½ inch and not less than ¼ inch, longitudinal joint widths not exceeding ¾ inch, and all joints sealed [2]. In roller‑compacted concrete pavements fresh vertical joints between lanes and fresh horizontal joints between lifts must be placed within 60 minutes of the preceding lane or lift, with moist surfaces maintained for horizontal joints; cold joints are prepared by cutting at least 6 inches from the exposed edge, cleaning and immediately moistening the face, and control joints are saw‑cut to one‑quarter of the compacted RCC thickness no later than 18 hours after placement, any joints shown on the plans being sealed as specified, with no reinforcement or additional load‑transfer systems prescribed beyond these joint treatments [2]. Concrete pavement patching requires full‑depth longitudinal and transverse saw cuts to define the patch joint; transverse cuts may extend up to full depth plus 2 inches into adjacent pavement provided dowel bars are not damaged, a ¾‑inch expansion‑joint filler is placed when an existing joint is replaced, Type B coated dowels receive bond‑breaker lubricant on at least half their length one hour before placement, anchoring material must set within five minutes and exhibit voids less than 0.50 inch around the perimeter, load‑transfer units are provided adjacent to existing joints as required, longitudinal joints of Type A and B patches receive a ¼‑inch full‑depth polystyrene board bond breaker (none for Type C which uses tie bars), and all constructed joints are sealed in accordance with Section 501.3(n) [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays existing transverse expansion joints must be identified, marked and precut in the plastic concrete; full‑depth saw cuts are then made on each side of the precut joint with a minimum depth of D/3 within 12 inches of the pavement edge and at least D/4 elsewhere, a minimum width of ¼ inch, and both transverse and longitudinal joints are sealed; joint spacing follows the project plans or an approved layout that avoids placing longitudinal joints in wheel paths, panel dimensions shall not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness with an aspect‑ratio not greater than 1.5, transitions at the overlay limits are required, and where a transition overlies existing treated base or concrete pavement a geotextile debonding layer is placed as specified [2]. Bonded concrete overlays require that all existing transverse joints receive joint backing or approved filling material placed within ±¼ inch of the surface and secured against displacement, after which the joints are saw‑cut to the full overlay thickness plus ½ inch with a width at least as wide as the underlying joint; intermediate transverse joints are required whenever the overlay panel length would exceed 15 feet and are cut to the same depth, existing longitudinal joints are located and a ¼‑inch sealant reservoir is sawed to the full depth of the overlay plus ½ inch and sealed, any exposed reinforcement or loose steel plates in the existing pavement are removed before placement, and paving notches are constructed at overlay transitions [2]. In bonded overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements joints are laid out according to the project plans while avoiding longitudinal joints in wheel paths; panel dimensions follow the same limits as other overlays, transverse joints are cut with a green‑cut saw to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness and a minimum width of 1/8 inch, and transitions are constructed at the overlay limits as shown on the plans [2]. Dowel retrofits create joints by cutting three parallel slots (six saw cuts) per wheel path at mid‑depth of the slab aligned with the existing crack or joint; slot surfaces are cleaned within 24 hours and final cleaning occurs no more than 30 minutes before concrete placement, a ¼‑inch preformed cellular polystyrene piece is cut to fit each slot with an opening for the dowel, bond‑breaker lubricant is applied to Type B coated dowels at least one hour before concreting, each dowel is seated on a plastic or non‑metallic chair leaving a minimum ½‑inch clearance to all slot faces, dowel ends receive 1½‑inch non‑metallic expansion caps with a ¼‑inch gap, and placement tolerances require vertical tilt or horizontal skew not to exceed 0.25 inch per end and longitudinal translation not to exceed 1 inch from the intended location [2]. Joint rehabilitation consists of constructing a sealant reservoir and sealing transverse contraction, construction, or expansion joints; no additional reinforcement, load‑transfer systems, transitions, or connections are specified for this work [2]. In plain cement concrete base courses the transverse joint provisions of Section 501.3(i) are retained, longitudinal joints formed for construction convenience are tied joints as shown on standard drawings, full‑width sections do not require sawing, reinforcement references are removed so no reinforcement is required, and sealing of joints and cracks is omitted [2]. Slip‑form concrete shoulders require both transverse and longitudinal contraction joints to be sawed, cured and protected as called for in Sections 501.3(i) and (j); the centre of each transverse joint shall be accurately marked with tacks or other approved methods, and sawing equipment must include guides, a blade guard, water‑cooling system and cut‑depth control, with adequate extra equipment and parts provided on site before concrete placement and during sawing operations [2]. Long‑life concrete pavement designs incorporate load‑transfer units anchored to the foundation as defined in a written quality‑control plan that specifies type, number and installation locations of fasteners; alignment of each LTU is measured before concrete placement and verified within 18 hours after finishing for projects exceeding 700 square yards using a nondestructive method on at least the first five transverse joint locations across the full width and one joint per 2,640 linear feet of pavement placed each day, with an action plan for any dowel bars that do not meet Section 501.3(i) and prescribed curing and protection to prevent moisture loss during and after joint sawing [2]. Joint material specifications require premolded expansion‑joint filler not less than ½ inch thick for the full pavement depth around the outer sides of structures, not less than ¾ inch thick adjacent to structures, and a minimum ¼ inch thick filler along each mortar‑filled rail; load‑transfer units such as dowel bars are treated as LTUs, joint sealing material follows Section 705.4, and bond‑breaker lubricant shall have a flash point of at least 149°F and shall not be graphite based [2]. Joint backing material may consist of expanded low‑density polyethylene foam supplied by a Bulletin 15‑listed manufacturer, conforming to ASTM D5249, and can serve as a bond breaker between the joint filler material and the joint sealer [2]. Asphalt joint and crack sealing requires joints to be cleaned, dried and cleared of debris for a minimum of 4 inches to 6 inches on each side, routing to a depth of ½ inch to form a reservoir, sealant application only when air temperature is between 40°F and 90°F, and the sealant film being wiped flush leaving a thickness of 1/32‑inch to 1/16‑inch and a width of 1 inch to 3 inches [2]. Pavement relief joints have joint faces painted with a thin coating of PG 64S‑22 before fresh asphalt placement, a transverse saw‑cut made directly above the interface within ±¼ inch forming a reservoir 1 inch deep by ½ inch wide, sealing performed after cleaning and drying when temperature is between 40°F and 90°F, a tape bond breaker placed on the bottom of the reservoir not extending more than 1/8 inch up the face, and the sealant filled to a level 1/8‑¼ inch below the pavement surface [2]. In pervious asphalt pavement systems longitudinal joints must overlap the edge of the previously placed lane by 1 inch to 2 inches while maintaining a uniform uncompacted depth adjacent to the compacted lane, and transverse construction joints are required at the end of each workday or after any interruption longer than 30 minutes by installing a temporary vertical bulkhead that forms a straight full‑depth and full‑width joint, or alternatively by sawing a full‑depth transverse joint before paving resumes; asphalt‑treated permeable base courses follow the same longitudinal‑joint overlap and transverse‑construction‑joint requirements [2].

Joint design for concrete pavement interfaces must provide load transfer by dowelling every patch joint and fully bonding the dowels along their embedded length, using either skewed or non‑skewed new‑pavement joints as required. When all adjacent lanes are reconstructed with jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) the transverse joints shall be spaced 30 feet apart, placed perpendicular to traffic, and equipped with coated dowel bars and appropriate seals; for jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) the spacing is reduced to 15 feet while retaining the same requirements. If only a single lane is reconstructed, its new joints must align with existing joints and an intermediate joint shall be placed whenever the existing joint spacing exceeds 20 feet, and tie bars or tie bolts are required only for patches longer than 65 feet. In plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements the joint layout is required to align new transverse and longitudinal joints with load‑transfer units and any adjacent concrete elements; dowel bars must be secured before paving, the sawed contraction joint placed directly over the centre of each dowel, and a transverse joint may not be constructed within 10 feet of another transverse joint. When additional concrete pavement, shoulders or structures are built next to an existing slab, the dominant joints are marked within 48 hours of activation and the first cuts in the new work are aligned with those marks, and the ends of all transverse joints and cracks are sealed to keep cement mortar from entering them. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs the mix design shall account for site temperature and exposure so that panel size and expansion‑joint locations are appropriate; transverse joints must be kept between a minimum of 1/4 inch and not exceed 1/2 inch in width while longitudinal joints must not exceed 3/4 inch, and vertical differentials across any joint may not exceed 1/4 inch (allowing up to 1/2 inch only before grinding). Load transfer at transverse joints of precast slabs is provided by the specified dowel‑bar or tie‑bar systems shown on the shop drawings, with bond‑breaker applied to dowels and grout ports installed for dowel and bedding grouts, and all such joints are sealed as required. In roller‑compacted concrete pavements an interface placed within 60 minutes of the preceding lane or lift is treated as a fresh joint that must retain a moist surface and have any loose material removed before the next placement; if the interval exceeds 60 minutes the interface becomes a cold joint that must be cut through the full depth at least 6 inches from the edge, cleaned of debris, and the face moistened immediately prior to placing the adjacent lane or lift. Control joints in concrete pavements are saw‑cut to one‑quarter the pavement thickness behind the rollers as soon as practicable but no later than 18 hours after placement, and when the plans call for it the joints shall be sealed in accordance with Section 501.3(n). For partial‑depth repairs the joint interface must be reconstituted by placing a rigid polystyrene board for longitudinal joints or a premolded expansion‑joint material for transverse joints, each sized equal in width to the existing joint, between the new patch and the adjoining slab; the patch shall not touch the adjacent slabs so that these intervening materials provide sufficient space for slab movement and eliminate compression or point‑loading failures. In concrete pavement patching the interface between new pavement and existing layers is defined by full‑depth longitudinal and transverse saw cuts placed before any other joint work; where an existing longitudinal joint is present a full‑depth cut shall be made along its length and a 1/4 inch polystyrene board bond breaker is required in the longitudinal joint of Type A and B patches but not in Type C patches, and expansion joints at the patch edge are restored with 3/4‑inch expansion‑joint material adjacent to an existing expansion joint or with 1/2‑inch material for corner repairs. Dowel bars installed in patches are placed in holes drilled a maximum of 1/8 inch larger than the bar diameter, lubricated on at least one‑half of their length and anchored with a material that sets within five minutes; load‑transfer units are provided adjacent to existing joints as indicated on the Standard Drawings, and all longitudinal and transverse joints created by the work shall be sealed in accordance with Section 501.3(n). At the pavement‑shoulder interface a sealant reservoir measuring 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch is constructed and filled with rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material, and transverse joints must have sealant reservoirs that satisfy the shape‑factor requirements given in Publication 408 Section 521; longitudinal joints between traffic lanes and shoulders are likewise cleaned and sealed according to the applicable specifications. Joint rehabilitation creates new sealant reservoirs before resealing when minor distress is observed, and joint and crack sealing must be performed in conjunction with slab stabilization to limit water entry; slab stabilization fills voids at the slab/subbase interface with approved grout material such as cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane, and if no preliminary testing has been performed at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized. For unbonded concrete overlays the interface joints are built exactly as they would be in a new concrete pavement, with joint spacing chosen according to the type and thickness of the concrete being placed and without attempting to line up the overlay’s transverse joints with those of the existing slab. In bonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements joint backing or other approved filling material shall be installed to within ±1/4 inch of the pavement surface in all existing transverse joints before placing the overlay, and it must be ensured that the joint backing cannot be dislodged by the paving operation; after the concrete reaches sufficient strength the transverse joints are saw‑cut to a depth equal to the full thickness of the overlay plus 1/2 inch, with cut width at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack, a sealant reservoir is then formed and the joint sealed per Section 501.3(n). Longitudinal joints in bonded concrete overlays are similarly located and after paving are saw‑cut to create a 1/4‑inch‑wide sealant reservoir that extends the full overlay depth plus 1/2 inch, while edge slump adjacent to shoulders may not exceed 1/4 inch and next to an adjacent lane may not exceed 1/8 inch. In bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavement the joint layout shall follow the project plan; if none is provided a department‑approved layout must be prepared before placement, longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths, panel sizes are limited to no more than 18 times the overlay thickness and an aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5, and after sawing all transverse and longitudinal joints are sealed using an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. In unbonded concrete overlay applications transverse expansion joints are first identified and precut in the plastic concrete, then two full‑depth saw cuts are made on each side of the underlying joint with a depth of at least D/3 within 12 inches of the slab edge and at least D/4 elsewhere; the intervening concrete is removed and all transverse and longitudinal joints are sealed, and panel dimensions are chosen so that the longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio is as close to 1.0 as feasible but does not exceed 1.5, with no panel exceeding 18 times the overlay thickness. In Superpave mixture design longitudinal joints should be offset about six inches from the joint in the layer below, with top‑layer joints placed near the pavement centreline on two‑lane roads or within twelve inches of lane lines on wider roadways and avoided beneath planned markings; prior to placing adjoining lanes the joint face must be painted with a uniform coating of the specified PG‑Binder (or PG 64S‑22), and after compaction the joints in surface courses are sealed with hot PG 64S‑22 binder applied at 265 °F–320 °F forming a straight band four inches wide (+/‑ one inch) and about one‑sixteenth inch thick (+/‑ one‑thirty‑second inch). In partial‑depth repair the interface must be cleaned first and then all exposed surfaces within the repair area should receive a thin coat of grout or epoxy bonding compound; concrete is placed before the grout dries or while the epoxy remains tacky, and expansion joint material together with preformed cellular polystyrene temporary forms that extend to the pavement surface are used to keep the new concrete out of existing joints. In pervious concrete pavements joints shall be placed at locations called for in the plans and formed either by tooling into the fresh concrete immediately after final finishing to a depth of approximately one‑quarter of the slab thickness with a cross‑rolling tool, or by saw cutting once the concrete has reached sufficient compressive strength; when joints are saw cut all fines generated must be removed by vacuum to keep the voids open. In pressure‑relief joints a full‑depth, full‑width opening four inches wide is created across the pavement, existing concrete within the joint is removed, vertical faces are cleaned of contaminants, and the joint sides are coated with a sealant or lubricant adhesive in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. Concrete shoulders require transverse and longitudinal contraction joints to be sawed, cured and protected; the centre of each transverse contraction joint must be accurately marked using tacks or other approved methods, and cuts are to be made with equipment equipped with guides, a blade guard, water‑cooling system and cut‑depth control. In slab stabilization when loss of support occurs at the slab/subbase interface, voids are filled with an approved grout material such as cement grout, hot asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane, and joint and crack sealing are performed concurrently to limit water entry; subsurface drains should be kept in good condition. Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Joint design must ensure that the existing drainage system remains functional and that water, chlorides, and incompressible materials cannot infiltrate the pavement structure. For concrete pavements that are to be patched rather than overlaid, all joints and cracks should be cleaned and sealed with the rubberized joint sealing material specified in Publication 408, and a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir is constructed for pavement/shoulder joints; transverse, longitudinal, and shoulder joints are likewise required to be cleaned and sealed during every CPR project in accordance with the shape‑factor requirements of Section 521 to maintain load transfer and protect against moisture effects. Joints must be sealed together with crack sealing to limit water entry that can cause pumping and loss of support, and existing subsurface drains shall be kept in good condition; when no preliminary testing has been performed, at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized, using cement grout, hot asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane as specified, thereby restoring full support beneath the slab while reducing moisture‑related deflections. In slip‑form concrete pavements each joint shall be completely dry and free of contaminants before any bond‑breaker or sealant is applied; the pavement sides and surface must be protected from rain until the concrete has set, and the pavement surface and sides shall be maintained at a temperature not less than 40 °F with high‑low thermometers used to monitor curing temperatures, an additional day added to the cure period when readings are between 40 °F and 35 °F, and any reading below 35 °F rendering the concrete defective; if rain threatens during placement, paving shall be halted and protective material placed over the sides and surface before normal curing and joint sealing resume. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs the mix design shall account for ambient average daily temperatures and exposure conditions so that panel size and expansion‑joint locations accommodate climate‑induced movement; transverse joint gaps shall not exceed 1/2 inch and shall be at least 1/4 inch, longitudinal joints shall not exceed 3/4 inch, all joints shall be sealed as specified in Section 501.3(n), foam gaskets may be placed on slab undersides to prevent grout leakage, and blockouts shall be provided for existing utility castings and proposed drainage structures so that joints do not interfere with drainage. In roller‑compacted concrete pavements fresh and cold horizontal joints shall be kept continuously moist to prevent excess moisture loss and, when indicated, sealed according to Section 501.3(n); placement is prohibited if the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·hr unless mitigation such as windbreaks or fog misting is employed, cold‑weather placement shall stop when the air temperature away from artificial heat falls to 40 °F, and hot‑weather placement shall be halted if the RCC mixture reaches 90 °F, with monitoring required once ambient temperatures rise above 85 °F. For concrete pavement patching an outlet shall be excavated through the shoulder at the lowest point of the patch when rain occurs while the patch is open to provide drainage; all longitudinal and transverse joints created as part of the work, as well as any saw‑cuts extending beyond the patch limits, shall be sealed, and cooling water, slurry, and dust from sawing shall not be allowed to enter any lane opened to traffic. In unbonded concrete overlays after sawing all transverse and longitudinal joints shall be sealed and cleaned of deleterious material; joint faces shall be protected from curing compound or thoroughly cleaned before sealant installation, and curing compound shall be applied at a minimum rate of 1 gal ± 0.1 gal per 150 ft² for pavements over 6 in thick and per 125 ft² for pavements 6 in or less; when ambient temperature is 40 °F or less, insulating blankets shall be placed to prevent rapid heat loss and may be removed only after the surface temperature exceeds 40 °F and the rate of temperature change does not exceed 40 °F in any one‑hour period. For bonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements standing water on the prepared surface shall be drained or removed by Department‑approved measures before overlay placement, joint backing or other approved filling material shall be installed within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface in all existing transverse joints before overlay, and joint faces shall be protected from curing compound until sealant installation. For bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements the surface must be fully drained and dried; any standing water after milling shall be removed immediately, insulating blankets shall be used when forecast air temperature is 45 °F or lower and removed so that concrete surface temperature does not change more than 40 °F in any one‑hour period, and after curing joint faces shall be kept free of curing compound before sealing all transverse and longitudinal joints with an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant. Under the long‑life concrete pavement specifications a concrete temperature control plan is required when temperatures exceed 80 °F at placement, and the quality‑control plan must specify how joints will be cured and protected during the curing period to prevent moisture loss from concrete in and adjacent to joints both during and after joint sawing. In pervious concrete pavement systems construction joints are placed at the locations specified and are cut to approximately one‑quarter slab thickness either with a pervious concrete cross‑rolling tool immediately after final finishing or by saw cutting once the concrete has achieved sufficient compressive strength so that aggregate is not dislodged; when saw‑cut, fines produced from the operation must be removed by vacuum to avoid clogging and preserve drainage; placement of the pervious concrete is prohibited when air temperatures are projected to fall below 40 °F or when rain is forecast before final finishing and curing; if a rainfall event occurs production must be stopped, a construction joint formed, and polyethylene sheeting applied until curing can resume, thereby protecting the pavement from moisture and climate effects. In unbonded concrete overlays that incorporate a geotextile interlayer joints receiving the interlayer are milled when faulting exceeds one‑quarter inch; the fabric is placed flat with edges overlapped 8 in ± 2 in and extended at least 4 in beyond the pavement edge, limiting any location to no more than three layers of geotextile; the interlayer is terminated in a drainable layer or at a daylighted location so that free drainage is not impaired, the fabric is dampened but not saturated before overlay placement, and the surface temperature of the geotextile (or asphalt interlayer) must not exceed 120 °F prior to placing the concrete overlay. Across all asphalt joint work joint faces must be thoroughly clean and dry before sealant placement; compressed‑air used for cleaning shall be free of oil, moisture or other contaminants and measured at least 100 psi at the source; sealant may not be placed when ambient air temperature or pavement surface temperature is below 40 °F (or above 90 °F unless otherwise allowed), hot asphalt cement shall be kept between 265 °F and 320 °F, silicone and rubberized seals shall function between –20 °F and +140 °F, exhibit ozone and ultraviolet resistance, polyurethane foam filler shall have a maximum water absorption of 30 % by volume, and all sealants must retain bond strength after exposure to humidity levels of 45 % to 55 % relative humidity as defined for tensile testing. For longitudinal joints sealed with hot asphalt binder the surface must be clean, dry and free of loose material before sealing; sealant may only be placed when air temperature is at least 40 °F unless the Representative permits otherwise, the binder shall be heated to between 265 °F and 320 °F and applied in a continuous band 4 inches wide (±1 inch) with a thickness of 1/16 inch (±1/32 inch), centered within 1 inch of the joint, thereby providing moisture exclusion and respecting climate temperature limits. When a wearing course abuts existing pavement the joint shall be sealed with hot asphalt material of the class and type designated for the wearing course; the sealant is to be applied evenly a minimum of six inches on each side so that residual asphalt completely fills surface voids and provides a watertight joint, any excess asphalt is removed and the sealed area is immediately covered with a light layer of dry sand acceptable to the Representative. Joint design for concrete and asphalt pavements must observe distinct geometric spacing rules that differ by pavement type; reinforced concrete‑pavement reconstruction requires joints spaced at thirty‑foot intervals while plain sections require fifteen‑foot intervals, all joints being perpendicular, incorporating coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, and when only one lane is rebuilt the new joints shall align with existing joints and an intermediate joint shall be added if the existing spacing exceeds twenty feet, with longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts required only for patches longer than sixty‑five feet [1]. For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements the longitudinal joints must be kept out of the travel lane, run parallel to and within twelve inches of final pavement markings unless the construction drawings specify otherwise, while transverse joints are required to be cut perpendicular to the centreline so that they extend across the full pavement width and no transverse joint may be placed within ten feet of another transverse joint [2]. Full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slab joints are limited to a maximum width of one‑half inch for transverse joints, with a minimum continuous length of one‑quarter inch along their entire extent, longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters inch in width, and the finished vertical differential across any joint shall not exceed one‑quarter inch; if a temporary vertical drop‑off greater than two inches occurs a temporary asphalt wedge transition must be provided, and when slabs are opened to traffic before grout placement at least two incompressible shims shall be installed in each transverse joint to prevent slab drift [2]. Unbonded concrete overlays require panel dimensions whose longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio be as close to one point zero as feasible and never exceed one point five, with overall panel size limited to no more than eighteen times the overlay thickness; bonded concrete overlays adopt the same aspect‑ratio limit and also restrict any panel from exceeding fifteen feet by allowing intermediate transverse joints, while all longitudinal joints must be kept out of wheel paths [2]. Saw‑cut joint depth for unbonded overlays shall be at least one‑third of slab thickness within twelve inches of each edge and at least one‑fourth of the thickness elsewhere, and all transverse and longitudinal joints shall be sealed after cleaning [2]. Concrete pavement patching work specifies that full‑depth saw cuts shall not be wider than one‑half inch, longitudinal joint cuts are limited to a quarter‑inch width and one inch depth centred over the existing joint, any parallel full‑depth cut must be placed no more than one foot from an existing longitudinal joint, and Type A and B patches shall contain a one‑quarter‑inch polystyrene board bond breaker in the longitudinal joint whereas Type C patches shall not provide a bond breaker; all joints created as part of this work must be sealed, traffic safety shall be protected by preventing drop‑off conditions, cooling water, slurry and dust from entering open lanes during sawing, saw cuts greater than one‑half inch width shall not be opened to traffic until the concrete attains at least one thousand psi compressive strength, and the finished joint surface must meet a geometric tolerance of no more than three‑sixteenths inch deviation measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge, any high points or depressions beyond this limit must be ground and retested before opening to traffic [2]. Partial‑depth repairs of joints shall keep repair depth no greater than half the slab thickness; spot repairs (Type 1) are used for lengths between fifteen inches and six feet while extended repairs (Type 2) apply to lengths greater than six feet, and a rigid polystyrene board or premolded expansion‑joint material equal in width to the existing joint shall be installed between the patch and the adjacent slab so that the patch does not contact adjoining slabs and sufficient space is provided for slab movement [1]. Joint maintenance on all concrete pavement reconstruction projects must include cleaning and sealing of longitudinal, transverse, and pavement‑shoulder joints; the pavement/shoulder joint requires a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir sealed with rubberized or asphalt material, while transverse joints need a sealant reservoir that meets the shape‑factor specified in Section 521, these dimensional specifications constitute the geometric requirements for joints [1]. Longitudinal joint cleaning and sealing shall follow the geometry shown on the Standard Drawing, with contraction joints formed or sawed to those dimensions; at structural interfaces and at the end of each workday a three‑quarter inch premolded expansion‑joint material, cut to match the gutter’s cross‑section, shall be installed, all original saw‑cut joints cleaned to a minimum depth of one‑and‑a‑half inches and formed construction joints to at least three‑quarters inch, water‑blasting equipment must have a capacity of ten gallons per minute at seven thousand pounds per square inch, and joints shall be sealed to a level that lies between one‑eighth inch and one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface [2]. Transverse joint cleaning and sealing requires cleaning to achieve at least one‑and‑a‑half inch depth, air blasting with a compressed‑air stream of at least one hundred pounds per square inch, placement of a backer rod whose top lies one to two times the joint width plus a quarter‑inch below the pavement surface to provide a recessed sealant depth‑to‑width ratio of one‑to‑one to two‑to‑one, and sealant may only be placed when the joint is clean and dry and ambient air and pavement temperatures are forty degrees Fahrenheit or greater; the sealant reservoir should be filled to a level of one‑eighth inch to one‑quarter inch below the surface [2]. Safety equipment shall be used during slab stabilization to protect traffic and crew from hot‑asphalt grout, with safety masks, protective gloves and heavy clothing in two or more layers that fit closely at the ankles, wrists and neck; traffic may not be opened until the material cools (minimum thirty minutes for asphalt, twelve hours for cement), all drilling and stabilization activities shall be protected by Maintenance and Protection of Traffic devices required by Publication 213, work shall be confined to a single lane at any one time, joint and crack sealing should accompany stabilization to reduce water infiltration, and subsurface drains must be kept in good condition [1]. For concrete‑pavement partial‑depth repairs the use of a rigid polystyrene board or premolded expansion‑joint material equal in width to the existing joint provides the required geometric allowance for slab movement while also contributing to safety by preventing direct contact between new and old slabs [1]. Joint sealing on asphalt surfaces must be performed only when the surface is clean, dry and cleared of debris; joints and cracks between one‑quarter inch and one inch wide are sealed with asphalt sealant, those wider than one inch are filled with an Asphalt Wearing Course 4.75 mm, sealant is applied level with the surface and wiped flush to leave a film from one‑thirty‑second to one‑sixteenth inch thick spanning one to three inches in width, and sealant placement is prohibited when air temperature is below forty degrees Fahrenheit or above ninety degrees Fahrenheit unless written department approval is obtained [2]. Notched wedge joints require that all loose material be removed before the lane is opened to traffic, vehicular loads shall be prohibited for twenty‑four hours or until the surface has cooled uniformly to one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit or less, and “Uneven Lane” signs required by MUTCD publications shall be installed half‑mile before and every half‑mile through the uneven pavement area when a joint abuts opposing traffic; the joint face must receive a thin coating of the appropriate binder before fresh mix is placed, and surface tolerance shall be checked with a twelve‑foot straightedge so that any irregularity exceeding three‑sixteenths inch is deemed defective [2]. Bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavement require longitudinal joints to be offset from the joint in the layer below by approximately six inches, positioned at the approximate pavement centreline for two‑lane roads and within twelve inches of the lane lines for roadways with more than two lanes; the joint face shall be painted with a uniform coating of the specified PG‑Binder, sealed with hot PG 64S‑22 binder maintained between two hundred sixty‑five°F and three hundred twenty°F, sealant may not be placed when air temperature is below forty degrees Fahrenheit unless authorized, and a pressure applicator must create a straight band four inches plus or minus one inch wide and one‑sixteenth inch plus or minus one‑thirty‑second thick centred within one inch of the joint [2]. Longitudinal joint design for bonded overlays shall exclude any joint located within one foot longitudinally of an obstruction such as a manhole, inlet grate, utility, bridge structure or pavement notch, and may also exclude small features like intersections, gore areas or transitions where consistent joint construction cannot be achieved; edge slump is limited to one‑quarter inch in the outer six inches adjacent to the shoulder and to one‑eighth inch in the outer six inches adjacent to an adjoining lane [2]. Joint backing material for bonded overlays shall be installed to within plus‑or‑minus one‑quarter inch of the pavement surface so it cannot be dislodged during placement, joint faces must be protected from curing compound or cleaned thoroughly prior to sealant installation, and transverse joints shall be saw‑cut directly over existing joints with a depth equal to the full overlay thickness plus one‑half inch and a width at least as wide as the underlying joint; intermediate transverse joints may be added so that no panel exceeds fifteen feet [2]. Dowel‑retrofit joints require slots cut so that the dowel centre lies at mid‑depth of the slab, aligned parallel to the roadway centreline with three dowel slots per wheel path centred over the crack or transverse joint, vertical tilt or horizontal skew not to exceed zero point two five inch displacement at each end, longitudinal translation and horizontal translation each not to exceed one inch, chairs providing at least one‑half inch clearance from all slot surfaces, expansion caps leaving a quarter‑inch gap, and exposed slot surfaces cleaned within twenty‑four hours before concrete placement and final cleaning completed within thirty minutes prior to placing concrete using an air blast of at least one hundred pounds per square inch free of oil or moisture [2]. Cross‑stitching work shall not be performed if either ambient air temperature or pavement surface temperature is below forty degrees Fahrenheit, sealing material shall also not be placed under those conditions, the joint must be routed to a width of three‑quarters inch and a depth of one inch before sealing, sealant filled to a level one‑eighth inch to one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface, and traffic shall not be opened until the anchoring material has fully cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations [2]. Gefoam lightweight fill block joints have accessibility and safety requirements that blocks be stored off the ground and away from standing water and heat sources, foot traffic and vehicle travel on placed blocks shall be kept to a minimum, and de‑icing salts or similar degradative chemicals shall never be applied to the blocks [2].

Verification and expected performance

The completed joint is first inspected against the applicable Standard Drawing; for slip‑form cement concrete pavements the dowel and load‑transfer assemblies must be positioned so that vertical tilt or horizontal skew does not exceed 0.25 inch, longitudinal translation does not exceed 1 inch and horizontal translation does not exceed 1 inch before concrete placement, and after curing the transverse and longitudinal joints are sawed to the specified depth and width, flushed with water, back‑filled with an oversized backer rod (width plus 50 percent) and monitored for movement during cure. Prior to sealing a QC wipe test performed in the presence of the Representative according to PTM No. 643 confirms that the joint face is free of dirt, dust or moisture, after which sealant is placed to the specified level. Surface smoothness across each joint is then checked with a 12‑foot straightedge and must not vary more than 3/16 inch when the straightedge is laid parallel with the centerline or perpendicular within a traffic lane; macrotexture of the finished pavement is measured in accordance with ASTM E965 and must achieve a Mean Texture Depth of at least 0.040 inches. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs the inspector confirms that each transverse gap measures between 1/4 inch and 1/2 inch and that any longitudinal gap does not exceed 3/4 inch; the finished vertical differential across every joint must be no more than 1/4 inch, or if it exceeds 1/4 inch but is not more than 1/2 inch the slab may be retained only after diamond grinding to bring it within tolerance. In concrete pavement patching the completed joint is verified through the project QC Plan that describes actions for joint sawing, sealing and testing before traffic opening; dowel bar anchoring is inspected by randomly coring three locations on the first day of anchoring and each core is accepted only if total voids around the dowel are less than 0.50 inch; surface continuity across the joint is checked with a 12‑foot straightedge and any high points or depressions of 3/16 inch or more must be ground smooth, retested, and if still out of tolerance the area is deemed defective and replaced. For unbonded concrete overlays each joint is verified after placement by confirming that saw cuts meet the required depth—no less than D/3 within 12 inches of the edges and no less than D/4 anywhere—and that all cut faces are cleaned of deleterious material and sealed; panel layout is checked to ensure the aspect ratio is as close to 1.0 as feasible and does not exceed 1.5, with overall dimensions not exceeding 18 times the overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter dimension also not exceeding 1.5; joint spacing follows the project plans or an approved layout that avoids longitudinal joints in wheel paths, and any cracking outside the sawed joints is treated as defective. In bonded concrete overlays the joint backing material must be installed to within ±1/4 inch of the pavement surface and cannot be dislodged during paving; after placement the transverse joint is saw‑cut to a depth equal to the full thickness of the overlay plus 1/2 inch with a width at least as wide as the underlying crack, flushed, filled with the approved backing material to the proper depth and sealed in accordance with Section 501.3(n); performance compliance is demonstrated by bond‑strength testing per ASTM C1583 where the average of three tests must not be below 175 psi and each individual test must not be below 160 psi, together with a compressive strength of at least 3,600 psi before traffic is allowed; edge slump adjacent to shoulders may not exceed 1/4 inch and adjacent to lanes may not exceed 1/8 inch. For longitudinal joints in asphalt pavement the compliance verification uses a two‑stage core program: on the first eligible day five random 6‑inch diameter cores are taken from the joint for density testing, and thereafter one core per sublot is taken within one day of construction; all cores are tested according to PTM No. 715 or PTM No. 716 and results are submitted to the Representative within 24 hours, where a lower specification limit of 91.0 % compaction determines any incentive or disincentive. In bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavement the completed joint is verified against the joint layout plan accepted by the Department, confirming that spacing follows the plan and panel dimensions do not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness nor a length‑to‑width ratio greater than 1.5; joints are cut with a green saw when the concrete is hardened enough to avoid excessive raveling, each cut being one‑third the slab thickness deep with at least a 1/8 inch width, extending cuts through the slab edge as soon as it can be done without damage and ensuring no crack exists within 1.5 feet of a joint before sawing; after cutting the faces are cleaned and later sealed with an approved hot‑pour or silicone sealant in accordance with the specified sealing provision. For Superpave mix asphalt courses each longitudinal joint is verified by confirming that it is offset about 6 inches from the joint below, located at the pavement centerline for two‑lane roads or within 12 inches of lane lines on wider roads, and that the joint face has been uniformly painted with the specified PG‑Binder before placement; after compaction the joint is sealed with hot PG 64S‑22 binder at 265°F–320°F applying a 4‑inch ± 1‑inch wide and 1/16‑inch ± 1/32‑inch thick band centered within 1 inch of the joint on a clean, dry surface; any missed or uneven areas are resealed and pavement markings restored. For dowel‑retrofit joints the inspector checks that saw cuts are made parallel to the roadway centerline with three dowel slots per wheel path centered over the crack or transverse joint, that the slot bottom is level at mid‑depth of the thinner slab, that final cleaning is completed within 30 minutes before concrete placement and that a QC wipe test confirms freedom from oil, moisture or other contaminants; dowel bars must be positioned so that vertical tilt or horizontal skew does not exceed 0.25 inch, longitudinal translation does not exceed 1 inch and horizontal translation does not exceed 1 inch, expansion caps provide a ¼‑inch clearance, chairs give at least a ½‑inch clearance, and the temporary joint material is sealed before concrete placement. For saw‑cut and seal joints in asphalt overlays a QC plan for locating and referencing each transverse joint must be submitted to the Representative at least 21 days before work begins, approved, and demonstrated to achieve results within a ± 1 inch tolerance; during construction the saw‑cuts are inspected to ensure they are made directly above each existing joint and fall within the same ± 1 inch tolerance, reservoir faces are checked to be thoroughly clean and dry before sealing, and sealing is only accepted when air temperature is between 40°F and 90°F, material temperature follows the manufacturer’s limits, tape bond‑breaker does not extend more than 1/8 inch up the face, and the sealant is filled to a level 1/8‑¼ inch below the pavement surface. For slab‑stabilization joints a crew supervisor with at least five years of comparable experience must be documented and submitted to the Representative ten working days in advance, and a field QC plan covering hole spacing, drilling methods, equipment lists, line‑and‑grade control, waste disposal, curing period and traffic opening times must be approved; each joint or crack is visually inspected and then deflection tested only when air temperature is not below 40°F nor exceeds 70°F, using ASTM D4695, and the loaded slab corner deflection must be 0.02 inch or more with a calculated joint efficiency of at least 65 % (or as approved), while upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed; any joint exceeding the 0.05‑inch allowable tolerance is ground to correct grade, retested after grouting, and if still out of tolerance the slab is regrouted and retested. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing‑course longitudinal joints the inspector confirms that they are formed as butt joints, rolled, and sealed with hot PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement heated between 265°F and 320°F applied only to clean, dry surfaces using a pressure applicator that produces a straight band 4 inches ± 1 inch wide and 1/16 inch ± 1/32 inch thick centered within 1 inch of the joint; excess sealant is removed and gaps re‑sealed, and transverse joints must be placed perpendicular to traffic, compacted, and kept to a minimum, with work stopped if the QC plan fails to limit their number. The completed pervious‑asphalt joint is verified by first confirming that longitudinal joints overlap the previously placed lane by one to two inches; transverse joints installed after any interruption longer than thirty minutes must use a temporary vertical bulkhead or a sawed full‑depth cut that extends the full depth and width of the pervious course; surface tolerance at the joint is then checked with a 12‑foot straightedge moved no more than five feet at a time, and any irregularity greater than three‑eighths of an inch must be corrected before final compaction; depth continuity across the joint is confirmed by drilling a six‑inch diameter test hole for each 3 000 sq yd of completed base and measuring course depth, with any section short by one‑half inch or more being removed and rebuilt to within half an inch of the specified depth; finally infiltration performance adjacent to the joint is evaluated on three locations per 10 000 sf using ASTM C1701 and any area that does not achieve at least a 200 inches‑per‑hour average rate must be replaced. The completed pavement relief joint is verified by confirming that the saw‑cut at the asphalt–concrete interface is located within ± ¼ inch of the required line, that it is made after the wearing course has cooled below 140°F and no later than seven days after the joint is placed; the resulting reservoir must be one inch deep by one‑half inch wide, clean and dry, and sealing may occur only when ambient air temperature is between 40 °F and 90 °F, with sealant kept at the manufacturer’s recommended pouring temperature and not held in a single batch for more than six hours; the tape bond breaker must not extend up the reservoir face more than one‑eighth inch, and the seal must be placed to leave a finish level between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface. The completed plain cement concrete gutter joint is inspected by confirming that contraction joints have been formed or sawed to the dimensions shown on the Standard Drawing and specified in Section 501.3(i), that a ¾‑inch premolded expansion joint material has been placed and cut to the gutter cross‑section at structures and where gutter placement ends each workday, and that the joints have been prepared and sealed or caulked according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. After a partial‑depth repair all repaired sections are resounded in the presence of the Representative, and any section found delaminated must be replaced at no cost to the Department, thereby confirming compliance, constructability, performance, and consistency with the project requirements. For long‑life concrete pavement projects the QC Supervisor performs a nondestructive verification of each completed joint within 18 hours after ending each day’s concrete placement and finishing when more than 700 SY are placed; the inspection confirms vertical and horizontal alignment of the LTU assemblies for at least the first five transverse joint locations across the full pavement width and for one joint per 2 640 LF of pavement placed that day, and the QC Plan must also describe how the joints will be cured and protected during the curing period to prevent moisture loss. The Representative shall verify that the concrete slab has reached at least 3 000 psi compressive strength before any traffic or heavy equipment is permitted, using the maturity method if desired; new pavement will not be opened to any traffic until all joints have been sealed unless an acceptable method to protect joints from damage and keep non‑compressible materials out is approved. Constructability checks require a ramp for entry and exit when moving on and off the pavement, sweeping the surface free of debris before placing protective mats, using equipment that does not damage or mar the surface, installing temporary vertical bulkheads where required, and ensuring that any work that could cause joint damage is suspended pending corrective action. Performance compliance is demonstrated by meeting macrotexture requirements of a Mean Texture Depth of at least 0.040 inches, bond‑strength test averages of at least 175 psi with each individual test not below 160 psi, compressive strengths of at least 1 000 psi for patching, 3 600 psi for bonded overlays, and 3 000 psi before traffic, LVDT differential deflection limits of not more than 7.5 mils at one million cycles with no increase greater than 2.5 mils at ten million cycles, verification MRI not exceeding acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches per mile on at least 90 percent of the 528‑foot lots, and infiltration rates of at least 200 inches per hour for pervious sections. All joints must conform to the joint layout plan or approved spacing, panel dimensions must not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness nor an aspect ratio greater than 1.5, longitudinal joints shall be avoided in wheel paths, offsets between layers shall follow the specified distances, sealant temperatures and application tolerances shall meet the limits given, and any deviation beyond the stated thresholds requires corrective action, documentation and approval by the Representative. Slab stabilization is expected to restore the slab’s structural integrity by filling voids beneath the concrete, which reduces deflections and thereby lowers the likelihood of future pumping, joint faulting, and cracking. The effectiveness of this outcome is judged by checking for the absence or reduction of the loss‑of‑support indicators listed in the guidelines—transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes), corner breaks, and slab settlement—and when these conditions are no longer observed, full support is considered restored. Joint performance in bonded concrete overlays is judged by achieving a minimum bond strength and compressive strength that indicate durability and readiness for traffic; bond strength must average at least 175 pounds per square inch with each individual test not less than 160 pounds per square inch as measured by ASTM C1583 testing seven days after placement, and the concrete must reach a compressive strength of 3,600 psi on cylinder tests in accordance with PTM No. 604 before opening to traffic. For notched wedge joints in plant‑mixed asphalt courses the expected performance is a smooth surface and correct course depth, with surface irregularities limited to no more than 3/16 inch as verified by a twelve‑foot straightedge test, and pavement depth that does not exceed a deficiency of 1/4 inch (or 1/8 inch in three or more adjacent core locations) as confirmed by core drilling according to PTM No. 737; traffic may only be allowed after the newly compacted joint has cooled uniformly to 140°F or less and at least 24 hours have elapsed. The joint treatment is expected to keep slab movement within limits that preserve structural integrity and ride quality by ensuring loaded‑slab corner deflection does not exceed the threshold that triggers stabilization and by maintaining joint efficiency at or above the specified percentage; these outcomes are verified through ASTM D4695 deflection testing, calculation of joint efficiency from unloaded versus loaded corner deflections, and continuous monitoring of vertical pavement lift with equipment capable of 0.001‑inch resolution, all performed only when air temperature is between 40°F and 70°F. For pervious asphalt pavement the joint construction is expected to deliver a smooth riding surface, proper structural thickness, and adequate drainage; surface tolerance is verified by using a twelve‑foot straightedge and any irregularity greater than 3/8‑inch must be corrected on the spot, depth uniformity is checked by drilling a 6‑inch diameter test hole for each 3,000 sq yd of base and sections deficient by 1/2‑inch or more are required to be removed and replaced, and drainage performance is assessed through ASTM C1701 infiltration testing on three locations per 10,000 sq ft with a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour. Joint‑related performance for rigid concrete pavements is evaluated against the defect criteria in Section 506.3(t); the pavement must not have an average depth less than the design thickness minus 1/2 inch, the Percent Within Limits for compressive strength or plastic air content must not be less than 55%, visual inspection must show no surface defects from rain, improper joint construction, cracking or honeycombing that cannot be satisfactorily repaired, and edge‑slumping must meet the requirements specified in Section 501.3(g)5. Meeting these measurements is used to determine whether the pavement will achieve its intended service life and distress resistance. [2]
