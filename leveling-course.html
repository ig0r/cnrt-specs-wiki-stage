Leveling Course

Part of: Overlay is-subtopic-of
Leveling Course is a topic under Overlay.

Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-28

The leveling course is a distinct asphalt (or cementitious) layer applied when an existing pavement surface fails to meet required geometric or smoothness criteria—such as wheel‑rutting, localized depressions, erratic longitudinal profile, or insufficient cross‑slope—to provide a uniform, stable platform for subsequent binder, wearing or block layers [1][2]. It must be at least one inch thick (counted in structural analysis) but no single compacted lift may exceed six inches; deeper sections are placed in two or more lifts of three to six inches each, and for block pavements a six‑inch loose bedding material graded parallel to the longitudinal profile serves as the leveling course [1][2]. The mix is designed under the Superpave volumetric procedure, with polymer‑modified binders such as PG 64E‑22 required when projected 20‑year traffic exceeds ten million ESALs or where severe rutting potential exists, ensuring resistance to permanent deformation and thermal cracking [1][2]. Prior to placement the existing surface must be clean, dry, and free of contaminants, and compaction is verified by an optimum‑rolling pattern for lifts one inch or thicker, guaranteeing the intended smoothness, ride quality, and drainage performance [1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

The leveling course is applied when the existing pavement surface does not meet required geometric or smoothness criteria such as wheel‑rutting, localized depressions, an erratic longitudinal profile, insufficient cross‑slope, or a rough milled face that would otherwise hinder proper placement and performance of subsequent layers. Its primary engineering purpose is to restore the intended grade, cross‑sectional shape, and smoothness by providing a uniform, stable platform that supplies even support to the binder, wearing or block courses placed thereafter. When isolated high spots exceed six inches in compacted depth, the guides require those areas be removed and the leveling material placed in two or more equal layers, each between three and six inches thick, to avoid excessive layer thickness while achieving a level surface. The course also fills wheel ruts and moderate depressions ranging from one‑half inch up to 1¼ inch deep, eliminating unevenness that could compromise ride quality and structural performance. For block pavements, a six‑inch loose bedding layer meeting specified material requirements is graded parallel to the roadway’s longitudinal profile and left free of debris and water so that the first block layer can be set at the correct grade with consistent smoothness. In situations where traffic loading exceeds ten million equivalent single axle loads and severe rutting potential exists, such as in stopping zones or on steep grades, a polymer‑modified binder (e.g., PG 64E‑22) is specified for the leveling course to resist permanent deformation and thermal cracking. Although treated as a non‑structural geometry fix and excluded from overlay thickness calculations, the corrective layer is essential for achieving the smoothness, ride quality, structural performance, and reliable adhesion required by the new pavement system. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89), Ch. 5 (pp. 111–121), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 178–179), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 263–329)

The leveling course is designed to eliminate existing rutting or unevenness and to provide a uniformly level, stable platform that restores the intended longitudinal grade, cross‑section and cross‑slope required for safe drainage and rideability while being graded parallel to the roadway profile to deliver precise longitudinal grade control and a smooth, even surface that supports subsequent block or overlay placement. Structurally the course must be at least one inch thick so that it may be treated as a one‑inch paving layer in the pavement’s structural analysis, contributing load‑bearing capacity for the overlying binder or wearing courses placed at uniform depth; when greater support is required the design calls for a six‑inch loose bedding layer whose compacted sublayers are limited to three to six inches each, and any total depth exceeding six inches is placed in two or more layers of roughly equal thickness. Durability is achieved by designing the mix with the Superpave volumetric procedure to resist the anticipated climatic and traffic conditions for a twenty‑year design life, providing resistance to permanent deformation and low‑temperature thermal cracking; when projected cumulative traffic exceeds ten million equivalent single axle loads a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder is specified to prevent severe rutting, and on grades steeper than five percent the mix must be proportioned for adequate compaction and shear‑rut stability, while compliance with Section 219.2(e) for bedding material and keeping the surface free of debris and water further minimizes moisture‑related damage. Safety considerations require that the existing surface be prepared free of dirt, water, oil or other contaminants so that the interlayer can achieve full adhesion and avoid delamination, with the pavement remaining moisture‑free during placement; additionally, safety edges are not required for base, leveling, or scratch courses, eliminating the need for edge treatment at this stage. Serviceability is maintained through strict control of grade, smoothness and layer thickness so that the finished pavement retains its intended alignment and ride quality over time, with a surface smooth enough after milling to receive the asphalt interlayer without debonding or slippage and to preserve the required grade and cross‑slope throughout service. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

Leveling courses are employed on both flexible pavements, such as asphalt‑concrete decks, and rigid pavements constructed of Portland cement concrete whenever the existing surface must be corrected to provide a level and stable platform for an overlay or other surface treatment [1][2]. For asphalt surfaces the course may follow scratch milling or conventional milling that leaves a rough texture, and it is placed ahead of new binder or wearing layers to establish a relatively uniform working platform [1][2]. On concrete pavements the leveling layer is applied as a cementitious slab beneath unbonded concrete overlays, with the explicit requirement that an asphalt separation layer not be used for this purpose [1]. The minimum thickness required to correct cross‑slope, wheel ruts, local depressions, or an erratic longitudinal profile is one inch, and this thickness may be counted as a structural paving course but must remain separate from any binder or wearing course to preserve quality control [1]. When the compacted depth of an isolated spot exceeds six inches, the leveling material must be placed in two or more lifts, each lift ranging between three and six inches, to satisfy compaction and stability criteria [2]. Courses that are less than one inch deep—or that weigh 110 pounds per square yard or less—may be accepted without movement, whereas courses one inch or deeper (or weighing at least 110 ppy) must be compacted using an optimum‑rolling pattern [2]. In high‑traffic projects where the estimated annual average daily traffic expressed in equivalent single axle loads exceeds ten million ESALs and severe rutting potential exists because of frequent stopping conditions or steep grades, a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder is specified for the leveling (or binder) course to prevent permanent deformation [1]. For cracked‑and‑seated concrete pavements the underlying base repair typically employs a Base Course or Superpave Base Course, while rubblized concrete pavements use AASHTO #1 aggregate beneath the one‑inch leveling layer [1]. When micro‑surfacing is selected instead of a Superpave scratch or surface course, approximately thirty percent of the material quantity required for a comparable Superpave leveling course is sufficient, and it may be applied as a nonsurface rut‑filling layer or as a re‑profiling surface layer [1]. Type B polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing can also serve as a leveling course to fill moderate ruts ranging from one‑half inch to about one and one‑quarter inches deep, acting as a scratch course, seal coat, or other surface treatment, with double applications required when the design calls for additional weight per square yard [2]. All paving grid installations except composite grids require a pre‑overlay leveling course, and the presence of an interlayer after milling that left a rough surface likewise mandates a leveling layer to remediate rutting before the interlayer is placed; in such cases the leveling layer can eliminate the need for a tack coat if the grid and overlay are installed on the same day [1]. For block pavement systems a six‑inch loose bedding material that meets Section 219.2(e) must be spread, graded parallel to the roadway’s longitudinal profile, and kept free of water or debris before the first layer of blocks is placed, functioning as the leveling course for those applications [2]. On rigid concrete pavements the leveling course serves solely to adjust geometry to meet minimum cross‑slope requirements; it does not contribute structural capacity, its thickness is excluded from overlay depth calculations, and it is not subject to the usual overlay thickness limits [1]. The leveling approach is also appropriate for limited‑width placements such as patching, road widening, shoulder adjustments, driveway work, parking lots, and other miscellaneous uses approved by the Representative, provided the material and weight satisfy the density‑acceptance criteria [2].

Design inputs and requirements

The design of a leveling course requires that the existing pavement condition be fully assessed: any potholes or cracks must be repaired, the surface cleaned of dirt, water, oil and other contaminants, rutting corrected by milling or a prior leveling layer, and the grade and cross‑slope confirmed. Environmental constraints dictate that the pavement be moisture‑free, installation not occur during precipitation, and ambient and surface temperatures fall within the limits specified (generally 40 °F to 140 °F for fiberglass mesh grid with adhesive). Material considerations are limited to these temperature requirements; the source does not provide site, traffic or geotechnical data. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (p. 120)

The design and placement of a leveling course are governed by a suite of PennDOT specifications together with AASHTO design procedures, FHWA policies, and any project‑specific constraints that may be imposed. All guides require a minimum compacted thickness of one inch for the leveling layer to provide a uniform working platform before binder or wearing courses are placed. The maximum allowable compacted depth for a single leveling or scratch course is six inches; when greater depth is needed the material must be placed in two or more layers each between three and six inches thick, with no layer less than three inches. Where an isolated area would exceed the six‑inch limit, a preliminary scratch or leveling course must first be installed to eliminate the deep zone before any full‑depth overlay is applied. The one‑inch minimum may be counted as a structural paving course in thickness tables, but slope‑correction material is permitted without counting toward prescribed depth limits so long as cross‑slope criteria are satisfied. Materials for the leveling course must conform to Superpave mix specifications, meeting the gradation and aggregate size requirements set out in the applicable tables and using approved base materials such as Base Course/Superpave Base Course or AASHTO #1 aggregate. For block‑paving projects the leveling course consists of a six‑inch loose bedding layer that meets the material criteria of Section 219.2(e) and is graded parallel to the longitudinal profile before block placement. Concrete pavements, as well as all paving‑grid applications except composite grids, also require a leveling course either by milling or prior to interlayer installation. Binder selection follows the Superpave system; when the projected 20‑year design ESALs exceed ten million, a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder—or an equivalent—must be used, with optional aramid‑fiber modification permitted under a special provision. The required performance temperature range for installations that incorporate fiberglass mesh grids is between 40 °F and 140 °F, and the surface must be moisture‑free, free of dirt, oil, debris, and precipitation at the time of placement. Density acceptance criteria differ by thickness: courses less than one inch or with a compacted density of 110 lb/yd² may be approved by a non‑movement check, whereas courses of at least one inch depth or exceeding that density require an optimum‑rolling pattern and verification with an approved nuclear or electrical impedance gauge as specified in PTM No. 402/403. Design life for the leveling course is required to be between eight and twenty years, with traffic inputs (ADT, growth, truck percentages) calculated over a 20‑year design horizon using the 1993 AASHTO pavement design procedures as implemented in DARWin. When an asphalt overlay is placed over concrete or brick for the first time, a minimum binder thickness of two and one‑half inches must be provided beneath the wearing course. Projects with grades steeper than five percent must proportion the asphalt mix to achieve adequate compaction and shear‑rut stability. Safety‑edge construction is expressly prohibited on base, leveling, or scratch courses. Any design exception involving a leveling course must be documented in accordance with Publication 10C Design Manual Part 1C Section 3.4.C and reviewed by the Highway Development and Technical Services (HDTS) team, while district pavement‑management guidance may impose additional constraints. Completion of Form D‑4332 for each resurfacing or overlay design is required for PDAU concurrence and FHWA approval, ensuring that all leveling‑course specifications are incorporated into the final contract documents. Design methods and criteria

The design of a leveling course begins with an analysis of projected traffic and climate conditions, from which a twenty‑year equivalent single axle load (ESAL) count is calculated and placed into one of five predefined ranges that drive the Superpave volumetric mixture design [1]. When the ESAL estimate exceeds ten million, the specifications require a polymer‑modified binder such as PG 64E‑22 to mitigate severe rutting, and the ESAL value also determines the appropriate performance grade binder and influences aggregate, filler, and asphalt cement proportions while limiting the number of distinct mix designs on a project typically to three [1]. The selected Superpave mixture is then quantified using the Superpave leveling rate, and if micro‑surfacing is employed in place of a conventional scratch or surface course, only about thirty percent of the standard rate is applied, for example reducing a 90 lb/yd² rate to roughly 27 lb/yd² [1]. For micro‑surfacing used as a rut filler, application rates are taken from the appropriate table that distinguishes non‑surface leveling (rut filling before an overlay) from re‑profiling (rut filling that serves as the surface layer) [1]. The physical placement of the leveling course requires a minimum thickness of one inch and is installed as a separate layer prior to any binder or wearing course, providing a uniform platform that corrects longitudinal profile and restores cross‑section, with its thickness counted in overall structural calculations and governed by tables that specify allowable minimum and maximum depths, structural coefficients, and traffic volume limits [1]. In overlay projects the standard 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures are executed through the DARWin software, which uses input traffic data such as average daily traffic, growth rates, and truck percentages to compute overall pavement thicknesses, while Table 10.5 is consulted to select the proper Superpave mixture size for the leveling course [1]. When a bonded concrete overlay is part of the design, the mechanistic‑empirical BCOA‑ME procedure replaces the conventional AASHTO method, but the same minimum and maximum layer‑thickness limits are observed [1]. Installation of the leveling course precedes any block layer and consists of a six‑inch loose bedding material that must meet the requirements of Section 219.2(e), be graded to follow the longitudinal profile, and be free of debris and water before placement of blocks or other surface treatments [2]. If the required compacted thickness exceeds six inches, the course is placed in two or more lifts of roughly equal depth, each lift limited to a compacted thickness between three and six inches; isolated spots that would exceed this limit are first reduced with a scratch or separate leveling layer before full‑depth paving proceeds [2]. Safety edges are excluded from base, leveling, and scratch courses [2]. Acceptance criteria for the leveling course depend on its thickness and unit weight: layers thinner than one inch or weighing no more than 110 lb/yd² may be approved by a non‑movement method, whereas layers at least one inch thick or weighing at least 110 lb/yd² require verification through an optimum‑rolling‑pattern density procedure [2]. The optimum rolling pattern is established on site with an approved nuclear gauge in accordance with PTM 402 or an approved electrical impedance gauge per PTM 403, both calibrated to ASTM D2950, and the selected pattern is recorded on the appropriate form and submitted for review, with density later confirmable by pavement cores [2]. When a fiberglass mesh grid is to be placed over the leveling course, an empirical rule requires that the course thickness be at least twice the nominal maximum aggregate size (NMAS) [2]. Throughout placement the surface temperature of the leveling course must remain between 40 °F and 140 °F and never exceed 140 °F, especially when a grid is to be installed, and preparation for grid installation includes cleaning and drying the pavement, sealing cracks, and providing a drainable surface [2].

The leveling course is defined as a distinct asphalt layer whose chief purpose is to furnish a uniformly level and stable platform for any subsequent overlay, and its design must satisfy several quantitative criteria. [1] It must be at least one inch thick in all cases, a thickness that may be counted in the structural analysis as a one‑inch paving course while remaining subject to the minimum‑and‑maximum limits established for resurfacing layers. [1][2] When the existing pavement is concrete or brick, a minimum of 2½ inches of binder material must first be placed before the leveling layer is applied. [1] No single compacted lift may exceed six inches in depth; if a greater thickness is required the course shall be placed in two or more lifts each ranging between three and six inches, and any isolated spot that would otherwise require a deeper section must first be treated with a scratch or an additional leveling layer before full‑depth paving proceeds. [2] The mix used for the leveling course must be designed under the Superpave system and must meet the same mix‑design limits as other paving courses; no reduction in thickness is permitted when fiber‑modified mixes are employed. [1] If the 20‑year traffic forecast expressed in equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) exceeds ten million, a polymer‑modified binder such as PG 64E‑22 is mandated to guard against severe rutting, and the ESAL calculation must be based on an eight‑to‑twenty‑year structural design life using reliable traffic inputs. [1] For grades steeper than five percent the mix proportions are required to provide adequate compaction and shear‑rut stability. [1] The course is also governed by performance thresholds: it must correct any existing cross‑slope deficiency, may be used as the sole material for re‑establishing proper longitudinal profile, but it cannot be combined with a binder layer because such blending would compromise quality control. [1] It is mandatory whenever rutting is present, on all concrete pavements, and on virtually every paving‑grid application except composite grids; it is optional after milling when the milled surface already achieves the required smoothness. [1] Installation assumptions require that the existing surface be stable under roller loads, clean, dry, and free of moisture or precipitation at the time of placement, and ambient as well as pavement temperature must remain between 40 °F and 140 °F (with the upper limit applying to newly placed asphalt and to fiberglass mesh grids with self‑adhesive glue). [1][2] The surface of the leveling course must be free of debris and water before block placement, and when a fiberglass mesh grid is installed the minimum thickness of the leveling course must be at least twice the nominal maximum aggregate size. [2] Density acceptance depends on both thickness and weight: if the compacted layer is less than one inch thick or weighs no more than 110 lb/yd² it may be accepted by the non‑movement criterion, whereas layers one inch or thicker or weighing 110 lb/yd² or more require an optimum rolling pattern established in accordance with PTM No. 402 or 403 and density verification with an approved nuclear or electrical‑impedance gauge; failure to achieve target density mandates a new rolling pattern or suspension of paving. [2] Safety edge treatments are expressly excluded from base, leveling, and scratch courses. [2] The overall design assumes that the material will achieve the required smoothness, shear strength, and rut resistance throughout the prescribed service life, thereby satisfying the reliability expectations of the pavement system. Structural section and dimensions

The guides limit the dimensional requirements for a leveling course to its thickness alone, leaving pavement surface, base, subbase, treated‑soil layer, slab, shoulder, edge support, and reinforcement without prescribed sizes [1][2]. One source establishes an absolute minimum thickness of one inch for any leveling course applied to create a uniform working platform [1]. The other source adds that when the course is intended to receive a fiberglass mesh grid, its thickness must be at least twice the nominal maximum aggregate size and the surface temperature must remain below 140 °F [2]. Aside from these thickness criteria, no further dimensional limits are provided for any of the associated layers in relation to the leveling course [1][2].

Materials and mixture design

A leveling course may be constructed either with a Superpave asphalt‑concrete mix or with a Micro‑Surfacing product, the latter being limited to roughly thirty percent of the quantity required for an equivalent Superpave mix—approximately 27 lb per square yard compared with about 90 lb per square yard for the Superpave option. The asphalt material must be identified by its performance‑grade (PG) binder, which is selected according to the projected design traffic expressed in ESALs over a twenty‑year design life; when the anticipated traffic exceeds ten million ESALs the specification calls for a polymer‑modified binder such as PG 64E‑22, or alternatively PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 when fiber modification is used. For an asphalt‑concrete leveling layer the minimum thickness is one inch, whereas another guide requires a six‑inch loose layer of bedding material that must satisfy the criteria set out in Section 219.2(e). The approved Superpave aggregate sizes for the mix include 25 mm, 19 mm, 12.5 mm and 9.5 mm, each to be placed at its prescribed minimum depth within the layer. When the work involves grades steeper than five percent, the asphalt mix should be proportioned to improve compaction and shear‑rut stability in accordance with the interlayer manufacturer’s recommendations. The specification may also permit the use of clean blotting sand or broadcast asphalt pavement material as a base, provided that any asphalt placed is applied at the proper temperature to ensure waterproofing and bond integrity. Finally, the leveling course shall not be combined with a binder course because such combination would compromise quality‑control requirements. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 106–121), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 178–179)

The selection and proportioning of a leveling course begin with the determination of the 20‑year design traffic in equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) for the segment. That ESAL value is placed into one of the five Superpave ESAL ranges, which then dictates the binder type. When the calculated range exceeds ten million ESALs, a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder must be specified for the leveling course to guard against severe rutting; lower traffic ranges may employ binders appropriate to their assigned range and can be adjusted when mix quantities are small. Once the binder is chosen, the mixture is designed using the Superpave volumetric procedure, and the number of distinct mixes on a project is limited to three (five for major projects) to keep costs and constructability manageable. If an eligible polymer‑modified binder such as PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22, or PG 64E‑22 is used, aramid‑fiber modification may be added to improve crack resistance.

A leveling course must be at least one inch thick and serves as a uniform platform that corrects longitudinal profile errors or re‑establishes the roadway cross‑section. That one‑inch thickness is counted in structural calculations. The mix size is selected from the standard Superpave aggregate gradations—25 mm, 19 mm, 12.5 mm, or 9.5 mm—to achieve the required depth while satisfying the limits set out in the structural coefficient tables and the minimum‑maximum placement depth tables (Tables 9.3, 9.5, 10.4, 10.5). By adhering to these prescribed thicknesses, aggregate sizes, and table‑based criteria, the leveling course meets structural strength requirements, provides long‑term durability, and remains practical to place. The practice of combining a separate binder layer with the leveling course is discouraged because it compromises quality control, further supporting constructability and performance goals. [1]

Structural details and interfaces

The leveling course must be reinforced with the specified interlayer material—geotextile paving fabric, fiberglass mesh grid, or composite reinforcing grid—and each sheet is saturated with tack coat at the manufacturer‑approved rate and laid so that the asphalt spread extends six inches beyond the edges of the reinforcement. All joints between sheets are required to overlap a minimum of one inch in the direction of traffic, with asphalt material placed between overlaps (or trimmed to achieve the one‑inch overlap) to ensure continuous bonding. When a fiberglass mesh grid is installed over a leveling course, the course thickness must be at least twice the nominal maximum aggregate size, and the first lift of overlay placed over any interlayer system must have a compacted thickness of no less than 1.5 inches. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 309–311)

Designing the interface for a leveling course begins with preparing the existing pavement surface so that it is dry, clean and free of dirt, water, oil, gravel, vegetation, loose material or any other foreign substances, and all cracks are sealed and potholes repaired according to specifications [1][2]. Any rutting present must be eliminated either by milling or by placing the leveling course itself before the next layer is installed, ensuring a sound bond between layers [1]. On concrete pavements and on all paving‑grid applications except composite grids, a leveling course is required to establish the proper grade and cross‑slope prior to installing an interlayer, providing a uniform working platform for subsequent binder or wearing courses [1][2]. When an asphalt overlay is placed over cracked or rubblized concrete, the leveling course must be at least one inch thick to correct cross‑slope and serve as the critical interface between the repaired concrete base and the new asphalt layer, while being coordinated with continuous pavement‑base drains and any guide rail or drainage structures that are reset or replaced [1]. Before placing the leveling material a clean blotting sand or broadcast asphalt bedding is spread over the area and excess sand removed to provide a uniform substrate for full contact between the subgrade and the first block layer, with vertical joint gaps limited to no more than 0.1 ft [1][2]. If the required compacted depth of the leveling material exceeds six inches, it is placed in two or more lifts, each lift being no less than three inches and no greater than six inches thick; isolated zones deeper than six inches are first treated with a scratch or an additional leveling lift before full‑depth paving proceeds [2]. The course itself may consist of a six‑inch loose bedding layer that satisfies the bedding material requirements, graded parallel to the longitudinal profile, and its surface must be cleared of debris and water prior to block placement to ensure full contact and prevent debonding [2]. At all vertical faces that will contact the course—such as curbs, gutters, adjacent pavements and other structures—a tack coat is uniformly painted before any new asphalt is placed; the tack coat may be an emulsified asphalt meeting PennDOT Material Class TACK or a hot‑asphalt binder of the appropriate class, applied in two or more passes to create a watertight joint [2][1]. Where the leveling mixture meets flush curbs, gutters and other abutting structures it is built slightly higher so that after compaction the finished surface projects above the edge, and a continuous band of hot‑asphalt sealant at least twelve inches wide is applied to reinforce the joint [2][1]. When interlayer systems such as geomembrane, geotextile or reinforcement grids are incorporated, additional interface criteria apply: geomembrane seams must overlap a minimum of three inches; geotextile overlaps require at least twelve inches with adhesive covering a minimum six‑inch portion of the overlap while avoiding bonding to the geomembrane; and if a reinforcement grid is placed on the leveling course, the course thickness shall be at least twice the nominal maximum aggregate size and its surface temperature must remain below 140 °F [2][1]. All joints between interlayer materials require a minimum one‑inch overlap with an asphalt tack coat applied between them, and any wrinkle larger than one inch is sliced and overlapped flat to maintain continuity [2]. After the leveling course has been compacted, a tack coat or asphalt layer is applied at the temperature and application rate recommended by the interlayer manufacturer—often within 40 °F to 140 °F for fiberglass mesh grids—to ensure waterproofing performance and prevent debonding or slippage of the overlying material [1]. For projects on grades steeper than five percent, the asphalt mix used in the overlay is proportioned to enhance compaction and shear‑rut stability, further supporting the integrity of the interface [1]. Traffic is prohibited until the overlay construction is complete and any paving fabric is placed while the underlying asphalt remains tacky, preserving the designed bond at the interface [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Before a leveling course is placed, the pavement’s longitudinal grade and cross‑slope must already be established to provide proper drainage. The underlying surface must be capable of draining freely, contain no low spots or depressions, and be completely clean, dry, and free of debris such as dirt, gravel, vegetation, oil, snow, ice, or standing water prior to any material placement. Any moisture present on the substrate must be eliminated or allowed to cure before the course is laid, and installation is prohibited during precipitation. The leveling course itself shall consist of a six‑inch loose bedding layer that meets the material specifications of Section 219.2(e). Materials for the course should be stored elevated, wrapped, and covered to protect them from precipitation, ultraviolet radiation, and chemicals until placement. Placement may only proceed when ambient weather conditions satisfy the manufacturer’s approved limits, which generally require an air temperature between 40 °F and 140 °F for the specific interlayer system. These combined requirements ensure that drainage, moisture control, and climatic factors are addressed to achieve a durable, well‑bonded leveling course. [1][2]

A leveling course provides a level, stable platform that meets the required grade and smoothness for the overlay, supporting safe and accessible travel [1]. The course must be at least one inch thick and is the only layer permitted to correct an erratic longitudinal profile, re‑establish the proper cross‑section, and set the required roadway cross‑slope [1]. When a concrete overlay is unbonded, the asphalt separation layer may not serve as the leveling material; all grade corrections must be accomplished with the concrete overlay itself [1]. For first‑time asphalt overlays on cement‑concrete or brick pavements, a minimum of 2½ inches of binder material is required, although within parking lanes the wearing course may taper to one inch at the curb provided that compaction is adequate and the pavement‑curb joint is sealed [1]. Prior to placement any rutting must be removed by milling or by installing a preceding leveling layer, and the existing surface must be completely clean, dry, and free of dirt, water, oil, or other foreign material; work must be halted during precipitation to keep the pavement moisture‑free [1]. If milling is performed, the smoothness achieved determines whether an additional leveling course is needed before the interlayer can be placed [1]. On projects where grades exceed five percent, the asphalt mix for the leveling course should be proportioned to ensure proper compaction and shear‑rut stability [1]. When the required compacted depth of the leveling course exceeds six inches, it must be placed in two or more layers of roughly equal thickness, each layer no less than three inches and no greater than six inches; isolated areas deeper than six inches must first be reduced by a scratch or leveling course before full‑depth paving proceeds [2]. The screed is set to spread and strike off the mixture across the entire lane width, providing the required cross‑section and depth while the outer edges are worked immediately after placement to eliminate sharp or ragged edges [2]. No safety edge treatment is applied to base, leveling, or scratch courses, but traffic is prohibited on the course except for construction vehicles needed for placement, thereby ensuring worker safety during installation [2]. The leveling course consists of a six‑inch loose bedding layer that meets the specifications of Section 219.2(e) and is graded so its surface runs parallel to the roadway’s longitudinal profile, creating geometric uniformity for subsequent pavement layers [2]. It must be laid on a clean, dry, drainable surface free of depressions, debris, and standing water before any blocks are placed, satisfying both accessibility and safety requirements [2]. When the course supports a fiberglass mesh grid, its thickness must be at least twice the nominal maximum aggregate size, ensuring structural integrity for user loads [2]. The leveling course must achieve the minimum cross‑slope required for drainage and vehicle stability, even when its thickness is used solely for slope correction and does not count toward overall overlay depth [1].

Verification and expected performance

To verify that a completed leveling course meets all project requirements, the Inspector‑in‑Charge first confirms that no single compacted lift exceeds six inches and that each lift falls within the three‑to‑six inch range; if the depth would be greater than six inches it must be placed in two or more layers of roughly equal thickness, none less than three inches. Immediately after placement the exposed outer edges are worked to remove any sharp, ragged or open edges, providing a finished appearance and reducing edge breakdown. Compaction is performed under the supervision of a certified asphalt field technician who ensures that the mixture attains the required density and shows no roller marks. The method used to accept density depends on the course’s thickness and weight: lifts thinner than one inch or weighing 110 lb/yd² or less are accepted by non‑movement, meaning the mix must not shift under the compaction equipment; thicker or heavier lifts require an optimum‑rolling pattern in which a calibrated nuclear or electrical impedance gauge is used in the presence of the Representative to establish and document the target density, with re‑compaction performed if the target is not achieved. When the compacted depth satisfies the minimum values shown in Table G and the base is uniform and stable, pavement cores may be extracted for laboratory verification of bulk specific gravity and density. Surface performance is further evaluated by measuring macro‑texture according to PTM No. 751; any pattern segregation that creates a texture‑depth difference greater than 0.024 inch is deemed unacceptable, and flushing is identified if the measured depth falls below the specified limit. Finally, mixture lot acceptance samples are taken from each sublot for laboratory testing of asphalt content, gradation and theoretical maximum specific gravity. All density‑acceptance forms, gauge calibration certificates and field observations are submitted to the Representative as evidence that the leveling course complies with the project’s performance, constructability and consistency requirements.
