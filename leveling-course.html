Leveling Course

Part of: Overlay is-subtopic-of
Leveling Course is a topic under Overlay.
leveling-courseleveling-coursesleveling-materialscratch-and-leveling-coursescratch-and-leveling-courses
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-30

The leveling course is a corrective asphalt layer placed whenever an existing pavement surface fails to meet required geometric or smoothness criteria—such as wheel‑rutting, localized depressions, erratic longitudinal profile, insufficient cross‑slope, or excess thickness—that would otherwise compromise the placement and performance of subsequent layers [1][2]. By spreading a minimum one‑inch (and often up to six inches) compacted Superpave mix—sometimes in two or more lifts when greater depth is needed—and, for projected traffic exceeding ten million ESALs, incorporating a polymer‑modified binder such as PG 64E‑22, the course restores the intended grade and cross‑section, provides a uniformly stable, drainable base, and is compacted using an optimum‑rolling pattern to meet density tolerances [1][2]. Treated as a non‑structural geometry fix excluded from overlay thickness calculations, the leveling course ensures proper cross‑slope, smoothness, ride quality, and reliable adhesion for the overlying binder, wearing or block courses [1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

The leveling course is applied whenever the existing pavement surface does not meet required geometric or smoothness criteria—such as wheel‑rutting, localized depressions, an erratic longitudinal profile, insufficient cross‑slope, or a rough milled face—that would otherwise impede proper placement and performance of subsequent layers. [1][2] It also addresses isolated thickness excesses that exceed the allowable compacted depth, typically more than six inches, which must be removed to re‑establish a uniform working platform before full‑depth paving can continue. [2] By placing at least a one‑inch layer of suitable material, the leveling course restores the intended grade and cross‑sectional shape, provides a uniformly stable base that supplies even support to the binder, wearing or block courses, and ensures adequate drainage. [1][2] The corrective layer is essential for achieving the smoothness, ride quality, structural performance, and reliable adhesion required by the new pavement while being treated as a non‑structural geometry fix excluded from overlay thickness calculations. [1] When traffic loading exceeds ten million equivalent single axle loads and severe rutting potential exists—such as in stopping zones or on steep grades—the guides require the use of a polymer‑modified binder, for example PG 64E‑22, within the leveling course to resist permanent deformation and thermal cracking. [1] If the required thickness of the leveling material would be greater than six inches compacted, the construction is divided into two or more lifts, each lift being no less than three inches and no greater than six inches, to achieve the target profile without compromising compaction quality. [2] In block paving applications a six‑inch loose bedding material graded parallel to the roadway’s longitudinal profile and cleared of debris and water serves as the leveling course that supports the first layer of blocks on a stable plane. [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89), Ch. 5 (pp. 111–121), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 182–184), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 269–336)

The primary functional objective of a leveling course is to eliminate existing rutting or surface unevenness and to create a uniformly level, stable platform that restores the intended longitudinal profile, cross‑section and cross‑slope needed for safe drainage and rideability, and it must be installed before any resurfacing work wherever irregularities are identified. [1][2] The design requires a minimum thickness of one inch so that the course can be treated as a one‑inch paving layer in structural analysis, while many specifications also call for a six‑inch loose bedding layer that complies with Section 219.2(e) to provide additional load‑bearing capacity for the subsequent binder or wearing courses placed at uniform depth. [1][2] Structurally, the course is placed parallel to the roadway’s longitudinal profile; when a fiberglass mesh grid is incorporated the thickness must be at least twice the nominal maximum aggregate size, and the surface must be dry, clean and free of depressions, dirt, water, oil or other contaminants prior to placement. [2][1] The mix design must follow the Superpave volumetric procedure to resist the climatic and traffic conditions anticipated for a twenty‑year design life, providing resistance to permanent deformation and low‑temperature cracking, and it must also satisfy specified air‑void, VMA, sieve‑gradation and asphalt‑content limits, with draindown sensitivity evaluated when total asphalt content exceeds 7 % and fibers added as required. [1][2] When the projected cumulative traffic exceeds ten million equivalent single axle loads, a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder is mandated to prevent severe rutting, and on grades steeper than five percent the mix must be proportioned for adequate compaction and shear‑rut stability. [1] Durability is further ensured by maintaining a clean, dry surface before block placement, applying an asphalt tack coat at a temperature between 290 °F and 320 °F while keeping the underlying surface below 140 °F, and certifying each batch daily against single‑sample and multiple‑sample JMF tolerances to preserve structural integrity throughout service. [2][1] Safety considerations require that traffic be limited to construction vehicles until the final overlay is placed, protecting the freshly installed course from premature loading, and that the prepared surface remain free of contaminants so full adhesion can be achieved and delamination avoided. [2][1] Serviceability is achieved by providing a sufficiently smooth surface after milling or leveling such that the overlying interlayer can bond without debonding or slippage, maintaining the required grade and cross‑slope throughout the service period, with ongoing quality‑control certification supporting continued performance. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 5 (pp. 111–121); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 182–184), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 248–319)

Scope and applicability

Leveling courses are employed on both asphalt‑concrete and Portland cement concrete pavements when the existing surface must be corrected to provide a level, stable platform prior to an overlay or other surface treatment [1]. They are also indicated for flexible‑pavement projects whenever a thin corrective layer is needed to achieve uniformity before placement of binder or wearing courses [2]. The minimum thickness required to correct cross‑slope, wheel ruts, local depressions, or an erratic longitudinal profile is one inch, and this layer must remain separate from any binder or wearing course to preserve quality control [1]. When the corrective layer is less than one inch in depth or weighs no more than 110 lb per square yard, an optimum‑rolling pattern is not required; layers of one inch or greater (or weighing at least 110 lb/yd²) must be compacted using an optimum‑rolling pattern [2]. If the intended corrective layer would exceed six inches in compacted depth, it must be placed in two or more lifts, each lift being no less than three inches and no greater than six inches thick, to eliminate isolated high spots before a full‑depth overlay [2]. In projects where estimated annual average daily traffic expressed in equivalent single axle loads exceeds ten million ESALs and severe rutting potential exists because of frequent stopping conditions or steep grades, a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder is specified for the leveling (or binder) course to prevent permanent deformation [1]. A separate pre‑overlay leveling course is required whenever a paving grid system other than composite grids is installed, when an interlayer follows milling that left a rough surface, or when rutting must be remedied before placement of an interlayer; in the latter case the leveling layer can eliminate the need for a tack coat if the grid and overlay are placed on the same day [1]. For rigid concrete pavements the course serves solely to adjust geometry to meet minimum cross‑slope requirements, does not contribute structural capacity, and its thickness is excluded from overlay depth calculations and from usual overlay thickness limits [1]. When underlying concrete pavement is cracked‑and‑seated, a Base Course or Superpave Base Course is used as the base repair beneath the leveling layer; when rubblized concrete is present, AASHTO #1 aggregate is placed beneath the one‑inch leveling course [1]. If micro‑surfacing is selected instead of a Superpave scratch or surface course, approximately thirty percent of the material quantity required for a comparable Superpave leveling course is sufficient, and it may be applied as a nonsurface rut‑filling layer or as a re‑profiling surface layer [1]. For moderate ruts ranging from one‑half inch to roughly 1¼ inches deep, a Type B polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing mix can be used as a leveling or scratch coat, with the option of double application to meet weight specifications [2]. Projects that involve expanded polystyrene (EPS) block fills require a six‑inch loose bedding material graded parallel to the longitudinal profile and cleared of debris and water as a leveling course before the first layer of blocks is placed [2]. The approach is suitable for narrow pavement sections less than four feet wide where equipment bridging could otherwise occur, and it is commonly employed in low‑critical applications such as patching, road widening, shoulder adjustments, driveway modifications, parking lots, and other miscellaneous work where density control is not critical [2]. The department permits the use of leveling courses when the total placement quantity is under 350 tons in a continuous operation unless directed otherwise by the Representative, and for any other conditions specifically approved by that Representative [2].

When an unbonded concrete overlay is specified, the asphalt separation layer cannot serve as a leveling course; all profile adjustments must be incorporated into the concrete overlay itself, making that approach preferable to applying a distinct leveling layer [1]. If projected traffic exceeds ten million equivalent single axle loads or severe rutting is anticipated because of frequent stopping locations or steep grades, a conventional leveling course does not provide sufficient rut resistance and the design should employ a polymer‑modified Superpave binder (PG 64E‑22), with fiber‑modified mixes deemed unacceptable as substitutes [1]. When the existing pavement base is stable and material removal will not degrade performance—or when major adjustments to curbs, shoulders, guide rails, or drainage structures are required—the guides recommend alternative treatments such as heater planing or milling rather than a separate leveling course [1]. In cases where rutting already exists in the surface, correction by milling is allowed instead of placing a leveling course before an interlayer; if the milled surface achieves adequate smoothness, the interlayer can be placed directly without a leveling layer [1]. For projects with grades steeper than five percent or with sharp curves and slopes, mix design for compaction and shear‑rut stability takes precedence over using a leveling course, requiring specific binder selection rather than a generic leveling layer [1]. When the required compacted depth of a leveling course would exceed six inches, a single thick layer is not permitted; the work must be split into two or more layers each between three and six inches in compacted depth, or a scratch (or other) course should be used to treat isolated spots deeper than six inches before proceeding with full‑depth paving [2]. Isolated areas where the compacted depth would exceed six inches also trigger the use of a scratch or leveling course to eliminate those spots prior to full‑depth paving, rather than applying an overly thick single layer [2].

Design inputs and requirements

The design of a leveling course requires that the existing pavement condition be fully assessed: any potholes or cracks must be repaired, the surface cleaned of dirt, water, oil and other contaminants, rutting corrected by milling or a prior leveling layer, and the grade and cross‑slope confirmed. Environmental constraints dictate that the pavement be moisture‑free, installation not occur during precipitation, and ambient and surface temperatures fall within the limits specified (generally 40 °F to 140 °F for fiberglass mesh grid with adhesive). Material considerations are limited to these temperature requirements; the source does not provide site, traffic or geotechnical data. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (p. 120)

The design and placement of a leveling course are governed by a suite of state specifications, national procedures, and federal policies that together define the allowable materials, thicknesses, and performance criteria for pavement surface correction [1][2]. PennDOT Publication 408 Restricted Performance Specification (Section 413) requires that any surface‑correction or cross‑slope adjustment be accomplished with a dedicated leveling course and that the existing pavement beneath an overlay be stable, properly leveled, sealed and patched before new surfacing is placed [1]. AASHTO design procedures, as implemented in the 1993 Pavement Design Guide and DARWin, set the structural design life between eight and twenty years and prescribe the traffic data and ESAL calculations that drive binder selection for the leveling course [1]. Federal‑aid Highway Program policy on pavement management further defines the overall project scope and mandates submission of Form D‑4332 for PDAU concurrence and FHWA approval [1]. The minimum thickness for a leveling course is specified as one inch in the rehabilitation specifications, while another guide requires that the thickness be at least twice the nominal maximum aggregate size, creating complementary requirements that must both be satisfied [1][2]. When the compacted depth of the course exceeds six inches, the specification mandates construction in two or more layers each no less than three inches and no more than six inches thick, and any isolated spot deeper than six inches must first receive a scratch or leveling treatment before full‑depth paving can proceed [2]. The material used for the leveling course must be a Superpave asphalt mix that complies with the gradation and size limits of the applicable tables, and may incorporate approved base materials such as Base Course/Superpave Base Course or AASHTO #1 aggregate where appropriate [1][2]. Binder selection follows the Superpave system; when projected 20‑year ESALs exceed ten million, a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder (or equivalent) is required, with optional aramid‑fiber modification permitted under a special provision [1]. Mixture acceptance is governed by Section 413.2(f), which requires each batch to be certified daily or accepted by lot in accordance with the single‑sample and multiple‑sample JMF tolerances for asphalt content and fine‑aggregate passing a 75‑µm sieve, and to meet the volumetric limits of the specified tables [2]. Density acceptance depends on course thickness and weight: courses less than one inch deep or weighing 110 lb/yd² or less may use non‑movement approval, whereas deeper or heavier courses require optimum‑rolling pattern acceptance measured with an approved nuclear gauge (PTM 402) or electrical impedance gauge (PTM 403) calibrated annually per ASTM D2950 and recorded on the required forms [2]. Ambient temperature limits are imposed for placement; surface temperatures must be between 40 °F and 140 °F when installing fiberglass mesh grids, and in all cases the surface temperature at placement may not exceed 140 °F [1][2]. The existing pavement surface must be dry, clean, free of dirt, water, oil, foreign material, vegetation or gravel, and any excess blotting sand must be removed before the leveling course is placed; installation may not occur during precipitation or when weather conditions do not meet manufacturer recommendations for interlayer systems [1][2]. For projects with grades steeper than five percent, the asphalt mix for the leveling course must be proportioned to achieve adequate compaction and shear‑rut stability, and a minimum binder layer of 2½ inches is required when an asphalt overlay is placed over concrete or brick for the first time [1][2]. All interlayer materials must be stored wrapped, protected from weather, elevated off the ground, labeled in accordance with ASTM D4873, and placement may proceed only after the project representative confirms compliance with manufacturer‑specified weather constraints [2]. Any design exception involving a leveling course must be documented according to the relevant design manual sections and reviewed by the Highway Design Technical Services (HDTS), while district pavement‑management guidance may impose additional project‑specific constraints such as cross‑slope criteria or minimum structural thicknesses for general courses [1].

Design methods and criteria

The design of a leveling course begins with an analysis of traffic and climatic conditions that yields a projected 20‑year equivalent single axle load (ESAL) count, which is placed into one of five predefined ESAL ranges to drive the Superpave volumetric mixture design and to select the appropriate performance‑grade binder; when the ESAL estimate exceeds ten million, a polymer‑modified binder such as PG 64E‑22 is specified to mitigate severe rutting, and the resulting aggregate, filler, and asphalt proportions are limited to no more than three distinct mix designs on a single project [1]. Once the Superpave mixture is defined, the required material for the leveling layer is obtained from the Superpave leveling rate, and when micro‑surfacing is used in place of a conventional scratch or surface course only about thirty percent of that standard rate is applied—for example, a nominal 90 lb/yd² rate becomes roughly 27 lb/yd²—and the design calls for selecting either a Type A or Type B scratch course together with the corresponding square yards of matching surface course as indicated in the applicable tables; application rates for micro‑surfacing employed as a rut filler are distinguished between non‑surface leveling before an overlay and re‑profiling when it serves as the surface layer [1]. The leveling course is placed as a separate preparatory layer prior to any binder or wearing course, providing at least a one‑inch minimum thickness that creates a uniform working platform to correct longitudinal profile and restore cross‑section, and this thickness is incorporated into overall structural calculations using the structural coefficient, minimum and maximum placement depths supplied in the standard tables [1][2]. The material is first spread as a six‑inch loose bedding that must satisfy the relevant specification, then graded so that its surface runs parallel to the roadway’s longitudinal profile and cleared of debris, water, or depressions before blocks or subsequent layers are placed [2]. Compacted depth is limited such that no single lift exceeds six inches; if a greater total thickness is required the course is built up in two or more roughly equal lifts, each lift being at least three inches and no more than six inches thick, and isolated spots deeper than six inches are first treated with a scratch or separate leveling layer before full‑depth paving proceeds [2][1]. Courses less than one inch in depth or weighing 110 lb/yd² or less are accepted by the non‑movement method, whereas thicker or heavier courses require an optimum‑rolling pattern whose target density is determined by a certified field technician and the Inspector‑in‑Charge using an approved nuclear gauge in accordance with PTM 402 or an approved electrical impedance gauge per PTM 403, with gauges calibrated annually to ASTM D2950 and operated by a licensed operator; the rolling pattern is documented on the appropriate form and, if the target density is not achieved, a new pattern is established and paving may be suspended until compliance [2]. The mixture for the leveling course follows volumetric analysis of compacted specimens in accordance with AASHTO T 312, from which bulk specific gravity is used to compute air voids (V_a) and Voids in Mineral Aggregate (VMA) at the design density using the AASHTO R‑35 equations referenced in Bulletin 27, and the resulting values must fall within the tolerances listed in Table C; daily certification of the mix is permitted when measured asphalt content and the percent passing the No. 200 sieve satisfy the single‑sample and multiple‑sample limits set out in Table B for scratch, leveling, and driveway adjustments, with any deviation triggering corrective action before further production [2]. When a fiberglass mesh grid is to be placed on the leveling course an empirical thickness requirement applies: the compacted layer must be at least twice the nominal maximum aggregate size (NMAS), and the surface temperature of the course must be below 140 °F prior to installation, with the surface remaining dry, clean and free of depressions to provide a drainable base for the grid [2]. For overlay projects the standard 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures are executed through the DARWin software, which uses input traffic data such as average daily traffic, growth rates, and truck percentages to compute the necessary overall pavement thicknesses, and Table 10.5 is consulted to select the appropriate Superpave mixture size for the leveling course; when a bonded concrete overlay is part of the design the mechanistic‑empirical BCOA‑ME procedure replaces the conventional AASHTO method while still observing the same minimum and maximum layer‑thickness limits from the standard tables [1]. Appropriate application of the leveling course is further verified against traffic‑volume limits contained in the designated tables for average daily traffic [1].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 106–120), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–177); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 182–184), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 248–319)

A leveling course is treated as a distinct asphalt layer whose primary purpose is to provide a uniformly level and stable platform for any subsequent overlay, and its design must satisfy several quantitative criteria. The minimum compacted thickness is one inch regardless of the type of overlay, and this thickness may be counted in the structural analysis as a one‑inch paving course while remaining subject to the minimum‑and‑maximum limits established for resurfacing layers; the maximum compacted thickness shall not exceed six inches, and if greater depth is required it must be placed in two or more layers of approximately equal depth, each layer being at least three inches and no more than six inches thick [1][2]. When the existing pavement is concrete or brick, a minimum of 2½ inches of binder material must first be placed before the leveling layer is applied, and the course may be provided solely to meet cross‑slope requirements on rigid pavements, in which case it is excluded from depth calculations and does not count toward overlay thickness restrictions [1]. The mix used for the leveling course must be designed under the Superpave system and must meet the same mix‑design limits as other paving courses; no reduction in thickness is permitted when fiber‑modified mixes are employed, and the specific Type B polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt specified for filling moderate ruts (½ to 1¼ inch) may be used, with a double‑pass application allowed to achieve the required design pounds per square yard [1][2]. If the 20‑year traffic forecast expressed in equivalent single axle loads exceeds ten million ESALs, a polymer‑modified binder such as PG 64E‑22 is mandated to guard against severe rutting, and the ESAL calculation must be based on an eight‑to‑twenty‑year structural design life using reliable traffic inputs such as average daily traffic, growth rates, and truck percentages [1]. For grades steeper than five percent the mix proportions are required to provide adequate compaction and shear‑rut stability, ensuring that the course can resist the additional stresses imposed by the slope [1]. The leveling course must correct any existing cross‑slope deficiency and may be used as the sole material for re‑establishing proper longitudinal profile; however, it cannot be combined with a binder layer because such blending would compromise quality control, and it is mandatory whenever rutting is present, on all concrete pavements, and on virtually every paving‑grid application except composite grids, while being optional after milling when the milled surface already achieves the required smoothness [1]. Installation assumptions require that the existing surface be stable under roller loads, clean, dry, and free of moisture or precipitation at the time of placement; ambient temperature must satisfy specified limits and pavement temperature during placement must remain between 40 °F and 140 °F (below 140 °F for newly placed asphalt), with the same temperature range applying when a fiberglass mesh grid with self‑adhesive glue is used [1][2]. When the compacted depth is at least one inch or the weight reaches or exceeds 110 lb per square yard, the course must be compacted using an optimum rolling pattern that is verified with a calibrated nuclear or electrical impedance gauge and documented on the appropriate forms; if the depth is less than one inch or the weight does not exceed this threshold, acceptance may be based on non‑movement of the mixture under compaction equipment [2]. Small placements not exceeding 500 tons in a continuous operation, as well as work on unstable bases, are treated as non‑critical and may be accepted by either method at the Representative’s discretion provided all density measurements are performed in the presence of the Representative [2]. Each day’s batch of leveling‑course mix must be certified; certification is granted only when single‑sample and multiple‑sample JMF production tolerances for asphalt content, percent passing the No. 200 sieve, and the volumetric tolerances of Table C are met, with a lower quantity threshold of 350 tons allowing acceptance unless directed otherwise, and any quality‑control result outside these limits requires corrective action before the mix can be certified [2]. A six‑inch loose layer of bedding material conforming to Section 219.2(e) must be placed, graded parallel to the longitudinal profile, and its surface must be completely free of debris and water before any blocks are installed; if a fiberglass mesh grid is to be installed on the leveling course, the course thickness must be at least twice the nominal maximum aggregate size and its surface temperature must not exceed 140 °F [2]. Overall, the design assumes that the material will achieve the required smoothness, shear strength, and rut resistance throughout the prescribed service life, thereby satisfying the reliability expectations of the pavement system.

Structural section and dimensions

Guidelines for a leveling course differ in the prescribed minimum thickness. One source sets the lower bound at one inch and does not impose any further dimensional limits on the pavement surface, base, subbase, treated‑soil layer, slab, shoulder, edge support or reinforcement associated with the course [1]. Another source requires the leveling course to be a six‑inch loose bedding layer that must satisfy the criteria of Section 219.2(e) and be graded parallel to the longitudinal profile of the roadway, while also stating that no additional size requirements are prescribed for the pavement, base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab, shoulder, edge support or reinforcement [2]. When a fiberglass mesh grid is placed on the leveling course, the second guide adds that the course thickness must be at least twice the nominal maximum aggregate size and its surface temperature must be kept below 140 °F; this condition does not appear in the first source [2].

Materials and mixture design

The leveling course shall be constructed from either a Superpave asphalt‑concrete mix or a Micro‑Surfacing product, and when the latter is selected its quantity is limited to roughly thirty percent of that required for an equivalent Superpave mix, which translates to about 27 lb per square yard compared with 90 lb per square yard for Superpave. The asphalt material must be identified by a performance‑grade (PG) binder chosen on the basis of the projected design traffic over a twenty‑year period, and if the anticipated cumulative ESALs exceed ten million the specification requires a polymer‑modified binder such as PG 64E‑22 or, when fiber modification is used, an eligible binder like PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22. The course must have a minimum installed thickness of one inch; any layer that meets or exceeds this thickness—or that weighs at least 110 lb per square yard—must satisfy density acceptance using an optimum‑rolling pattern, whereas thinner or lighter courses may be accepted by non‑movement density criteria. Approved Superpave aggregate sizes for the mix include 25 mm, 19 mm, 12.5 mm and 9.5 mm, each placed at its prescribed minimum depth to achieve the required cross‑sectional profile. When the pavement grade exceeds five percent, the mixture proportions shall be adjusted in accordance with the interlayer manufacturer’s guidance to improve compaction and shear‑rut stability. The material composition also requires a six‑inch loose bedding layer that conforms to the specifications for Section 219.2(e), graded parallel to the roadway’s longitudinal profile, and its surface must be kept free of debris and water prior to placement of the leveling mix. Daily certification of the leveling mixture is mandatory; the asphalt content and the percentage passing a 75‑µm (No. 200) sieve must fall within the prescribed acceptance limits, and volumetric properties such as bulk specific gravity, air voids and voids in mineral aggregate measured at the design density must satisfy the tolerances set out in Table C. The specification may permit clean blotting sand or broadcast asphalt pavement material as a base, but any asphalt placed shall be applied at the proper temperature to ensure waterproofing and bond integrity, and the leveling course shall not be combined with a binder course to preserve quality control. The first step in selecting a leveling course is to calculate the 20‑year design traffic expressed as equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) for the segment and place that value into one of the five Superpave ESAL ranges defined by the guides. That range determines the required binder grade, with any range exceeding ten million ESALs mandating a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder to protect against severe rutting, while lower ranges may use binders appropriate to their assigned class and can be adjusted when mix quantities are small. When an eligible polymer‑modified binder such as PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22, or PG 64E‑22 is selected, the addition of aramid fiber may be specified to enhance crack resistance. The leveling course must provide a uniform platform at least one inch thick, and that thickness is incorporated in the structural calculations for the pavement section. To achieve the required depth while satisfying structural coefficient limits, the aggregate size is chosen from the standard Superpave gradations of 25 mm, 19 mm, 12.5 mm, or 9.5 mm and placed within the minimum‑maximum depths given in the appropriate tables. The number of distinct mix designs on a project is limited to three, with major projects allowed up to five, in order to control cost and maintain constructability. Once the binder and aggregate size are fixed, the mixture is designed using the Superpave volumetric procedure and must meet the bulk specific gravity, air‑voids, and voids‑in‑mixture‑asphalt (VMA) tolerances prescribed in Table C. In addition, the sieve gradation and asphalt content of the mix must fall within the limits set out in Table B. Production quality control requires sampling each batch according to the QC plan, preparing specimens per AASHTO T 312, and calculating air voids and VMA as required by AASHTO R‑35. The mixture is certified on a daily basis only when both single‑sample (n=1) and multiple‑sample results for asphalt content and the percent passing the 75 µm sieve satisfy the tolerances of Table B. For mixes containing more than seven percent total asphalt, a draindown sensitivity test is performed at the maximum mix temperature reduced by five degrees Fahrenheit from the value in Table B, using AASHTO T 305. If the draindown result exceeds the limits of Table A, fibers or other stabilizers are incorporated to mitigate the problem before production continues. Any deviation from the specified tolerances triggers immediate corrective action and re‑sampling within 150 tons of production to ensure that durability and performance criteria are maintained. The guides discourage placing a separate binder layer beneath the leveling course because it compromises quality control and therefore undermines constructability and long‑term performance. [1][2]

Structural details and interfaces

The leveling course requires longitudinal joints that are offset about six inches from the joint in the underlying layer. For two‑lane roads the joint should lie near the pavement centreline, and on multi‑lane highways it must be within twelve inches of a lane line. Prior to compaction the joint area is painted with a uniform coat of the same asphalt binder used for the course, typically PG 64S‑22, and after placement the joint is sealed with a hot band of that binder approximately four inches wide at the prescribed thickness. No additional reinforcement or load‑transfer devices are mandated beyond these joint preparations. When reinforcement is specified, the leveling course must be laid on a clean, dry, drainable surface and its thickness increased to at least twice the nominal maximum aggregate size; the surface temperature during placement must not exceed 140 °F. Reinforcement may consist of geotextile paving fabric, fiberglass mesh grid, or a composite reinforcing grid, each installed according to the manufacturer’s overlap and coverage guidelines. All interlayer sheets are overlapped by a minimum of one inch, with tack coat applied over the overlap extending six inches beyond each side of the reinforcement width, creating a continuous asphalt film that ties the reinforcement to the surrounding course. No special load‑transfer systems or unique connections are required beyond these joint sealing and reinforcement detailing, ensuring proper alignment, continuity, and structural integrity of the leveling course. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 276–319)

Designing the interface for a leveling course begins with thorough cleaning of the existing pavement surface, removing all dirt, water, oil, foreign material, loose debris, excess joint sealer and crack filler to provide a moisture‑free substrate [1][2]. Any rutting or localized low spots must be eliminated either by milling or by placing the leveling course itself before the next layer is installed, and when milling has been performed a leveling course may be required prior to applying an asphalt interlayer [1]. The prepared surface must remain completely moisture‑free, work should not be conducted during precipitation, and the placement temperature must fall within product limits—for example between 40 °F and 140 °F when a fiberglass mesh grid is used [1]. Prior to placing the leveling material a clean blotting sand or broadcast asphalt bedding is spread over the area and excess sand removed to create a uniform substrate, and for loose‑bedding approaches a six‑inch layer of bedding material meeting Section 219.2(e) requirements is graded parallel to the longitudinal profile [1][2]. Vertical faces that will contact the new mix—such as curbs, gutters, guide rails, structures and adjoining pavements—are painted with a uniform coating of either emulsified asphalt (PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT applied in two or more passes) or an approved hot binder to provide a consistent bonding surface [2]. A tack coat is then applied in accordance with Section 460 unless the contract specifies otherwise, establishing an adhesive layer between the existing substrate and the forthcoming leveling material; for self‑adhesive paving grids a tack coat is used only when specifically required by the manufacturer [1][2]. When the required compacted depth of the leveling course exceeds six inches it must be placed in two or more layers of approximately equal thickness, each layer no less than three inches and no greater than six inches, and any isolated spots that would otherwise exceed this limit are first treated with a scratch coat or an additional leveling layer before full‑depth paving [2]. For asphalt overlays over cracked or rubblized concrete the leveling course must be at least one inch thick to correct cross‑slope and serves as the critical interface between the repaired concrete base and the new overlay, while being coordinated with continuous pavement‑base drains and any guide rail or drainage structures that are reset or replaced to preserve proper alignment [1]. On grades steeper than five percent the asphalt mix used for the leveling course is proportioned to enhance compaction and shear‑rut stability [1]. After compacting the leveling layer, a tack coat or asphalt overlay is applied at the temperature and application rate recommended by the interlayer manufacturer, taking into account existing pavement condition, overlay type and ambient temperature to ensure waterproofing performance and prevent debonding or slippage of the overlying material [1]. In areas adjacent to flush curbs, gutters and other abutting structures the wearing‑course mixture is built up slightly higher so that after compaction the finished surface sits just above the edge of the structure, and the joint is sealed with hot asphalt applied over a minimum width of twelve inches from the curb to create a watertight transition [2]. The surface of the completed leveling course must be free of debris and water before placement of blocks or overlying geotextile, geomembrane and capping materials, guaranteeing full contact between subgrade and the first block layer and allowing proper development of subsequent interfaces without gaps [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Before installing the leveling course the pavement’s longitudinal grade and cross‑slope must already be established so that water can shed away from the surface [1][2]. The course itself is placed as a six‑inch loose bedding layer that must meet the material specifications of Section 219.2(e) [2]. It is spread and graded in line with the roadway’s longitudinal profile, creating a surface that is free of depressions and capable of draining water rather than allowing ponding [2][1]. The surface to receive the course, as well as the top of the placed material before any paving units are set, must be clean, dry and devoid of standing water or debris [1][2]. Placement is prohibited when the underlying pavement is wet or when precipitation is occurring, ensuring that moisture does not become trapped between layers [1][2]. Ambient conditions must fall within the temperature range approved by the interlayer manufacturer; one guide specifies a permissible air temperature of 40 °F to 140 °F for the particular fiberglass‑mesh, self‑adhesive system [1][2]. If the ambient temperature or other weather parameters lie outside the approved limits, work must be suspended until conditions improve [2][1]. During storage and handling the leveling material is kept wrapped, elevated and covered to shield it from precipitation, ultraviolet radiation, chemicals and other environmental influences that could degrade its performance [2]. By maintaining a drainable base without depressions, keeping all surfaces dry, observing the specified temperature window, and protecting the material from adverse weather during storage, the leveling course can fulfill its function of providing a smooth, stable platform while resisting moisture‑related distress [1][2].

A leveling course must provide a level, stable platform that meets the required grade and smoothness in order to support safe and accessible travel. It is the only layer permitted to correct an erratic longitudinal profile, re‑establish the proper cross‑section, and set the required cross‑slope of the roadway. Minimum thickness is one inch, but geometric criteria also require that the course be at least twice the nominal maximum aggregate size of the underlying material. When compacted depth exceeds six inches, the course must be placed in two or more lifts of approximately equal depth, each lift being no less than three inches and no more than six inches thick, and any isolated area that would exceed a six‑inch compacted depth must first be treated with a scratch or an additional leveling layer before full‑depth paving proceeds. For grades steeper than five percent the asphalt mix used for the leveling course should be proportioned to ensure proper compaction and shear‑rut stability, thereby maintaining geometric integrity and vehicle stability. The surface of the existing pavement must be completely clean, dry, free of dirt, water, oil, or other foreign material before placement, and work must be halted during precipitation to keep the pavement moisture‑free; the surface temperature during placement may not exceed 140 °F. All rutting must be removed either by milling or by placing a leveling course prior to interlayer installation, and if milling is performed the smoothness of the milled surface determines whether an additional leveling layer is required. For first‑time asphalt overlays on cement concrete or brick pavements a minimum of 2 ½ inches of binder material is required, although within parking lanes the wearing course may taper to one inch at the curb provided that compaction is adequate and the pavement‑curb joint is sealed to preserve geometric continuity and user safety. The leveling course must be graded parallel to the roadway’s longitudinal profile and must achieve the minimum cross‑slope required for drainage and vehicle stability, satisfying both geometric and safety criteria. No safety edge treatment is applied to leveling, base, or scratch courses, and the specifications do not prescribe separate accessibility or user‑related criteria for this layer beyond the general requirement for a smooth, level surface that supports safe travel. Verification and expected performance

The completed leveling course is subjected to a sequence of checks that together verify compliance with design tolerances, constructability, performance expectations, and overall project consistency. First, the physical characteristics are examined: if the layer is less than one inch thick or does not exceed 110 lb per square yard, the Inspector‑in‑Charge may accept it by non‑movement; when the thickness reaches or exceeds one inch or the weight meets or surpasses 110 lb/yd², an optimum‑rolling pattern must be established and executed. This rolling is performed with a licensed nuclear gauge—or an approved electrical impedance gauge—while the Representative and the contractor’s certified field technician are present, and the pattern is recorded on the required TR‑4276B or TR‑4276C forms together with calibration certificates. Concurrently, the course must consist of a six‑inch loose bedding layer that satisfies Section 219.2(e), be graded parallel to the roadway’s longitudinal profile, and present a surface free of debris and standing water before any block placement, confirming that it can be built without obstruction and will function as intended. Daily mix certification further ensures material quality: samples taken according to the producer’s QC plan are prepared per AASHTO T‑312 and tested against the asphalt content, percent passing the 75 µm sieve, and the volumetric limits for bulk specific gravity, air voids, and VMA as listed in Tables B and C. Results are compared with both single‑sample and multiple‑sample JMF tolerances; any failure triggers a hold on certification, corrective action, and retesting of a new sample taken within 150 tons of production before work may resume. Acceptance follows the criteria set for scratch courses, leveling courses, and driveway adjustments, and production may be suspended until a written explanation and approved solution are provided.
