California Bearing Ratio

Part of: Subgrade is-subtopic-of
California Bearing Ratio is a topic under Subgrade.
cbr-valuecbr-valuescalifornia-bearing-ratiocbr-test
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

The California Bearing Ratio (CBR) is a laboratory test performed on soaked subgrade, sub‑base or base‑course specimens whose particles are less than three‑quarters of an inch, conducted in accordance with AASHTO T 193 to quantify the material’s bearing strength [1]. The resulting numeric CBR value is converted to an estimated resilient modulus for pavement design through the Heukelom‑Klomp relationship (M_r = 1500 × CBR), and it also guides decisions on subgrade improvement, with values above ten or below five triggering higher‑level review and approval by geotechnical engineers [1]. Because CBR is used only when supported by companion laboratory tests—soil classification, gradation, Atterberg limits, maximum dry density and optimum moisture content—it serves as a calibrated indicator of subgrade load‑bearing capacity while acknowledging its limitations at extreme values (greater than twenty may not be employed in the modulus correlation) [1].

Purpose and investigation objectives

The investigation evaluates the load‑bearing capacity of the subgrade soil as expressed by its California Bearing Ratio value obtained from soaked laboratory samples (section 6.2 SUBGRADE SOILS). It also evaluates the potential strength of cohesive materials—specifically subgrade, subbase and base‑course soils whose particles are less than ¾ inch. The investigation must determine what CBR values actually exist across the project and whether the measured CBR falls within the typical range for the soils present. It must identify if the value is greater than 10 or less than 5, which triggers a higher level of analysis and a required recommendation by the District Geotechnical Engineer, and it must verify that the CBR result is supported by companion laboratory tests such as soil classification, gradation, percent passing #200 sieve, Atterberg limits, Group Index, maximum dry density and optimum moisture content. The investigation must confirm that samples represent cut, fill or at‑grade locations to capture variability along the alignment, determine whether the lowest values occur only in isolated areas or are widespread, identify which soil type provides the controlling (lowest) CBR for design, and assess if any measured values are uncertain enough to require additional laboratory or field testing or subgrade improvement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–150)

The laboratory CBR test on soaked samples yields a numeric value that, through the Heukelom and Klomp equation Mr = 1500 × CBR, provides an estimated resilient modulus for the subgrade; this modulus is used directly in flexible pavement design and converted to an effective modulus of subgrade reaction for rigid pavements. When project subgrade CBR values are greater than 10 a higher level of analysis shall be required and any such value must be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, while CBR values less than 5 designers shall justify any recommended undercutting or other improvement methods. The CBR results also direct where subgrade improvement is required: isolated low‑value zones are targeted for treatment, whereas when lower values are widespread the design adopts the lowest representative CBR so that pavement thickness accommodates the weakest soil type. For rehabilitation overlays the conversion factor is reduced from 1,500 to 1,000, giving a more conservative modulus estimate. No CBR value greater than 20 may be used in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation, preventing overly thin sections. When the tested CBR is uncertain, additional laboratory or field tests such as FWD or DCP are performed to validate the modulus before final design decisions. After verification against companion laboratory tests—soil classification, gradation, percent passing #200 sieve, Atterberg limits, maximum dry density and optimum moisture content—the CBR values give confidence for planning, design, maintenance or rehabilitation decisions. [1]

Scope and applicability

The California Bearing Ratio test is appropriate for characterizing subgrade soils that support interstate and expressway pavements, particularly when a resilient modulus value is unavailable; it is applied to laboratory‑tested, soaked samples of the subgrade material in accordance with AASHTO T 193. It is also used to evaluate the strength of cohesive materials having a maximum particle size less than 3/4 inch for subgrade, subbase and base‑course layers in flexible pavement designs, including new construction, reconstruction projects and rehabilitation overlays where the existing pavement is not removed. For low‑strength sections—specifically CBR values less than five—the test result should trigger a requirement for justified subgrade improvement such as undercutting or stabilization, and any recommendation for significant undercutting must be documented. Values above ten require recommendation by the District Geotechnical Engineer and approval by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer; in rehabilitation overlays the same approval by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer applies to all CBR values greater than ten. Moreover, a CBR value greater than twenty may not be used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation. The test results must be corroborated with other soil properties—including classification, gradation, fines content, Atterberg limits, maximum dry density and optimum moisture—and should reflect the specific site condition of the sample location (cut, fill, or at‑grade). [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–150)

The California Bearing Ratio (CBR) has several limitations as a sole indicator of subgrade strength. Values greater than 10 used in pavement designs shall be recommended by the District Geotechnical Engineer and approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, while values less than 5 require justification for significant undercutting or other improvement methods. In addition, any CBR value greater than 20 is not allowed to be used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation (1,500 × 20 = 30,000 psi maximum). Isolated low CBR readings may not represent overall conditions; when the lowest values are located in isolated areas of the project designers should consider improving those zones so that the CBR values become representative of the whole project. Likewise, a single high or low laboratory result that does not appear to be representative of the soil type tested and of other samples should be disregarded in determining the design CBR. Wide ranges of CBR across the project or outliers create considerable uncertainty about the validity of the laboratory‑tested CBR. When such uncertainty exists, additional laboratory or field testing—such as Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) and/or Dynamic Cone Penetration (DCP)—should be performed to back‑calculate the resilient modulus for validation. For rehabilitation projects where in‑situ density is significantly less than maximum dry density, the laboratory CBR may overestimate strength, so a more conservative conversion factor is required. The project CBR results must also be corroborated with a full suite of laboratory tests (soil classification, gradation, fines content, Atterberg limits, etc.) to confirm the estimated resilient modulus. If CBR values are unattainable, designers should consult the Highway Design and Technology Section for other possible options. Consequently, another or complementary investigation method is warranted whenever CBR values fall outside the 5‑10 range, exceed 20 in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation, appear as isolated outliers, show broad variability, or when verification through additional soil property tests indicates uncertainty. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–150)

Existing information and records

The design process depends on laboratory CBR values, so field investigation must close gaps where the recorded CBR does not reflect the full range of subgrade conditions; this includes confirming that samples represent cut, fill, and at‑grade locations and that the measured CBR is supported by companion tests such as soil classification, gradation, fines content, Atterberg limits, maximum dry density, optimum moisture content, and Group Index. When reported CBR values exceed ten or fall below five, a geotechnical engineer review is required to prevent unrealistically high or low resilient modulus estimates, indicating uncertainty that can only be resolved by additional field sampling and verification. The investigation also needs to address any inconsistency between the CBR result and typical value ranges for the identified soil types, ensuring the subgrade’s ability to achieve the estimated Mr. Field investigation is further required when laboratory CBR results show isolated low values, outlier high or low readings, or a wide spread that may not represent the prevailing soil type; such inconsistencies trigger verification of the in‑situ density and back‑calculation of resilient modulus with FWD or DCP testing. If the back‑calculated Mr exceeds 15,000 psi, it must be verified by another form of testing before the value can be used for design. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–150)

Field measurements and testing

Within the subgrade soils section, a Falling Weight Deflectometer may be used in the field to obtain a nondestructive measurement of subgrade deflection and stiffness; the resulting surface‑deflection data are then used to back‑calculate a resilient modulus (M_r) that indicates the elastic response of the subgrade and any underlying stiff layers. When the CBR value is uncertain, such FWD measurements may be employed to identify appropriate locations for collecting samples that will later be tested for CBR or resilient modulus, and if back calculation of M_r from FWD data yields a value above 15,000 pounds per square inch it must be verified by another form of testing prior to its use in the pavement design process. The CBR value itself is derived from soaked laboratory specimens according to AASHTO T 193 but must be corroborated with companion laboratory measurements such as moisture‑density (PTM No. 106), unconfined compressive strength (AASHTO T 208), soil classification (AASHTO M 145) and resilient‑modulus testing (AASHTO T 307). In addition, a Dynamic Cone Penetration test may be employed; the DCP drives a standard cone into the ground with repeated blows and records penetration per blow, providing a continuous in‑situ resistance to shear that can be converted to an M_r or CBR estimate. Resilient‑modulus values around 4,000 pounds per square inch are considered poor, about 8,000 pounds per square inch are considered fair, and approximately 16,000 pounds per square inch are considered high. Both methods are used only where appropriate to support the design CBR. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–150)

Sampling and laboratory investigation

For CBR testing the material to be sampled is the subgrade soil, and samples must be taken from every type of condition present on the project such as cut, fill, or at‑grade sections so that variability along the entire roadway alignment can be evaluated; each representative location shall provide three 50‑pound bags of material for laboratory testing, and the sampling plan should be designed to capture all observed subgrade conditions across the site. No specific depths or sampling frequencies are prescribed in the source beyond ensuring coverage of all condition types. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143)

The California Bearing Ratio test is the sole laboratory test required for representative subgrade, sub‑base or base‑course material—including existing subgrade and borrow soils—when the material consists of cohesive particles whose maximum size does not exceed ¾ inch, and the resulting CBR value is used to develop the resilient modulus and k values needed for pavement design; proper CBR testing must be carried out to substantiate the value used in design calculations, and conversions from Standard Penetration Tests are not permitted. To obtain a California Bearing Ratio value the laboratory must test soaked subgrade samples in accordance with AASHTO T 193, and the result is used together with the Heukelom‑Klomp equation to estimate resilient modulus; the PennDOT Laboratory also runs companion tests on the same material—Moisture Density (PTM 106), Unconfined Compressive Strength (AASHTO T 208), Soil Classification (AASHTO M 145) and Resilient Modulus (AASHTO T 307)—so that the CBR can be verified against classification, gradation, fines content (#200 sieve), Atterberg limits, maximum dry density, optimum moisture content and AASHTO Group Index, which together indicate moisture sensitivity, compressibility and potential for frost‑heave deterioration; when subgrade CBR values exceed 10 or fall below 5 the design requires recommendation by the District Geotechnical Engineer and approval by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer to ensure unrealistic Mr estimates are not used. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–150)

Structural and functional performance

Evaluating structural capacity and functional condition with a CBR approach requires a laboratory CBR value that reflects bearing strength, an assessment of in‑situ density to compare against the lab result, and a resilient modulus (Mr) derived from field measurements; specifically, deflection data collected with a Falling Weight Deflectometer at 9,000 lb load (including center‑point and offset readings) can be back‑calculated to Mr, and Dynamic Cone Penetration testing provides an in‑situ shear‑resistance profile to about five feet depth that can also be converted to Mr for validation. The source does not address other functional measurements such as load‑transfer efficiency, layer thickness, roughness, friction, or ride quality. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Measured CBR values that fall below the controlling soil type’s low end trigger subgrade improvement or strengthening, especially when they are less than 5, to ensure the pavement can sustain the anticipated traffic loads and meet service‑life expectations; design practice requires using the lowest representative CBR (or discarding outliers) so the structure is adequate for all areas, while any CBR greater than 10 must obtain Chief Geotechnical Engineer approval and no CBR greater than 20 may be used in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation (which caps Mr at 30,000 psi), with higher resilient modulus values (20,000–30,000 psi) warned as potentially leading to premature failure. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Data interpretation and diagnosis

During pavement design the CBR result is not used in isolation; visual observations of the subgrade—such as noting whether a sample comes from cut, fill, or at‑grade locations—and field measurements guide where laboratory specimens are taken, and the reported CBR must be cross‑checked against companion lab tests for soil classification, gradation, fines content, Atterberg limits, Group Index, maximum dry density and optimum moisture to confirm that the estimated resilient modulus is realistic. Visual identification of isolated low‑CBR zones leads to a discussion of subgrade improvement and the selection of a design CBR that reflects the controlling soil type. If the CBR exceeds 10, the value shall be recommended by the District Geotechnical Engineer and approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer; all CBR values greater than 10 for use in pavement designs shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer. If it falls below 5, a justification for subgrade improvement must be provided. Laboratory CBR results are screened for representativeness; high or low values that do not appear representative of the tested soil type or other samples are disregarded, and when considerable uncertainty exists additional laboratory and/or field testing is performed. Field testing may consist of FWD and/or DCP testing to back‑calculate Mr for validation. In rehabilitation projects a subgrade whose in‑situ density is significantly less than maximum dry density leads to multiplying the CBR by 1,000 instead of 1,500 in the Heukelom and Klomp equation, and in no case will a CBR value greater than 20 be allowed to be used in that correlation (i.e., 1,500 × 20 = 30,000 psi maximum). Performance thresholds for Mr are observed—values around 4,000 psi are considered poor, 8,000 psi fair, and 16,000 psi high, and any back‑calculated Mr above 15,000 psi requires verification by another test before it can be used in the pavement design. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–150)

The observed low CBR values are most often linked to moisture‑related loss of bearing capacity and weak or heterogeneous subgrade conditions; when the in‑situ density is significantly less than maximum dry density the field CBR will likely be lower than laboratory results, and frost‑susceptible soils combined with water can produce frost heave that further reduces strength. Differential heaving caused by abrupt changes in soil type, groundwater level, or the presence of large stones or shallow bedrock also creates weak support zones that manifest as low CBR readings. In isolated spots where such low values appear, subgrade improvement or strengthening (especially when CBR is below 5) should be considered to bring the subgrade up to a representative value for the project. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150)

Findings, confidence, and next actions

The investigation demonstrates that a laboratory California Bearing Ratio test performed on soaked samples in accordance with AASHTO T 193 can be used in place of a direct resilient‑modulus test to estimate the subgrade resilient modulus for interstate and expressway designs through the relationship Mr (psi) = 1500 × CBR, provided that the result is corroborated by soil classification, gradation, percent passing #200 sieve, Atterberg limits, Group Index, maximum dry density and optimum moisture content. The CBR also can be used to identify subgrade zones that require improvement, to select a conservative design CBR based on the lowest‑bearing soil type, and, when appropriate, to split a highway into separate design sections where distinct CBR values exist; for rehabilitation overlays the in‑situ density is considered by using a multiplier of 1,000 rather than 1,500 in the Heukelom & Klomp equation. All CBR values greater than 10 for use in pavement designs shall be recommended by the District Geotechnical Engineer and approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, and any value greater than 20 may not be used in the Heukelom & Klomp correlation (i.e., 1,500 × 20 = 30,000 psi maximum). CBR values less than 5 are subject to verification that recommendations for significant undercutting or other methods of subgrade improvement are necessary and justified. Laboratory CBR results that appear unrepresentative must be discarded, and when considerable uncertainty exists with regard to the validity of a laboratory‑tested CBR value, additional laboratory and/or field testing such as FWD or DCP is required; back‑calculated resilient modulus from FWD is recognized as unreliable if it exceeds 15,000 psi. Variability among cut, fill or at‑grade samples introduces uncertainty that requires a thorough site‑wide sampling program, and large areas of a project with significantly different soil types and CBR values may be divided into a limited number of subsections with different pavement design sections. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–150)

After a CBR test, the designer shall verify the result with a suite of companion laboratory tests—including moisture‑density, unconfined compressive strength, soil classification, and resilient modulus—to confirm that the subgrade can achieve the estimated Mr; the verification must also examine gradation curves, percent passing the #200 sieve, Atterberg limits, maximum dry density, optimum moisture content, and AASHTO Group Index while ensuring samples represent cut, fill, or at‑grade conditions across the alignment. When project subgrade CBR values are greater than 10, a higher level of analysis is required and the values shall be recommended by the District Geotechnical Engineer and approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer; the same procedural rigor applies when CBR values are less than 5 to justify any undercutting or other improvement measures. If isolated low‑CBR zones are located, consider improving the subgrade in those areas to achieve CBR values that are representative of the overall project conditions and coordinate the improvement details with the District Geotechnical Engineer for inclusion in the PS&E specifications. If a laboratory CBR value appears unrepresentative or shows wide variation, perform additional laboratory and/or field testing—such as Falling Weight Deflectometer or Dynamic Cone Penetration—to back‑calculate Mr for validation, noting that all CBR values greater than 10 shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and no CBR value greater than 20 may be used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation. When uncertainty persists, adopt the lowest controlling soil type’s CBR, may be permissible to divide the project into subsections with different pavement designs where soil types differ markedly, and evaluate subgrade strengthening techniques (cement or lime stabilization, rock capping, geogrids, or geotextiles) especially when CBR is less than 5. Document the complete evaluation of laboratory results, selected CBR, and resulting Mr with the pavement design submission. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–150)
