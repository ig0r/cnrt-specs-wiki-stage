Precast Concrete Pavement

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Precast Concrete Pavement is a topic under Rigid Pavement.
precast-concrete-pavementprecast-slabprecast-slabsprecast-pavement-slabsprecast-pavement
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Precast Concrete Pavement is a high‑strength, durable concrete slab fabricated off‑site and installed rapidly to replace or repair conventional Portland cement concrete pavement, thereby reducing work‑zone exposure and enabling quick traffic reopening[1][2]. It is engineered to meet the same structural loading requirements as standard PCC, using a mix that exceeds Class AA specifications with a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and flexural strength of 650 psi, and it incorporates load‑transfer dowel bars at transverse joints and tie bars along longitudinal joints for uniform support and lane‑to‑lane ties[2]. Each slab is limited to a single travel‑lane width, ranges from 6 ft to 15 ft in length (up to 20 ft for prestressed units), includes a sacrificial ¼‑inch thickness above the nominal design depth, and is delivered at an elevation at least ¼ inch higher than the existing surface so that post‑installation diamond grinding can restore profile and ride quality[1][2]. Joint geometry is tightly controlled—transverse gaps of ¼–½ in, longitudinal gaps ≤¾ in, vertical differential ≤¼ in—and traffic is not permitted until dowel and bedding grouts achieve at least 2,500 psi compressive strength, ensuring safety and long‑term durability comparable to cast‑in‑place concrete[2].

Purpose and design objectives

Precast Concrete Pavement is intended to mitigate the engineering problem of excessive shrinkage cracking that can occur with high‑early‑strength or accelerated concrete patches and to satisfy the need for rapid traffic reopening in time‑sensitive repairs where work‑zone safety is paramount; it does so by providing a high‑strength, durable slab fabricated off‑site and installed quickly, thereby reducing work‑zone exposure and minimum project time [1]. In addition, precast concrete pavement is employed to provide reliable load‑transfer capability, lane‑to‑lane tie reinforcement, and uniform support beneath the slab, addressing deficiencies in existing pavement that require intermittent patching or continuous replacement applications with cast‑in features for establishing load transfer, lane‑to‑lane ties and uniform support under the slabs, including installation of dowel bars at transverse joints [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 426)

Precast Concrete Pavement Systems must be usable in any situation where conventional Portland cement concrete pavement would be specified, thereby satisfying the functional requirement of replacing standard PCC [1]. They are required to be designed to carry the same range of traffic loading conditions as conventional PCC, meeting the structural objective [1]. The concrete mix shall exceed the Class AA Cement Concrete requirements, providing a high‑strength, durable material that fulfills durability goals [1]. Additionally, each slab shall have a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a minimum flexural strength of 650 psi, reinforcing the durability criteria [2]. To accommodate construction tolerances, slabs are cast with an additional 1/4 inch of sacrificial thickness [2]. Joint geometry is limited so that transverse joints are between 1/4 inch and 1/2 inch wide, longitudinal joints do not exceed 3/4 inch, and vertical differentials across any joint do not exceed 1/4 inch; slabs failing these tolerances are rejected, supporting serviceability objectives [2]. The system is expected to provide a safer work zone for both travelers and construction crews, addressing safety objectives [1]. Traffic may not be allowed until the dowel and bedding grouts reach a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi, further ensuring safety for service [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Scope and applicability

Precast Concrete Paving Systems (PCPS) may be employed wherever conventional Portland cement concrete pavement would be specified, including as the primary concrete pavement on high‑speed roadways with very high average daily traffic, as a concrete shoulder adjacent to reinforced or plain cement concrete pavements, and as an alternative to accelerated‑setting concrete for full‑depth patching when minimum project time or work‑zone safety is paramount. Because PCPS is a higher‑cost product, it should not be selected where reasonable detour routes can be provided or where maintenance‑preservation‑technology concerns are insignificant. For intermittent lane‑width repairs the precast slabs must be no more than one lane width wide and have lengths of at least 6 ft but not exceed 15 ft (20 ft for prestressed units), and they should be placed with an as‑delivered elevation at least ¼ in higher than the surrounding pavement to allow post‑installation diamond grinding. The specifications also permit precast concrete pavement slabs to be used for both intermittent patching and continuous applications, provided that existing pavement thickness is verified by cores—at least one per lane for repair areas less than 100 ft in length and at minimum 100‑foot intervals when the repair exceeds 100 ft. The scope of work must include engineering design, saw‑cutting, subbase preparation, bedding, installation of load‑transfer dowel bars at transverse joints and tie bars along longitudinal joints, as well as fabrication of concrete with reinforcing steel and associated quality‑control measures. Ancillary procedures such as slab undersealing, temporary asphalt wedge transitions, diamond grinding, joint sawing and sealing are also required as part of the installation process. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 426)

Precast Concrete Paving Systems should be avoided when project cost, detour availability, or lack of MPT concerns make a lower‑cost alternative more appropriate, because the system is a higher‑cost product and is only justified where rapid installation or work‑zone safety are critical. In addition, if the site cannot accommodate the very large and heavy precast units—meaning special hauling permits, designated haul routes, unloading equipment, a storage yard, or extra staging space are not feasible—a conventional cast‑in‑place concrete design should be preferred. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89)

Design inputs and requirements

The design of a precast concrete paving system should account for site constraints that may require special hauling permits, designated haul routes, a storage yard and extra space on the project site to accommodate delivery trucks and slab‑lifting equipment; traffic conditions can be designed to support the same range of loading as conventional PCC pavement and are often used on high‑speed roadways with very high ADT; material specifications call for a high‑strength, durable mixture that typically exceeds Class AA cement concrete requirements and the manufacturer’s bedding material is assumed not to contribute any strength to the pavement structure; geotechnical information must include the acceptable subgrade soil and subbase thickness determined during the design phase per Section 2.2, Subgrade Soil Evaluation, with the subgrade treated as a separate construction item; existing‑condition data such as joint spacing, slab widths of no more than one lane width, slab lengths of at least 6 ft (up to 15 ft or 20 ft for prestressed slabs) and alignment of transverse joints with adjacent lanes are required to define the replacement areas. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89)

Precast Concrete Paving Systems (PCPS) are governed by the same design and construction standards as conventional Portland cement concrete pavement, specifically the pavement‑design procedures in Chapter 8 of the Pavement Policy Manual and the material requirements of Publication 408, Specifications Section 704 for Class AA Cement Concrete. [1] The design and construction of full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs are also governed by the provisions of Section 529.1 and Section 529.3 of the Rigid Pavements specifications. [2] Designers must follow joint spacing and load‑transfer criteria used for jointed plain or reinforced concrete pavements, and the slab dimensions shall not exceed one lane width in width, with lengths ranging from a minimum of 6 ft to a maximum of 15 ft (up to 20 ft when prestressed). [1][2] Each slab must be cast with a sacrificial thickness of ¼ in above the nominal design thickness as shown on shop drawings, and an as‑delivered elevation shall be at least ¼ in higher than the surrounding surface. [1][2] The engineered slab must rest on a properly prepared subbase and an accepted fine‑graded bedding material, and it must incorporate load‑transfer dowel bars at transverse joints together with tie bars along longitudinal joints. [2] All precast elements—including concrete, reinforcing steel, dowel and tie bars—must be fabricated under quality‑control procedures, transported, stored, installed with appropriate erection equipment, sealed, and subjected to material certifications and testing. [2] The PCPS manufacturer shall be listed in Bulletin 15 and concrete mix designs must receive approval at least 28 days before production. [2] The concrete shall achieve a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a flexural strength of 650 psi. [2] Surface texture shall follow the requirements of Section 501.3(k), dowel placement at transverse joints shall be within ±1/8 in, lifting devices shall comply with Section 1105, and joint geometry is limited to transverse gaps between ¼ in and ½ in, longitudinal gaps not exceeding ¾ in, with vertical differentials across joints not exceeding ¼ in. [2] Grout used for dowels or bedding must attain a compressive strength of at least 2,500 psi before traffic is permitted. [2] Verification of existing pavement thickness requires core sampling; a minimum of one core per lane shall be taken at each full‑depth repair location when the repair area is less than 100 ft long, and when the repair exceeds 100 ft cores shall be obtained at intervals not greater than 100 ft. [2] Project constraints include the need for special hauling permits, designated haul routes, storage yards, and equipment capable of handling the large, heavy units; PCPS may only be selected after a cost/benefit review and consultation with HDTD because of its higher cost. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 426–435)

Design methods and criteria

The precast concrete pavement system is designed using the same structural analysis and slab‑thickness procedures that apply to conventional jointed plain concrete pavement, following the Chapter 8 pavement‑design guidelines and the high‑strength Class AA cement‑concrete mix requirements of Publication 408 Section 704. [1] Designers specify slab widths not to exceed one lane width and slab lengths ranging from a 6‑foot minimum up to a 15‑foot maximum, with a 20‑foot maximum permitted for prestressed slabs; the as‑delivered elevation shall be at least ¼ inch higher than the surrounding pavement. [1] When prestressing is employed, the rule of thumb that each 50 psi of mid‑slab prestress equals a 1‑inch reduction in thickness may be used to calculate a slab‑thickness saving, and the manufacturer’s shop drawings provide the required prestress force and bedding details. [1] The installation procedure begins with taking pavement cores of the existing traffic lanes to verify thickness, using a minimum of one core per lane at each full‑depth repair location for a repair area less than 100 feet; if the length of repair exceeds 100 feet, coring shall be performed at minimum 100‑foot intervals, with additional cores possible as directed by the Representative. [2] After core verification, engineering and design of the precast paving slabs are completed, followed by saw‑cutting and removal of the existing pavement, preparation of an existing or new subbase, and fine grading of bedding material. [2] Load transfer units (dowel bars) are installed at transverse joints and tie bars along longitudinal joints before slab placement. [2] The slabs are fabricated incorporating concrete, reinforcing steel, dowel and tie bars under quality‑control procedures; concrete mix designs must be submitted at least 28 days before production together with trial‑batch test data that demonstrate a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a minimum flexural strength of 650 psi. [2] Thermal‑expansion effects are evaluated using the standard expansion‑coefficient values from the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide to size panels and locate expansion joints. [2] The manufacturer’s PCPS system must be listed in Bulletin 15, and fabrication shall follow the shop and working drawings with tolerances shown in Table A; each slab includes a sacrificial thickness that is 1/4 inch thicker than the nominal design thickness and provides the specified dowel bars, tie bars, grout ports, lifting devices, bond‑breaker coating, foam gaskets and setting‑bolt hardware. [2] Placement steps—from subbase preparation through bedding‑grout injection—must meet the detailed dimensional and strength thresholds described in Section 529.3. [2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 426–435)

Precast Concrete Pavement Systems must be designed using the same Chapter 8 procedures as conventional jointed plain concrete pavement, with the slab structural behavior treated identically and subgrade and subbase strength parameters incorporated while assuming the manufacturer‑specified bedding material contributes no structural strength. The concrete mix shall meet or exceed the high‑strength Class AA requirements of Publication 408 Section 704. Under the construction specifications, the precast concrete pavement must be fabricated with a mix that attains at least 4,925 psi compressive strength and 650 psi flexural strength. Each slab is required to be cast 1/4 inch thicker than the nominal design thickness, and the shop drawings must show a sacrificial thickness of at least ¼ inch for diamond grinding, which is excluded from the calculated structural thickness. For intermittent repairs the slab width may not exceed one lane width and the length must be between 6 feet minimum and 15 feet maximum (20 feet maximum for prestressed slabs). Under Section 529.3 the slab lengths are limited to a maximum of 15 feet (20 feet for prestressed slabs) with a minimum length of 6 feet, and all dimensions must fall within the tolerances shown in Table A. The as‑delivered elevation shall be at least ¼ inch higher than the surrounding pavement to allow final grinding. When prestressing is used, thickness reductions are limited to a minimum overall slab thickness of 8 inches, with up to 2 inches allowable on granular base and up to 4 inches on stabilized base. Joint geometry is tightly controlled; the transverse joint width may not exceed ½ inch and must be at least ¼ inch along its entire length, longitudinal joints may not exceed ¾ inch, and vertical differential across any joint must not exceed ¼ inch (or up to ½ inch before grinding). Load‑transfer dowels and tie bars shall be placed within ±1/8 inch of the locations shown on the shop drawings, and all dowel and bedding grouts must reach a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is permitted. Thermal expansion design shall use the standard coefficient values from the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide based on site temperature conditions. The precast concrete pavement design must verify existing pavement thickness by coring at least one core per lane for any full‑depth repair area less than 100 feet long, and when the repair length exceeds 100 feet cores shall be taken at minimum 100‑foot intervals. Engineering and design of the precast paving slabs shall include subbase preparation, approved bedding material, installation of load transfer units and tie bars, fabrication using specified concrete, reinforcing steel, dowel and tie bars, and compliance with all material certifications and testing. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 426–435)

Structural section and dimensions

Precast concrete pavement slabs are limited to a width that does not exceed one lane width, and each slab must have a length of at least 6 ft but no more than 15 ft, with an extended maximum length of 20 ft permitted for prestressed units [1][2]. The structural thickness of the slab may be reduced when prestressing is employed, yet it shall never be less than 8 inches [1]. In addition, a sacrificial concrete layer of at least ¼ inch must be provided; this layer is not counted in the structural design thickness and the slabs are to be cast ¼ inch thicker than the nominal design thickness [1][2]. Joint spacing follows conventional criteria: jointed reinforced concrete pavement requires joints spaced at 30 ft, while jointed plain concrete pavement uses a 15‑foot spacing [1]. The transverse gap between adjacent slabs shall not exceed ½ inch and must be no less than ¼ inch along its entire length, whereas longitudinal joints may be as wide as ¾ inch [2]. Vertical differential across any joint is limited to ¼ inch; if the differential exceeds this value grinding may be applied provided the resulting unevenness does not exceed ½ inch [2]. The prepared subbase surface must lie within a tolerance of plus or minus ½ inch of the specified grade and contour, and the bedding surface on which slabs are placed shall not vary more than ¼ inch over the length of each slab [2]. Lifting devices embedded in the slab are required to be recessed at least 1 inch below the top surface [2]. Load‑transfer dowels, tie bars and their slots must be cast within ±⅛ inch of the positions shown on the shop drawings, thereby defining the reinforcement geometry for the precast units [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Designers should base the required slab thickness on the conventional jointed plain or reinforced concrete pavement design (Chapter 8) and, when prestressed, may reduce that thickness by up to four inches but must not go below eight inches; each 50 psi of prestress is treated as equivalent to a one‑inch reduction, with a maximum two‑inch reduction on granular bases and a maximum four‑inch reduction on stabilized bases. Thicknesses are set by casting each slab 1/4 inch thicker than the nominal design thickness and by meeting the minimum compressive (4,925 psi) and flexural (650 psi) strengths required in the shop drawings. Slab widths should not exceed one lane width and slab lengths should be at least six feet and no more than fifteen feet, with a twenty‑foot maximum for prestressed slabs; wherever possible transverse joints should align with transverse joints in adjacent lanes, using the standard joint spacing of thirty feet for JRCP or fifteen feet for JPCP. Joint spacing is controlled through the shop‑drawn longitudinal joint locations, with slab lengths adjusted to match existing transverse joints, and the finished transverse joint width must not exceed 1/2 inch and must be at least 1/4 inch while longitudinal joints must not exceed 3/4 inch. The as‑delivered elevation should be at least one‑quarter inch higher than the surrounding pavement so that after installation the entire section can be diamond‑ground (including a sacrificial ¼‑inch thickness) to restore the designed cross‑slope, profile, and IRI. Cross‑slope requirements are achieved by grading the compacted bedding material with a screeding device that can be adjusted to the required cross slope and tolerance, ensuring the slab surface does not vary more than 1/8 inch over its length. Transitions require that any permanent vertical differential between new and existing pavement be no greater than ¼ inch; if the drop‑off exceeds 2 inches a temporary asphalt wedge transition is provided, and excess differential up to ¼ inch may be removed by diamond grinding. Tie‑ins and transitions are achieved by matching joint locations and elevations as described, ensuring continuity of load transfer across the new precast slabs. Tie‑bars are installed along longitudinal joints for continuous sections longer than 65 feet, using tie‑bar slots at the slab bottom or cross‑stitching as shown on the accepted shop drawings. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Materials and mixture design

Design documents for precast concrete pavement must call for a high‑strength, durable cement concrete that meets or exceeds the Class AA Cement Concrete requirements of Publication 408 Section 704. When prestressed slabs are used, the specifications shall include the required prestress force and the resulting compression capacity, which may be up to 200 psi, and shall justify any permitted thickness reduction according to the guidance that each 50 psi of prestressing at the mid‑slab location is equivalent to a 1‑inch saving. Manufacturer’s shop drawings will detail any reinforcement and bedding material, but those items are considered incidental to the specified concrete mix. The description of full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs requires that each fabricated slab be provided with all concrete, reinforcing steel, dowel bars, tie bars, and any accepted bedding material needed for installation, and quality control must encompass certification and testing of each material. For construction, the contract mandates that concrete mix designs be submitted at least 28 days before slab production and must include trial‑batch test data demonstrating that each mix will achieve a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a minimum flexural strength of 650 psi. The mix design also has to incorporate the appropriate thermal‑expansion coefficient for the site’s average daily temperature and exposure conditions as specified in the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide. For prestressed precast slabs, the design must specify the type, layout, and applied force of the prestressing tendons together with calculations showing the mid‑slab prestress level, and it must detail slab‑setting hardware such as steel bearing plate size and thickness, corrosion‑protection provisions, and bolt and insert designs referenced to a manufacturer’s PCPS system listed in Bulletin 15. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 426–435)

Precast Concrete Pavement must be produced with a high‑strength, durable concrete that meets or exceeds the Class AA Cement Concrete requirements of Publication 408 Section 704 [1]. Its structural design is performed using the same Chapter 8 procedures applied to conventional jointed plain concrete pavement, so mix proportioning should achieve the required traffic‑load capacity, durability and constructability while adhering to those high‑strength specifications [1]. Designers should limit slab widths to no more than one lane width and specify slab lengths of at least 6 feet and not more than 15 feet, with a maximum of 20 feet for prestressed slabs, to ensure proper performance [1]. The precast pavement mix design must be submitted at least 28 days before slab production and include trial‑batch test data proving that the concrete will meet the minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and the minimum flexural strength of 650 psi [2]. The design also has to account for ambient average daily temperatures and exposure conditions at the project site so that appropriate panel sizes and expansion joint locations are selected using standard aggregate expansion coefficients from the AASHTO guide [2]. Each slab is cast with a sacrificial thickness of 1/4 inch over the nominal design thickness [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Structural details and interfaces

Precast concrete pavement slabs must be sized so that each slab is no wider than a single lane and its length is at least 6 ft but not more than 15 ft (up to 20 ft when prestressed) [1]. The delivered top of the slab should sit at least ¼ in above the adjacent finished surface so that post‑installation diamond grinding can restore profile [1]. Transverse joints on the precast units are required to line up with the existing transverse joints in neighboring lanes whenever feasible [1]. Load transfer across those joints is provided by dowel bars, and high‑performance dowels are recommended when the repaired section is expected to remain in service for more than 20 years [1]. Any reinforcement, including prestressing tendons or conventional steel, is detailed on the manufacturer’s shop drawings, which also specify the bedding material and connection details [1]. The full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs must incorporate load‑transfer units (dowel bars) at each transverse joint and tie bars along all longitudinal joints [2]. Reinforcing steel is embedded within the concrete, with size and position shown on the shop drawings, and must meet at least 4 925 psi compressive and 650 psi flexural strength [2]. The slabs are fabricated with a sacrificial thickness of one‑quarter inch above the nominal design thickness [2]. Load‑transfer dowels and tie bars are cast square to the edge and parallel to the top surface within ±1/8 inch of the indicated locations, and a bond‑breaker coating is applied to the dowel portions; slots for these members are provided to the same tolerance [2]. Transverse joints are limited to a maximum width of one‑half inch and a minimum of one‑quarter inch (saw‑cut if less), while longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters inch [2]. Load transfer at transverse joints may be achieved by bottom‑edge dowel slots with embedded dowels, conventional surface slots about 2 ½ inches wide at the face, or narrow‑mouth surface slots approximately one inch wide tapering to three inches below mid‑slab depth, all grouted through ports to a minimum compressive strength of 2 500 psi [2]. Tie bars are installed along longitudinal joints for continuous sections longer than 65 feet [2]. Where a temporary vertical drop exceeds two inches, a temporary asphalt pavement wedge transition is required before traffic can be opened [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–89); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 426–435)

For precast concrete slabs the design requires that each slab be no wider than a single lane and have a length of at least 6 ft but not exceed 15 ft, with a maximum of 20 ft for prestressed units.[1] Transverse joints should be positioned to line up with the transverse joints of adjacent lanes wherever feasible.[1] The as‑delivered slab surface must sit at least ¼ in above the existing pavement or final design grade so that after placement the whole section can be diamond‑ground to achieve the required cross‑slope, profile and IRI.[1] Slabs are placed on a prepared foundation—either the existing concrete, a fine‑graded bedding course, or a cementitious bedding—and the subgrade and subbase must meet the usual strength criteria; the bedding material is considered incidental and does not contribute structural strength.[1] Load transfer across the slab edges is provided by dowel bars that are fully bonded along their embedded length, and high‑performance dowels should be specified when the repaired pavement is expected to remain in service for more than 20 years.[1] The interface layout follows the approved shop and working drawings, with slab dimensions including a sacrificial thickness of ¼ in.[2] Transverse joint widths are limited so that they do not exceed ½ in and must be at least ¼ in long along their entire length, while longitudinal joints must not exceed ¾ in; the finished vertical differential across any joint shall not exceed ¼ in.[2] Load transfer is achieved by casting load‑transfer dowels and tie bars as shown, coating the dowel portions with bond breaker, providing grout ports for dowel, tie‑bar and bedding grouts, and installing foam gaskets or setting‑bolt hardware when required by the manufacturer, all positioned within ±⅛ in of the shop drawing locations.[2] Expansion joint positions are determined from the mix design’s temperature coefficient and incorporated into the slab layout, and lifting device recesses shall be at least 1 in below the surface and later sealed with cementitious grout.[2] Proper layer‑to‑layer and feature interfaces begin with preparation of the existing or new subbase and placement of an accepted fine‑graded bedding material.[2] Load transfer units (dowel bars) are installed at each transverse joint and tie bars along longitudinal joints to maintain uniform support and lane‑to‑lane ties, completing the structural connection between slabs.[2] After slab placement, undersealing of the slab, patching of dowel‑bar and tie‑bar slots, and sealing of all joints finalize the interface treatment, and where a transition to adjacent pavement is required a temporary asphalt pavement wedge transition is used to blend the precast slab with the adjoining surface.[2]

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Provide blockouts in the slabs for existing utility castings and proposed drainage structures, and the mix design must account for ambient average daily temperatures and exposure conditions at the project site to determine correct panel size and expansion joint locations. Lay out precast slab transverse joint locations in a manner that will allow for expansion and contraction at the existing and proposed joints, and remove any soft, wet, or unsuitable subbase and subgrade material before placement. Precast concrete pavement units shall be laid in dimensions that do not exceed the width of a single travel lane, with slab lengths ranging from at least 6 ft to no more than 15 ft (up to 20 ft for prestressed slabs) [1][2]. Transverse joints between adjacent lanes must be aligned and their widths shall not exceed ½ inch and shall be no less than ¼ inch along the full length of the joint, while longitudinal joints shall not exceed ¾ inch in width [2]. The finished vertical differential across any joint may not exceed ¼ inch (allowing up to ½ inch only before diamond grinding), and each slab is cast ¼ inch thicker than the nominal design thickness to accommodate this tolerance [2]. Slabs must incorporate blockouts for existing utility castings and proposed drainage structures, and lifting devices shall be recessed at least 1 inch below the surface and subsequently filled with an accepted cementitious grout; when required by the manufacturer, foam gaskets and slab‑setting bolt hardware indicated on the shop drawings are to be provided [2]. Traffic may not be permitted onto the pavement until dowel and bedding grouts have attained a minimum compressive strength of 2 500 psi, joint gaps must be sealed as specified, and any temporary exposure of ungrouted slabs is limited to no more than three days [2]. Load‑transfer dowels, tie bars, and the prescribed grout ports shall be installed in accordance with the shop drawings, with transverse joint gaps maintained between ¼ and ½ inch and appropriate sealing applied to longitudinal joints [2]. Each slab shall be delivered at an elevation at least ¼ in higher than the surrounding surface so that after placement the pavement can be diamond‑ground to restore cross‑slope, profile and International Roughness Index, thereby meeting geometric and ride‑quality expectations for users [1]. The system is required to support the same traffic loading conditions as conventional Portland cement concrete and is approved for high‑speed roadways with very high average daily traffic, providing a safer work zone for both travelers and workers [1].

Verification and expected performance

The Representative must verify that each precast slab matches the authorized shop drawings and falls within the dimensional tolerances shown in Table A, that the concrete meets the minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and flexural strength of 650 psi, and that a sacrificial thickness of 1/4 inch has been added to the top surface; placement checks require the finished vertical differential across any joint not to exceed 1/4 inch, transverse joint widths between 1/4 inch and 1/2 inch, longitudinal joints no greater than 3/4 inch, and that all dowel‑bar and bedding grouts achieve at least 2,500 psi compressive strength before traffic is allowed. Meeting these tolerances, material strengths, joint geometry, and grout performance demonstrates constructability and consistency with the project requirements. [2]

Precast Concrete Pavement Systems are expected to provide long‑term durability comparable to or better than cast‑in‑place high‑early‑strength concrete, supporting the same range of traffic loading conditions as conventional jointed plain concrete pavement and thereby resisting distress over a service life that justifies their higher cost. Ride quality is addressed by delivering slabs at least ¼ inch above adjacent pavement so that after installation the surface can be diamond ground to restore cross‑slope, profile, and International Roughness Index (IRI) throughout the project limits. The structural design follows the same Chapter 8 procedures used for conventional PCC, and performance of the installed pavement is verified by checking that the IRI meets specifications after grinding.
