Distress

Part of: Pavement Condition Assessment is-subtopic-of
Distress is a topic under Pavement Condition Assessment.
distressdistresses
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-28

Distress in pavement engineering denotes any observable deterioration such as cracking, rutting, raveling, bleeding, potholes, edge deterioration, joint spalling or faulting, flushing and other surface or internal defects that signal loss of structural integrity[1]. It is seen on concrete slabs, joints, shoulders, asphalt‑over‑concrete overlays, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses and hot‑in‑place recycled surfaces, where saturated subgrades may produce transverse cracks, trapped water can cause joint tenting, and a softened base often yields surface roughness or rutting that mirrors underlying deformation[1]. These manifestations arise from traffic loading, inadequate subsurface drainage, freeze‑thaw cycling, material aging and design or construction shortcomings, which reduce subgrade support, propagate cracks and ridges, and consequently lower the pavement’s structural capacity, ride quality and service life[1]

PennDOT publications covering “Distress” also include the following subtopics: Corner Break, Cracking, Depression / Settlement, Pumping, Spalling, and Void (loss of support).

Corner Break and Spalling represent specific surface manifestations of structural failure where loss of support or material fragmentation occurs at slab corners and edges. [1][2] Cracking and Depression / Settlement quantify the extent of fissuring and localized low spots that signal underlying weakness, while Pumping describes the hydraulic mechanism that expels fines to create these voids. [1][2] Void (loss of support) constitutes the subsurface condition generated by pumping and consolidation that drives excessive deflection and progressive distress across all rigid pavement types. [1][2]

Description and identification

Distress in pavement engineering denotes any observable deterioration such as cracking, rutting, raveling, bleeding, potholes, edge deterioration, joint spalling or faulting, flushing, and other surface or internal defects that indicate loss of structural integrity.[1][2] On concrete pavements saturated subgrades may provide poor support and can result in transverse cracks under traffic loads.[1] When a joint deteriorates into particles small enough to impede gravity drainage, water can become trapped beneath the joint and, in winter, freeze to cause tenting of the joint.[1] In asphalt layers a fluid base lacking support can produce surface roughness.[1] One type of rutting observed at the surface may be a reflection of rutting occurring in the subgrade.[1] For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses acceptable distress includes low severity cracking or raveling, infrequent corrugations, settlements, heaves or slippage cracks, and medium severity rutting that appear as minor surface cracks, loss of aggregate, occasional wave‑like corrugations, localized vertical movements and longitudinal depressions not deeper than a medium severity level.[1] Overlays consider existing distresses such as alligator cracking that spans the entire roadway surface; water intrusion into the structural section is a major cause of many types of distress including loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, localized failures and crack propagation.[1] In bonded PCC overlays any great extent of cracking or faulting in the underlying concrete slab is regarded as distress and, if significant and not repaired, may be reflected up through the overlay to become visible on its surface.[1] For hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) candidate pavements distress is limited to surface‑level deterioration; rutting severity should be < 1 ½ inches, the average crack width should not exceed the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and minor corrugation, shoving or slippage should have an average depth < 1 ½ inches.[1] Typical asphalt distresses such as rutting, shoving, slippage and thermal cracking manifest as longitudinal depressions, displaced material and temperature‑induced cracks, often concentrated at intersections or steep grades.[1] Full‑depth reclamation surveys record surface defects including rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems with severity levels to guide rehabilitation.[1] Annual automated pavement condition surveys identify distress such as edge deterioration, joint spalling, joint faulting and premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements, which may be linked to inadequate subbase material.[1] In the 60‑month warranty specification a distress is any observable pavement defect such as flushing, potholes, cracks of varying severity, or rutting that must be identified and measured during performance surveys.[2] Flushing and potholes are recorded by manual surveys.[2] Low‑severity cracks contribute to a threshold whereby if 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more such distresses, remedial action is required.[2] Medium‑severity cracks are considered deviations affecting six inches on each side along their entire length.[2] Rut depth is quantified using a twelve‑foot straight edge across the lane width.[2]

In section 2.3 DRAINAGE of the Pavement Policy Manual, engineers recognize distress caused by inadequate subsurface drainage by observing transverse cracks that develop under traffic loads when a saturated subgrade cannot support the slab, tenting of joints produced by localized frost heave, and surface ruts that mirror the shape of underlying subgrade deformation, thereby distinguishing these moisture‑related symptoms from purely temperature‑driven cracking. They further differentiate drainage‑induced damage from thermal shrinkage cracks because adequate drainage does not retard shrinkage or thermal cracking but does prevent the adjacent moisture‑associated damage that accompanies a thermal crack. In section 4.1 CONCRETE PAVEMENT RESTORATION (CPR), distress is first recognized by measuring and tabulating the exact quantities of visible damage on the pavement surface, and targeted core sampling near deteriorated joints and at random locations confirms subbase or subgrade conditions and the depth of deterioration below the surface. Non‑destructive techniques such as deflection tests and faulting measurements are then applied to pinpoint which joints require replacement or slab stabilization, allowing separation of surface cracking from underlying structural problems. CPR also classifies distress into load‑related versus climate/materials‑related categories—including drainage‑related distresses—so that engineers can distinguish the cause of observed damage; if the measured percent patching needed exceeds 10 % (excluding existing good patches), a cost‑effectiveness analysis must be performed before selecting a repair strategy. Section 5.5 ULTRA‑THIN BONDED WEARING COURSE specifies that candidate pavements may only exhibit low‑severity cracking or raveling, infrequent corrugations, settlements, heaves or slippage cracks, and medium‑severity rutting; any other types or higher severities fall outside the conditions for use. In section 5.19 HOT IN‑PLACE RECYCLED ASPHALT, distress is recognized by confirming that damage exists only on the surface of the pavement, with rutting severity required to be less than 1 ½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth limited to less than 1.0 inch and an average crack width not exceeding the Medium Rating of ≤ ½ inch, and raveling/weathering severity not exceeding the Medium Rating even though the surface may be rough or pitted; minor corrugation, shoving, slippage, bleeding, or low‑friction conditions are acceptable provided their average depth is less than 1 ½ inches and bleeding occurs only in isolated areas, thereby separating true surface distress from deeper structural issues. Section 10.6 BONDED PCC OVERLAY of the same manual states that a great extent of cracking and/or faulting in the existing concrete pavement signals significant distress that will be reflected up through the bonded overlay if it is not repaired, distinguishing severe underlying distress from minor surface imperfections that do not preclude bonding. Section 13.7 PAVEMENT PROJECT CRITERIA describes how annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (Videologging) under the STAMPP program record distinct categories of distress—cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting—enabling engineers to tell one form of damage apart from another; the program also notes that premature mid‑slab cracking caused by inadequate subbase material may not be detectable and therefore requires field observation. In SECTION 496—ASPHALT CONCRETE PAVEMENT, a 60‑month warranty requires distress to be recognized through annual performance surveys that use automated data collection in accordance with Publication 336, while flushing and potholes are identified by manual surveys; each distress is measured and rated according to the prescribed procedures, with low or medium severity applied to determine area thresholds such as the 30 % segment‑area limit for two or more low‑severity distresses that triggers remedial action. A medium‑severity crack is distinguished by treating it as a deviation affecting six inches on either side of the crack along its full length, and rutting severity is confirmed with a 12‑foot straight‑edge measurement across the lane width, thereby separating true pavement distress from other conditions excluded from contractor responsibility. SECTION 704—CEMENT CONCRETE requires a visual examination report that records any cracking and joint expansion observed on each structure; to separate ASR‑related cracking from other forms, cores are taken and petrographic analysis per ASTM C856 is performed to detect alkali‑silica gel formation and associated microcracking, with the service record required to include at least ten structures older than ten years. Where it occurs

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, distress is observed on a range of pavement types including concrete pavements—such as slab surfaces, joints and integral shoulder sections—as well as asphalt‑over‑concrete overlays and conventional asphalt layers, where typical manifestations include cracking, joint tenting, surface rutting, loss of support, shrinkage cracks, transverse cracking, raveling, moderate rutting and potholes that arise from insufficient subsurface drainage. [1] The manual further notes that the Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course experiences surface distress on its asphalt concrete wearing layer, with allowable limits described as low‑severity cracking or raveling, infrequent corrugations, settlements, heaves or slippage cracks, and medium‑severity rutting. [1] Both rigid Portland cement concrete (PCC) overlays and flexible asphalt concrete overlays are applied to correct existing deterioration in underlying pavements that may be an asphalt surface, a PCC surface or a composite structure such as an asphalt overlay over a concrete base; the presence of distress on the underlying pavement—cracking, faulting, rutting, shoving, slippage or thermal cracking—is recognized and must be addressed because it can be reflected through the new overlay. [1] For bonded PCC overlays placed on existing PCC pavements any significant amount of cracking or faulting is considered distress because it will be transmitted up through the overlay if not repaired, while bonded PCC overlays over an asphalt substrate require a minimum milled‑asphalt thickness of 3 inches and may have a nominal concrete thickness between 2.5 and 4 inches for thin bonded overlays or 4 inches or more for conventional bonded concrete overlays. [1] Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) projects address distress that appears on the surface of the existing pavement, which may be an asphalt wearing course, a full‑depth asphalt structure, or an asphalt layer over a concrete base, with acceptable limits of rutting severity less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth less than 1.0 inch and average crack width not exceeding ½ inch, and medium‑rated raveling or weathering. [1] The STAMPP program also records distress on shoulder elements, including shoulder drop‑off, rutting and fatigue cracking. [1] In the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications for asphalt‑concrete pavement covered by a 60‑month warranty, distress—defined as cracking, flushing, potholes, rutting and other listed conditions—is identified and measured on the paved surface through surveys that follow Publication 336, and the definition is expressly limited to the asphalt‑concrete pavement covered by the warranty, not extending to concrete slabs, composite structures, overlays, or shoulders. [2] Under the same specifications for cement‑concrete (including extra‑cement concrete) structures, distress is documented during the visual examination required by service record provisions, where inspectors look for cracking and joint expansion and record any symptoms of distress as part of that examination; this portion does not mention asphalt, composite, overlay or shoulder pavements. [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 5 (pp. 107–133), Ch. 10 (pp. 183–186), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221), Ch. 14 (p. 227); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 664–665)

In the Pavement Policy Manual, distress associated with inadequate drainage is observed at deteriorated joints where water becomes trapped beneath the joint, appears in the concrete slab interior as transverse cracks caused by saturated subgrades, and manifests in underlying layers when a softened or fluid base produces surface roughness and rutting that reflects deformation of the subgrade (section 2.3 DRAINAGE) [1]. The same manual notes that during concrete pavement restoration, distress is identified at deteriorated joints on the concrete slab and may extend into the underlying layers, prompting cores to be taken near those joints to evaluate whether deterioration reaches the subbase or subgrade (section 4.1 CONCRETE PAVEMENT RESTORATION) [1]. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, distress is described as a surface phenomenon affecting the UTWC layer itself, with surface distresses such as raveling and moderate rutting being the primary forms addressed by this treatment (section 5.5 ULTRA‑THIN BONDED WEARING COURSE) [1]. The manual further explains that alligator cracking can appear across the entire roadway surface rather than being confined to wheel paths, reflective cracking propagates through existing cracks at layer interfaces, and active pumping signals a poor subgrade condition that must be addressed before applying an interlayer (section 5.12 OVERLAYS) [1]. In the hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt guidelines, distress is limited to the pavement’s exposed layer; rutting severity shall be < 1 ½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking severity shall have a depth < 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and raveling/weathering shall not exceed the medium rating (section 5.19 HOT IN‑PLACE RECYCLED ASPHALT) [1]. The same source states that bonded PCC overlays of existing concrete pavements exhibit distress in the existing concrete slab itself, appearing as cracking or faulting of the slab and potentially reflecting up through the overlay if not repaired (section 10.6 BONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT) [1]. Recorded Interstate system distress includes cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting; these manifestations appear at joints, within cracks that may extend across slab interiors such as premature mid‑slab cracking caused by inadequate subbase material, along edges, and in wheel paths where rutting develops (section 13.7 PAVEMENT PROJECT CRITERIA) [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require a visual examination of each structure for cracking, including expansion at joints where applicable, and record any symptoms of distress that are found; thus distress is identified at crack locations and at joint expansions (section 704.1 GENERAL – ASR Mitigation Flowchart) [2].

Causes and mechanisms

Distress in pavements is linked to several cause groups identified by the Pavement Policy Manual, including traffic loading as a primary driver reflected by ESAL data used for priority setting; under traffic loads saturated subgrades provide poor support leading to transverse cracks and surface rutting that may mirror subgrade rutting. The same manual notes climate effects, especially freeze‑thaw cycles, promote water‑induced deterioration such as loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, crack propagation and overall degradation, and they aggravate shrinkage cracking when moisture is present. Moisture‑related drainage problems are treated as a separate cause: inadequate subsurface drainage can produce transverse cracking, surface roughness from lack of support of a fluid base, and active pumping usually signals a poor subgrade that must be remedied before overlay work. Material influences cited include age oxidation of asphalt surfaces, which is most likely responsible for alligator cracking extending across the entire roadway, as well as aggregate issues such as Vanport Limestone concerns and Alkali Silica Reaction that can cause deterioration even when not captured by surveys. Design or construction deficiencies are mentioned where premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements may be caused by inadequate subbase material. The Policy Manual requires identification of these causes before restoration because uncorrected causes will cause the work to fail; Table 4.1 in Section 4.1 categorizes concrete‑pavement distress into two primary groups—load‑related distresses and climate/materials‑related distresses, the latter encompassing drainage‑related problems. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation warranty specification attributes pavement distress that is beyond the contractor’s control to several factors: excessive traffic loadings when cumulative ESALs exceed the design estimate, an unacceptable base condition revealed by core testing, routine maintenance activities performed by the Department, destructive procedures such as coring or milling carried out by the Department, uncontrolled forces of nature (e.g., floods, earthquakes), and traffic‑accident related spills or gouging. The warranty document notes that if during the warranty period cumulative ESALs exceed 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life ESALs, the contractor is not responsible for repair of rutting or base condition distresses; similarly, detection of fatigue cracking or other signs of an unacceptable base condition also relieves the contractor of responsibility. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351)

In asphalt concrete pavements, distress appears as observable failures such as cracking, flushing, potholes and rutting that are recorded by the annual automated or manual surveys required under Section 496.3(c) [2]. When a year‑5 survey shows that 30 % of a half‑mile segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses, microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment must be applied, and if combined deviations exceed 20 % of the segment’s surface area the entire segment must be removed and replaced [2]. The progression of this asphalt distress is accelerated by an unacceptable base condition indicated by fatigue cracking and by traffic loading that exceeds design expectations—specifically when cumulative ESALs exceed 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life, at which point the contractor is not responsible for repair of rutting or base‑condition distresses and repair areas spaced less than 100 ft apart must be merged into a single continuous repair [2]. In cement concrete pavements, distress develops when an alkali‑silica reaction generates expansive gel that induces microcracking; petrographic analysis confirms the presence of gel formation and associated cracking [2]. This ASR‑related progression is accelerated whenever the mix contains aggregates classified as potentially reactive and when the cement used possesses appreciable alkali content, conditions that trigger expansion documented in field performance histories [2]. In concrete pavements, distress develops when water infiltrates the pavement structure, reducing subgrade or base support so that traffic loads generate transverse cracks in the concrete and surface rutting that mirrors a softened subgrade in asphalt [1]. The progression is accelerated whenever drainage is inadequate—ponded surface water can penetrate the layers, saturated subgrades act as fluid support, frozen underlying soil prevents percolation during spring thaw, trapped moisture may freeze causing joint tenting, and capillary water held in fine soils impedes removal; consequently poor permeability, low subbase slope, wide drain spacing, high water tables or seepage from cuts increase the rate of moisture‑related cracking, rutting and surface roughness [1]. Section 4.1 identifies two primary mechanisms: repeated traffic loads that produce structural damage such as cracking and faulting, and climate or material durability factors—including frost damage, polishing‑susceptible aggregate, and inadequate drainage—that degrade the concrete matrix and joint sealants; these mechanisms progress more rapidly when traffic loads exceed design expectations, when the subgrade or subbase is weak, when drainage is poor, and when aggregates prone to polishing are present [1]. Water infiltration into the structural section of an existing pavement also weakens subgrade support leading to stripping, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation and overall deterioration; freeze‑thaw cycles further expand and contract that moisture, accelerating cracking, while age oxidation of the asphalt produces alligator cracking across the surface [1]. In bonded PCC overlays, distress develops when pre‑existing cracks or faulting in the underlying concrete are bonded to a new overlay, causing the two layers to act as a monolithic slab so that existing imperfections propagate upward into the overlay and become visible surface distress; progression is accelerated whenever the original pavement contains an extensive extent of cracking or faulting that has not been repaired because the bonded interface transmits those defects directly to the new layer [1]. Annual surveys record distress as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and faulting; progression under repeated traffic loading (ESAL) is accelerated when underlying deficiencies exist such as inadequate subbase material that may cause premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements, or aggregate issues like Vanport Limestone concerns or Alkali Silica Reaction which can degrade skid resistance, and when patching exceeds 10 % of the pavement area, indicating extensive deterioration [1].

Severity levels and rating

In the Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course (section 5.5 Conditions for Use), acceptable distress is limited to low‑severity cracking or raveling, infrequent corrugations, settlements, heaves or slippage cracks, and medium‑severity rutting, where “low” and “medium” denote relative intensity and “infrequent” describes occurrence frequency [1]. For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) mixtures (section 5.19 Characteristics of HIR Mixtures), the manual quantifies low‑ to medium‑severity limits by stating that rutting severity shall be less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth shall be less than 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding the Medium Rating of ≤ ½ inch, raveling or weathering shall not exceed the Medium Rating described as a rough, pitted surface that may have loose particles, and minor corrugation, shoving or slippage are permissible provided their average depth is less than 1½ inches [1]. Under the 60‑month asphalt concrete pavement warranty (section 496.3(c)), a low‑severity level is defined such that if the year‑5 performance survey identifies at least two distresses of this class occupying 30 % of a segment’s area, remedial microsurfacing is required [2]. The same warranty specification treats medium‑severity cracks as deviations extending six inches on each side of the crack along its full length when evaluating exceedance of the Table A thresholds [2]. If the aggregate of such deviations exceeds the Table A limits over more than 20 % of a segment’s surface, the entire segment must be removed and replaced, and any individual distress type that surpasses its Table A limit triggers the remediation actions prescribed in the table [2].

The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) specifies that condition ratings for distress are assigned using the criteria of Publication 336 (Automated Pavement Condition Survey Field Manual) or Publication 343 for certain treatments, such as Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course and Full‑Depth Reclamation projects [1]. For candidate pavements considered for an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course, only low‑severity cracking or raveling is permitted, while medium‑severity rutting is the maximum allowable level [1]. In full‑depth reclamation project selection, the Pub. 336 criteria require that rutting severity be less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth be under 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and raveling/weathering severity must not exceed the medium rating describing a rough, pitted surface that may have loose particles [1]. The hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt selection guidelines also apply the Pub. 336 thresholds, using the same limits for rutting, cracking depth/width, and raveling severity as described above [1]. Under the 60‑month warranty provisions of Publication 408 (Section 496.3(c)), distress condition ratings are again derived from the survey techniques, rating procedures, and extent‑estimation methods set forth in Publication 336 [2]. The warranty table specifies that if a year‑5 performance survey shows that 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment must be applied [2]. For removal or replacement decisions, a medium‑severity crack is treated as affecting six inches on each side of the crack along its full length [2].

Measurement, survey and extent

In the concrete pavement restoration context, the condition of existing pavement is determined by measuring the exact quantities of visible distress and tabulating them [1]. Deflection tests, faulting measurements, or other non‑destructive techniques are then applied to identify problem joints and guide slab stabilization decisions [1]. Cores are taken near deteriorated joints and at random locations to evaluate subbase or subgrade conditions [1]. Under the Section 496.3(c) warranty for asphalt concrete pavement, distress types are identified and quantified primarily through automated surveys that follow Publication 336, with the survey techniques, rating procedures, and extent estimation procedures prescribed therein [2]. Flushing and potholes are captured by manual surveys also using Publication 336 [2]. Rutting severity is determined by measuring rut depth with a 12‑foot straight edge placed across the lane width [2]. When base‑condition problems are suspected, 6‑inch diameter cores are drilled in the affected area and the core depth is measured to assess the distress [2]. For cracking, the extent is estimated by multiplying the maximum transverse width by the maximum longitudinal length of each crack [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351)

Under the 60‑month asphalt concrete pavement warranty (Section 496), distress is primarily captured through automated surveys performed in accordance with Publication 336, while flushing and potholes are identified by manual surveys; nondestructive testing may also be used by the contractor with prior Department approval. The Department conducts these surveys on an annual basis throughout the warranty period, adding extra surveys when needed. Distress limits are evaluated for each defined segment—approximately a half‑mile of state route—or partial segment as established in the Location Reference System. Extent is reported as a percentage of the segment’s surface area (e.g., 30 % of segment area) and compared to the Table A thresholds that trigger remedial action. [2]

Effects on performance

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), inadequate subsurface drainage leads saturated subgrades to lose their ability to support the pavement, producing transverse cracks in concrete and surface rutting that mirrors a softened subgrade; the resulting fluid base also creates surface roughness and undulations that diminish ride quality [1]. The manual further states that these moisture‑related distresses reduce the structural capacity of the pavement by lowering subgrade support, increase maintenance needs, and ultimately shorten the pavement’s service life [1]. Because distress manifested as structural deficiencies or surface deterioration directly diminishes a pavement’s structural capacity and ride quality, overlays are prescribed in Section 5.12 to correct those conditions [1]. When water infiltrates the structural section, it can cause loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation and overall deterioration, thereby compromising durability and shortening service life [1]. The manual also requires that existing distresses—including rutting, bleeding, raveling or moisture‑related cracking—be considered in interlayer selection; appropriate interlayers such as geotextile fabric or reinforcing grids provide a moisture barrier that mitigates water‑induced distress, and addressing these distresses through overlays or interlayers improves structural performance, rideability, safety and longevity [1]. For hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) candidate pavements, the manual defines limits—rutting greater than 1 ½ inches, crack depth deeper than 1.0 inch, average crack width larger than ½ inch, or raveling beyond a medium rating—and states that exceeding any of these limits indicates loss of structural capacity, reduced ride quality, compromised safety, diminished durability and shortened service life; the HIR process is intended to remediate those distresses, thereby recovering performance, improving appearance, and prolonging service life [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications (Publication 408/2020, Section 496.3(c) Warranty) require that low‑severity cracking or flushing occupying more than 30 % of a segment’s area trigger remedial action—typically microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment—indicating loss of structural capacity and ride quality [2]. The same specifications state that when combined deviations exceed the Table A limits for more than 20 % of the surface, removal and replacement of the entire segment is mandated, reflecting that durability and service life have been compromised [2]. Rut depth must be verified with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width, and medium‑severity cracks are treated as affecting six inches on either side for their full length, providing the measurements used to assess severity of distress [2]. If a performance survey determines that pavement condition is potentially harmful or unsafe, the Department may order immediate emergency repairs, demonstrating the safety impact of distress [2].

Inadequate subsurface drainage leaves water in the base, so saturated subgrades first reduce support and cause transverse cracks in concrete pavements under traffic loads; as joints deteriorate and particles block gravity flow, trapped water may freeze in winter producing tenting of the joint, while the softened, fluid‑like subgrade permits surface rutting that mirrors the underlying rutting and can result in surface roughness that degrades ride quality, ultimately cascading into broader structural failures and a shortened pavement service life (section 2.3 DRAINAGE) [1]. If concrete‑pavement distress is left uncorrected during restoration, the same load‑related or climate/materials‑related problems reappear, allowing surface cracking to evolve into deeper slab deterioration; when the percent patching needed exceeds 10 % of the project area a cost‑effectiveness analysis must be performed to consider alternatives (section 4.1 CONCRETE PAVEMENT RESTORATION) [1]. Untreated distress also permits water intrusion that can trigger a cascade of failures—including loss of subgrade support, stripping of asphalt from aggregate, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation and freeze‑thaw induced deterioration—and alligator cracking that spreads across the entire surface signals age oxidation and will continue to worsen without remedial action such as an appropriate overlay or interlayer; other distresses like rutting, bleeding or raveling persist because they are not mitigated by interlayers, eventually demanding thicker structural repairs (section 5.12 OVERLAYS) [1]. When an existing concrete pavement exhibits a great extent of cracking or faulting, the distress is considered significant for a bonded PCC overlay; because a bonded overlay and the underlying slab act as a single monolithic unit, any untreated cracks or faults are transmitted upward into the new concrete layer, causing the overlay to develop similar deterioration (section 10.6 BONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT) [1]. Distress such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling or joint faulting is first recorded in the annual STAMPP surveys; if it is not repaired, routine maintenance activities are applied, but when patching exceeds 10 % of the pavement area preservation ends and more extensive rehabilitation becomes necessary, and continued neglect ultimately makes reconstruction required once functional pavement life has been exceeded (section 13.7 PAVEMENT PROJECT CRITERIA > E) [1]. Under the 60‑month warranty for asphalt concrete pavement, early signs of distress that are not corrected can accumulate until the performance thresholds in Section 496.3(c) are breached; when a year‑5 survey shows that 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, the warranty requires microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment (Section 496.3 c) [2]. If the combined deviations beyond the Table A limits occupy more than 20 % of the segment surface—counting a medium‑severity crack as a six‑inch influence zone on each side—the specification mandates removal and replacement of the entire segment (Section 496.3 c) [2]. Moreover, when individual repair patches are spaced less than 100 ft apart they must be merged into one continuous repair, indicating that untreated cracking or flushing can quickly evolve into larger contiguous failure zones that demand increasingly extensive remediation (Section 496.3 c) [2].

Repairs and treatments

When inadequate subsurface drainage is identified as the cause of pavement distress, the manual calls for installing continuous pavement base drains along the outside edge and low side of superelevated sections, lateral underdrains in wet subgrade areas and cut‑to‑fill transitions, and subgrade drains where water damage or sagging is evident; these measures are intended to mitigate concrete transverse cracking, joint tenting, asphalt surface rutting that reflects a softened subgrade, surface roughness from loss of base support, and moisture‑related damage adjacent to thermal cracks (section 2.3) [1]. Surface runoff control such as clean ditches, positive outlet low points, clean inlets and pipes, and removal of excess shoulder material is required whenever ponded water could infiltrate the pavement structure, thereby addressing the same set of distresses linked to drainage deficiencies (section 2.3) [1]. The ultra‑thin bonded wearing course may be placed over an existing pavement to correct surface distresses such as raveling and moderate rutting while also sealing the pavement to reduce oxidation and weathering, which helps maintain resistance to fatigue and low‑temperature cracking (section 5.5) [1]. Thin asphalt overlays consisting of 6.3 mm or 9.5 mm Superpave wearing courses may be applied only when the existing surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than 2 percent patching required, and both surface and subsurface drainage are good so that no structural upgrade is needed; these overlays address surface cracking or roughness (section 5.12) [1]. Interlayers of geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh, or composite reinforcing grids are recommended when existing distresses consist of reflective or alligator cracking not tied to structural loss, providing a moisture barrier that helps prevent water‑induced distress such as potholes; they are not normally used for rutting, bleeding, or raveling (section 5.12) [1]. Full depth reclamation is described as an effective method for rehabilitating roads with severe distress and may be applied when the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, or drainage problems stemming from inadequate support layers must be addressed; the treatment eliminates those existing distresses and provides a renewed pavement base without additional surface elevation adjustments (section 5.15) [1]. The selection of the stabilization mechanism for full depth reclamation follows observed conditions: pulverization stabilization is used when only reclaimed in‑place material and moisture are employed; mechanical stabilization is chosen when additional aggregate or RAP is added during pulverization to improve gradation or raise elevation; chemical stabilization is applied when wet or dry additives such as cement, lime or fly ash are needed to increase strength (section 5.15 B) [1]. Hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt may remediate surface rutting, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, and minor corrugation or shoving, but only when those distresses are confined to the pavement surface and satisfy severity limits of rutting less than 1½ inches, crack depth less than 1.0 inch with average width not exceeding a medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and raveling not exceeding a medium rating; additional prerequisites include retaining at least 1½ inches of asphalt thickness beyond any milling depth, being free of rubber or fiber in the top two inches, having raised markers removed, and containing no mastic patches larger than five square feet, with no load‑related distress or base problems required (section 5.19) [1]. A bonded Portland cement concrete overlay is permitted only where the existing concrete pavement does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting; if significant distress is present it will appear through the bonded overlay unless repaired first, so the treatment is not recommended under such conditions (section 10.6) [1]. A thin bonded concrete overlay on asphalt (BCOA) with a concrete thickness between 2.5 inches and 4 inches may be used for typical distresses such as rutting, shoving, slippage and thermal cracking on streets, intersections or roads where those defects are present, while a conventional BCOA of four inches or more is also applied when a significant increase in structural capacity or life extension is desired; all applications are limited to areas like intersections, steep grades or locations expecting at least 2,500 ESALs per day (section 10.8) [1]. Routine‑maintenance activities that repair or retard existing distress include crack sealing for asphalt surface cracking and joint sealing for concrete joint cracking, with crack sealing scheduled cyclically every 3 to 5 years and joint sealing every 5 years; additional actions such as spall repair, mechanized patch, spray patch and minimal base repair also address existing distress (section 13.7) [1]. Preservation treatments intended to retard future deterioration—micro surfacing, asphalt resurfacing with leveling or milling, diamond grinding, base repair and concrete patching—must be selected before extensive distress appears; any patching exceeding 10 percent of the pavement area is generally not cost‑effective or permitted for preservation projects (section 13.7) [1]. The STAMPP program generates recommended treatments for each roadway segment, and a surface‑treatment recommendation from STAMP is normally taken as an indication that the pavement requires surfacing, typically an asphalt overlay (section 14.3) [1]. Under the 60‑month warranty for asphalt‑concrete pavement, if the year‑5 survey shows that at least thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, the segment must be treated with microsurfacing or another Department‑approved method (section 496.3(c)) [2]. If any combination of distress deviations exceeds twenty percent of the segment’s surface area, removal and replacement of the entire segment is required (section 496.3(c)) [2]. The warranty provisions also allow crack sealing for cracks listed in Table A, while all other identified distress zones must be repaired using square or rectangular patches; when individual repair locations lie less than one hundred feet apart they are combined into a single continuous repair, and all repairs must comply with the surface tolerance specified in Section 413.3(l) (section 496.3(c)) [2]. For cement‑concrete structures experiencing distress attributable to alkali‑silica reaction, the specification permits an exemption from using aggregates classified as potentially reactive if a service record documenting nonreactivity can be provided for at least ten structures each older than ten years (preferably fifteen) and visual examination reports any cracking or expansion symptoms together with petrographic analysis confirming no ASR gel (section 704.1(g)2) [2]. Alternatively, where twenty‑five percent extra cement is allowed instead of the standard anti‑washout admixture, the extra cement may be replaced with other cementitious material in the same proportions established in the mix design, providing an alternative mitigation for ASR‑related distress (section 704.1(c)) [2].

For asphalt‑concrete pavements that are covered by the 60‑month warranty, microsurfacing or any Department‑approved alternative becomes applicable when, after the year‑5 performance survey, at least thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses [2]. If combined deviations that exceed the Table A thresholds occupy greater than twenty percent of the segment surface, removal and full‑segment replacement is required [2]. When individual repair locations are spaced less than one hundred feet apart they must be treated as a single continuous repair, and all other distress patches are to be repaired with square or rectangular patches whose area stays within the limits set out in Table A (except where crack sealing is allowable) [2]. For cement‑concrete pavements, an exemption from the cement/cement‑SCM requirements is permitted only if a service record of nonreactivity can be documented; if field performance history or subsequent testing indicates that the aggregate source has begun to form ASR expansion the exemption is ruled out and only the prescriptive methods may be used [2]. In Section 4.1 Concrete Pavement Restoration, concrete patching is limited to projects in which the total percent patching required does not exceed 10 % (excluding existing patches in good condition); if that threshold is exceeded an analysis must be performed to assess cost‑effectiveness versus alternative treatments such as an overlay, and when the observed distresses are primarily load‑related the restoration work may need to include structural improvement [1]. In Section 5.5 Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course, the UTWC may be applied when surface distress is limited to low‑severity cracking or raveling, infrequent corrugations, settlements, heaves or slippage cracks, and medium‑severity rutting (including moderate rutting) [1]. In Section 5.12 Overlays, an asphalt‑paving interlayer may be specified only when widespread alligator cracking extends across the entire surface and is not confined to wheel paths; other distress such as rutting, bleeding, raveling, or active pumping indicating poor subgrade must be treated before an interlayer can be used [1]. In Section 5.15 Full Depth Reclamation Techniques, full depth reclamation is indicated when a pavement exhibits severe distress [1]. In Section 5.19 Hot‑In‑Place Recycled Asphalt, HIR may be used only if rutting severity is less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth is less than 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding ≤ ½ inch, raveling or weathering does not exceed the medium rating, and any corrugation, shoving or slippage has an average depth under 1½ inches; exceeding these surface‑only thresholds rules out HIR [1]. In Section 10.6 Bonded PCC Overlay of PCC Pavement, a bonded PCC overlay may be used only where the existing concrete pavement does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting, and if significant distress is present it must be repaired or corrected before the overlay can be applied [1]. In Section 10.8 Bonded PCC / Thin Bonded PCC Overlay of AC Pavement, a bonded concrete overlay on asphalt may be applied when an existing milled asphalt pavement still retains at least a 3‑inch asphalt layer and shows typical surface distresses such as rutting, shoving, slippage or thermal cracking; the treatment is specifically recommended for intersections or steep grades (including ramps) where those distresses exist and where traffic is anticipated to be at least 2,500 ESALs per day [1]. In Section 13.7 Pavement Project Criteria > E. Pavement Testing, a pavement preservation treatment is considered applicable only when the required patching does not exceed ten percent of the pavement area; if the needed patching exceeds ten percent of the pavement area, preservation is generally not cost‑effective or permitted [1].

Prevention in design and construction

The Pavement Policy Manual recommends installing a subsurface drainage system—continuous edge drains, lateral underdrains placed in wet subgrade areas and at cut‑to‑fill transitions together with highly permeable base and subbase materials, maximized subbase slopes and closely spaced drain pipes or outlets—to remove water from beneath the pavement; this measure is intended to reduce transverse cracking of concrete pavements, joint tenting caused by frozen trapped water, and surface rutting or roughness in asphalt that arise from a softened, poorly supported subgrade, and it applies wherever moisture‑associated damage adjacent to thermal cracks is identified (section 2.3 DRAINAGE) [1]. The guide further advises that when the cause analysis classifies distress as primarily load‑related, structural improvement such as load‑transfer restoration using dowel retrofits should be incorporated to reduce those load‑related distresses (section 4.1 CONCRETE PAVEMENT RESTORATION) [1]. When the primary cause is climate or material durability—including drainage problems—the work should be selected to reduce the climatic effect, for example by installing pavement base drains (section 4.1) [1]. The Ultra‑thin Bonded Wearing Course seals the pavement, thereby reducing oxidation and weathering; this reduction helps keep the surface resilient to fatigue and low‑temperature cracking and can also correct surface distresses such as raveling and moderate rutting (section 5.5 ULTRA‑THIN BONDED WEARING COURSE) [1]. Rigid or flexible overlays are employed when the existing pavement condition warrants an overlay to correct structural deficiencies, surface deterioration such as cracking, rutting or potholes, and rideability; thin Superpave asphalt overlays may be applied on sound asphalt surfaces that are structurally sound, require less than 2 % patching, have good surface and subsurface drainage and need no structural upgrade (section 5.12 OVERLAYS) [1]. The Safety Edge is used as a standard pavement edge treatment to address alligator cracking not tied to structural loss (section 5.12 OVERLAYS) [1]. Full Depth Reclamation is specified for pavements experiencing severe distress; by pulverizing completely through the asphalt and underlying base layers it eliminates existing distresses, removes the potential for reflective cracking, restores drainage or cross slopes, and can add structure in stages, and it applies wherever severe distress is present and agencies seek to reclaim bases (section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES) [1]. In the Full Depth Reclamation selection process any identified wet subgrade locations must be corrected by installing effective drainage before reclamation, a step intended to eliminate water‑related distress such as loss of material integrity and weakened structural capacity (section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES) [1]. Mechanical stabilization may improve the structural integrity of the reclaimed base and therefore reduce cracking or rutting when a stronger support layer is needed (section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES) [1]. Generally, increasing the amount of chemical stabilizer increases strength, which can mitigate strength‑related distresses (section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES) [1]. The Hot‑In‑Place Recycled asphalt process may remediate rutting and surface distress for pavements whose distresses are limited to the surface and meet the candidate limits of rutting severity less than 1½ in., transverse or miscellaneous cracking depth less than 1.0 in. with average crack width ≤ ½ in., and raveling/weathering not exceeding a medium rating; it is intended as an interim wearing surface that must be followed by a permanent surface before the end of the construction season, and milling prior to HIR is recommended when deeper cracks are present (section 5.19 HOT IN‑PLACE RECYCLED ASPHALT) [1]. A bonded Portland cement concrete overlay can reduce pavement distress by forming a monolithic slab with the existing concrete, thereby addressing cracking and faulting, but it should be applied only where the existing concrete does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting (section 10.6 BONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT) [1]. Bonded Concrete Overlay on Asphalt is used to add structural capacity and mitigate surface distresses; thin BCOA may be appropriate where rutting, shoving, slippage or thermal cracking are present or anticipated, particularly at intersections, steep grades or locations that anticipate at least 2,500 ESALs per day, while conventional BCOA is also appropriate in the same distress situations when a significant improvement in structural capacity or pavement‑life extension is desired without thick asphalt overlays (section 10.8 BONDED PCC / THIN BONDED PCC OVERLAY OF AC PAVEMENT) [1]. Routine maintenance measures such as crack sealing, joint sealing, spall repair, mechanized patch, spray patch and minimal base repair are intended to repair or retard existing distress including cracking, joint faulting and spalling; cyclic crack sealing should be performed every 3–5 years on asphalt surfaces and joint sealing every 5 years on concrete pavements (section 13.7 PAVEMENT PROJECT CRITERIA > E) [1]. Pavement‑preservation measures—including micro surfacing, asphalt resurfacing with leveling or milling, diamond grinding, base repair and concrete patching—are cost‑effective treatments that retard future deterioration such as cracking, rutting or edge deterioration, and these preservation projects must be selected prior to extensive deterioration and in accordance with the appropriate treatment cycle (section 13.7) [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania specifications require furnishing the chemical admixtures specified in Section 711.3 as a design step intended to limit alkali‑silica reaction–induced cracking and microcracking (section 704.1 GENERAL) [2]. If a documented service record of nonreactivity—showing at least ten structures each over ten years of age and preferably over fifteen—is available, the Department may exempt aggregates that test as potentially reactive from the cement or supplementary cementitious material requirements specified in Section 704.1(g)3.c, thereby reducing ASR‑related distress under that documented condition (section 704.1 GENERAL) [2]. Additionally, when a project is permitted to use 25 % extra cement instead of the standard anti‑washout admixture referenced in Section 1001.3(k)3.a, the extra cement may be substituted with other cementitious material and additional admixtures as needed, which can also help control distress when that substitution is applied (section 704.1 GENERAL) [2].

Highly permeable base and subbase materials are recommended to mitigate moisture‑related distress such as shrinkage, thermal cracking, rutting and loss of ride quality by allowing gravity‑drainable water to be removed rapidly (Section 2.3) [1]. The use of lateral underdrains in wet subgrade areas and at cut‑to‑fill transitions is strongly encouraged to prevent surface rutting, transverse cracking in concrete and joint tenting by removing trapped moisture (Section 2.3) [1]. Continuous pavement base drains along the outside edge, on the low side of superelevated sections and in medians are required to eliminate moisture that can cause surface rutting and cracking (Section 2.3) [1]. Subgrade drains should be considered wherever existing roadway shows water damage or sagging to address the same moisture‑related mechanisms (Section 2.3) [1]. When distress is primarily load‑related, restoration work may need to include structural improvement such as dowel retrofits to transfer loads and alleviate load‑related distresses (Section 4.1) [1]. Installation of pavement base drains is also prescribed for climate‑ or material‑related distress that includes drainage problems (Section 4.1) [1]. Joint cleaning and sealing after diamond grinding must be performed to obtain the proper sealant reservoir shape factor and avoid sealant damage, thereby preventing further cracking (Section 4.1) [1]. An ultra‑thin bonded wearing course seals the surface, reducing oxidation and weathering, which helps retain resilience against fatigue and low‑temperature cracking and can correct surface raveling and moderate rutting (Section 5.5) [1]. A scratch or leveling course placed to fill wheel ruts and other small depressions before an overlay prevents further rutting‑type surface deterioration (Section 5.12 A) [1]. Asphalt interlayers such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh or composite reinforcing grids provide a moisture barrier and stress‑absorbing layer that retards reflective cracking, blocks water intrusion and prevents potholes, stripping and crack propagation when non‑structural cracking and moisture damage are present (Section 5.12 F) [1]. Full‑Depth Reclamation pulverizes through the entire asphalt layer into the underlying base, eliminating existing distresses and preventing reflective cracking or failure caused by a weak base, especially where wet subgrade locations have been identified and effective drainage installed beforehand (Sections 5.15 B, 5.15 C) [1]. Stabilization of reclaimed material with chemical agents, asphalt or calcium chloride may be employed to further mitigate identified rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues (Section 5.15 C) [1]. Hot‑In‑Place Recycled asphalt is permitted only where rutting severity is less than 1½ in., transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth is under 1 in. with average crack width not exceeding ½ in., raveling/weathering does not exceed the Medium Rating, corrugation/shoving/slippage depth is under 1½ in., and only isolated bleeding is present; these limits are intended to prevent further rutting, cracking and raveling (Section 5.19 C) [1]. A bonded PCC overlay of existing concrete pavement may be used only where the existing surface does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting, allowing the overlay to act as a monolithic unit that avoids transmitting pre‑existing distress upward (Section 10.6) [1]. Similarly, a bonded concrete overlay placed over an asphalt base is prescribed to mitigate surface rutting, shoving, slippage and thermal cracking when the existing pavement retains good‑to‑fair structural condition, the location is an intersection or steep grade exhibiting those distresses, or traffic volumes are expected to reach at least 2 500 ESALs per day (Section 10.8) [1]. Cyclic crack sealing of asphalt surfaces every three to five years and joint sealing of concrete pavements every five years are emphasized to retard existing cracking and joint deterioration (Section 13.7) [1]. A suite of preservation treatments—including micro‑surfacing, asphalt resurfacing with leveling or milling, diamond grinding, base repair and concrete patching—are identified as cost‑effective for retarding future deterioration such as cracking, rutting, edge loss, raveling, spalling or faulting when projects are selected before extensive deterioration (Section 13.7) [1]. Providing the chemical admixtures required in Section 711.3 is intended to mitigate alkali‑silica reaction (ASR)‑related cracking, including microcracking that may appear as distress (Section 704—CEMENT CONCRETE > 704.1 GENERAL > 3.c) [2]. The Department may waive cement or supplementary cementitious material requirements for aggregates classified as potentially reactive when a documented service record of nonreactivity covering at least ten structures each older than ten years is submitted, thereby preventing ASR‑induced cracking (Section 704—CEMENT CONCRETE > 704.1 GENERAL > 3.c) [2]. If the project permits 25 % extra cement in place of the standard anti‑washout admixture per Section 1001.3(k)3.a, the extra cement may be used to satisfy performance criteria and avoid related distress (Section 704—CEMENT CONCRETE > 704.1 GENERAL > 3.c) [2].

Specification limits and acceptance

In the Concrete Pavement Restoration guidelines (Chapter 4, Section 4.1), distress is quantified by measuring the total percent of concrete that must be patched; the acceptance criterion states that if the percent patching needed on the current project exceeds 10 %—excluding existing patches in good condition—an additional cost‑effectiveness analysis is required, and the visible distress quantities are to be measured and tabulated as part of the preliminary engineering evaluation. Under the Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course conditions for use (Chapter 5, Section 5.5), distress of candidate pavements should be limited to low severity cracking or raveling, infrequent corrugations, settlements, heaves, or slippage cracks, and medium severity rutting. For non‑Interstate thin asphalt overlays the specification limits the amount of existing distress by requiring that the existing pavement be structurally sound and that less than 2 % of the pavement requires patching; this condition is evaluated by surveying the percentage of surface needing patch repair (Section 5.12, Subsection B Thin Asphalt Overlays). In the Hot‑In‑Place Recycled Asphalt guidelines (Chapter 5, Section 5.19, subsection C), the acceptance limits for pavement distress are defined by the Pub. 336 criteria: rutting severity must be less than 1 ½ inches measured by rut depth; transverse and miscellaneous cracking severity must have a depth less than 1.0 inch and an average crack width that does not exceed the Medium Rating of ≤ ½ inch; raveling or weathering severity may not exceed the Medium Rating, which is judged by a surface that is rough, pitted and may contain loose particles; minor corrugation, shoving and slippage are also permissible provided their average depth is less than 1 ½ inches.
