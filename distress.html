Distress

Part of: Pavement Condition Assessment is-subtopic-of
Distress is a topic under Pavement Condition Assessment.
distressdistresses
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-30

Distress in pavement engineering is defined as any observable deterioration—cracking, rutting, raveling, bleeding, potholes, edge deterioration, joint spalling or faulting, and other surface or internal defects—that signals a loss of structural integrity[1]. It is seen on concrete slabs, joints, shoulders and on asphalt layers or overlays, where saturated subgrades produce transverse cracks, deteriorated joints trap water that can freeze and cause tenting, and fluid bases generate surface roughness or rutting that mirrors underlying deformation[1]. These conditions result from traffic loading, inadequate subsurface drainage, freeze‑thaw action and material aging, leading to reduced structural capacity, poorer ride quality and shortened pavement service life[1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Distress” also include the following subtopics: Bleeding, Broken Slab, Corner Break, Corrugation and Shoving, Cracking, Depression / Settlement, Distress Severity, Faulting, Joint Deterioration, Pumping, Raveling, Rutting, Slab Migration, Spalling, Stripping, Void (loss of support), and Wheel Path.

Description and identification

The Pavement Policy Manual defines distress in pavement engineering as observable deterioration such as cracking, rutting, raveling, bleeding, potholes, edge deterioration, joint spalling or faulting, and other surface or internal defects that indicate loss of structural integrity. [1] In concrete pavements saturated subgrades may provide poor support and can result in transverse cracks under traffic loads. [1] When a joint has deteriorated into particles small enough to impede gravity drainage, water can become trapped beneath the joint and, in winter, may freeze and cause tenting, an upward movement of the joint due to localized frost heave. [1] In asphalt layers a fluid base lacking support can produce surface roughness, and one type of rutting of the surface is a reflection of rutting of the subgrade. [1] For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses acceptable distress includes low severity cracking or raveling, infrequent corrugations, settlements, heaves or slippage cracks, and medium severity rutting that appear as minor surface cracks, loss of aggregate, occasional wave‑like corrugations, localized vertical movements and longitudinal depressions not deeper than a medium severity level. [1] Overlays consider existing distresses such as alligator cracking that spans the entire roadway surface; water intrusion into the structural section is identified as a major cause of many types of distress including loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, localized failures and crack propagation. [1] In bonded PCC overlays any great extent of cracking or faulting in the underlying concrete slab is regarded as distress and, if significant and not repaired, may be reflected up through the overlay to become visible on its surface. [1] For hot‑in‑place recycled candidate pavements distress is limited to surface‑level deterioration; rutting severity should be < 1 ½ inches, the average crack width should not exceed the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and minor corrugation, shoving or slippage should have an average depth < 1 ½ inches. [1] Typical asphalt distresses such as rutting, shoving, slippage and thermal cracking manifest as longitudinal depressions, displaced material and temperature‑induced cracks, often concentrated at intersections or steep grades. [1] Full‑depth reclamation surveys record surface defects including rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems with severity levels to guide rehabilitation. [1] Annual automated pavement condition surveys identify distress such as edge deterioration, joint spalling, joint faulting and premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements, which may be linked to inadequate subbase material. [1] In the 60‑month warranty for asphalt concrete pavement, distress is defined as any observable deterioration—including flushing, potholes and rutting—that is identified during the required performance surveys. [2] These distresses appear on the pavement surface as flushing or pothole openings that are recorded by manual surveys and as longitudinal depressions whose depth is verified with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width; internal failures such as fatigue cracking may also be noted. [2] In the full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slab specification, distress is manifested as surface spalling or cracking that can occur during handling, construction, or service. [2] Spalls are visible loss of concrete on the top surface or edges and must be repaired according to Section 501.3(t). [2] Cracks are evaluated by width and depth relative to slab thickness D: any crack wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than D/3 requires full‑depth slab replacement; cracks wider than 0.007 inches but shallower than D/3 are repaired per ACI 224R recommendations; and cracks 0.007 inches wide or less are sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate followed by sand after about 20 minutes. [2]

In section 2.3 DRAINAGE of the Pavement Policy Manual, engineers recognize distress caused by inadequate subsurface drainage by observing specific forms and locations that differ from purely temperature‑driven cracking; concrete slabs develop transverse cracks under traffic loads when a saturated subgrade cannot support the slab, frozen water trapped beneath a deteriorated joint produces tenting, and surface ruts that mirror the shape of underlying subgrade deformation indicate a softened base rather than pure asphalt rutting, and by noting whether moisture‑related symptoms such as trapped water or frost heave accompany the distress they can distinguish drainage‑induced damage from thermal shrinkage cracks which are not slowed by drainage but may have adjacent moisture‑associated damage. [1] In section 4.1 CONCRETE PAVEMENT RESTORATION (CPR) of the same manual, distress is first recognized by measuring and tabulating the exact quantities of visible damage on the pavement surface; targeted core sampling near deteriorated joints and at random locations then confirms subbase or subgrade conditions and the depth of deterioration, while non‑destructive techniques such as deflection tests and faulting measurements are applied to pinpoint which joints require replacement or slab stabilization, allowing separation of surface cracking from underlying structural problems. [1] The manual further classifies observed distress into load‑related or climate/materials‑related categories—including drainage‑related distresses—thereby distinguishing it from other conditions that may appear similar but have different causes; if the measured percent patching needed exceeds 10 % (excluding existing good patches), a cost‑effectiveness analysis must be performed before selecting a repair strategy. [1] In section 5.5 ULTRA‑THIN BONDED WEARING COURSE, subsection B, acceptable distress on candidate pavements is limited to low‑severity cracking or raveling, infrequent corrugations, settlements, heaves or slippage cracks, and medium‑severity rutting; any other types or higher severities fall outside the conditions for use. [1] In section 5.19 HOT IN‑PLACE RECYCLED ASPHALT, subsection C, distress is recognized by confirming that damage exists only on the surface of the pavement; the criteria require rutting severity to be less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth to be less than 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding the Medium Rating of ≤ ½ inch, and raveling/weathering severity not to exceed the Medium Rating even though the surface may be rough or pitted, while minor corrugation, shoving, slippage, bleeding, or low‑friction conditions are acceptable provided their average depth is less than 1½ inches and bleeding occurs only in isolated areas, thereby separating true surface distress from deeper structural issues. [1] Section 10.6 BONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT states that a great extent of cracking and/or faulting in the existing concrete pavement signals significant distress; such extensive damage would be reflected through a bonded overlay unless it is repaired, thus distinguishing severe underlying distress from minor surface imperfections that do not preclude bonding. [1] Section 13.7 PAVEMENT PROJECT CRITERIA, subsection E, describes how annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (Videologging) under the STAMPP program record distinct categories of distress—cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting—enabling engineers to tell one form of damage apart from another; the program also notes that certain conditions such as premature mid‑slab cracking caused by inadequate subbase material may not be detectable and therefore require field observation. [1] In Section 496—Asphalt Concrete Pavement, under the 60‑month warranty of the Department of Transportation specifications, distress on the asphalt concrete pavement is first recognized through automated surveys that identify, measure and report each type of distress in accordance with Publication 336; flushing and potholes are confirmed by manual surveys. [2] The severity and extent of each identified distress are quantified using prescribed measurement methods—for example rut depth is verified with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width—and then compared against the limits set out in Table A, so that if a year‑5 survey determines that 30 % of segment area has two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action is required. [2] To distinguish a distress from similar conditions, the Department evaluates probable cause and excludes those attributable to base condition failures, traffic overloads, routine maintenance, destructive procedures, uncontrolled forces of nature or accident‑related damage, treating only contractor‑responsible distresses for repair. [2] In Section 529—Full Depth Precast/Prestressed Concrete Pavement Slabs, distress is identified by visual inspection and precise measurement of crack opening and depth; cracks that open wider than 0.007 inches and extend deeper than one‑third of the slab thickness (D/3) are classified as severe enough to require full slab replacement at no additional cost to the Department. [2] Cracks wider than 0.007 inches but shallower than D/3 are treated by repair according to the latest version of Control of Cracking in Concrete Structures (ACI 224R), while cracks 0.007 inches wide or less are sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate. [2] Surface spalling is recognized as damage to the top surface or edges and must be repaired according to Section 501.3(t); any recurring distress prompts a stop‑work until the cause is resolved. [2]

Where it occurs

Distress is observed on concrete pavements, including slab surfaces, joints and shoulder sections integral to the concrete structure, where inadequate drainage leads to cracking, joint tenting, surface rutting, loss of support and shrinkage cracks. Asphalt‑over‑concrete overlays and conventional asphalt layers also exhibit distress such as transverse cracking, raveling, moderate rutting and potholes that arise from insufficient subsurface drainage. In the Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course, surface distress appears on the asphalt concrete wearing layer, and the specification limits candidate‑pavement distress to low‑severity cracking or raveling, infrequent corrugations, settlements, heaves or slippage cracks, and medium‑severity rutting. Rigid Portland cement concrete (PCC) overlays and flexible asphalt concrete overlays are applied over existing pavements that may be an asphalt surface, a PCC surface or a composite structure such as an asphalt overlay on a concrete base; the presence of distress on the underlying pavement—cracking, faulting, rutting, shoving, slippage or thermal cracking—is recognized and must be addressed because it can be reflected through the new overlay. For bonded PCC overlays placed on existing PCC pavements, any significant amount of cracking or faulting is considered distress because it will be transmitted up through the overlay if not repaired. When a bonded PCC overlay is to be placed on an asphalt substrate, the guidance requires a minimum milled‑asphalt thickness of 3 inches; thin bonded concrete overlays may have a nominal concrete thickness no less than 2.5 inches and no more than 4 inches, while conventional bonded concrete overlays shall have a thickness of 4 inches or more. Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) projects address distress that appears on the surface of the existing pavement, which may be an asphalt wearing course, a full‑depth asphalt structure, or an asphalt layer over a concrete base; acceptable limits are rutting severity less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth less than 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding ½ inch, and medium‑rated raveling or weathering. The STAMPP program also records distress on shoulder elements, including shoulder drop‑off, rutting and fatigue cracking. Distress is evaluated on the asphalt‑concrete pavement covered by Section 496’s 60‑month warranty, where automated and manual surveys identify flushing, potholes and rutting in accordance with Publication 336 and apply performance criteria to each roughly one‑half‑mile segment. For that warranted asphalt‑concrete pavement—including any overlays or shoulders that form part of the same structure—if the year‑5 survey determines that 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action such as microsurfacing must be performed. Distress is also addressed for full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs covered in Section 529 (rigid pavements); the specification requires repair of all spalls and defines crack treatment limits: cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third of the slab depth must trigger slab replacement, while cracks wider than 0.007 inches but shallower than one‑third of the depth are to be repaired according to the latest ACI 224R guidance. [1][2]

The Pavement Policy Manual notes that distress linked to inadequate drainage is observed at deteriorated joints where water becomes trapped beneath the joint, appears in the concrete slab interior as transverse cracks caused by saturated subgrades, and manifests in underlying layers when a softened or fluid base produces surface roughness and rutting that reflects deformation of the subgrade (section 2.3 DRAINAGE) [1]. In concrete pavement restoration, distress is identified at deteriorated joints on the concrete slab and may extend into the underlying layers; consequently cores are taken near those joints to evaluate whether the deterioration reaches the subbase or subgrade below the pavement surface (section 4.1 CONCRETE PAVEMENT RESTORATION) [1]. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, distress is described as a surface phenomenon affecting the UTWC layer itself, with surface distresses such as raveling and moderate rutting being the primary forms addressed by this treatment (section 5.5 ULTRA‑THIN BONDED WEARING COURSE) [1]. The manual also indicates that alligator cracking can appear across the entire roadway surface rather than being confined to wheel paths, reflective cracking propagates through existing cracks at layer interfaces, and active pumping signals a poor subgrade condition that must be treated before applying an interlayer; interlayers are intended for cracking not tied to structural deficiency (section 5.12 OVERLAYS) [1]. In the hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt candidate‑pavement guidelines, distress is required to be limited to the pavement’s exposed layer; rutting severity shall be less than 1 ½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth shall be less than 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding the medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and raveling/weathering shall not exceed the medium rating (section 5.19 HOT IN‑PLACE RECYCLED ASPHALT) [1]. For bonded PCC overlays of existing concrete pavements, relevant distress is found in the existing concrete slab itself, appearing as cracking or faulting of the slab; if a significant amount of distress exists in that concrete it will be reflected up through the bonded overlay unless repaired (section 10.6 BONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT) [1]. Recorded Interstate system distress includes cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting; these manifestations appear at joints, within cracks that may extend across slab interiors such as premature mid‑slab cracking caused by inadequate subbase material, along edges, and in wheel paths where rutting develops (section 13.7 PAVEMENT PROJECT CRITERIA) [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications describe distress for full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs as surface spalling, cracking of the concrete slab, and excessive joint vertical differentials; these appear on the top face, at edges, and at longitudinal or transverse joints of the precast slabs (section 529.3 CONSTRUCTION) [2]. Cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third the slab depth (D/3) must be replaced, while narrower cracks are repaired in accordance with ACI 224R; any spalls on the surface or edges are repaired according to Section 501.3(t), and joint vertical differential may not exceed ¼ inch across any joint, otherwise grinding or re‑grading is required (section 529.3(f)6‑7) [2].

Causes and mechanisms

Distress in pavements is attributed to several distinct cause groups. Traffic loading is a primary driver, as reflected by Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) data that are used to set priorities; under traffic loads saturated subgrades provide poor support for concrete pavements, which can result in transverse cracks, and surface rutting may mirror rutting of the subgrade. Climate effects, especially freeze‑thaw cycles, contribute to distress by promoting water‑induced deterioration such as loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, crack propagation, and overall pavement degradation; they also aggravate shrinkage cracking when moisture is present. Moisture‑related drainage problems are identified as a separate cause: inadequate subsurface drainage can lead to transverse cracking, surface roughness from lack of support of a fluid base, and the presence of active pumping usually indicates a poor subgrade condition that must be treated before overlay work. Material‑related influences include age oxidation of asphalt surfaces, which is most likely responsible for alligator cracking that extends across the entire roadway, as well as aggregate issues such as Vanport Limestone concerns and Alkali Silica Reaction that can cause deterioration even when not captured by surveys. Design or construction deficiencies are noted where premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements may be caused by inadequate subbase material. The guidelines in Section 4.1 (Concrete Pavement Restoration) require identification of these causes before restoration because uncorrected causes will cause the work to fail; Table 4.1 in that section categorizes concrete‑pavement distress into two primary groups—load‑related distresses and climate/materials‑related distresses, the latter encompassing drainage‑related problems. [1]

Within section 2.3 DRAINAGE, distress develops when water infiltrates the pavement structure, reducing subgrade or base support and permitting traffic loads to produce transverse cracks in concrete and surface rutting that reflects a softened subgrade in asphalt. The progression is accelerated whenever drainage is inadequate—ponded surface water can penetrate layers, saturated subgrades act as fluid support, frozen underlying soil prevents percolation during spring thaw, trapped moisture may freeze causing joint tenting, and capillary water held in fine soils impedes removal; consequently, poor permeability, low subbase slope, wide drain spacing, high water tables, or seepage from cuts increase the rate of moisture‑related cracking, rutting and surface roughness. Section 4.1 identifies two primary mechanisms: repeated traffic loads that produce structural damage such as cracking and faulting, and climate or material durability factors—including frost damage, polishing‑susceptible aggregate, and inadequate drainage—that degrade the concrete matrix and joint sealants. These mechanisms progress more rapidly when traffic loads exceed design expectations, when the subgrade or subbase is weak, when drainage is poor, and when aggregates prone to polishing are present. Section 5.12 notes that distress develops when water infiltrates the structural section of an existing pavement, weakening subgrade support and leading to stripping, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation and overall deterioration; freeze‑thaw cycles further expand and contract that moisture, accelerating cracking, while age oxidation of the asphalt produces alligator cracking across the surface. Poor surface or subsurface drainage, active pumping that signals subgrade problems, and inadequate structural condition of the existing pavement also accelerate these mechanisms. In section 5.15, excess moisture infiltrating the pavement structure causes loss of material integrity and weakens structural capacity, linking rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems to moisture accumulation; poor drainage that allows water to collect in the subgrade and any pre‑existing drainage issues further accelerate progression, while high future traffic loading can exacerbate damage. Section 10.6 explains that when a bonded PCC overlay is placed over pre‑existing cracks or faulting, the two layers act as a monolithic slab so that the underlying imperfections propagate upward into the overlay and become visible surface distress; the progression is accelerated whenever the original pavement contains an extensive extent of unrepaired cracking or faulting because the bonded interface transmits those defects directly to the new layer. Section 13.7 records that annual surveys observe distress as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and faulting, which progresses under repeated traffic loading (ESAL); acceleration occurs when underlying deficiencies exist such as inadequate subbase material that may cause premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements, aggregate issues like Vanport Limestone concerns or Alkali Silica Reaction, and when patching exceeds 10 % of the pavement area. Under the 60‑month warranty in SECTION 496, distress develops when pavement performance criteria are not met, typically appearing as fatigue cracking or rutting that stem from an inadequate base condition and traffic loads that exceed design expectations. The mechanism is accelerated when cumulative ESALs surpass 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life ESALs, when the base fails to meet minimum thickness requirements or a density greater than 92 % of theoretical maximum, or when external forces such as floods, earthquakes, or accidents act on the pavement; at that point the warranty shifts responsibility away from the contractor. [1][2]

Severity levels and rating

In the Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course, distress on candidate pavements is limited to low‑severity cracking or raveling, infrequent corrugations, settlements, heaves or slippage cracks, and medium‑severity rutting; the descriptors “low” and “medium” denote relative intensity while “infrequent” characterizes occurrence frequency[1]. For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) asphalt mixtures, the manual specifies that rutting severity shall be less than 1 ½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth shall be less than 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding the Medium Rating of ≤ ½ inch, raveling or weathering may not exceed the Medium Rating described as a rough, pitted surface that may have loose particles, and minor corrugation, shoving or slippage are acceptable provided their average depth is less than 1 ½ inches[1]. Under the 60‑month warranty for asphalt concrete pavement, only a single severity rating called low severity is defined; if the year‑5 performance survey finds that 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more distresses with low severity, microsurfacing remediation is required, and the specific performance criteria limits for distresses are presented in Table A of Section 496.3(c) without further numeric thresholds[2]. In the provisions for full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, cracks are evaluated by width and depth: any crack wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third of the slab thickness (D/3) mandates replacement of the slab at no additional cost to the Department; a crack wider than 0.007 inches but shallower than D/3 requires repair in accordance with the latest ACI 224R recommendations; cracks that are 0.007 inches wide or less are merely sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate, and vertical differential across any joint must not exceed 1/4 inch finished vertical differential[2].

Measurement, survey and extent

The condition of existing pavement is determined by measuring the exact quantities of visible distress and tabulating them [1]. Deflection tests, faulting measurements, or other non‑destructive techniques are then applied to identify problem joints and guide slab stabilization decisions [1]. Cores are taken near deteriorated joints and at random locations to evaluate subbase or subgrade conditions [1]. Distress is quantified through surveys that identify, measure, and estimate the extent of each defect in accordance with Publication 336, with flushing and potholes captured by manual surveys and all other distresses potentially recorded by automated surveys [2]. When rutting must be evaluated, the depth is verified by placing a 12‑foot straight edge across the full lane width and reading the depression to assess severity and extent [2]. The resulting measurements—severity, area or length as defined by the extent estimation procedures—are then reported for each segment [2].

Effects on performance

When subsurface drainage is inadequate, saturated subgrades lose their ability to support the pavement, leading to transverse cracks in concrete and surface rutting that mirrors a softened subgrade; the resulting fluid base also creates surface roughness and undulations that diminish ride quality. These moisture‑related distresses reduce the structural capacity of the pavement by lowering subgrade support, increase maintenance needs, and ultimately shorten the pavement’s service life. Distress manifested as structural deficiencies or surface deterioration directly diminishes a pavement’s structural capacity and ride quality, which is why overlays are prescribed in Section 5.12 to correct those conditions. When water infiltrates the structural section it can cause loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation and overall deterioration, thereby compromising durability and shortening service life. Existing distresses—including rutting, bleeding, raveling or moisture‑related cracking—must be considered in the interlayer selection, and appropriate interlayers such as geotextile fabric or reinforcing grids provide a moisture barrier that mitigates water‑induced distress; consequently, addressing these distresses through overlays or interlayers improves structural performance, rideability, safety and longevity. When surface distresses such as rutting, transverse or miscellaneous cracking, and raveling exceed the limits set for HIR candidate pavements—rutting greater than 1 ½ inches, crack depth deeper than 1.0 inch, average crack width larger than ½ inch, or raveling beyond a medium rating—the pavement is considered deteriorated, indicating loss of structural capacity, reduced ride quality, compromised safety, diminished durability and shortened service life; the HIR process is intended to remediate those distresses, thereby recovering performance, improving appearance, and prolonging service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–133)

Inadequate subsurface drainage leaves water in the base, so saturated subgrades first reduce support and cause transverse cracks in concrete pavements under traffic loads; as joints deteriorate and particles block gravity flow, trapped water may freeze in winter producing tenting of the joint, while the softened, fluid‑like subgrade permits surface rutting that mirrors the underlying rutting and can result in surface roughness that degrades ride quality, ultimately cascading into broader structural failures and a shortened pavement service life (section 2.3 DRAINAGE) [1]. When concrete‑pavement distress is left uncorrected during restoration, the same load‑related or climate/materials‑related problems reappear, allowing surface cracking to evolve into deeper slab deterioration; if the percent patching needed exceeds 10 % of the project area a cost‑effectiveness analysis must be performed to consider alternatives (section 4.1 CONCRETE PAVEMENT RESTORATION) [1]. Water intrusion can trigger a cascade of failures—loss of subgrade support, stripping of asphalt from aggregate, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation and freeze‑thaw induced deterioration—and alligator cracking that spreads across the entire surface signals age oxidation and will continue to worsen without remedial action such as an appropriate overlay or interlayer; other distresses like rutting, bleeding or raveling persist because they are not mitigated by interlayers, eventually demanding thicker structural repairs (section 5.12 OVERLAYS) [1]. When an existing concrete pavement exhibits a great extent of cracking or faulting, the distress is considered significant for a bonded PCC overlay; because a bonded overlay and the underlying slab act as a single monolithic unit, any untreated cracks or faults are transmitted upward into the new concrete layer, causing the overlay to develop similar deterioration (section 10.6 BONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT) [1]. Distress such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling or joint faulting is first recorded in the annual STAMPP surveys; if it is not repaired, routine maintenance activities that repair and/or retard existing distress are applied, but when needed patching exceeds 10 % of the pavement area preservation ends and more extensive rehabilitation becomes necessary, and continued neglect ultimately makes reconstruction required once functional pavement life has been exceeded (section 13.7 PAVEMENT PROJECT CRITERIA > E) [1]. Unaddressed cracking in a precast concrete slab can grow beyond the allowable width of 0.007 inches and, if its depth exceeds one‑third of the slab thickness (D/3), the slab is to be replaced at no additional cost under Section 529.3(c); shallower cracks that remain wider than 0.007 inches are required to be repaired according to ACI 224R, while very fine cracks (≤0.007 inches) are sealed with methacrylate as described in the same section (Section 529.3) [2]. Spalling of the top surface or edges must be repaired per Section 501.3(t), and recurring damage or distress triggers a stop to installation until the cause is identified and mitigated, preventing further deterioration (Section 529.3(c)) [2].

Repairs and treatments

When distress is linked to inadequate subsurface drainage, the manual calls for installing continuous pavement base drains along the outside edge and low side of superelevated sections, lateral underdrains in wet subgrade areas and cut‑to‑fill transitions, and subgrade drains where water damage or sagging is evident; surface runoff control such as clean ditches, positive outlet low points, clean inlets and pipes, and removal of excess shoulder material is required whenever ponded water could infiltrate the pavement structure (section 2.3) [1]. Those drainage measures address concrete transverse cracking and joint tenting, asphalt surface rutting that reflects a softened subgrade, surface roughness from loss of base support, and moisture‑related damage adjacent to thermal cracks (section 2.3) [1]. An ultra‑thin bonded wearing course may correct surface distresses such as raveling and moderate rutting when placed over the existing pavement (section 5.5) [1]. The course also seals the pavement, reducing oxidation and weathering of the surface, which helps keep the pavement resilient to fatigue and low‑temperature cracking (section 5.5) [1]. Thin asphalt overlays—specifically 6.3 mm and 9.5 mm Superpave wearing courses—may be used only when the existing surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than 2 % patching required, and surface and subsurface drainage are good so that no structural upgrade is needed; they address surface distress such as cracking or roughness (section 5.12) [1]. Interlayers of geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh, or composite reinforcing grids are recommended when existing distresses consist of reflective or alligator cracking not tied to structural loss, providing a moisture barrier that helps prevent water‑induced distress such as potholes; they are not normally used for rutting, bleeding, or raveling (section 5.12) [1]. Full depth reclamation is described as an effective method for rehabilitating distressed roads and may be applied when severe distress is present; the treatment eliminates existing distresses—including rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, or drainage problems that stem from inadequate support layers (section 5.15) [1]. The selection of the stabilization mechanism follows observed conditions: pulverization stabilization is used when only reclaimed in‑place material and moisture are employed; mechanical stabilization is chosen when additional aggregate or RAP is added during pulverization to improve gradation or raise elevation; chemical stabilization is applied when wet or dry additives such as cement, lime or fly ash are needed to increase strength (section 5.15 B) [1]. Hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt may remediate surface rutting, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, and minor corrugation or shoving, but only when the distresses are confined to the pavement surface and satisfy severity limits of rutting less than 1½ inches, crack depth less than 1.0 inch with average width not exceeding a medium rating of ≤ ½ inch, and raveling not exceeding a medium rating (section 5.19) [1]. Additional prerequisites are that the pavement have no load‑related distress or base problems, retain at least 1½ inches of asphalt thickness beyond any milling depth, be free of rubber or fiber in the top two inches, have raised markers removed, and contain no mastic patches larger than five square feet; under those conditions HIR may be applied regardless of ADT (section 5.19) [1]. A bonded Portland cement concrete overlay is permitted only where the existing concrete pavement does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting; if significant distress is present it will appear through the bonded overlay unless repaired first, so the treatment is not recommended under such conditions (section 10.6) [1]. A bonded concrete overlay on asphalt adds structural capacity and mitigates surface distresses on pavements that are in good‑to‑fair condition, provided the remaining asphalt after milling is at least three inches thick; a thin BCOA—defined by a concrete thickness between 2.5 inches and 4 inches—is appropriate for typical distresses such as rutting, shoving, slippage and thermal cracking on streets, intersections or roads where those defects are present, while a conventional BCOA of four inches or more covers the same distress types but is also applied when a significant increase in structural capacity or life extension is desired; all applications are limited to areas like intersections, steep grades or locations expecting at least 2,500 ESALs per day (section 10.8) [1]. Crack sealing for asphalt surface cracking and joint sealing for concrete joint cracking are routine‑maintenance actions that repair existing distress; crack sealing of asphalt pavements is scheduled cyclically every 3 to 5 years and joint sealing of concrete pavements every 5 years (section 13.7) [1]. Additional routine‑maintenance activities such as spall repair, mechanized patch, spray patch and minimal base repair also address existing distress (section 13.7) [1]. Preservation treatments intended to retard future deterioration—micro surfacing, asphalt resurfacing with leveling or milling, diamond grinding, base repair and concrete patching—must be selected before extensive distress appears; any patching exceeding 10 % of the pavement area is generally not cost‑effective or permitted for preservation projects (section 13.7) [1]. Rehabilitation may employ the same actions as preservation but with more extensive base repair, patching and overlays to increase structural capacity (section 13.7) [1]. Reconstruction is reserved for situations where functional pavement life has been exceeded (section 13.7) [1]. The STAMPP program generates recommended treatments for each roadway segment; a surface‑treatment recommendation from STAMP is normally taken as an indication that the pavement requires surfacing, typically an asphalt overlay (section 14.3) [1]. Under the 60‑month asphalt concrete pavement warranty, a year‑5 performance survey that determines at least 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses triggers microsurfacing—or any other Department‑approved treatment—to address those multiple low‑severity distresses (section 496.3(c)) [2]. For individual distress locations the warranty permits allowable crack sealing and square or rectangular patch repairs, provided the repairs conform to Table A limits and meet the surface tolerance of Section 413.3(l); when the distance between two repair patches is less than 100 ft the Department requires a single continuous repair rather than separate patches (section 496.3(c)) [2]. If a distress is deemed potentially harmful or unsafe, the Department may order an emergency repair, with the contractor required to acknowledge within 24 h and commence work within 48 h (section 496.3(c)) [2]. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, spalls are addressed by repairing them as called for in Section 501.3(t) (section 529.3) [2]. Cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third the slab depth (D/3) trigger a full‑slab replacement at no extra cost to the Department (section 529.3) [2]. Cracks wider than 0.007 inches but with a depth less than D/3 must be repaired according to the latest Control of Cracking in Concrete Structures (ACI 224R) (section 529.3) [2]. Cracks that are 0.007 inches wide or less are sealed with high molecular weight methacrylate after cleaning, squeegeeing and sand topping before traffic is allowed (section 529.3) [2].

Prevention in design and construction

To limit moisture‑induced distress the manual recommends installing a subsurface drainage system composed of continuous edge drains, lateral underdrains placed in wet subgrade areas and at cut‑to‑fill transitions, highly permeable base and subbase materials, maximized subbase slopes and closely spaced drain pipes or outlets; this measure is intended to reduce transverse cracking of concrete pavements, joint tenting caused by frozen trapped water, and surface rutting or roughness in asphalt that arise from a softened, poorly supported subgrade, and it applies wherever moisture‑associated damage adjacent to thermal cracks is identified (section 2.3 DRAINAGE) [1]. When the cause analysis classifies distress as primarily load‑related, the guide advises incorporating structural improvement such as load‑transfer restoration through dowel retrofits to reduce those load‑related distresses (section 4.1 CONCRETE PAVEMENT RESTORATION) [1]. If the primary cause is climate or material durability, work should be selected to reduce the climatic effect, for example by installing pavement base drains (section 4.1) [1]. The ultra‑thin bonded wearing course seals the surface, thereby reducing oxidation and weathering; this reduction helps maintain resilience to fatigue and low‑temperature cracking and can also correct surface distresses such as raveling and moderate rutting (section 5.5 ULTRA‑THIN BONDED WEARING COURSE) [1]. Rigid or flexible overlays are employed when the existing pavement condition warrants an overlay to correct structural deficiencies, surface deterioration such as cracking, rutting or potholes, and rideability; thin Superpave asphalt overlays may be applied on sound asphalt surfaces that are structurally sound, require less than 2 % patching, have good surface and subsurface drainage and need no structural upgrade (section 5.12 OVERLAYS) [1]. The Safety Edge is prescribed as a standard pavement edge treatment to address alligator cracking not tied to structural loss (section 5.12 OVERLAYS) [1]. Full depth reclamation is specified for pavements experiencing severe distress; by pulverizing completely through the asphalt and underlying base layers it eliminates existing distresses, removes the potential for reflective cracking, restores drainage or cross slopes, and can add structure in stages, applying wherever severe distress is present and agencies seek to reclaim bases (section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES) [1]. In the full‑depth reclamation selection process any identified wet subgrade locations must be corrected by installing effective drainage before reclamation, a step intended to eliminate water‑related distress such as loss of material integrity and weakened structural capacity (section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES) [1]. Mechanical stabilization may improve the structural integrity of the reclaimed base and therefore reduce cracking or rutting when a stronger support layer is needed (section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES) [1]. Generally, increasing the amount of chemical stabilizer increases strength, mitigating strength‑related distresses (section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES) [1]. The hot‑in‑place recycled asphalt process may remediate rutting and surface distress for pavements whose distresses are limited to the surface and meet candidate limits of rutting severity less than 1½ in., transverse or miscellaneous cracking depth less than 1.0 in. with average crack width ≤ ½ in., and raveling/weathering not exceeding a medium rating; it is intended as an interim wearing surface that must be followed by a permanent surface before the end of the construction season, and milling prior to HIR is recommended when deeper cracks are present (section 5.19 HOT IN‑PLACE RECYCLED ASPHALT) [1]. A bonded Portland cement concrete overlay can reduce pavement distress by forming a monolithic slab with the existing concrete, thereby addressing cracking and faulting, and it should be applied only where the existing concrete does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting (section 10.6 BONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT) [1]. Bonded concrete overlay on asphalt is used to add structural capacity and mitigate surface distresses; thin BCOA may be appropriate where rutting, shoving, slippage or thermal cracking are present or anticipated, particularly at intersections, steep grades or locations that anticipate at least 2 500 ESALs per day, while conventional BCOA is also appropriate in the same distress situations when a significant improvement in structural capacity or pavement‑life extension is desired without thick asphalt overlays (section 10.8 BONDED PCC / THIN BONDED PCC OVERLAY OF AC PAVEMENT) [1]. Routine maintenance measures such as crack sealing, joint sealing, spall repair, mechanized patch, spray patch and minimal base repair are intended to repair or retard existing distress including cracking, joint faulting and spalling; cyclic crack sealing should be performed every 3–5 years on asphalt surfaces and joint sealing every 5 years on concrete pavements (section 13.7 PAVEMENT PROJECT CRITERIA > E) [1]. Pavement‑preservation measures—including micro surfacing, asphalt resurfacing with leveling or milling, diamond grinding, base repair and concrete patching—are cost‑effective treatments that retard future deterioration such as cracking, rutting or edge deterioration, and these preservation projects must be selected prior to extensive deterioration and in accordance with the appropriate treatment cycle (section 13.7) [1]. The specification limits transverse joint openings to a maximum of ½ inch and longitudinal joint openings to a maximum of ¾ inch, thereby limiting joint‑related distress; it also restricts vertical differential across any joint to ¼ inch, permitting diamond grinding only when the excess is between ¼ inch and ½ inch and mandating slab removal if the differential exceeds ½ inch (Section 7 and Section 6) [2]. All spalls must be repaired in accordance with Section 501.3(t), addressing spalling distress directly (Section 11.a) [2]. Cracks wider than 0.007 inches that exceed D/3 are remedied by slab replacement, shallower cracks are repaired according to ACI 224R, and cracks ≤0.007 inches are sealed with methacrylate, thereby managing cracking distress (Section 11.b) [2]. Load transfer at transverse joints is ensured through the use of dowel slots or embedded dowels as detailed in Section 7.a, which mitigates distress from joint movement (Section 7.a) [2].

To mitigate moisture‑related distress such as shrinkage, thermal cracking, rutting and loss of ride quality caused by a softened subgrade, the guide recommends using highly permeable base and subbase materials that allow rapid gravity drainage (Section 2.3) [1]. In locations with wet subgrades or cut‑to‑fill transitions, the installation of lateral underdrains is strongly encouraged to remove trapped moisture and thereby prevent surface rutting, transverse cracking in concrete and joint tenting (Section 2.3) [1]. Where edge drains are missing, continuous pavement base drains should be provided along the outside edge, on the low side of superelevated sections and in medians to avert similar moisture‑related distress (Section 2.3) [1]. Subgrade drains are also recommended wherever existing roadway shows water damage or sagging, with the same intent of eliminating moisture that can cause rutting and cracking (Section 2.3) [1]. When distress is primarily load‑related, the guide calls for load‑transfer restoration through dowel retrofits to address load‑induced cracking and loss of continuity (Section 4.1) [1]. After diamond grinding, joint cleaning and sealing must be performed to obtain the proper sealant reservoir shape factor and avoid damage to the sealant, which helps prevent further joint deterioration (Section 4.1) [1]. Application of an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course seals the surface, reduces oxidation and weathering, thereby preserving resilience against fatigue and low‑temperature cracking and correcting surface raveling and moderate rutting (Section 5.5) [1]. Installing an asphalt interlayer such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh or composite reinforcing grids provides a moisture barrier and stress‑absorbing layer that retards reflective cracking, blocks water intrusion and thus prevents potholes, stripping, crack propagation and other moisture‑related distress when the existing pavement exhibits non‑structural cracking (Section 5.12 F) [1]. Full‑Depth Reclamation is prescribed for severely distressed roads; by pulverizing through the entire asphalt layer into the underlying base it eliminates existing distresses and, when wet subgrade locations are identified and effective drainage installed beforehand, prevents reflective cracking or failure caused by a weak base (Sections 5.15 B, 5.15 C) [1]. Hot‑In‑Place Recycled asphalt is permitted only where surface rutting is less than 1½ in., transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth is under 1 in. with average crack width not exceeding ½ in., raveling or weathering does not exceed the Medium Rating, minor corrugation, shoving or slippage depth is under 1½ in., and only isolated bleeding is present; these limits are intended to prevent further rutting, cracking and raveling (Section 5.19 C) [1]. A bonded PCC overlay of existing concrete pavement may be used only where the existing surface does not exhibit a great extent of cracking or faulting, allowing the overlay to act as a monolithic unit with the slab and avoid transmitting pre‑existing distress upward (Section 10.6) [1]. Similarly, a bonded concrete overlay placed over an asphalt base is prescribed to mitigate surface rutting, shoving, slippage and thermal cracking when the existing pavement retains good‑to‑fair structural condition, the location is an intersection or steep grade exhibiting those distresses, or traffic volumes are expected to reach at least 2 500 ESALs per day (Section 10.8) [1]. Routine preservation actions such as cyclic crack sealing of asphalt surfaces every three to five years and joint sealing of concrete pavements every five years are emphasized to retard existing cracking and joint deterioration (Section 13.7) [1]. A suite of preservation treatments—including micro‑surfacing, asphalt resurfacing with leveling or milling, diamond grinding, base repair and concrete patching—are identified as cost‑effective for retarding future deterioration such as cracking, rutting, edge loss, raveling, spalling or faulting when projects are selected before extensive deterioration (Section 13.7) [1]. Load‑transfer dowels and tie bars must be cast into each precast slab in accordance with the shop drawings to prevent joint distress such as cracking or loss of continuity (Section 529.3(f)8) [2]. The portions of those dowels that are embedded in the slab must be coated with a bond breaker so that bonding does not occur and cause joint‑related cracking (Section 529.3(f)12) [2]. Transverse joints are limited to widths between ¼ in. and ½ in., longitudinal joints may not exceed ¾ in., and vertical differentials across any joint must stay within ¼ in.; these geometry limits are required during slab placement to keep edge‑distress and faulting from developing (Sections 7 and 6) [2]. The subbase shall be prepared with compacted No. 2A aggregate shaped to a surface tolerance of ±½ in., a condition intended to prevent settlement‑related rutting or cracking (Section 5) [2]. Slabs may only be opened to traffic after the dowel and bedding grout achieve at least 2 500 psi compressive strength, a requirement that averts premature cracking under load (Section 10) [2].

Specification limits and acceptance

In the Concrete Pavement Restoration guidelines (Chapter 4, Section 4.1) distress is quantified by measuring the total percent of concrete that must be patched and the acceptance criterion states that if the percent patching needed on the current project exceeds 10 %—excluding existing patches in good condition—an additional cost‑effectiveness analysis is required, with the visible distress quantities measured and tabulated as part of the preliminary engineering evaluation. Under the Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course conditions for use (Chapter 5, Section 5.5) distress of candidate pavements should be limited to low severity cracking or raveling, infrequent corrugations, settlements, heaves, or slippage cracks, and medium severity rutting. For non‑Interstate thin asphalt overlays the specification limits the amount of existing distress by requiring that the existing pavement be structurally sound and that less than 2 % of the pavement requires patching; this condition is evaluated by surveying the percentage of surface needing patch repair. In the Hot‑In‑Place Recycled Asphalt guidelines (Chapter 5, Section 5.19, subsection C) the acceptance limits for pavement distress are defined as rutting severity less than 1½ inches measured by rut depth, transverse and miscellaneous cracking severity with a depth less than 1.0 inch and an average crack width that does not exceed the Medium Rating of ≤ ½ inch, raveling or weathering severity may not exceed the Medium Rating judged by a surface that is rough, pitted and may contain loose particles, and minor corrugation, shoving and slippage are permissible provided their average depth is less than 1½ inches. Under Section 496—Asphalt Concrete Pavement, 60‑Month Warranty, subsection (c) Warranty the performance criteria in Table A specify that if the year‑5 automated survey determines that thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action such as microsurfacing must be performed. Distress identification, measurement and extent estimation are judged by the automated surveys required by Publication 336, while flushing and potholes are determined by manual surveys. In Section 529—Full Depth Precast/Prestressed Concrete Pavement Slabs, subsection 529.3 Construction, any crack wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third of the slab depth must be replaced at no additional cost to the Department; cracks wider than 0.007 inches but shallower than one‑third of the slab depth are to be repaired in accordance with the latest version of ACI 224R, and cracks that are 0.007 inches wide or less are sealed using high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate. Vertical‑differential distress is limited to a maximum finished differential of one‑quarter inch across any joint, with grinding permitted when the differential exceeds one‑quarter inch but does not exceed one‑half inch, and removal and re‑grading required when it exceeds one‑half inch. Joint‑gap distress is controlled by restricting transverse joint widths to between one‑quarter inch and one‑half inch and limiting longitudinal joints to a maximum of three‑quarters inch. [1][2]
