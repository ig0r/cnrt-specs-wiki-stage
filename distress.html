Distress

Part of: Pavement Condition Assessment is-subtopic-of
Distress is a topic under Pavement Condition Assessment.
distressdistresses
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-27

Distress is any observable pavement deterioration that compromises support, ride quality, durability or serviceability, encompassing surface‑oriented failures such as rutting, flushing, potholes, transverse, fatigue, thermal and alligator cracking, shoving, raveling, edge deterioration and concrete‑specific problems like joint spalling and faulting[1][2]. Its purpose is to trigger preservation or rehabilitation actions that repair the damage and return the pavement to its intended functional performance, with treatment decisions based on quantified survey thresholds—rut depth not exceeding 1½ in, crack depth under 1 in and average width no greater than ½ in—and on segment‑level percentages of low‑severity defects[1][2]. Consequently, distress functions as a measurable condition indicator: when roughly thirty percent of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses, microsurfacing or another approved method is required, while exceeding about twenty percent of the surface area mandates full removal and replacement to restore functional performance and structural integrity[1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Distress” also include the following subtopics: Corner Break, Cracking, Depression / Settlement, Pumping, Spalling, and Void (loss of support).

Corner Break and Spalling are concrete-specific distresses that manifest as structural failures at slab corners or surface edges, while Cracking represents the visible loss of integrity across both flexible and rigid pavements. [1][2] Pumping acts as a mechanism where water infiltration creates voids beneath the slab, directly causing Depression / Settlement by reducing subgrade support and inducing excessive deflection. [1] These conditions define Void (loss of support), which serves as the underlying cause for the observed surface deterioration and requires stabilization to restore structural functionality. [1]

Purpose and treatment objectives

Distress is the term used to denote any observable pavement deterioration that compromises support, ride quality, durability or serviceability, encompassing surface‑oriented failures such as rutting, flushing, potholes, cracking (including transverse, fatigue, thermal and alligator types), shoving, slippage cracks, edge deterioration, raveling, weathering and localized settlements, as well as concrete‑specific problems like joint spalling, joint faulting and other fault‑type failures[1][2]. The guides treat these distresses as the conditions that trigger preservation or rehabilitation actions; when surveys identify rut depths less than one‑and‑a‑half inches, crack depths under one inch with average widths not exceeding half an inch, or low‑severity cracking and raveling, the pavement is considered eligible for surface treatments such as microsurfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, hot‑in‑place recycling, interlayer systems or full‑depth reclamation to restore structural capacity and functional performance[1][2]. Warranty specifications further require remedial treatment when a five‑year survey shows that at least 30 % of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses, and mandate complete removal and replacement if the extent of distress exceeds the Table A thresholds for more than 20 % of the segment area, with all repairs required to meet surface tolerance criteria[2]. Across the sources, Distress is therefore intended to address any observable deterioration—whether load‑related, climate‑induced or material‑related—that threatens the functional performance and structural integrity of both asphalt and concrete pavements[1][2].

Across the guides, the central purpose of addressing pavement distress is to repair the damage that has occurred and return the pavement to its intended functional performance. [1][2] The diagnostic step commonly required is a distress survey that documents the condition of the surface and reveals the underlying causes such as load‑related stresses, climate influences or material deficiencies, thereby informing the selection of appropriate treatment. [1] When the survey indicates that a defined proportion—specifically, thirty percent of the segment area exhibiting two or more low‑severity distresses—is reached, the specifications call for remedial action by microsurfacing or another department‑approved method within the prescribed time frame. [2] All repairs must satisfy the surface tolerance requirements set out in Section 413.3(l), and any features removed or damaged during the remediation must be restored according to departmental standards. [2] Manufacturing imperfections, handling damage, or construction damage identified on slabs are also required to be corrected at no additional cost, with each case inspected individually. [2] If recurring distress is observed, work must be halted until the root cause is determined and mitigated, emphasizing that the immediate focus remains on fixing existing defects rather than on broader preventive measures. [2] Nevertheless, one guide expands the objective to include not only repair but also the prevention of the same damage mechanisms from re‑occurring by incorporating structural improvements where load‑related distress is dominant, thereby slowing further progression and extending the pavement’s service life. [1] This broader approach recommends a planned strategy of cost‑effective treatments that both retard future deterioration and maintain or improve the functional condition of the highway section. [1] In summary, while both sources concur that repairing damage and restoring functionality are essential objectives, they differ on whether preventing further deterioration and extending service life constitute explicit goals of distress treatment. [1][2]

Distress characterization and causes

The distress associated with inadequate subsurface drainage is found beneath concrete joints and within the subgrade where water becomes trapped, producing transverse cracks in the slab, surface rutting that mirrors subgrade deformation, and surface roughness or undulations that degrade ride quality. [1] These manifestations can be extensive enough to be visible across the pavement surface, but specifications confine them to the surface layer and limit their depth and width, with rutting not exceeding roughly one‑and‑half inches and transverse cracks limited to less than one inch deep and an average width of no more than half an inch, corresponding to a medium severity rating. [1] The extent of the distress is quantified on defined pavement segments of about half‑mile length, with the affected area expressed as a percentage of the segment; when roughly thirty percent of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses, microsurfacing is required, and if the combined remedial area exceeds about twenty percent of the segment surface, full removal and replacement are mandated. [2] Severity classifications range from low severity to medium‑severity cracking, the latter being treated as influencing a corridor six inches on either side of the crack for its entire length. [1][2] Progression can be rapid when trapped water freezes during freeze‑thaw cycles, causing joint tenting that accelerates crack propagation and quickly escalates surface undulations to noticeable loss of ride comfort and increased vehicle wear. [1] In addition, annual performance surveys track the condition, and by the fifth year failure to meet the defined thresholds triggers corrective action; if the pavement is judged potentially harmful or unsafe, immediate emergency repairs may be undertaken, which can lead to road closures and associated liquidated damages. [2] Although direct safety metrics are not quantified for surface‑confined distress, the degradation of ride quality, heightened vehicle wear, more frequent maintenance, and the possibility of emergency closures together represent significant operational consequences. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351)

Excess moisture that penetrates the pavement structure is identified as the dominant catalyst for most forms of distress, because water accumulation in the base or sub‑base saturates the subgrade and reduces its bearing capacity, which manifests as transverse cracking in jointed concrete pavements and surface rutting in asphalt layers under traffic loads. [1] Inadequate subsurface drainage—such as missing edge drains, insufficient cross slopes, or blocked outlets—allows water to linger, promoting frost‑heave during freeze‑thaw cycles and causing trapped ice to expand beneath joints, producing tenting, additional cracking, and surface roughness that degrades ride quality. [1] Heavy traffic loading compounds these moisture‑related mechanisms; repeated loads accelerate crack propagation, deepen rutting, intensify raveling, and can trigger reflective cracking when the underlying support is weak. [1] Climate‑driven oxidation and weathering erode the fatigue resistance of aged asphalt, leading to alligator cracking across the surface, while aggregate degradation—including concerns with Vanport limestone and alkali‑silica reaction—contributes to skid‑resistance loss, spalling, and premature mid‑slab cracking in concrete pavements. [1][2] The alkali‑silica reaction (ASR) between reactive aggregates and cement alkalis generates an expansive silica gel that first creates microcracks and later visible joint expansion and cracking; detection relies on petrographic analysis of cores per ASTM C856 and documentation of service history, with exemptions possible only when ten or more non‑reactive units older than ten years are demonstrated. [2] Insufficient structural capacity in concrete mixes—failure to achieve the prescribed minimum compressive and flexural strengths—as well as inadequate subbase support that produces excessive vertical differentials greater than a ½ inch, both generate distress by allowing excessive deflection and promoting cracking or joint faulting. [2] Damage introduced during handling or installation can also precipitate cracking and joint faulting, prompting removal of any slab whose finished surface exceeds the ½‑inch differential criterion and regrading of the underlying bedding. [2] Freeze‑thaw cycles further strip bond between asphalt and aggregate, generate potholes, and promote crack growth, especially when water intrusion has already weakened the structural section. [1] Active pumping observed in the pavement indicates a poorly supported subgrade that must be treated before any overlay or interlayer is placed, because it signals loss of support and potential for reflective cracking. [1] Temperature‑induced movements of expansion joints are addressed by designing mix proportions and panel dimensions based on average daily ambient temperature and site exposure conditions, ensuring that joint locations can accommodate expected thermal expansion without inducing additional distress. [2] Overall, the underlying mechanisms accelerating pavement distress across both flexible and rigid systems therefore include water infiltration and drainage failure, heavy traffic loading, climate‑related oxidation and freeze‑thaw, aggregate reactions such as ASR, inadequate structural capacity, weak subbase support, handling damage, and thermal movements of joints. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 5 (pp. 107–126), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 664–665)

Applicability and treatment selection

Distress is applied when assessing Portland cement concrete pavements that show visible deterioration, because the underlying mechanisms are classified as load‑related or climate/materials‑related [1]. In such evaluations the guides require consideration of site conditions including subgrade and subbase quality, drainage performance, surface friction, ride quality, and the presence of aggregates prone to polishing, all of which affect the type and severity of distress observed [1]. For flexible pavements that are covered by a performance warranty, distress becomes the primary metric when automated or manual surveys detect measurable cracking, flushing, rutting, or potholes that meet the prescribed thresholds [2]. The warranty criteria specify that if a five‑year survey shows roughly thirty percent of the segment area containing two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial treatment such as microsurfacing is triggered, whereas combined deviations exceeding about twenty percent of the segment surface require full removal and replacement [2]. These assessments assume that traffic loads remain within the projected equivalent single axle load (ESAL) values for the design life; once cumulative ESALs exceed one hundred percent of the design‑life estimate, responsibility for rutting or base‑condition distress shifts away from the contractor [2]. Distresses that arise from conditions beyond the control of the contractor—such as poor base conditions, traffic volumes exceeding the design expectation, routine maintenance activities, destructive testing, natural disasters like floods or earthquakes, and accident‑related spills—are excluded from the warranty‑based remediation triggers [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351)

Distress‑based assessment or treatment is considered suitable only when the observed pavement problems remain within low‑severity cracking or raveling, infrequent corrugations, settlements, heaves, slippage cracks, and at most medium‑severity rutting. When any of these conditions progress to high‑severity cracking, frequent corrugations, severe rutting, bleeding, active pumping that signals a weak subgrade, or water intrusion from springs or standing water, reliance on distress alone is no longer sufficient and another maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair strategy must be selected. The guides also agree that the suitability of distress‑based treatment is bounded by quantitative performance limits; for example, if after five years a segment exhibits low‑severity distresses over thirty percent of its area with two or more such distresses, a remedial action such as microsurfacing (or another department‑approved method) must be applied. If the combined deviations across a segment exceed twenty percent of its surface area, the warranty provisions require complete removal and replacement of that segment rather than continued distress‑based repairs. Patch‑only repair becomes unsuitable when the total patching required exceeds roughly ten percent of the pavement surface; at that point a cost‑effectiveness analysis should be performed and alternatives such as an asphalt or concrete overlay, structural rehabilitation, or other preservation treatments considered. Moreover, when individual patches would be spaced less than one hundred feet apart, the guides require that they be merged into a single continuous repair, and routine crack‑sealing actions are excluded from warranty coverage, necessitating separate preservation measures. When the underlying cause of distress is inadequate subsurface drainage, simple patching or resurfacing will not correct the problem; instead drainage‑oriented work such as installing or repairing edge drains, lateral underdrains, and subgrade drains must be undertaken before any surface treatment. Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) treatment is limited to surface‑only damage that does not exceed a rut depth of about one‑and‑a‑half inches, transverse crack depth of one inch, and average crack width of half an inch; the presence of rubberized or fiber‑reinforced layers, extensive mastic patches larger than five square feet, or overbanded rubberized joint sealants disqualifies HIR and forces selection of another rehabilitation method. Bonded Portland cement concrete overlays are unsuitable where the existing concrete exhibits extensive cracking or faulting because those flaws will propagate through the new slab unless repaired first; in such cases an unbonded overlay or repair of the underlying pavement should be chosen. Routine crack‑sealing treatments become inappropriate when distress originates from deeper problems such as premature mid‑slab cracking caused by inadequate subbase material, aggregate deficiencies (e.g., Vanport limestone, alkali‑silica reaction), or skid‑resistance issues not captured in surveys; these conditions require more extensive preservation, rehabilitation, or full reconstruction. Full‑depth reclamation is appropriate only where support‑layer deficiencies exist; surface‑only distresses such as shallow rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, or drainage problems are better addressed with other maintenance or preservation techniques. When a distress‑based recommendation is generated by the STAMPP program for a segment, it should not be used as the basis of a formal rehabilitation design; instead a comprehensive strategy—typically an asphalt overlay surfacing—must be selected. Finally, once the functional life of the pavement has been exceeded, only complete reconstruction satisfies acceptance criteria. During installation of precast concrete slabs, recurring damage or distress signals that the slab‑repair method is unsuitable; installation must be halted, the cause investigated, and an alternative maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair approach adopted. Condition assessment and timing

Determining need for distress treatment starts with a recent pavement‑condition survey conducted according to the procedures in Publication 336, Publication 343 or the STAMPP automated videologging protocol, which provides a systematic inventory of all observable defects. The automated survey records type, location and magnitude of distresses such as cracking, rutting, raveling, joint spalling, faulting, edge deterioration and drainage problems, and when the system flags potential issues manual spot checks are performed to verify flushing and potholes. Surface‑type distress is quantified by measuring rut depth with a 12‑foot straight edge across the full lane width and confirming that it does not exceed the accepted threshold of 1½ in, while transverse or miscellaneous crack depth is measured and must be less than 1 in and average crack width no greater than ½ in. Additional surface observations include raveling severity, bleeding, low‑friction conditions and asphalt thickness relative to planned milling depths, with a targeted friction test performed whenever loss of skid resistance is suspected. Traffic classification counts are taken during the survey to verify that calculated ESAL values remain consistent with contract assumptions. A detailed visual examination of each structure follows, noting any cracking, joint expansion, spalling or other symptoms and documenting concrete mix characteristics such as cement content, alkali levels and supplementary cementitious materials. When surface indications suggest underlying problems, core samples are extracted from deteriorated joints as well as from randomly selected locations to evaluate subbase or subgrade soil properties and to assess hidden deterioration. The cores are tested in the laboratory for density, void content, asphalt content and gradation according to prescribed PTM methods, and petrographic analysis per ASTM C856 is performed to detect alkali‑silica reaction products or microcracking. Recovered aggregates from the cores are classified under ASTM C295 to verify mineralogical compatibility with the intended material. In‑situ non‑destructive testing typically begins with a Falling Weight Deflectometer assessment to obtain pavement deflection profiles, which together with Dynamic Cone Penetrometer or Light‑Weight Deflectometer measurements quantify subgrade stiffness and support capacity. Faulting measurements, ground‑penetrating radar, MIRA scans, MIT‑Scan 2/T2 assessments or tensile bond tests may be employed to pinpoint the extent of distress and identify specific joints that require replacement or slab stabilization. For concrete pavements a DARWin condition survey calculates an Effective Existing Thickness, and if extensive cracking or faulting beyond acceptable limits is observed those defects must be repaired before any bonded PCC overlay is placed because untreated distress will propagate through the new layer. The combined results of the visual inventory, measured severity thresholds, core sampling, laboratory analyses and the suite of non‑destructive tests provide a comprehensive picture that allows engineers to locate precisely where joint replacement, slab stabilization or surface rehabilitation is warranted. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–133), Ch. 10 (p. 183), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221), Ch. 14 (p. 227); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 664–665)

Distress treatment is considered once a recent condition or performance survey identifies visible surface problems while the pavement remains in an early‑to‑moderate stage of deterioration. [1] At this stage the pavement typically exhibits low‑severity cracking or raveling, occasional corrugations, settlements, heaves or infrequent slippage cracks, and medium‑severity rutting that does not exceed a depth of one and a half inches. [1] Specific surface‑distress thresholds for applying preservation treatments such as hot‑in‑place recycling or an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course require rutting less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking shall be shallower than 1.0 inch with average crack widths no greater than a half‑inch (medium rating), raveling or weathering not exceeding the medium rating, and any corrugation, shoving or slippage remaining under 1½ inches; additionally the existing asphalt layer must be at least 1½ inches thicker than the planned milling depth and prohibited materials such as rubber or fiber must be absent. [1] When these mild to moderate surface conditions are present a distress survey is performed to document severity before selecting an appropriate rehabilitation strategy. [1] If, however, the pavement shows severe distress—extensive cracking, deep rutting, or potholes that threaten structural performance—the earlier surface treatments become insufficient and full‑depth reclamation is indicated. [1] In parallel, a performance survey that finds at least thirty percent of a segment’s area containing two or more low‑severity distresses triggers the application of microsurfacing or another department‑approved preservation method. [2] Should the combined deviations exceed the quantitative thresholds set out in Table A and affect more than twenty percent of the segment surface, the required remedial action escalates to complete removal and replacement of that segment. [2]

Materials and repair details

Distress on pavement can be addressed through a selection of surface‑level or full‑depth treatments that are defined by the guides. [1][2] The repair options include an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course, a hot‑in‑place recycled overlay, microsurfacing, or other Department‑approved treatments when distress thresholds are exceeded, and full‑depth removal and replacement must use plant‑mixed asphalt concrete unless another material is expressly authorized. [1][2] An ultra‑thin bonded wearing course begins with the placement of a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane using a self‑priming paver, which is immediately overlaid with hot‑mix asphalt concrete; the hot mix may be placed in one of three gradations—Type A, B or C—with nominal maximum aggregate sizes of 6.3 mm, 9.5 mm and 12.5 mm respectively, and the finished thickness is about one‑half inch for Type A and three‑quarters inch for Types B and C. [1] When distress is confined to the surface layer a hot‑in‑place recycled overlay is applied at a typical depth of one and a half inches, with an allowable range from one to two inches; for composite pavements the existing asphalt beneath the overlay must be at least one and a half inches thick, while full‑depth pavements require an underlying thickness of two to three inches and any paving fabric must remain at least one and a half inches below the cut depth. [1] The guides restrict the use of the hot‑in‑place recycled method to sections where rutting is less than one and a half inches, transverse or miscellaneous cracks are shallower than one inch and no wider than a half‑inch, and only medium‑severity raveling is present. [1] Under the Penn DOT STAMPP program any identified surface distress that triggers a treatment recommendation is addressed by placing an asphalt overlay on the affected segment, although the program does not prescribe a specific thickness or configuration beyond the requirement for surfacing. [1] Microsurfacing becomes mandatory when a five‑year performance survey shows that thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, and other Department‑approved treatments may be substituted where appropriate. [2] Repair patches are required to be square or rectangular, must conform to the specified surface tolerance, and when individual repair zones lie less than one hundred feet apart they are combined into a single continuous patch; medium‑severity cracks are treated as deviations that extend six inches on each side for the full length of the crack. [2] All remedial work, including temporary repairs performed under adverse weather conditions, must satisfy the material and performance criteria set forth in the warranty specification. [2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 107–133), Ch. 14 (p. 227); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351)

The performance of any distress‑mitigating pavement treatment begins with the inherent qualities of the existing base and subbase, which must be highly permeable so that water drains by gravity, avoiding capillary rise and preventing saturation that would otherwise reduce support for overlying concrete slabs and manifest as transverse cracking or surface rutting reflecting subgrade deformation. [1] For Portland‑cement concrete pavements the slab’s compressive strength, air content, and the percentage of existing patching are critical parameters, while aggregates such as Vanport limestone can lower skid resistance and polishing‑susceptible aggregates increase susceptibility to surface grinding; additionally, the potential for alkali‑silica reaction, though not readily detected by standard data systems, must be considered when selecting a rehabilitation strategy. [1] Asphalt‑based treatments rely on polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membranes that seal the surface, limiting oxidation, weathering, raveling and moderate rutting, and are designed for an expected service life of eight to ten years; the required thickness of these ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses is governed by aggregate gradation, with approximately one‑half inch needed for Type A gradations and three‑quarters inch for Types B and C. [1] The interlayer material used beneath such membranes must be thermally stable, physically durable, and chemically compatible with the asphalt binder to develop a strong internal adhesive or mechanical bond, and the overlying Superpave wearing course (6.3 mm) is limited to sound pavements that contain less than two percent patching and provide adequate surface‑and‑subsurface drainage. [1] In addition, the installed asphalt layer must achieve a density greater than ninety‑two percent of its theoretical maximum and its measured percent asphalt and gradation must fall within the tolerances established in the contractor’s mix design; only plant‑mixed asphalt concrete is permitted unless an alternative material receives written approval from the district executive. [2] When reclaimed or chemically stabilized layers are incorporated, their aggregate gradation, moisture content, and stabilizer dosage must be matched to the existing material strength, because excessive additive can produce brittleness while insufficient strength limits durability. [1] Prior to rehabilitation, a Falling Weight Deflectometer test should verify subgrade stiffness, and the severity of existing distresses must meet defined thresholds—rutting less than about one‑and‑a‑half inches, transverse or miscellaneous cracking deeper than one inch nor wider than half an inch, and the remaining asphalt thickness after milling exceeding the milled depth by at least one‑and‑a‑half inches. [1] For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled overlays, rubberized binders or fiber reinforcement may not be present in the top two inches, and the same rutting and cracking limits apply; bonded Portland‑cement concrete overlays are appropriate only when underlying slabs exhibit minimal cracking or faulting, while bonded concrete overlays placed over asphalt require a minimum of three inches of milled asphalt remaining after milling. [1] The warranty specifications further require that if performance surveys detect fatigue cracking or other indications of an unacceptable base, core testing must confirm that the underlying layer meets minimum thickness, adequate density, low void content, and proper asphalt content and gradation before the contractor can be held liable for repairs. [2] Moreover, when cumulative traffic loading exceeds the design estimate of twenty‑year equivalent single axle loads, the contractor is released from responsibility for rutting corrections, and remedial action is mandated whenever at least thirty percent of a segment exhibits two or more low‑severity distresses, prompting the use of compatible repair methods and materials. [2] Finally, concrete joints must be installed so that finished vertical differentials do not exceed one‑quarter inch across any joint, preserving load transfer and preventing recurrence of distress.

Treatment procedures

Preparation for distress treatment begins with a thorough survey and subgrade testing, identification and correction of any drainage problems, and the establishment of traffic control while crews mark proposed longitudinal joints or sections slated for asphalt replacement [1][2]. Where the existing surface is uneven, a separate scratch or leveling course at least one inch thick is placed to provide a uniform working platform, and alternative methods such as heater‑planing or milling may be employed to improve transverse or longitudinal profile [1]. When milling over a concrete base, a minimum of 1½ inches of the existing asphalt should be retained to preserve structural integrity [1]. Raised pavement markers, durable thermoplastic or epoxy markings, and any mastic patches larger than five square feet are removed before hot‑in‑place recycling, and deteriorated concrete or composite pavement is cut full‑depth with a diamond saw along defined joint lines and lifted out without disturbing the subbase [1][2]. Shallow milling is then performed to open deeper cracks while maintaining vertical clearance for overhead structures [1]. For asphalt repairs, plant‑mixed asphalt concrete is placed unless other materials have been approved in writing, following removal of the deteriorated material [2]. Full‑depth reclamation starts by pulverizing the existing asphalt layer through to the underlying base or subgrade, forming the foundation for stabilization [1]. Mechanical stabilization adds aggregate or reclaimed asphalt pavement during pulverization, while chemical stabilization mixes dry or slurry additives such as cement, fly ash, or lime kiln dust either ahead of the reclaimer or within its mixing chamber [1]. The stabilized reclaimed mixture is placed back on the roadway grade, shaped with motor graders and compacted to achieve the required stiffness, and any added binders are allowed to cure according to laboratory mix designs [1]. Surface distress that can be corrected by an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course is treated in a single pass using a self‑priming paver that first lays a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane directly ahead of the screed and immediately overlays it with hot‑mix asphalt concrete [1]. The finished thickness for this treatment is specified as one‑half inch for Type A gradations or three‑quarters inch for Types B and C, roughly 1–1.5 times the largest aggregate size [1]. After placement there are no traffic restrictions for the paver‑placed surface treatment, although other repairs require traffic control to be maintained throughout the operation [1][2]. For precast slab repairs, each slab is positioned to the line and grade shown on the working drawings using approved leveling methods—setting bolts, polyurethane injection, or strongback beams—and dowels, tie bars, and grout ports are installed as indicated [2]. Finishing requires that slabs be set within the tolerances specified in Section 529.3(f)6 and 7, with vertical joint differentials kept under one‑quarter inch and joint widths meeting prescribed limits before traffic is reopened [2]. The compacted No. 2A coarse aggregate bedding placed beneath new asphalt must be finely graded to within one‑eighth inch of the theoretical plane [2]. Curing of grout must reach a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is allowed, and temporary repairs may be used under adverse weather until permanent work can be completed [2]. All features removed or damaged during remediation are restored according to Department specifications [2]. The entire remedial action must be finished within sixty calendar days after the Department’s written notification [2]. The ultra‑thin bonded wearing course treatment is expected to provide eight to ten years of service life [1].

Implementation begins with condition surveys using automated video‑loggers or other automated survey tools and manual methods prescribed in Publication 336, often employing a 12‑foot straight edge for rut depth verification [1][2]. Non‑destructive testing equipment such as Falling Weight Deflectometers, dynamic cone penetrometers, light weight deflectometers, ground‑penetrating radar, friction meters and roughness gauges may be deployed to distinguish load‑related from climate or material‑related distress [1]. For concrete pavements the construction sequence typically proceeds through slab stabilization or slabjacking, partial‑depth repairs, load‑transfer retrofits, cross‑stitching, full‑depth patching, base‑drain installation, diamond grinding or grooving, shoulder rehabilitation and joint cleaning and sealing, with joint sealing deferred until after grinding; some activities can run concurrently [1]. When an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course is specified, a self‑priming paver lays the polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane directly ahead of the screed and a single paving machine applies the hot‑mix overlay in one uninterrupted pass, allowing placement under open traffic [1]. Distress‑mitigating overlays require surface preparation that includes a minimum one‑inch scratch or leveling course before placing Superpave binder and wearing layers; milling machines, heater planers, coring tools and non‑tracking tack‑coat applicators are used to ensure proper bonding, and at least 1½ inches of existing asphalt should remain when milling over concrete [1]. Full‑depth reclamation follows a strict order: pulverization of the existing asphalt and base with a road reclaimer, optional stabilization, placement of the reclaimed mix, grading with motor graders and final compaction; traffic may not return until the compacted base is achieved [1]. Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) treatment similarly mandates pre‑HIR milling to address deep cracks, after which the HIR mixing and laying system installs the new surface that can be reopened once it cools to the required temperature [1]. Environmental constraints include adherence to weather limits set out in Publication 408 for overlay work, avoidance of wet subgrade conditions before reclamation, and prohibition of permanent repairs during inclement or adverse weather, requiring temporary fixes until conditions improve [1][2]. Traffic restrictions vary with the treatment: ultra‑thin bonded courses may be placed under open traffic; non‑tracking tack coats are recommended where stopping, turning, or slow moving traffic occurs; ADT thresholds may limit overlay activities; high‑traffic corridors may require accelerated concrete mixes to meet maintenance windows; and all full‑depth reclamation or HIR operations remain closed to traffic until compaction or cooling is complete [1]. All remediation work must be accompanied by an approved traffic control plan that maintains normal traffic flow, with penalties applied if the roadway is not opened to unrestricted traffic, and liquidated damages may accrue if repairs are not started within a sixty‑day window after schedule submission [2]. Notification requirements dictate at least fourteen days’ notice before manual surveys, written notification of required repairs, a five‑day period for schedule submission, and a sixty‑day window to commence remediation; failure to meet these timelines can trigger liquidated damages [2].

Quality and workmanship

The inspection process begins with a comprehensive visual examination that records any cracking, joint expansion where applicable, and other visible symptoms of distress while also noting the specific material mix such as concrete class or asphalt proportions and cement characteristics including alkali content and supplementary cementitious materials. Automated surface surveys conducted in accordance with Publication 336 are then used to locate and quantify distresses such as cracking, flushing, potholes, rutting and similar defects. When rutting is identified its severity is confirmed by measuring depth with a twelve‑foot straight edge across the full lane width, and the acceptable limit is set at less than one‑and‑a‑half inches. Transverse and miscellaneous cracks are evaluated for both depth and average opening, with permissible depth not exceeding one inch and average width remaining at or below a half‑inch medium rating. The presence of rubber in the pavement material is flagged as rendering the section unsuitable for High‑Intensity Repair processes. If visual or survey results suggest an unacceptable base condition, six‑inch diameter cores are extracted from the affected zone as well as from locations near visibly deteriorated joints and random spots to assess subbase or subgrade quality beneath the surface. Extracted concrete cores are examined for compressive strength and for the percentage of material that would require patching, with any required patching exceeding ten percent—excluding existing good patches—triggering a supplemental cost‑effectiveness analysis. The same cores are tested to verify that density exceeds ninety‑two percent of the theoretical maximum, that void content, asphalt content and gradation meet the limits prescribed in PTM 715, AASHTO T‑209, PTM 702 and PTM 739. Petrographic analysis performed according to ASTM C856 is used to detect alkali‑silica gel formation and associated microcracking within the core material. Throughout the warranty period the contractor may conduct nondestructive testing of the pavement course, provided a minimum seven‑day notice is given to the district executive, and all observations, material data and test results are compiled to determine whether the completed work satisfies surface‑tolerance criteria and material specifications. Acceptance of distress begins with a systematic measurement and tabulation of all visible defects, and the total area that would require patching is limited to ten percent of the pavement surface; exceeding this proportion triggers a cost‑effectiveness analysis and precludes preservation treatment in favor of more extensive rehabilitation. In addition, performance‑criteria established for periodic surveys require that by the fifth‑year inspection no more than thirty percent of a segment may be occupied by low‑severity distresses occurring two or more times, otherwise remedial microsurfacing or an approved treatment is mandated. The combined area of all deviations that surpass the severity thresholds defined in the reference tables must remain below twenty percent of the segment’s surface; if this limit is breached the entire segment is slated for removal and replacement. Acceptable pavement condition also limits observable damage to low‑severity cracking or raveling, infrequent corrugations, settlements, heaves, slippage cracks, and rutting that does not exceed medium severity. For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled overlays the specific distress tolerances are a rut depth of less than one and a half inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracks shall be shallower than one inch with an average width no greater than one‑half inch, raveling or weathering may reach only a medium rating characterized by a rough, pitted surface with possible loose particles, and minor corrugation, shoving or slippage are permissible provided their average depth is also under one and a half inches; any load‑related distress or base problems disqualify the pavement. Bonded Portland cement concrete overlays are deemed satisfactory only when the existing concrete exhibits limited cracking and faulting, because significant pre‑existing distress would be transmitted through the new slab if not repaired. Preservation programs reinforce these limits by requiring crack sealing of asphalt surfaces at intervals of three to five years and joint sealing of concrete pavements every five years, while still observing the ten‑percent patching ceiling. All repairs that are performed must conform to the surface tolerance specified in the contract documents, ensuring that restored areas do not exceed the allowable roughness or deviation limits. Performance and service life

Distress such as rutting, cracking, potholes, raveling, edge deterioration, shoving, slippage, thermal cracking and drainage‑related damage signals a decline in pavement condition and loss of functional performance [1][2]. When water is retained in the base or subbase, subgrade support deteriorates, increasing deflections and surface roughness, which reduces ride quality and accelerates further deterioration unless effective drainage measures are provided [1]. Load‑related distresses reflect inadequate structural capacity and may require structural improvement in addition to any climate or material corrections [1][2]. The severity of each distress governs the treatment choice; for surface rutting less than about 1½ inches and crack depth under 1 inch, a hot‑in‑place recycled overlay can restore appearance, improve performance and extend service life as an interim wearing course [1]. When rutting or cracking exceeds those thresholds, thicker overlays of at least 1½ inches—typically standard or thin Superpave wearing courses of 6.3 mm or 9.5 mm—are prescribed, with a patching limit of less than 2 % to achieve the intended life‑extension benefits [1]. Cracking that is not associated with structural deficiency may be mitigated by placing interlayers such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh or composite grid beneath the wearing course, whereas rutting, bleeding or raveling are addressed through appropriate overlay thicknesses rather than interlayers [1]. When support‑layer deterioration is evident—signaled by severe rutting, extensive cracking, raveling, potholes or drainage problems—a full‑depth reclamation using mechanical, chemical or pulverization stabilization can restore the subgrade and provide a durable foundation for subsequent surface treatments [1]. Bonded concrete overlays, whether placed over asphalt (BCOA) or existing concrete, add structural capacity and mitigate surface distresses when the underlying pavement is in good‑to‑fair condition; extensive cracking must be repaired before bonding to prevent propagation through the new slab [1]. Routine preservation actions such as crack sealing, joint sealing, micro‑surfacing, milling or grinding retard distress progression, preserve functional condition and delay the need for more invasive rehabilitation [1]. The programmatic approach that compiles accumulated cracking and rutting triggers a surface‑treatment flag; an asphalt overlay applied at that point restores riding quality, improves resistance to further deterioration and consequently prolongs the segment’s expected service life relative to leaving the distress untreated [1]. Warranty specifications require annual automated surveys (manual for flushing and potholes) to identify and measure distresses, and if a year‑5 survey shows that 30 % of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses, microsurfacing or another approved treatment must be applied within the prescribed time frame to restore durability [2]. If combined deviations affect more than 20 % of the segment area, removal and replacement with plant‑mixed asphalt concrete is mandated, ensuring that the design service life based on projected ESALs is maintained despite the presence of distress [2]. By addressing identified distresses through appropriate drainage, structural overlays, interlayers, full‑depth reclamation, bonded concrete overlays or timely microsurfacing and replacement, pavement condition, functionality, durability and expected service life are restored or extended [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–133), Ch. 10 (pp. 183–186), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221), Ch. 14 (p. 227); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 346–351)

Distress in pavements encompasses repeatable failures observed in both concrete and asphalt layers, most commonly manifested as various cracking types (transverse, miscellaneous, thermal/shrinkage, premature mid‑slab, reflective), surface deformations such as rutting, shoving, corrugation, shallow depressions, raveling or weathering of the wearing course, edge deterioration, joint spalling and faulting, potholes, flushing, and water‑induced stripping [1][2]. The occurrence and severity of these distresses are governed by a combination of load‑related factors—such as heavy traffic volumes, daily equivalent single axle loads exceeding roughly 2 500 ESALs, high axle weights, and fatigue loading—and climate or material‑related influences including moisture intrusion, inadequate drainage, freeze‑thaw cycles, temperature swings that cause thermal cracking, and the presence of reactive aggregates that can trigger alkali‑silica reaction [1][2]. Saturated or poorly drained subgrades reduce support, trap water beneath joints where freezing creates joint tenting, and promote stripping and loss of structural capacity, thereby accelerating crack propagation and pothole formation [1][2]. Structural deficiencies such as thin asphalt bases below the three‑inch minimum required before a Portland cement concrete overlay, insufficient sub‑base material, unstable base layers, or weak grout that has not reached the specified compressive strength of 2 500 psi also amplify reflective cracking, mid‑slab failures, and edge damage [1][2]. Material issues including low concrete compressive strength, high air content, aggregate polishing susceptibility, reactive aggregates like Vanport limestone, or insufficient supplementary cementitious materials further predispose pavements to cracking and loss of skid resistance [1][2]. When the existing pavement exhibits more than about 10 % surface patching, a cost‑effectiveness analysis is required before rehabilitation, because extensive patching often signals underlying distress that will be reflected through any bonded overlay unless corrected [1]. Acceptance criteria for hot‑in‑place recycled overlays limit rutting severity to less than 1½ inches, crack depth to under 1 inch with average widths no greater than 0.5 inch, raveling to a medium rating, and corrugation, shoving or slippage to depths below 1½ inches; exceeding these limits indicates that structural improvement rather than surface treatment is needed [1]. Warranty‑triggered remedial actions are also mandated when a year‑five survey shows that at least thirty percent of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses—defined as cracks whose influence extends six inches on either side for their full length—prompting microsurfacing or, where warranted, full replacement [2]. Flushing and potholes are similarly monitored through manual surveys, and when performance thresholds are exceeded they require treatment under the contract provisions [2]. Interlayers such as geotextile fabrics or fiberglass meshes can mitigate reflective cracking by providing moisture barriers and stress absorption, but they do not address rutting, bleeding, raveling, or shoving, which must be resolved through structural rehabilitation like full‑depth reclamation that stabilizes the base with cementitious binders [1][2]. Full‑depth reclamation effectively eliminates reflective cracking by creating a stronger, more flexible foundation and also addresses drainage deficiencies that contribute to moisture‑related distress [1][2]. Preventive maintenance practices such as cyclic crack sealing for asphalt every three to five years and joint sealing for concrete about every five years help limit the propagation of existing cracks and reduce water infiltration, thereby extending pavement performance [1]. Ultimately, recurring pavement distresses arise from moisture intrusion, inadequate drainage, subgrade weakness, excessive traffic loading, climatic temperature swings, structural deficiencies, and insufficient remediation of existing damage; addressing these root causes through appropriate structural treatment and preventive maintenance offers the best chance of extending service life [1][2].

After the repair is finished, the rehabilitated segment should be evaluated with in‑situ post‑construction testing to verify that the new pavement has attained the required structural stiffness and subgrade support. [2] When the automated results indicate potential problems, a supplemental manual inspection is carried out to verify the presence of flushing, potholes, rutting, cracking, or joint expansion. When surface rutting mirrors subgrade deformation and its depth exceeds roughly one‑and‑a‑half inches, the pavement begins to feel rough or unsupported, signalling a loss of structural capacity; this condition is compounded when cracks become deeper than an inch or wider than half an inch, when transverse and miscellaneous cracking reach medium‑rating widths, when raveling or weathering move beyond a medium rating, and when corrugation or shoving exceed one‑and‑a‑half inches, all of which indicate that short‑term interim solutions are no longer adequate [1]. Persistent ponded water on the deck also points to inadequate drainage and potential moisture infiltration that can further weaken the structure [1]. If the amount of visible distress requiring patching surpasses about ten percent of the pavement surface—excluding patches already in good condition—the cost‑effectiveness of simple preservation diminishes, prompting consideration of more extensive rehabilitation such as structural overlays or reconstruction [1]. Likewise, when routine crack sealing for asphalt surfaces has not been performed within three to five years, or joint sealing for concrete exceeds five years, the untreated distress signals that scheduled maintenance windows have been missed and additional action is needed [1]. Performance surveys provide quantitative triggers: if by the fifth‑year survey at least thirty percent of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, the segment must be treated with microsurfacing or another department‑approved method, whereas if deviations beyond prescribed thresholds cover more than twenty percent of the segment surface, complete removal and replacement is required [2]. When individual repair patches are located less than one hundred feet apart they must be combined into a single continuous repair to avoid fragmented treatment [2]. For precast concrete slabs, any finished vertical differential across a joint greater than a quarter‑inch, or a differential exceeding half an inch relative to the adjacent pavement, signals that the slab should be removed, the bedding regraded, and the slab reset before grouting; joint gaps falling outside the prescribed ranges also demand corrective action [2]. Additionally, dowel or bedding grout that has not attained the required compressive strength of 2 500 psi prior to traffic loading must halt installation until the material reaches the specified strength, ensuring structural integrity [2]. The appearance of recurring cracks or other damage after placement, together with high‑severity observations such as fatigue cracking, shoulder drop‑off, edge deterioration, and automated condition‑survey flags (e.g., STAMPP treatment recommendations) confirm that the segment has exceeded acceptable limits and requires additional maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation to restore functional performance [1][2].
