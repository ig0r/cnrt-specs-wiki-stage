Asphalt Content

Part of: Mix Design is-subtopic-of
Asphalt Content is a topic under Mix Design.
asphalt-contentbinder-contentminimum-binder-content
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Asphalt Content is the measured proportion of asphalt binder—or emulsified asphalt—incorporated into a hot‑mix pavement blend, most often a Stone Matrix Asphalt wearing‑course, and is expressed as a percent of the total mix weight or mass [1]. It is obtained from laboratory testing of mixture‑acceptance samples using prescribed methods such as PTM No. 757 or PTM No. 702 (Modified Method D), and the result is checked against the binder‑proportion tolerances in the Job Mix Formula to verify that the mix will achieve the target air‑void level, adequate mortar coating, and the intended rut‑resistant durability [2]. If the measured content lies outside the single‑sample or multiple‑sample limits—whether by deficiency, excess, observable runoff, or failure to meet coating requirements—the material is classified as defective work and must be excluded from placement, making Asphalt Content a primary quality‑control parameter for pavement performance [1].

Material purpose and applicability

Asphalt content refers to the amount of asphalt binder incorporated into a Stone Matrix Asphalt (SMA) wearing‑course mix. By selecting an asphalt content that provides the desired air‑void level, the mixture obtains enough rich mortar binder to give the pavement durability while maintaining the stone‑to‑stone contact that supplies strength and rut resistance. Asphalt Content is the measured proportion or percentage of asphalt binder present in a pavement mixture, expressed either as a percent of total weight or by mass, and it is determined through laboratory testing of mixture‑acceptance samples using the prescribed test methods (e.g., PTM No. 1, PTM No. 757, PTM No. 702 Modified Method D) during production or from loose samples taken at the plant or on pavement cores. In Section 413.3(h) the measured amount of asphalt in a hot‑mix paving material serves as a quality‑control parameter; if the mix contains either a deficiency or an excess of asphalt content it is considered unsuitable for addition to the pavement mat, thereby helping ensure that the finished pavement meets design specifications and performs properly. Section 413.2 1.a.3 requires Asphalt Content to be measured to verify that each hot‑mix batch conforms to the binder‑proportion tolerances set out in Table A, which ensures the mix satisfies the required quality‑control specifications. In Section 412.3 the proportion of asphalt binder determined by PTM No. 757 or PTM No. 702 (Modified Method D) must fall within the tolerances listed in Table B; when it does not, the material is classified as defective work and production must be stopped until corrective action is approved. Section 419.3 (construction) requires Asphalt Content of a Stone Matrix Asphalt mixture to be sampled at the plant and on pavement cores during the trial demonstration and tested to verify that the mix meets the specification limits, providing a key quality‑control check on material composition before full‑production placement. Section 419.3 (field technician) evaluates Asphalt Content measured from loose mixture acceptance samples taken from each sublot against the single‑sample (n=1) or multiple‑sample (n≥3) tolerances in Table D; when the content satisfies those limits the material is accepted and full payment is made, whereas failure to meet the tolerances classifies the work as defective. Section 360.2(e) specifies an initial target Asphalt Content of 2.5 % by weight that may be adjusted within the range given in Table A to achieve a uniform coating of the aggregate while preventing any observable runoff of excess binder; maintaining this proportion ensures that aggregate particles are fully bonded together, delivering the intended structural performance without loss of material. Section 489.2 states that Asphalt Content expressed by mass in the plant‑mixed asphalt concrete wearing course that includes fine aggregate, coarse aggregate and filler functions as a primary acceptance value in the quality‑control program; the mixture must meet the Asphalt Content tolerance specified in the Job Mix Formula for the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course to be certified and to ensure proper binding and performance of the pavement. Section 460.3 uses Asphalt Content, reported as Asphalt Residue Content (ARC) on the Bill of Lading, together with the desired residual rate to calculate the required application rate that will leave a uniform asphalt residue on the existing surface, thereby providing the necessary bond between successive pavement courses. Section 413 requires Asphalt Content to be a required acceptance value measured on every production batch; it is compared with the Joint Mix Formula tolerances in Table A and certification demands that at least 90 % of daily printed ticket results be within 0.2 percentage points of the JMF before the mix can be certified and shipped. Section 412 mandates that Asphalt Content be a quality‑control parameter; the producer’s QC plan must include regular sampling and testing to confirm that each batch falls within the tolerances set out in Table B, thereby ensuring the mix meets its design specifications, and when laboratory results are unavailable printed ticket results may be used for QC provided the producer is approved under Bulletin 27, with daily results staying within 0.2 percentage points of the JMF. Section 360 requires Asphalt Content to be kept within production tolerances so that the binder adequately coats the aggregate particles; if less than 95 % of the aggregates are coated or any observable runoff of excess asphalt occurs, the asphalt‑treated permeable base course is considered defective. Section 312 defines Asphalt Content as the portion of emulsified asphalt—Class RS‑1 or CRS‑1 as required by Section 702—that is added to the mixture; this asphalt material is combined with aggregate, calcium chloride and water so that the resulting compacted mix meets the specified gradation and the limits of the reviewed job‑mix formula. Composition and characteristics

Asphalt Content is defined as the proportion of asphalt binder (or emulsified asphalt) incorporated with uniformly graded aggregate fractions and any approved warm‑mix additive, expressed either as a weight percent of the total mix or as a mass fraction depending on the specific pavement application. In an Asphalt Rubber Gap‑Graded mixture the binder consists of asphalt cement, certain additives and reclaimed ground tire rubber, with the rubber portion required to be at least 15 % by weight of the total blend. The rubber particles used in that binder may be up to No. 8 mesh (2.38 mm) and give the blended binder a higher viscosity than conventional neat binders, which together with the high void content of the gap‑graded aggregate permits a higher overall binder content and thicker film thickness in the pavement structure. The asphalt rubber binder must satisfy the physical requirements specified in ASTM D6114, Table 1, Type II. For stone matrix asphalt mixtures the binder together with uniformly graded aggregate fractions and any approved warm‑mix additive must be sized, uniformly graded, and combined in proportions that meet the material, gradation, and volumetric requirements of the job‑mix formula, and laboratory quality‑control testing of a single sample must show results within the tolerances listed in Table D. The mixture must achieve a particle coating of at least 95.0 % to be acceptable. In asphalt‑treated permeable base courses the initial target asphalt content is 2.5 % by weight for the joint mix formula, and during production the measured content must remain within 0.8 % of that target; the aggregate particles must be uniformly coated and no observable runoff of excess asphalt may appear, with a defect declared when coating falls below 95 %, runoff is observed, or production tolerances are exceeded. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses the binder proportion by mass is determined for a plant‑mixed course that also contains fine aggregate, coarse aggregate, and filler sized and uniformly graded to meet the composition limits of Table C, and acceptance requires the measured asphalt content by weight to fall within the tolerances listed in Table E. Tack‑coat specifications refer to Asphalt Residue Content (ARC), which is the percentage of binder in the emulsified asphalt expressed as a decimal on the Bill of Lading and used together with the desired residual rate to calculate application rates. Superpave thin‑asphalt overlay mixtures similarly require the binder to be blended with uniformly graded aggregate fractions and any approved WMA additive in specific proportions, and calibration factors for both asphalt content and the 75 µm sieve are provided to PTM No. 757. Crushed‑aggregate base courses use emulsified asphalt (Class RS‑1 or CRS‑1) mixed with Type A or Type B aggregate, calcium chloride, and a predetermined amount of water in a uniform blend; the mix must be proportioned according to the job‑mix formula so that after adding water the compacted material satisfies the specified gradation limits, and twice‑daily control testing ensures the binder remains within the tolerance set by Table A. Quality‑control testing for asphalt content includes checking that QC test results on a single sample for percent passing the 4.75 mm (No. 4), 2.36 mm (No. 8), and 75 µm (No. 200) sieves, as well as the asphalt content itself, are within the tolerances in Table D; failure to meet these tolerances constitutes a defect in asphalt content. In the Stone Matrix Asphalt wearing course, selecting durable gap‑graded stones that achieve stone‑on‑stone contact establishes the framework for the amount of binder required, and a higher asphalt content matrix—often combined with fiber or polymer‑modified binder—enhances durability and rut resistance while raising material cost. Adjusting the proportion of asphalt to meet the desired air void level directly influences mixture stability and moisture susceptibility, and the use of specialized processing equipment such as fiber machines together with additional mineral filler further tailors performance characteristics. In the Asphalt Rubber Gap‑Graded wearing course, adding reclaimed ground tire rubber to the binder at a minimum of 15 % by weight changes the composition from a neat binder to a rubberized binder that exhibits higher viscosity because specialized mixing equipment agitates and heats the blend until the rubber particles swell, producing particles up to No. 8 mesh (2.38 mm). This increased viscosity is compatible with the high‑void structure of gap‑graded mixtures, permitting a greater overall binder content and thicker film thickness while delivering performance comparable to polymer‑modified binders. Variations in the proportioning of asphalt binder that produce either a deficiency or an excess render a mixture unsuitable for placement; when the measured asphalt content is not within the single‑sample (n=1) or multiple‑sample (n≥3) tolerances in Table A for two consecutive lots or a total of three lots, production of the job‑mix formula must be stopped and corrective actions taken. A percent‑within‑limits for asphalt content (PWLAC) that is less than 50 % classifies the lot as defective, while a PWL of 90 % or greater triggers the pay factor calculation specified in the contract equation; values between 50 % and 90 % produce a pay factor from 70 upward according to the same equation. The pavement is also considered defective when the payment factors for asphalt content, the 75‑µm (No. 200) sieve, and the 2.36‑mm (No. 8) sieve are all 85 %. Under Section 412 for Superpave thin‑asphalt overlay, any measured asphalt content that falls outside the tolerances set out in Table B requires an immediate stop of production and corrective action because composition or proportioning variations directly affect acceptability. For mixtures whose total asphalt content exceeds 7 % by weight, draindown sensitivity must be determined using AASHTO T 305 and fibers or stabilizers must be employed to keep binder loss within the limits of Table A. Acceptance of daily production also depends on at least 90 % of printed‑ticket values being within 0.2 percentage points of the job‑mix formula, so even small processing variations can jeopardize certification. In the asphalt‑rich base course, reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) may not exceed 20 % by weight and recycled asphalt shingle (RAS) material is prohibited; any increase in RAP or inclusion of RAS violates the composition requirement and a new job‑mix formula must be submitted. In pervious asphalt pavement systems, polymer additives or ground‑tire rubber may be used to meet the required PG grade, RAP is limited to a maximum of 10 % of the mixture, an anti‑strip additive must be present at not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt, and cellulose fibers are mandated between 0.2 % and 0.4 % by weight of the total mix; these dosage ranges directly influence the measured asphalt content and are verified through quality‑control tests for asphalt content, gradation, and air voids. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, any change in material sources must be accompanied by a revised job‑mix formula submitted to the DME/DMM before use; the mixture must remain within the tolerances of Table E, and if acceptance values deviate from those tolerances in consecutive tests, necessary changes must be made immediately. Production must be suspended after 150 tons if compliance cannot be achieved, demonstrating that any alteration that pushes the asphalt‑content by mass beyond the allowed limits directly affects mixture acceptance. When the aggregate blend, binder type, or a warm‑mix additive is altered, a completely new job‑mix formula—including calibration factors for both asphalt content and the 75‑µm sieve as required by PTM No. 757—must be submitted. In asphalt‑treated permeable base courses, the target asphalt content is 2.5 % by weight; changes in aggregate composition, source material, processing method, or proportioning may require adjustment of the content within the range specified in Table A so that aggregates are uniformly coated and excess runoff does not occur. Production must stay within 0.8 % of the job‑mix formula (n=1); a lot is defective if production tolerances are exceeded, if less than 95 % of aggregate particles are coated, or if observable runoff of excess asphalt is present. Across all specifications, when the binder source or grade changes, a new mix design is required; similarly, any modification to warm‑mix technology, fibers, or other additives must be shown not to be detrimental to moisture resistance. Plants used for production must be fully automated and listed in Bulletin 41, with aggregates and mixtures free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture as specified in Section 413.2(e)1.d.1, and proportioning devices must be interlocked with the plant system and controlled to ±10 % of the required fiber weight. Three loose mixture samples are obtained at the plant for asphalt content, gradation, and draindown; if any test result fails to meet specification limits, another demonstration is performed before proceeding, and when samples tested for asphalt content and percent passing the 75‑µm sieve meet the n=1 criteria of Table I and density meets Table F, payment is made at 100 % of the contract unit price; failure to meet those criteria results in the material being classified as defective work. Key material properties

The performance of asphalt content in an SMA wearing course depends on achieving the target air‑void level, which is set by selecting the proper binder proportion (physical) [1]. Sufficient stone‑to‑stone contact supplies the mechanical strength needed for rut resistance (mechanical) [1]. A rich mortar binder and high‑asphalt‑content matrix give chemical durability (chemical) [1]. The mixture must also be evaluated for moisture susceptibility and asphalt‑cement draindown sensitivity because these factors can degrade the binder’s effectiveness (chemical/physical) [1]. For Superpave, thin overlay, stone matrix asphalt and ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, the asphalt content measured by mass must be kept within laboratory‑verified limits and the mixture must meet the percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve; additional gradation tolerances for the 2.36 mm (No. 8) and 6.3 mm (1/4‑inch) sieves are also required (physical) [2]. The theoretical maximum specific gravity (Gmm) is determined for each sublot and, together with bulk specific gravity calculated in accordance with AASHTO T 312, is used to obtain air voids (Va) and Voids in Mineral Aggregate (VMA) at the design density; when a warm‑mix additive is employed the specification may permit VMA to be reduced to a minimum of 16.5 % (physical) [2]. Percent coated particles must be greater than or equal to 95.0 % to prevent binder stripping (chemical/adhesive property) [2]. The compacted density of pavement cores must align with Gmm within ± 0.030, and density acceptance is achieved by proper compaction and optimum rolling patterns that eliminate roller marks and avoid flushing of the asphalt binder (mechanical/physical) [2]. Apparent moisture content in the produced mixture may not exceed 0.5 %, and both aggregates and the finished mix must be free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture; moisture susceptibility analysis shall be performed according to Bulletin 27 (chemical/moisture) [2]. Draindown testing shall follow AASHTO T 305 with a one‑hour reading, especially for mixes containing greater than 7 % total asphalt content, where draindown sensitivity is evaluated at the maximum mixture temperature listed in Table B minus 5 °F (chemical/thermal) [2]. Asphalt production temperatures must remain within the range specified for normal paving (and any special condition ranges), and placement shall not occur when surfaces are wet or when the air or surface temperature is 50 °F or lower; an exception for a 6.3 mm wearing course permits a minimum surface temperature of 50 °F with an air temperature of 40 °F, while all mixes must be placed at temperatures no lower than the minimum temperature specified in Table A (thermal) [2]. For tack‑coat applications, the thermal condition requires that the coating be placed only when the air temperature is 40 °F and rising, ensuring proper breakage and setting of the emulsified binder (thermal) [2]. The chemical characteristic for tack coat is expressed as Asphalt Residue Content (ARC), a percentage divided by 100 recorded on the Bill of Lading and certificate of compliance; this ARC value determines the amount of asphalt present in the emulsion for calculating the residual rate (chemical) [2]. Physical application of tack coat must be controlled to an uniform distributing pressure with an application‑rate tolerance of 0.02 gallon per square yard so that the intended residual rate is achieved uniformly across the surface (physical) [2]. When the emulsified asphalt material is stored for 30 days or longer after certification, it must be resampled and retested at no additional cost to confirm that its ARC still satisfies Bulletin 25 specification requirements (chemical quality control) [2]. In an asphalt‑treated permeable base course, performance of asphalt content depends on maintaining the specified binder quantity and ensuring adequate coating of the aggregate; the mixture is deemed defective when the percent of coated aggregate particles is less than 95 %, when observable runoff of excess asphalt occurs, or when production tolerances are exceeded (chemical/physical) [2].

Behavior and mechanisms

The high asphalt content in Stone Matrix Asphalt creates a tough, stable, rut‑resistant mixture that sustains its strength under traffic loading, while the mix design explicitly requires evaluation of moisture susceptibility and asphalt cement draindown sensitivity to guard against moisture‑related deterioration; the source does not address temperature changes, chemical exposure, or other service conditions, so no behavior for those factors is described. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 123–124)

In a Stone Matrix Asphalt (SMA) wearing course the mechanical mechanism consists of stone‑to‑stone contact achieved through a gap‑graded aggregate that supplies strength. [1] The physical mechanism involves regulation of air voids, whereby the selected binder amount must produce the desired void level. [1] The chemical mechanism is provided by a rich mortar binder—often polymer‑modified and stabilized with fibers—that resists moisture susceptibility and draindown, thereby delivering durability. [1] In an Asphalt Rubber Gap‑Graded (AR‑GG) wearing course the physical mechanism is the swelling of crumb‑rubber particles during heated mixing, which raises the binder’s viscosity higher than conventional, neat asphalt binders and permits a thicker film on the aggregate skeleton. [1] The mechanical mechanism in the AR‑GG mix is the relatively high air‑void content of the gap‑graded mixture, which supplies the structural space needed for that high‑viscosity binder while maintaining stone‑to‑stone contact. [1] The chemical mechanism requires that the rubberized binder, including its additives, satisfy the physical requirements specified in ASTM D6114 Table 1 Type II. [1] In the Asphalt Treated Permeable Base Course (ATPBC) the behavior of asphalt content is governed primarily by a physical distribution mechanism; the content must be adjusted so that the aggregate is uniformly coated and no observable runoff of excess asphalt occurs. [2] Mechanical control in the ATPBC is provided by meeting the specified gradation requirements and keeping the measured asphalt content within 0.8 % of the JMF during production, thereby maintaining consistency with the design target. [2] In Superpave thin‑asphalt overlay courses the behavior of asphalt content is governed by the mixture’s volumetric properties—bulk specific gravity, air voids and Voids in Mineral Aggregate—which define how much binder can be retained. [2] When the total asphalt content exceeds greater than 7% the physical phenomenon of draindown becomes critical and must be evaluated at the maximum mixture temperature listed in Table B minus 5°F. [2] Fibers are employed to mechanically impede binder movement if draindown exceeds Table A limits, while stabilizer types specified in Section 419.2(d) are used as chemical means to further address draindown; any deviation of measured asphalt content from specified tolerances triggers immediate corrective actions. [2] In an emulsified tack coat the amount of asphalt present is expressed as Asphalt Residue Content (ARC), a decimal fraction that directly scales the desired residual rate into the required application rate, so the chemical makeup of the emulsion governs how much binder remains on the pavement. [2] Physical control of ARC is achieved by using a calibrated distributor that maintains uniform pressure and limits the spray flow to within a tolerance of 0.02 gallon per square yard, with temperature monitoring of the tank contents to keep the material at appropriate viscosity. [2] The emulsified asphalt must chemically break and set before paving, and the specification restricts application to air temperatures of 40°F or higher and rising on dry surfaces, ensuring that the binder cures without tracking; mechanical adjustments such as spray‑bar height, lateral and vertical positioning, and extending coverage beyond joints further influence the uniformity of the asphalt content across the treated surface. [2]

Durability and deterioration

In SMA mix design the asphalt content must be selected to achieve the target air‑void level while also protecting against moisture‑related damage, and the mixture’s moisture susceptibility and asphalt cement draindown sensitivity are required to be evaluated because water infiltration can leach binder and erode durability [1]. The rich mortar binder that provides rut resistance in SMA means that loss of binder through draindown or excessive moisture can undermine performance even when a high asphalt content is used [1]. The guidance further warns against placing SMA over pavements with underlying problems, as such issues can exacerbate deterioration despite the higher asphalt content [1]. A deficiency or an excess of asphalt content may produce pattern segregation, flushing, or other defective pavement that must be removed and replaced; a flushing condition is declared defective when the measured macrotexture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 inches [2]. If the asphalt content or the percent passing the 75 mm (No. 200) sieve is not within the single‑sample (n=1) or multiple‑sample (n≥3) tolerances in Table A for two consecutive lots or a total of three lots, production must be stopped until the cause is identified and corrected, and the same stop requirement applies when the asphalt content is not within the tolerances in Table B for two consecutive lots or a total of three lots [2]. An area of pattern segregation contains defective pavement if the mixture is defective in asphalt content [2]. Rollers may not be used in vibratory mode unless it can be demonstrated to the satisfaction of the Representative that no breaking of aggregate or flushing of asphalt binder results from the vibration, and vibration must be discontinued if either condition is observed [2]. Asphalt paving mixtures shall not be placed when surfaces are wet or when the air or surface temperature is 50 °F or lower, and both the aggregates and the completed mixture must be free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture as specified in Section 413.2(e)1.d.1 [2]. Material shall be shipped within two hours of plant mixing; if the emulsified asphalt material is stored for 30 days or longer after certification, it must be resampled and retested at no additional cost to determine compliance with Bulletin 25 specification requirements [2]. The warm‑mix asphalt (WMA) technology additive, modifier, or process shall not be detrimental to the moisture resistance of the mixture [2]. Draindown testing shall be performed according to AASHTO T 305, and the mix must achieve at least 95.0 % coated aggregate particles [2]. For mixtures with greater than 7 percent total asphalt content, draindown sensitivity shall be determined using AASHTO T 305 at the maximum mixture temperature listed in Table B minus 5 °F, and if draindown exceeds the requirements of Table A, fibers shall be used to reduce binder draindown [2]. Reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) shall be limited to a maximum of 10 % of the mixture [2]. The additive shall be blended with the asphalt cement before adding the additive and asphalt cement to the mixture, using the manufacturer’s recommended dosage but not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt [2]. When a stabilizer is required, cellulose fibers shall be added in accordance with Table B at a dosage rate within 0.2 % to 0.4 % by weight of the total mix [2]. If no history exists for an approved dense‑graded mixture, testing in accordance with ASTM D3625 shall be performed on the finished mix with a minimum of 90 % coating, and an anti‑strip additive must be blended with the cement at not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt [2]. For an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base Course, the asphalt content is considered defective when production tolerances are exceeded, when the percent of coated aggregate particles is less than 95 %, or when observable runoff of excess asphalt occurs; the material must be removed and replaced unless written direction otherwise is provided [2]. In pervious asphalt pavement, a low asphalt content often coincides with insufficient coating (less than 95 %) or excessive fine material (more than 4 % passing the No. 200 sieve), which constitutes a durability concern that can compromise the integrity of the pervious layer and requires removal and replacement [2]. The mixture should be compacted while it is in proper condition, and roller speed, pattern, and size (or amplitude and frequency if vibratory rolling is approved) shall be adjusted to eliminate displacement, shoving, cracking, and aggregate breakage [2]. Tack coat shall be applied only when the air temperature is 40 °F and rising and the existing surface is dry; the treated surface shall be maintained and protected against damage, and any uncoated, lightly coated or otherwise damaged spots shall be repaired to the Representative’s satisfaction before placing the next pavement course [2].

Susceptibility of asphalt content to deterioration increases when, after spreading, the mix is segregated, below the minimum temperature, or contains either a deficiency or an excess of binder; such material must not be added to the pavement mat. Placement when surfaces are wet or when the air or surface temperature is 50 °F or lower also heightens susceptibility, and the minimum surface temperature of 50 °F and an air temperature of 40 °F must be strictly enforced; maintaining temperatures above these thresholds reduces the risk of binder loss or uneven content. The asphalt content must remain within the single‑sample tolerances in Table A for two consecutive lots, otherwise production shall be stopped. For Superpave and SMA mixtures, moisture and residual fuel oil in the aggregates increase deterioration risk, so both aggregates and the completed mixture must be free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture as specified, and hot‑mixture storage beyond two hours degrades the asphalt content; material shall be shipped within 2 hours of plant mixing and any stored SMA that does not consistently meet the quality of freshly discharged material shall be rejected. A coating level of at least 95 % of particles is required to keep the binder from stripping, so insufficient coating raises deterioration risk; percent coated particles ≥ 95.0 % must be achieved. Moisture susceptibility analysis shall be performed according to Bulletin 27 and any WMA additive, modifier, or process must not be detrimental to the moisture resistance of the mixture. Draindown testing according to AASHTO T 305 using a 1‑hour reading flags excessive binder loss; for mixtures with greater than 7 % total asphalt content draindown sensitivity shall be determined at the maximum mixture temperature listed in Table B minus 5 °F, and fibers shall be used if draindown exceeds the requirements of Table A, while stabilizer types specified in Section 419.2(d) may be employed as needed. For a pervious asphalt pavement system (Section 420), susceptibility rises when the percent of coated aggregate particles falls below 95 % or when fines exceed the 4 % maximum passing the No. 200 sieve, and defects also include insufficient surface tolerance, depth, or asphalt content; maintaining coating of at least 95 %, limiting fine content to no more than 4 % passing the No. 200 sieve, keeping surface irregularities under 3/8‑inch, limiting depth variations within 1/2‑inch, and achieving an average infiltration rate of at least 200 inches per hour all reduce the risk of asphalt‑content related failure. Material interactions and compatibility

In Stone Matrix Asphalt the asphalt content is deliberately high so that a rich mortar binder surrounds the stone‑to‑stone framework, providing durability while still allowing the target air void level to be met; this elevated dosage also increases sensitivity to moisture and binder draindown, which must be evaluated, and the binder is often fiber‑ or polymer‑modified, a modification that works together with the high asphalt dosage to improve adhesion to the durable aggregates and to mitigate stripping, while the aggregate gradation is selected to support stone‑on‑stone contact and satisfy the specified high binder demand [1]. In an Asphalt Rubber Gap‑Graded wearing course the crumb‑rubber modifier raises the viscosity of the binder, and the gap‑graded aggregate framework retains a high void content; this high void content makes the use of the higher‑viscosity rubberized binder feasible and allows a greater overall binder proportion, producing a thicker film that still accommodates stone‑to‑stone contact, with rubber particles blended into the asphalt cement with appropriate additives to achieve a rubber content of at least 15 % by weight of the total blend, and the resulting rubberized binder must satisfy the physical requirements of ASTM D6114 Table 1 Type II [1]. Under Section 413.2 of the Superpave mixture design the asphalt content must be verified each production day by testing the mixture and comparing results to the Joint Mix Formula tolerances, with at least ninety percent of printed ticket results required to stay within 0.2 percentage points of the JMF; when reclaimed asphalt pavement or surface (RAP/RAS) is incorporated, its average asphalt content is measured and blended with virgin materials so that the completed mix meets the JMF requirements, and anti‑strip additives—either a compatible heat‑stable amine‑based liquid or an alternate additive—are employed to improve binder‑aggregate adhesion, while a moisture susceptibility analysis is performed if the warm‑mix additive already contains an anti‑strip component and additional anti‑strip additive or other adjustments to the JMF may be made to satisfy the specified moisture‑susceptibility criteria [2]. Section 419.1 requires that the asphalt content be managed so that at least 95 % of aggregate particles are coated, a level verified by AASHTO T 195 testing to provide sufficient binder coverage and avoid stripping during handling and placement; moisture susceptibility analysis required by Bulletin 27 ensures that any warm‑mix additive, modifier, or process does not reduce the mixture’s resistance to moisture damage, and the specification prohibits the use of reclaimed asphalt material, so blending RAP or RAS binder with virgin binder is not permitted in this mix design [2]. In the Asphalt Treated Permeable Base Course the Joint Mix Formula is designed with an initial target asphalt content of 2.5 % by weight and may be adjusted within the range specified in Table A to achieve uniform coating of the aggregate without observable runoff, and production must keep the asphalt content within 0.8 % of the JMF, which guarantees that the aggregate remains uniformly covered and prevents excess binder migration [2]. For a cold‑recycled asphalt base course the emulsion or foamed asphalt is placed at the mix‑design recommended optimum moisture and binder level so that it can adequately coat the reclaimed aggregates and provide the necessary adhesion for compaction; when RAP gradations or composition vary sufficiently, field adjustments prescribed in the QC plan are made to obtain satisfactory coating and the specified compaction, underscoring the interaction between asphalt content and aggregate surface characteristics [2]. When a mixture contains more than 7 % total asphalt content the specification mandates determining its draindown sensitivity using AASHTO T 305 at the maximum mixture temperature listed in Table B minus 5 °F; if draindown exceeds the requirements of Table A, fibers are used to reduce binder draindown and stabilizers may be employed as needed, and anti‑strip additives are called out in Section 413.2(g) linking asphalt content to additional measures that protect the binder‑aggregate bond [2]. In pervious asphalt mixes approved polymer additives or ground‑tire rubber may be used to achieve the required PG grade, but any anti‑strip additive must be compatible with both the aggregate and the asphalt cement and is blended into the binder before mixture assembly at a dosage not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt; RAP may be used only up to a maximum of 10 % of the mixture to ensure proper blending with virgin binder, and when a stabilizer is required cellulose fibers are added within 0.2 % to 0.4 % by weight of the total mix to improve moisture resistance [2]. Under Section 360.3 a mix is classified as defective in asphalt content if the percentage of coated aggregate particles falls below 95 %, indicating insufficient adhesion, or if observable runoff of excess asphalt occurs, and production tolerances that are exceeded also trigger a defect classification [2].

Influence of production and placement

In Section 413, plant mixing, production and placement temperatures must be maintained at the laying temperatures specified in Table A for the type and class of material used; delivering or placing mixtures below the minimum temperature or with a deficiency or excess of asphalt content is prohibited because such deviations would produce a mixture that fails acceptance tests and can lead to defective pavement. Proper compaction, performed while the mix is still within its proper condition, must meet density‑acceptance requirements and avoid displacement, shoving, cracking or aggregate breakage, otherwise the asphalt binder distribution may be compromised, and finishing actions such as spreading, striking off, edge work and avoiding segregation or flushing are required to keep the asphalt content uniform and to prevent surface defects that would reduce performance. In Section 412, plant mixing, production and placement temperatures must be kept at the laying temperatures specified in Table B; placing mixtures when surface temperature falls below 50 °F (or air temperature below 40 °F for approved exceptions) is prohibited because low temperatures can prevent proper compaction and lead to asphalt‑content samples that fall outside the tolerances, triggering a stop in production. Hauling and placement must use the prescribed equipment—steel‑wheeled rollers of at least 10 tons and an MTV for delivery—to ensure the hot mix arrives without cooling or contamination, and rolling must begin immediately after placement with appropriate speed, pattern and roller size to avoid displacement, shoving, cracking, aggregate breakage, or flushing that would affect density and the effective asphalt content. The mixture acceptance program requires LTS testing of each lot for asphalt content; if the measured content is not within the single‑sample (n = 1) or multiple‑sample tolerances in Table B, production must be halted until the cause—often linked to temperature control, handling or compaction—is corrected, thereby protecting the final performance of the pavement. In Section 419, proper plant mixing must achieve a uniform blend of any WMA additives or stabilizers by using interlocked proportioning devices controlled to ±10 % of the required weight and by extending dry and wet mixing times until fibers are evenly distributed before binder injection; this ensures that the intended asphalt binder proportion is maintained. Production practice requires that the hot‑mixture be shipped within 2 hours of mixing, because stored SMA material that does not consistently match the quality of material discharged directly into hauling vehicles will be rejected, preventing loss of asphalt content through cooling or segregation. Placement may only occur on dry surfaces and when air or surface temperature exceeds 50 °F; otherwise the binder may not achieve proper compaction and density. Compaction must use at least three steel‑wheeled rollers (each ≥10 tons) operated per manufacturer recommendations with watering or soapy systems, and vibratory rolling is allowed only after a demonstration shows no aggregate breakage or binder flushing, thereby preserving the designed asphalt content throughout finishing. Selection, specification, and improvement

Specifying Stone Matrix Asphalt begins only after a series of material checks are completed; first, appropriate aggregates must be selected and an aggregate gradation that yields stone‑on‑stone contact must be established, then the gradation must meet or exceed the minimum voids in mineral aggregate (VMA) requirement or otherwise permit use of the minimum binder content, after which the asphalt content is set to provide the desired air‑void level, and finally the mix’s moisture susceptibility and binder draindown sensitivity are evaluated [1]. For Superpave mixture designs the asphalt‑content specification is governed by the limits set out in Table A for each job‑mix formula, and mixture‑acceptance samples taken from every sublot must be tested for both asphalt content and sieve gradation; if a mix shows either a deficiency or an excess of asphalt content it may not be added to the pavement mat and production is halted after repeated failures [2]. The producer’s quality‑control plan must prescribe a sampling frequency and employ PTM 757, 702 or 742 for each test, with at least three test results required to check single‑sample tolerances and, when five or more results are available, the running average of the last five consecutive results is used to confirm multiple‑sample compliance; printed ticket results may be accepted provided that ninety percent of daily tickets fall within 0.2 percentage points of the joint mix formula [2]. Compliance is defined as the percent within limits for asphalt content (PWLAC), a lot is defective when PWLAC is less than 50, and the contractor may request a retest if the asphalt‑content pay factor for the lot is less than 80.00; payment factors are calculated as 100 + 0.4 × (PWL‑90) when PWL is ≥ 90 and as 70 + 0.75 × (PWL‑50) when PWL is between 50 and 90 [2]. For the 6.3 mm thin asphalt overlay the acceptance tolerances in Table B must be met for both single‑sample (n=1) and multiple‑sample (n≥3) evaluations, and the laboratory must determine asphalt content using PTM 757 or, when problematic aggregates are present, PTM 702 Modified Method D on a minimum 1000 g sample; any result outside the Table B limits is subjected to extreme‑value analysis per PTM 4 at the 5 % significance level and production must be stopped if out‑of‑tolerance results occur for two consecutive lots or three lots in total until a corrective solution is approved [2]. In Stone Matrix Asphalt mixtures described in Section 419 each lot’s asphalt content is evaluated against the single‑sample (n=1) and multiple‑sample (n≥3) tolerances listed in Table D using loose samples analyzed according to PTM 757 or, when problematic aggregates are present, PTM 702 with the calibration factors supplied with the job‑mix formula, and paving operations are stopped when asphalt content does not meet the single‑sample or multiple‑sample tolerances for two consecutive lots or a total of three lots [2]. For an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base Course the specification requires that the measured asphalt content remain within production tolerances, that at least ninety‑five percent of the aggregate particles be coated with binder, and that no observable runoff of excess asphalt appear; failure to satisfy any of these conditions renders the mix defective in asphalt content [2]. For a Crushed Aggregate Base Course, Type DG, the asphalt binder must be an emulsified asphalt of Class RS‑1 or CRS‑1 as required by Section 702 and its proportion is fixed through the job‑mix formula established within the composition limits shown in Table A and verified by testing according to AASHTO T 27; uniformity is controlled by sampling twice daily and keeping at least ninety percent of test results inside those tolerance limits, and if results fall outside the limits the job‑mix formula may be revised or the supply source changed, while water added for compaction must stay within two percentage points of the optimum moisture determined by PTM No. 106 Method B [2].

To improve the performance of asphalt content, projects may employ quality‑control measures such as Percent Within Limits (PWL), which captures both mean and standard deviation and directly influences pay factors for asphalt content. Contractors are required to achieve consistent density near the middle of the target field density range of 92.0% to 98.5% for Standard and RPS wearing courses. Incorporating reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP) into hot‑mix, warm‑mix or cold‑recycled base mixes provides an alternative source of binder and aggregates that conserves material while maintaining required content levels; when full‑depth reclamation is used, stabilizing the recycled layer with asphalt creates a stabilized product whose support characteristics are comparable to those of conventional cold‑recycling, thereby mitigating potential deficiencies in asphalt content. In Stone Matrix Asphalt, improving asphalt content performance is achieved by using a gap‑graded aggregate that creates stone‑on‑stone contact together with a fiber or polymer‑modified, high‑asphalt‑content matrix and by adding a higher amount of mineral filler in a different manner than usual; selecting durable aggregates and ensuring the gradation meets or exceeds the minimum VMA allows the minimum binder content to be used while still attaining the target air‑void level, and moisture susceptibility and asphalt‑cement draindown sensitivity must be evaluated as part of quality control. Because SMA requires a special mix design, a fiber machine, a 100‑ton test strip, and often special aggregate production, these procedural controls help mitigate the weaknesses associated with higher binder contents. Incorporating reclaimed ground tire rubber (CRM) as a crumb‑rubber modifier into the asphalt binder—at least 15% by weight of the total blend—creates an Asphalt Rubber Gap‑Graded mixture whose higher viscosity permits a greater binder content and thicker film thickness within the high‑void, gap‑graded aggregate structure, delivering performance comparable to polymer‑modified binders; compliance with the physical requirements of ASTM D6114 Table 1 Type II provides the necessary quality control for this modified asphalt content. A certified asphalt field technician qualified according to Publication 351 shall supervise placement and ensure that mixtures are delivered at the specified temperatures and that no material with a deficiency or an excess of asphalt content is added after spreading. Each lot (and sublot) must provide loose‑mixture acceptance samples that the Laboratory Testing Services evaluates using PTM No. 757 (or PTM No. 702, Modified Method D, and PTM No. 739 when previously identified problematic aggregates are used) to verify asphalt content against Table A tolerances; production shall be halted for two consecutive lots if those limits are exceeded until a corrective solution is approved. Use of a Material Transfer Vehicle for RPS pavements and proper adjustment of screed assemblies further help keep the binder proportion uniform, while sealing curb edges with hot or emulsified asphalt fills surface voids that could otherwise expose content variations. Approved warm‑mix (WMA) technologies and compatible anti‑strip additives may be introduced, dosed, blended and documented according to the manufacturer’s recommendations and recorded in the plant QC plan; each daily production batch larger than 50 tons shall be sampled and asphalt content tested with PTM 757, 702 or 742, confirming compliance with Table A using at least three results and a running average of the last five consecutive test results, while ninety percent of printed‑ticket results must remain within 0.2 percentage points of the Joint Mix Formula. When a WMA technology additive is used as the stabilizing agent, the minimum VMA may be lowered to 16.5 %; the mixture shall achieve at least 95 % coated particles, and if this target is missed the plant mixing time should be increased or other adjustments made. Daily draindown testing per AASHTO T 305 with a one‑hour reading shall be performed; when total asphalt content exceeds seven percent, draindown sensitivity shall be evaluated at the maximum mixture temperature listed in Table B minus 5 °F and fibers (or other stabilizers) shall be added if draindown exceeds Table A requirements, with fiber dosage not less than 0.2 % to 0.4 % by weight of the total mix. If the water absorption of a coarse aggregate, determined according to AASHTO T 85, exceeds 2.0 %, the mixture shall be sampled according to PTM 1 and the apparent moisture content shall not exceed 0.5 %. The asphalt‑content pay factor (PFAC) is derived from the Percent Within Limits for asphalt content (PWLAC); when PWLAC is at least 50 the lot receives a PFAC calculated by the prescribed equations, while any PWLAC below 50 makes the lot defective. A retest may be requested when the PFAC is less than 80 %, with retest cores taken 24 inches from the original sample locations and dried immediately; the pavement will also be considered defective if the payment factor for asphalt content, percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve, and percent passing the 2.36 mm (No. 8) sieve are all 85 %. A detailed paving‑operation QC Plan shall be submitted before construction and a pre‑placement meeting held to review mixture acceptance criteria, temperature limits and equipment use; LTS testing of each lot with PTM 757 (or PTM 702 for problematic aggregates) on a minimum 1000‑g sample determines asphalt content and the percent passing the 75 µm sieve, and any two consecutive lots that fall outside the single‑sample tolerances shall trigger an immediate production stop and corrective action. Steel‑wheeled rollers weighing at least 10 tons shall be used, pneumatic rollers are prohibited, and roller wheels must be kept moist without excess water; paving may not occur when the air or surface temperature is 50 °F or lower (or 40 °F for approved extensions) and surfaces must be dry. Plant modifications to incorporate WMA technology additives, modifiers or foaming processes shall be made as directed by the Technical Representative; aggregates and the finished mix shall be free of unburned fuel oil and excess moisture, and a dedicated stabilizer supply system shall meter fibers with devices interlocked to the plant and controlled to ±10 % of the required weight while providing low‑level, no‑flow indicators and visual flow monitoring. For cold‑recycled base courses the performance can be improved by furnishing performance‑graded binders such as PG 64S‑22, PG 64V‑22, PG 64E‑22, PG 58S‑28 or PG 58V‑28 that conform to AASHTO M 332, providing QC testing and certification as specified in Sections 106.03(b) and 702.1(b)1, and adding an emulsified or foamed asphalt stabilizing agent according to the approved mix design; field adjustments are made when RAP gradations and composition vary sufficiently to obtain satisfactory coating and compaction, and a formal QC plan reviewed before work begins together with a signed bill of lading for each binder batch provides additional control over asphalt‑content quality. In Stone Matrix Asphalt mixtures the performance of the asphalt‑binder proportion is protected through rigorous lot‑acceptance controls in which each 2 500‑ton lot is divided into five sublots that must supply both a mixture‑acceptance sample for asphalt‑content verification and a Gmm verification sample with single‑sample tolerances applied; if those tolerances are not met the material is deemed defective work and paving must stop until the Representative investigates the cause and implements corrective measures, including furnace calibration factors (Cf and 200 Cf), while independent‑assurance sampling on at least one lot per ten compared to HOLA acceptance values further reinforces quality assurance. For Superpave thin overlay courses the mixture must be sampled and tested according to PTM No. 1 and AASHTO T 312, using bulk specific gravity to calculate air voids and VMA and checking all results against the tolerances in Table B; any single‑sample or multiple‑sample result that falls outside those tolerances triggers immediate corrective action and a repeat sample within 150 tons. In Asphalt‑Rich Base Course mixtures recycled asphalt pavement is limited to no more than twenty percent by weight, manufacturer waste recycled asphalt shingles are prohibited, and Warm‑Mix Asphalt technology additives or modifiers may be employed after the volumetric design is completed; such WMA components can enhance workability, reduce moisture susceptibility, or enable foamed asphalt when plant mechanical modification alone cannot achieve required temperature, compaction or blend uniformity. In pervious asphalt pavement systems approved polymer additives or ground‑tire rubber may be used to meet the required PG grade, RAP is limited to a maximum of ten percent of the mixture, an anti‑strip additive or hydrated lime is incorporated at no less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt, and when moisture‑susceptibility testing indicates a stabilizer is needed cellulose fibers are added as a pervious‑asphalt stabilizer at a dosage within 0.2 % to 0.4 % by weight of the total mix; all such modifications must be documented in a QC plan and verified through the specified acceptance tests. In Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course applications ensuring that the asphalt binder complies with the Performance‑Graded specification (AASHTO M 332) and is sourced from an approved producer provides a baseline for correct asphalt content, while the class UTFCEM emulsified asphalt offers an alternative binder option; the mix design must exclude fly ash as filler when projected traffic equals or exceeds three million ESALs, relying instead on manufactured sand and approved coarse aggregate to achieve the required JMF proportions. In crushed‑aggregate base course Type DG performance can be improved by incorporating calcium chloride as specified in Section 721, selecting emulsified asphalt of Class RS‑1 or CRS‑1 per Section 702, and tightly controlling the added moisture so that it does not vary more than 2 percentage points from the optimum moisture determined by PTM No. 106 Method B; twice‑daily sampling of the mixed material and adjustment of the job‑mix formula when needed helps keep at least 90 % of test samples within the tolerance limits of Table A, and any material that fails those limits should be removed and replaced. Performance evaluation and implications

Evaluation of asphalt content relies on both laboratory test results and field density measurements that are combined into the Percent Within Limits (PWL) quality measure, which captures the mean and the standard deviation of the results [1]. The PWL approach incorporates laboratory sieve data such as the percent passing the No. 200 sieve together with in‑place density obtained from pavement cores, and contractors are required to achieve consistent density near the middle of the target field density range—for example 92.0% to 98.5% for Standard and RPS wearing courses [1]. The standard deviation component of PWL then influences the graduated pay factors applied to asphalt content, linking these laboratory and field evidences directly to performance assessment [1]. Laboratory determination of asphalt content is performed on mixture‑acceptance samples using PTM No. 757 or PTM No. 702 (Modified Method D), with PTM No. 739 applied when previously identified problematic aggregates are present, and PTM No. 742 may be used as specified in the producer quality‑control plan [2]. For 6.3 mm mixes a minimum sample size of 1000 g is required, and calibration factors Cƒ and 200 Cƒ supplied with the Job Mix Formula are applied to the results [2]. The laboratory also determines the theoretical maximum specific gravity (Gmm) from a second loose mixture‑acceptance sample for each sublot, enabling calculation of binder percentage [2]. After obtaining at least three test results compliance is checked against the multiple‑sample tolerances in Table A (or Table B or Table D as applicable), and when five or more results are available the running average of the last five consecutive results is used; any individual result outside the single‑sample tolerance is analyzed for extreme values according to PTM No. 4 at the 5 % significance level [2]. Volumetric analysis of compacted specimens prepared from field samples is required, with a minimum of two specimens tested per sample in accordance with AASHTO T 312 to determine bulk specific gravity and binder content, and a deviation greater than ±0.030 from the contractor’s daily Gmm values triggers use of the laboratory value for density calculations [2]. Moisture‑susceptibility testing, including TSR data, is employed during mix design to verify that the asphalt content satisfies Superpave volumetric requirements [2]. Field evidence is obtained by coring the placed pavement and, with the Inspector present, testing those cores at the plant for density, asphalt content and gradation according to the applicable PTMs, and the result is expressed as percent‑within‑limits for asphalt content (PWLAC) which the Department converts into a payment factor for asphalt content (PFAC) [2]. A pavement is deemed defective when both the PFAC and the payment factor for the percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve equal 85%, at which point offset cores must be retested using the same laboratory method [2]. Daily field verification is performed for mixtures over 50 tons, with volumetric analysis added for mixes over 150 tons; printed ticket results may substitute laboratory data provided that ninety percent of those tickets remain within 0.2 percentage points of the Job Mix Formula, and the Department may take IA or QA samples for additional testing as described in the specifications [2]. The specifications do not describe separate long‑term performance monitoring for asphalt content, relying instead on the laboratory tests, field measurements and associated payment‑factor calculations to evaluate compliance over the life of the pavement [2].

In the Pavement Policy Manual, variability of asphalt content captured by Percent Within Limits influences graduated pay factors, so contractors must achieve consistent density results near the middle of the target field density range (e.g., 92.0% to 98.5% for Standard and RPS construction of Wearing Courses) to maximize payments, which helps ensure designed structural performance. For Stone Matrix Asphalt, an asphalt content is selected to provide the desired air‑void level; a higher binder proportion gives the SMA its noted rut resistance and improved durability but also raises material cost because the mix relies on more durable aggregates, modified binders and stabilizers. In an Asphalt Rubber Gap‑Graded Wearing Course, the high void fraction of gap‑graded mixtures makes the use of a high‑viscosity rubberized binder feasible, allowing a higher binder content and thicker film thickness coating the aggregate; this thicker coating delivers performance comparable to polymer‑modified binders and makes the mixture suitable for the same applications as stone mastic aggregate pavements. The rubber content in that mix must be at least 15% by weight of the total blend, and the asphalt rubber binder must meet the physical requirements of ASTM D6114 Table 1 Type II. For Superpave plant‑mixed courses the asphalt content is a required acceptance value; daily certification is granted only when quality‑control test results meet the production tolerances in Table A and at least 90% of printed ticket results are within 0.2 percentage points of the joint mix formula, otherwise the mixture cannot be certified and corrective action must be taken. For daily production of each joint mix formula greater than 50 tons, asphalt content, gradation and theoretical maximum specific gravity are determined from a single sample at least once per day and the producer QC plan must include sampling frequency according to PTM No. 1 so that results conform to Table A tolerances; ninety percent of printed ticket results must be within 0.2 percentage points of the JMF and apparent moisture content may not exceed 0.5%. After spreading, mixture that is segregated, below minimum temperature, or contains a deficiency or an excess of asphalt content must not be added to the pavement mat because excessive binder can produce flushing when the average surface macrotexture depth is less than or equal to 0.006 in., which is deemed defective and requires removal and replacement of the wearing course. If the asphalt content or the percent passing the 75 mm (No. 200) sieve falls outside the single‑sample or multiple‑sample tolerances in Table A for two consecutive lots or a total of three lots, all production of the joint mix formula must be stopped until the cause is corrected. The percent‑within‑limits for asphalt content (PWLAC) governs payment; when PWLAC is less than 50% the lot is declared defective, when it lies between 50% and 90% the asphalt‑content pay factor is calculated as PF = 70 + 0.75(PWLAC − 50), and when it equals or exceeds 90% the factor increases by 0.4 per percent point above 90, while a pay factor below 80.00 permits a retest of the material. For thin‑asphalt overlay courses the LTS must test acceptance samples according to PTM No. 757 or PTM No. 702 to determine asphalt content and the percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve, and if either result is out of tolerance for two consecutive lots or three total lots production must be stopped; any spot that shows an excess or deficiency of asphalt material is removed and replaced with fresh hot 6.3 mm paving mixture and compacted to match the surrounding surface. The payment factor for asphalt content (PB) together with the sieve factor must each reach at least 85% for the pavement to be accepted, otherwise the work is considered defective and retesting procedures are initiated. For stone‑matrix asphalt mixtures loose‑mixture acceptance samples are taken for each lot and the measured binder percentage must meet the single‑sample or multiple‑sample tolerances in Table D; production is halted when out‑of‑tolerance results occur for two consecutive lots or three total lots because such deviations can cause flushing or displacement, and full contract payment is awarded when the n=1 criteria are satisfied. Because asphalt content directly determines bulk specific gravity, air voids and VMA, the mixture is produced so that its volumetric properties conform to the tolerances of Table C. For mixtures containing more than 7% total asphalt content draindown sensitivity is evaluated at the maximum mixture temperature listed in Table B minus 5 °F using AASHTO T 305, with fibers or stabilizers added if draindown exceeds the requirements of Table A; certification again requires that at least ninety percent of printed ticket results be within 0.2 percentage points of the JMF. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses the asphalt content by mass must stay within the joint mix formula tolerances in Table E, and when consecutive tests show a deviation the contractor must make immediate adjustments; if compliance cannot be restored within 150 tons of production the work is suspended and the inspector‑in‑charge is notified. The asphalt residue content (ARC), expressed as a decimal fraction of the percentage of asphalt in an emulsified tack coat, governs the calculation of the required application rate (AR) because AR equals the desired residual rate divided by ARC; selecting an AR that achieves a uniform residual rate for the specific surface type provides consistent bonding between pavement layers, which is essential for durability and prevents premature tracking or loss of adhesion. The construction procedure also mandates applying the tack coat only when the air temperature is 40 °F and rising and the existing surface is dry, ensuring the asphalt residue can break and set properly before subsequent paving and protecting the treated surface until curing supports long‑term pavement performance. ARC must be documented on the Certificate of Compliance because it directly reflects the binder proportion that will affect bond quality and thus pavement durability; if the material is stored for 30 days or longer after certification, the specification requires resampling and retesting to verify that ARC still satisfies Bulletin 25 requirements, thereby ensuring any change in binder content does not compromise construction performance or long‑term serviceability. The asphalt‑treated permeable base course must stay within its production tolerances for asphalt content; if those tolerances are exceeded, if fewer than 95% of the aggregate particles are coated, or if excess asphalt visibly runs off, the layer is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced. In a pervious asphalt pavement system the proportion of binder serves as a quality‑control parameter; when the asphalt content does not meet the required level the pavement is deemed defective, and any pervious asphalt that is deficient in asphalt content must be removed and replaced, directly impacting construction acceptance and subsequent maintenance actions.
