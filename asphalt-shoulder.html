Asphalt Shoulder

Part of: Shoulder is-subtopic-of
Asphalt Shoulder is a topic under Shoulder.
asphalt-shouldersasphalt-shoulder
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

An Asphalt Shoulder is an asphalt pavement placed adjacent to a concrete slab—or incorporated into a widened lane—to serve as the roadway shoulder on low‑volume rural Interstates and Expressways (ADT < 10,000) where a dedicated incident‑management lane is not required[1]. Its chief purpose is to safeguard the pavement‑shoulder joint from water, chlorides and other infiltrants by mandating that every concrete pavement rehabilitation project clean and seal the joint with a 0.75‑inch × 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir filled with rubberized or asphalt sealing material, while also providing a smooth, safe transition for vehicles leaving the travel lane and preventing edge breakdown[1][2]. The shoulder is built to the bottom elevation of the proposed base course—scarified no more than four inches, primed and treated with Superpave asphalt layers (PG 64S‑22 for traffic under 0.3 million ESALs)—and is finished with a Safety Edge at least 1.5 inches deep (no more than five inches) that is extruded from the same mix as the roadway and shaped to a 26°–40° angle measured from the extended pavement cross‑slope[2].

Purpose and design objectives

In the Pavement Policy Manual, section 4.3 JOINTS, the Asphalt Shoulder is intended to mitigate the risk of water, chlorides and other incompressible materials infiltrating the joint between a concrete pavement and its adjacent shoulder, which can compromise pavement durability [1]. Accordingly the design requires that the pavement‑shoulder joint be cleaned and sealed during every CPR project by constructing a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir and applying rubberized/asphalt joint sealing material to maintain an effective barrier [1]. In section 8.14 WIDENED LANES FOR CONCRETE PAVEMENT, the Asphalt Shoulder is used to supply an effective roadway shoulder on low‑volume rural Interstates and Expressways with two‑way ADTs less than 10,000 where a dedicated incident management lane is not required, by pairing a widened concrete slab limited to a maximum width of 14 feet with a Type 1 asphalt shoulder to achieve the needed shoulder width while keeping the slab within the prescribed limits [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that the Asphalt Shoulder is intended to prevent edge breakdown and provide a safe, smooth transition for vehicles that drift off the travel lane [2]. To meet this need the construction specifications require that the exposed outer edges be worked immediately after placement to eliminate sharp, ragged, and open edges, and that a Safety Edge be built as the standard treatment on the outside edge of both pavement and shoulder [2]. The Safety Edge must be formed with the same material as the roadway course, have a depth of at least 1.5 inches, and be shaped to an angle of 26 to 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope extended, thereby reducing edge deterioration and improving safety [2].

The asphalt shoulder is required to be incorporated into the pavement‑shoulder joint cleaning and sealing requirement for every concrete pavement rehabilitation (CPR) project, which involves constructing a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir at the joint and filling it with rubberized or asphalt joint‑sealing material, thereby satisfying durability and serviceability objectives for the pavement system. [1] For functional support, the shoulder shall be shaped and compacted to the bottom elevation of the proposed base course so that it provides a functional support surface at the designed level. [2] Structural integrity is achieved by scarifying the existing shoulder to a maximum depth of 4 inches below the existing grade and then backfilling and compacting aggregate base material until the compacted layer does not move under the compaction equipment. [2] Durability requirements are met by applying an asphalt prime coat in accordance with Section 461.3 followed by an asphalt surface treatment as prescribed in Section 480.3. [2] Safety is addressed by satisfactorily repairing any guide rail damaged or destroyed by the construction operations and, when a guide rail remains in place, excavating without disturbing it; additionally, immediately after placement the exposed outer edges shall be worked to eliminate sharp, ragged or open edges, preventing edge breakdown and providing a smooth, serviceable surface. [2] A Safety Edge shall be provided on the outside edge of the shoulder for both wearing and binder courses, with a depth of 1.5 inches or greater but not placed at total depths greater than 5 inches, using the same material as the roadway course unless otherwise specified, and the completed angle must be 26 to 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope extension, and the Safety Edge is omitted only where curbs, sidewalks or guide rails directly over the edge exist. [2] The shoulder shall be compacted in accordance with the specifications so that density acceptance criteria are met, roller marks are eliminated, and flushing of the binder is avoided, satisfying the mat‑density requirements of Section 413.3(j). [2] Where density is critical, acceptance shall be by pavement cores as specified in Section 413.3(j)4.a; for non‑critical shoulders the same criteria applied to other miscellaneous applications allow non‑movement acceptance when the mixture depth is less than 1 inch or equal to or less than 110 pounds per square yard, and permit optimum‑rolling pattern acceptance for placements not exceeding 500 tons. [2]

Scope and applicability

For Portland cement concrete pavements that include an adjacent asphalt shoulder, the Asphalt Shoulder design approach is appropriate in Concrete Pavement Restoration projects where the pavement‑shoulder joint must be cleaned and sealed; the required treatment calls for constructing a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir and sealing the joint with rubberized/asphalt joint sealing material, and this work should be performed whether or not the asphalt shoulder will be reconstructed. [1] The Asphalt Shoulder is also appropriate when it forms part of a widened concrete slab that does not exceed 14 feet in width, providing a partial asphalt right shoulder (often Type 1) alongside the concrete roadway; this configuration is considered for low‑volume rural Interstates and Expressways with two‑way average daily traffic less than 10,000 where the need for an incident‑management lane is low. [1] An asphalt shoulder is appropriate when the project uses an asphalt pavement, in particular Stone Matrix Asphalt (SMA) courses that are at least 1.5 inches deep; under those conditions the Safety Edge is applied as the standard edge treatment on the outside edge of both the pavement and its shoulder using the same material that constructs the roadway course or, if specified, the paved shoulders, and the Safety Edge is not required where a curb, sidewalk, or the face of a guiderail directly overlies the pavement edge. [2] For asphalt shoulders the density may be accepted by non‑movement or an optimum‑rolling pattern when the shoulder material is placed in small quantities not exceeding 500 tons in a continuous placement, when it is applied on unstable or non‑uniform bases, when the course depth is less than 1 inch (or weight ≤110 lb/yd² for scratch or leveling courses), or when the paved width is under 4 ft so that compaction equipment must bridge the area; in such cases the Inspector‑in‑Charge may treat shoulders as a non‑critical density application, while shoulders requiring critical density still require pavement cores per Section 413.3(j)4.a. [2] The Asphalt Shoulder construction described in Section 654—Paved Shoulders, Type 4 is intended for paved shoulders built alongside existing roadways where the existing shoulder material must be removed to the bottom of the proposed base course or to a directed depth and any guide rail is to remain undisturbed; the method calls for scarifying the shoulder to a maximum depth of 4 inches below the existing grade, shaping and compacting graded or aggregate‑base material to the bottom elevation of the proposed base course, excavating and backfilling with aggregate base course when required, and then applying an asphalt prime coat followed by an asphalt surface treatment. [2] The paved shoulder design in Section 656 permits use of an asphalt surface treatment, a slurry seal, or an asphalt tack coat applied according to the referenced sections and may include a backfill as required by Section 350.2; when a structural asphalt layer is specified, the Superpave mixture design for either a base course (Section 313.2) or a wearing course (Sections 410‑413) uses a PG 64S‑22 binder and is limited to traffic of less than 0.3 million ESALs unless otherwise directed. [2]

Design inputs and requirements

The pavement‑shoulder joint shall be treated in accordance with Publication 408 specifications by applying the joint‑sealing provisions of Sections 512 and 521; the joint must be cleaned, a sealant reservoir sized at 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch constructed, and sealed with rubberized/asphalt joint sealing material, and this cleaning and sealing work should be performed whether or not the asphalt shoulder will be reconstructed (section 4.3 JOINTS) [1]. For widened concrete slabs the slab shall not exceed 14 feet in width and must be striped at 12 feet, after which the remaining slab portion provides an effective shoulder when combined with a Type 1 asphalt shoulder; this configuration is limited to low‑volume rural Interstates and Expressways where two‑way average daily traffic is less than 10,000 and the need for an incident‑management lane is low, and design calculations must employ the Load Transfer Coefficient (J) specified for widened lanes with asphalt or RCC shoulders as listed in Table 8.2 (section 8.14 WIDENED LANES FOR CONCRETE PAVEMENT) [1]. The Safety Edge treatment shall be provided on the outside edge of asphalt pavements and shoulders as described in Section 413.3(h)1.e, requiring a minimum depth of 1.5 inches, prohibiting a total depth greater than 5 inches, and mandating that the finished face angle be 26 to 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope extension (section 413.3(h)1.e) [2]. The edge shall be constructed with the same material as the roadway course or, if specified, the paved shoulder, using an approved paver‑mounted device that extrudes a wedge‑shaped profile; it is not required where curbs, sidewalks, or guiderails intersect the pavement and shall not be placed on base, leveling, or scratch courses (section 413.3(h)1.e) [2]. The Safety Edge shall be verified each day after final compaction and may not be placed on organic material or where curbs, sidewalks, or guiderails directly overlie the edge (Section 419) [2]. The shoulder’s mixture and density shall satisfy the lot‑acceptance criteria of Section 419.3, which normally defines a lot size of 2,500 tons divided into five equal sublots (n = 5) unless operational conditions or project size dictate otherwise; for small quantities of ≤150 tons acceptance is based on certification and density acceptance using an optimum rolling pattern, and all compaction shall be performed as required by the specifications without delaying rolling adjacent to the Safety Edge (Section 419.3) [2]. The mat‑density acceptance criteria applied to other pavement courses shall also apply to asphalt shoulders under Section 413.3(j); the Inspector‑in‑Charge may approve a non‑critical shoulder by non‑movement or may use an optimum‑rolling pattern when density is not critical, but if density is deemed critical it must be verified with pavement cores in accordance with Section 413.3(j)4.a (Section 413.3(j)) [2]. The Inspector‑in‑Charge may limit application to mixtures placed in small quantities not exceeding 500 tons in a continuous placement or to courses less than 1 inch deep or equal to or less than 110 pounds per square yard, and any nuclear gauge used for density measurement shall be operated by a licensed operator and calibrated per ASTM D2950 (Section 413.3(j)) [2]. The construction of a paved shoulder of Type 4 is directed by Section 654.3, which requires removal of surplus material to the bottom of the proposed base course, scarifying the shoulder area to a maximum depth of 4 inches below the existing grade, and shaping and compacting to the bottom elevation of the proposed base course; aggregate‑base placement follows Section 350.3, and any backfill excavated below that elevation shall be compacted with rollers as prescribed in Section 108.05(c)3.a, (c)3.b, (c)3.e, or (c)4 (Section 654.3) [2]. The final surface treatment includes an asphalt prime coat per Section 461.3 and an asphalt surface treatment per Section 480.3 (Section 654.3) [2]. Paved shoulders of Type 6, Type 6‑F, Type 6‑S, or Type 6‑SP shall use the asphalt surface treatment required by Section 480.2, shoulder backfill when needed per Section 350.2, slurry seal as described in Section 482.2, and an asphalt tack coat referenced in Section 460.2 (Section 656) [2]. The asphalt base course shall be designed with a Superpave mixture following Section 313.2 using PG 64S‑22 binder for traffic less than 0.3 million ESALs or as directed, and the wearing course (SRL‑L) must meet the Superpave design criteria of Sections 413.2, 410.2, or 412.2 with PG 64S‑22 and the same <0.3 million ESALs limit (Section 413) [2]. Paved shoulders of Type 4 must comply with Section 350.2 for aggregate base and shoulder backfill, an asphalt prime coat in accordance with Section 461.2, and an asphalt surface treatment as specified in Section 480.2 (Sections 654–656) [2].

Design methods and criteria

In the Pavement Policy Manual, the Asphalt Shoulder joint cleaning and sealing procedure requires constructing a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir at each pavement‑shoulder joint and sealing it with rubberized/asphalt joint sealing material; this work must be performed during every CPR project whether or not the asphalt shoulder will be reconstructed. For widened concrete slabs that may be up to 14 feet wide and are identified as a Type 1 asphalt shoulder, the design procedure calls for using the load‑transfer coefficient (J) specified for “widened lane with asphalt or RCC shoulders” as given in Table 8.2, and this coefficient governs how traffic loads are transferred between the concrete slab and the adjacent asphalt shoulder. The Superpave Asphalt Mixture Design is employed for both the base and wearing courses of an asphalt shoulder; the base course shall use a PG 64S‑22 mix when traffic does not exceed 0.3 million ESALs (or as directed), and the wearing course, identified as SRL‑L, uses the same PG 64S‑22 specification with the same <0.3 million ESALs limit. The shoulder must also incorporate an asphalt surface treatment (Section 480.2), shoulder backfill if required (Section 350.2), a slurry seal (Section 482.2) and an asphalt tack coat (Section 460.2). Construction follows the same spreading and finishing rules as the lane, and the exposed outer edge is worked immediately after placement to eliminate sharp, ragged or open edges; when adjacent to curbs a 12‑inch wide sealant of hot asphalt or an approved emulsified tack coat is applied to form a watertight gutter. The standard edge treatment for shoulders is the Safety Edge, which must be built with the same mix as the pavement or the specified shoulder material, compacted to a depth of at least 1.5 inches but not more than 5 inches, and extruded in a wedge shape using an approved device that produces a final slope of 26 to 40 degrees measured from the extended cross‑slope; the angle is verified each day after compaction and paving must stop until corrective action restores compliance if the angle falls outside the 26–40° range. Acceptance of the placed mixture is performed on a lot‑by‑lot basis, where a normal lot size is 2,500 tons divided into five equal sublots; each sublot provides mixture acceptance and Gmm verification samples tested according to PTM No. 757/702 and AASHTO T 209 as modified in Bulletin 27. Density cores are required to be taken within one day after placement and evaluated against the percent compaction criteria of Table I, with extreme values analyzed at the 5% significance level per PTM No. 4; if density or asphalt content fails for two consecutive lots or three total lots, paving must stop until a corrective solution is approved. Compaction begins immediately after placement using steel‑wheel rollers kept moist and clean, with roller speed, pattern, size or vibratory settings adjusted as needed to meet the density acceptance requirements without causing flushing. For shoulders where density is critical, pavement cores taken within one day are used; however, the department may accept density by non‑movement when the shoulder consists of a scratch or leveling course less than 1 inch in depth or equal to or less than 110 pounds per square yard, or when the placed area is narrower than four feet. Density measurements are performed in the presence of the Representative and a certified asphalt field technician using an approved nuclear gauge according to PTM No. 402 or an approved electrical‑impedance gauge according to PTM No. 403; the bulk specific gravity of core samples is determined according to AASHTO T 209 as modified in Bulletin 27, and the results are compared to the daily theoretical maximum specific gravity values to calculate percent theoretical density per ASTM E29. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 8 (p. 165); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 270–301), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (p. 575)

In the Pavement Policy Manual, section 4.3 (JOINTS), the pavement‑shoulder joint for an asphalt shoulder must be cleaned and sealed during every CPR project, and this work requires constructing a sealant reservoir that is 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch and sealing the joint with rubberized/asphalt joint sealing material; the requirement applies whether or not the asphalt shoulder will be reconstructed. The same manual, section 8.14 (WIDENED LANES FOR CONCRETE PAVEMENT), permits a widened concrete slab to be used together with an asphalt shoulder only when the slab does not exceed 14 feet in width and is striped at 12 feet, leaving the remaining portion to provide an effective shoulder when combined with a Type 1 asphalt shoulder; this arrangement is limited to low‑volume rural Interstates or Expressways that have two‑way ADTs less than 10,000 and where incident‑management lane demand is low, and design shall use the Load Transfer Coefficient (J) for “widened lane with asphalt … shoulders” from Table 8.2. The Department of Transportation specifications require that an asphalt shoulder incorporate a Safety Edge as the standard edge treatment on the outside edge of the pavement. The Safety Edge shall be provided for both wearing and binder courses when the course depth is at least 1.5 inches, and it shall not be placed where the total depth exceeds 5 inches; the finished wedge angle must fall between 26 and 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope extended. The Safety Edge may be omitted where a curb, sidewalk or guiderail directly overhangs the edge, and it is not required for base, leveling or scratch courses. The Safety Edge shall be built with the same material as the roadway course (or the specified shoulder material) using an approved paver device, and its final shape shall be verified after compaction. For shoulders constructed with Stone Matrix Asphalt, the Safety Edge requirement follows the same depth condition of 1.5 inches or greater, and compaction of the shoulder shall follow the applicable specifications without delaying rolling of the adjacent mat. The paved shoulder shall be built from the materials identified in Section 656, including an asphalt surface treatment, any required backfill, slurry seal and tack coat. Both the base course and the wearing course shall employ a Superpave mixture design as described in Sections 313.2, 413.2, 410.2 or 412.2, using a performance grade of PG 64S‑22 for traffic volumes less than 0.3 million ESALs unless directed otherwise. When existing shoulder material is scarified, the depth may not exceed 4 inches below the existing grade. All backfilled aggregate base material shall be shaped and compacted to the bottom elevation of the proposed base course with the rollers listed in Section 108.05; compaction is considered satisfactory only when the material does not move under the compaction equipment, and any surplus material must be removed to the bottom of the proposed base course or to a directed depth before these operations. Mat‑density acceptance for asphalt shoulders follows the rules in Section 413.3(j). For non‑critical shoulders the Inspector‑in‑Charge may approve density by non‑movement when the work consists of scratch or leveling courses less than 1‑inch deep or equal to or less than 110 lb/yd², when the paved area is less than 4 ft wide (or narrow enough to cause bridging), and when the quantity placed does not exceed 500 tons in a continuous placement. For shoulders where density is critical the Department requires acceptance by pavement cores as specified in Section 413.3(j)4.a; the Representative may require verification of target density with an optimum‑rolling pattern and may suspend paving if the pattern is not followed. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 8 (p. 165); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 270–301), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 574–575)

Structural section and dimensions

The Pavement Policy Manual specifies that for pavement/shoulder joint sealing a sealant reservoir must be constructed that is 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch, and this work shall be performed whether or not the asphalt shoulder will be reconstructed; no other dimensions for pavement, base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab, edge‑support, or reinforcement are provided. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require that an asphalt shoulder include a safety‑edge treatment built with the same material as the roadway course (or the paved shoulder if specified) and having a depth of at least 1.5 inches but not exceeding a total depth of 5 inches; after compaction the finished safety edge must present an angle measured from the pavement cross‑slope extended of between 26° and 40°. The same specifications state that scarifying existing shoulder areas shall be limited to a maximum depth of 4 inches below the existing grade, and that aggregate base, backfill, and asphalt treatments are to be placed to the bottom elevation of the proposed base course or to depths indicated in referenced sections, but no specific numeric dimensions are prescribed for those layers. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 270–301), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (p. 574)

When an asphalt shoulder course exceeds six inches in compacted depth, it shall be built in two or more layers of approximately equal depth, with each layer no less than three inches and no more than six inches thick; the safety edge that treats the outer shoulder must have a depth of at least 1.5 inches but may not exceed five inches total, and its finished face must form an angle of 26 to 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope extension. Automatic transition is permitted at intersections, driveways, guiderail sections, and other obstructions, while near flush curbs or gutters the wearing course is placed uniformly higher and sealed with hot (or approved emulsified) asphalt in a minimum twelve‑inch width to create an asphalt gutter that ties the shoulder to the adjoining structure. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 270–276)

Materials and mixture design

The Asphalt Shoulder shall be built with the same asphalt material that is used for the adjacent roadway course unless a different paved‑shoulder mix is specifically called out (Section 419). When the shoulder is an SMA course, a Safety Edge must be provided; its depth shall be 1.5 inches or greater and its final face angle shall fall between 26° and 40° measured from the extended pavement cross‑slope (Section 419). The mixture placed on the shoulder must satisfy the standard SMA acceptance criteria – asphalt content and the percent passing a No. 200 (75 µm) sieve must meet the tolerances set out in Table I, and the compacted density must be within the limits of Table F with Gmm verification values agreeing with the contractor’s daily Gmm to within ±0.030 (Section 419). Production is normally organized into lots of 2,500 tons divided into five equal sublots, unless operational conditions or project size dictate otherwise (Section 419). The Asphalt Shoulder mix may be accepted by non‑movement when it is a scratch or leveling course less than 1 inch in depth or equal to or less than 110 pounds per square yard, or when the paved area is less than 4 feet wide or narrow enough to cause bridging of the equipment; the Inspector‑in‑Charge also may accept density for shoulders that are not critical by non‑movement if the material is placed in small quantities not exceeding 500 tons in a continuous placement, or if the mixture is placed on an unstable or non‑uniform base (Section 413). When density is critical for a shoulder, acceptance shall be by pavement cores as specified in Section 413.3(j)4.a, and an optimum‑rolling pattern may be used for shoulders where density is not critical under the same quantity and base conditions (Section 413). The asphalt shoulder must be built using the materials identified in Section 656.2, which include an Asphalt Surface Treatment (per Section 480.2), any required Shoulder Backfill (per Section 350.2), a Slurry Seal (per Section 482.2) and an Asphalt Tack Coat (per Section 460.2) (Section 656). For the structural layers the design shall follow Superpave asphalt mixture specifications: the base course is to be designed according to Section 313.2 using a PG 64S‑22 binder for traffic of less than 0.3 million ESALs unless otherwise indicated, and the wearing course (SRL‑L) must meet the same PG 64S‑22 binder and <0.3 million ESALs criteria as prescribed in Sections 413.2, 410.2 or 412.2 (Section 656). The asphalt shoulder specification requires that four material categories be identified: an aggregate base as defined in Section 350.2, an asphalt prime coat per Section 461.2, an asphalt surface treatment according to Section 480.2, and a shoulder backfill material also referenced in Section 350.2; each of these items must be described by the appropriate reference section, which contains the detailed properties and mixture proportions for that material (Section 654). Structural details and interfaces

During every concrete pavement rehabilitation (CPR) project, the joint between the concrete slab and its asphalt shoulder must be cleaned and sealed; this operation includes constructing a sealant reservoir measuring 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch and applying rubberized/asphalt joint sealing material, and it is required whether or not the shoulder itself will be rebuilt [1]. For the design of a widened concrete slab that incorporates an asphalt shoulder, the applicable load‑transfer coefficient (J) is taken from Table 8.2 for a “widened lane with asphalt or RCC shoulders,” and no extra joints, reinforcement, transitions, or connections are called for in this situation [1]. The exterior edge of an asphalt shoulder shall be provided with a Safety Edge, which uses the same asphalt mix as the adjacent roadway course (or the specified shoulder mix) and must have a depth of at least 1.5 inches but not exceed 5 inches; SMA courses require a minimum depth of 1.5 inches [2]. An approved extrusion device attached to the paver is used to confine the material at the end gate and extrude it in a wedge shape so that, after compaction, the edge angle lies between 26° and 40° measured from the extension of the pavement cross‑slope [2]. Where the shoulder terminates against flush curbs, gutters, or other abutting structures, the wearing course is placed uniformly higher and sealed with hot‑asphalt material over a minimum width of 12 inches to form a watertight joint commonly referred to as an asphalt gutter [2]. The Safety Edge is omitted where a curb, sidewalk, or guiderail directly overlies the edge, but it must be provided with automatic transitions at intersections, driveways, guiderail sections, and other obstructions [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 8 (p. 165); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 270–301)

When an asphalt shoulder abuts a concrete pavement, the interface is classified as a pavement‑shoulder joint that must be cleaned and sealed during every CPR project, and this requirement applies whether or not the shoulder will be reconstructed; the prescribed method includes constructing a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir at the joint and filling it with rubberized/asphalt joint sealing material [1]. For the outer edge of an asphalt shoulder, the design calls for a Safety Edge constructed from the same material as the roadway or the specified shoulder; the edge must have a depth of at least 1.5 inches but shall not exceed a total depth of 5 inches, and after compaction the face of the edge must form an angle between 26 and 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope extension [2]. Where the shoulder terminates against flush curbs, gutters, or other abutting structures, the wearing course is to be placed slightly higher so that after compaction the surface sits above the edge of the structure, and a hot (or approved emulsified) asphalt sealant shall be applied for a minimum width of 12 inches from the curb to create a watertight joint [2]. For Stone Matrix Asphalt (SMA) shoulders, the same Safety Edge concept is required when the mixture depth reaches at least 1.5 inches; the edge is extruded in a wedge shape so that after final compaction its slope measures between 26 and 40 degrees from the extended pavement cross‑slope, and it must not be installed where a curb, sidewalk or guiderail face directly overlies the edge [2]. The Safety Edge construction shall occur immediately after the adjacent mat is rolled, and the angle of the edge must be verified each day before paving resumes; automatic transition of the edge is permitted at intersections, driveways, guiderail sections, and other obstructions [2]. At the interface between the asphalt shoulder and its underlying layers, surplus material shall be removed down to the bottom of the proposed base course (or to a directed depth), after which the existing shoulder may be scarified up to four inches below the existing grade (or as directed); the area is then shaped and compacted to the bottom elevation of the proposed base course, aggregate base‑course material placed where needed, and compacted with rollers in accordance with Section 108.05 until the material does not move under the equipment [2]. Following preparation of the underlying layers, an asphalt prime coat as specified in Section 461.3 and an asphalt surface treatment as specified in Section 480.3 are applied as the final layers of the shoulder construction [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

For a concrete pavement rehabilitation (CPR) project the asphalt shoulder can be placed only after the existing drainage system is functional and the subgrade is stable, which are prerequisites for any shoulder that abuts concrete pavement [1]. The design must prevent future infiltration of water, chlorides, and incompressible materials into the pavement structure; therefore the pavement‑shoulder joint is to be cleaned and sealed by constructing a 0.75‑inch by 0.75‑inch sealant reservoir and applying rubberized/asphalt joint sealing material [1]. When the shoulder abuts a flush curb, gutter or similar structure, the wearing‑course mix shall be placed uniformly higher so that after compaction the finished surface sits slightly above the edge of the adjoining element; the adjacent surface must then be cleaned and sealed to form an asphalt gutter using hot (or permitted emulsified) asphalt applied at a minimum width of 12 inches from the curb, with the application rate controlled so that residual material completely fills voids and creates a watertight joint, thereby providing drainage and moisture protection for the shoulder [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 270–276)

The asphalt shoulder joint with adjacent concrete pavement must be cleaned and sealed in every CPR project, which entails constructing a sealant reservoir measuring 0.75 inch by 0.75 inch and applying rubberized/asphalt joint sealing material; this work is required even when the shoulder itself is not being reconstructed [1]. The outside edge of an asphalt shoulder shall incorporate the standard Safety Edge, using the same material as the roadway course or the specified paved‑shoulder mix for both wearing and binder layers [2]. The Safety Edge depth shall be at least 1.5 inches but shall not exceed a total depth of 5 inches, and its face must be shaped to an angle between 26 degrees and 40 degrees measured from the extension of the pavement cross‑slope [2]. Immediately after placement the exposed outer edges must be worked to eliminate sharp, ragged or open edges, remove any unfinished appearance, and reduce edge breakdown, with the wedge shape being formed by an approved paver device and compacted without delay [2]. The Safety Edge shall not be placed where a curb, sidewalk or guiderail face lies directly over the edge, nor on organic material, although automatic transition is permitted at intersections, driveways, guiderail sections and other obstructions [2]. Prior to placement the shoulder must be prepared by removing surplus material down to the bottom of the proposed base course; if an existing guide rail is retained it shall be excavated without disturbing that rail, while any damaged rail shall be repaired as directed [2]. At locations identified in the contract the existing shoulder may be scarified to a maximum depth of 4 inches below the existing grade, then shaped and compacted to the bottom elevation of the proposed base course, with low spots filled using graded material or aggregate base as needed [2]. Where excavation extends below that elevation, the resulting void shall be backfilled, shaped and compacted with aggregate‑base course material using rollers specified in Section 108.05, and the work is considered satisfactory when the material does not move under the compaction equipment [2]. Finally an asphalt prime coat and a surface treatment shall be applied as required by Sections 461.3 and 480.3 respectively [2].

Verification and expected performance

After the asphalt shoulder is placed, a certified field technician verifies that the Safety Edge has been constructed with the same material as the roadway (or the designated shoulder mix for SMA courses) to a depth of at least 1.5 inches and not exceeding 5 inches total; the final cross‑slope angle of the Safety Edge is measured after compaction and shall fall between 26° and 40° measured from the pavement cross‑slope extended, with an angle check performed at the beginning of each day’s paving and paving stopped if the angle is out of range until corrective action is taken. The shoulder surface is then compacted in accordance with the project specifications, and compaction is considered satisfactory only when the material does not move under the rollers; rolling adjacent to the Safety Edge shall not be delayed. For shoulders where density is non‑critical, the Inspector‑in‑Charge may accept density by observing non‑movement of the placed mixture or by an optimum‑rolling pattern measured with a calibrated nuclear or electrical impedance gauge in the presence of the Representative and the contractor’s certified field technician; for shoulders where density is critical, pavement cores are extracted within one day of placement and tested for bulk specific gravity and bulk density, and the results must meet the prescribed percent of theoretical maximum density, otherwise paving is stopped until a corrective solution approved by the Representative is implemented. In addition, all surplus material must be removed to the bottom of the proposed base course or to the directed depth, any required scarifying shall not exceed four inches below existing grade (or the directed depth), and the shaped and backfilled aggregate base is compacted with rollers as prescribed; guide rails shall be repaired as directed, excess or unsuitable material shall be disposed of according to project instructions, and the prime coat and surface treatment are applied in accordance with Sections 461.3 and 480.3. The Asphalt Shoulder is expected to maintain a properly formed Safety Edge with a depth of at least 1.5 inches but not exceeding 5 inches and an angle between 26 and 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope extended; these geometric criteria are verified by measuring the edge angle at the start of each paving day and after final compaction. Distress resistance is demonstrated by the absence of pattern segregation, which is confirmed when the average macrotexture depth difference between segregated and non‑segregated areas does not exceed 0.024 inch as measured with PTM No. 751, and by preventing flushing, where a measured average texture depth of 0.006 inch or less would indicate a defect.
