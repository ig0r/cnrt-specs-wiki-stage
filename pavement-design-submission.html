Pavement Design Submission

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Pavement Design Submission is a topic under Pavement Design.

Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

A Pavement Design Submission is a formal, documented package that links the engineering design, life‑cycle cost analysis (LCCA), and condition‑based information required before any pavement type may be approved [1][2]. It presents LCCA results—requiring at least a 10 % agency‑only cost difference or a 20 % total cost difference including user delay costs—along with DARWin structural calculations, traffic data, geotechnical reports, and existing pavement condition data so that the District Pavement Type Selection Committee, HDTD Chief, BODD, or FHWA can evaluate and formally approve the design within the prescribed timing windows [1][2]. The submission must include a cover letter, Form D‑4332, typical section drawing, location map, DARWin printouts, traffic and geotechnical documentation, LCCA printouts, any special provisions such as a STAMPP Treatment Summary for Federal projects, and any post‑approval changes must be resubmitted to the original approving authority [1][2].

Purpose and decision context

According to Publication 242, the Pavement Design Submission is intended to support the decision to select a project’s pavement type by providing life‑cycle cost analysis results, and when a difference of 10 % or more in life‑cycle costs excluding user delay costs, or a difference of 20 % or more including user delay costs, is identified, the District Pavement Type Selection Committee may use engineering judgment and regional factors to justify the choice; the LCCA, meeting minutes and conclusions shall be recorded in the design package submitted for HDTD approval. The same submission also supports the decision to approve a project’s pavement design and its associated life‑cycle cost analysis, because after alternate designs are completed the District shall submit the designs and LCCA to BODD for review, the HDTD Chief provides preliminary approval, a three‑week comment period follows, and final approval of the design and LCCA is granted by the HDTD Chief or forwarded to FHWA for Federal Oversight. For moderate‑complexity projects the submission must enable the decision that pavement design approval be achieved within 1 year after Design Field View (or environmental approval) but at least six months before the Final Design Office Meeting, while most complex projects have a two‑year window with the same six‑month minimum; these timing requirements guide the authority’s decision to proceed with final design. Completed forms shall be submitted to the District Executive for approval, and upon DE approval the pavement design package shall be sent to BODD; this chain of submissions supports the District Executive’s decision to authorize project letting and construction. Any subsequent design changes must be resubmitted to the original approving authority—FHWA, BODD, or the District—and that authority will evaluate the design for concurrence before work orders can proceed, thereby supporting its decision on whether to accept the change. For PennDOT Oversight projects requiring an LCCA, certified PME/PMs will recommend approval using Form D‑4332 based on sound engineering principles and conformance to Publication 242, and the DE’s approval is final, which supports the DE’s decision to approve the pavement design. The submission also supplies the Chief Geotechnical Engineer with the full evaluation of laboratory CBR results, the selected resilient‑modulus and any special subgrade improvement provisions so that a decision can be made to approve CBR values greater than 10 and to verify that no CBR value greater than 20 is used in the Heekelom and Klomp correlation. Under the specifications for full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, the Pavement Design Submission process is used to enable the Representative to decide whether the contractor’s design documents—shop drawings, working drawings and the manufacturer’s quality‑control plan—are acceptable before fabrication proceeds; the submission must be provided at least 21 calendar days before the pre‑production meeting, after which the Representative has 21 calendar days to review and issue a notice of acceptance or rejection. Additionally, concrete mix designs must be delivered to the Representative at least 28 calendar days before the commencement of precast slab production so that the Representative can decide on their acceptance prior to fabrication. [1][2]

The Pavement Design Submission process exists to meet the need for a controlled, documented pathway that links design packages to the engineering, economic and condition‑based information required before any pavement type may be approved. It provides the mechanism by which a life‑cycle cost analysis and its supporting documentation are formally presented for review so that reviewers can verify that an alternative meets the defined thresholds—at least a 10 % difference in agency‑only life‑cycle costs or a 20 % difference when user delay costs are included—and thereby prevent unapproved changes after approval. The process also ensures that alternate pavement designs satisfy PennDOT’s engineering and life‑cycle cost requirements, that they are “equivalent,” meaning they provide comparable levels of service and performance over the same analysis period. All required data—traffic information, geotechnical reports, subgrade CBR values, resilient modulus, International Roughness Index, friction data, existing pavement condition and maintenance history—must be included in the submission. By requiring the inclusion of the STAMPP Treatment Summary with each Form D‑4332 for Federal Oversight projects, the process anchors the design package to the condition‑based treatment information generated by PennDOT’s STAMPP program, ensuring that designs are consistent with observed pavement distresses and meet federal oversight requirements for informed rehabilitation planning. The submission must be clear to facilitate review, retain an approved copy of Form D‑4332 for at least seven years, and any deviations from the manual must be submitted as a waiver, thereby addressing regulatory compliance and providing justification for the proposed work. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete slab systems the need is to prevent non‑conforming slabs, coordinate production schedules, and guarantee safe and durable service; this is addressed by requiring mix designs that demonstrate the minimum compressive and flexural strengths, shop and working drawings that show dimensions within the tolerances listed in Table A, and a 21‑calendar‑day review period during which the Representative must accept or reject the package. For asphalt concrete pavements covered by the 60‑month warranty the need is to guarantee that the pavement will satisfy the warranty performance criteria; this is addressed by requiring the contractor to select appropriate asphalt, aggregate and filler materials, develop a Superpave volumetric mix design submitted on the current TR‑448A form at least 7 calendar days before use, include a quality‑control plan submitted at project start, and provide certified test results within 48 hours of sampling, with the submission linked to warranty bonds equal to 50 % of contract value for the 60‑month period. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

Pavement Design Submission is appropriate for any new‑construction, reconstruction, or rehabilitation project that meets the length thresholds of more than 500 continuous feet on divided roadways, more than 1 000 continuous feet on undivided roadways, or bridge approaches where the total paving of both sides exceeds 1 000 feet, as required by the Pavement Policy Manual. [1] The process also applies to any PennDOT‑Oversight or Federal‑Oversight project, including those that require a life‑cycle cost analysis, and to projects where an alternate pavement type bidding is considered when a LCCA is not required, provided the designs satisfy the same scope and performance criteria. [1] Short pavement‑section projects that do not meet the length thresholds are required to submit a design only when directed by the District Pavement Management Engineer. [1] Pavement preservation projects generally do not need a design analysis unless they involve geometric or bridge issues, in which case a waiver to BODD is required before any submission; when a preservation project seeks Pavement Type Approval, a Pavement Design Submission must be prepared after the scoping field view documentation is completed and must include existing pavement data, International Roughness Index and friction data, Falling Weight Deflectometer results where appropriate, geotechnical data, Form D‑4332, and any unique material provisions. [1] Pervious pavement designs for trails and parking lots shall follow Appendix K and be submitted to the District PME for review and approval. [1] For projects that employ full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs as defined in Section 529 of Publication 408/2026, Pavement Design Submission includes shop drawings, working drawings, and the manufacturer’s quality‑control plan delivered at least twenty‑one calendar days before the pre‑production meeting; a precast pavement planning meeting is required before any drawing preparation or slab fabrication, a pre‑fabrication meeting must occur at least fourteen calendar days prior to the start of slab production, and a pre‑placement meeting is held ten to fourteen calendar days before installation begins. [2] Asphalt‑concrete pavement projects that are covered by the sixty‑month warranty provision of Section 496 also require Pavement Design Submission; a mix design prepared on the current TR‑448A form must be submitted at least seven calendar days before the material is used, and a quality‑control plan as specified in Section 106 must be submitted at the start of the project before any work begins. [2] A preliminary pavement design may be submitted before the Design Field View approval so that recommended changes can be incorporated without adversely affecting the schedule, and the complete pavement design is reviewed and approved in final design; the resulting package must be sent to the District Executive for approval before being forwarded to the Bureau of Design and Delivery. [1] A single pavement design analysis and type determination may be used for two or more adjacent projects on the same route when total eighteen‑kip ESALs, effective modulus of subgrade reaction or roadbed soil resilient modulus, initial and terminal serviceability, reliability level, estimated contract unit costs of the pavement materials, and existing pavement section (for rehabilitation) remain substantially unchanged. [1] For PennDOT Oversight projects whose mainline pavement surface area is less than thirty thousand square yards, a formal type‑determination is not required but sufficient justification for type selection and proposed work must be documented in the project file. [1] Pavement designs for Federal Oversight projects—including preservation work—must obtain FHWA approval unless FHWA waives the requirement based on risk. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 77–79), Ch. 6 (pp. 134–139), Ch. 12 (p. 206); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 351–352), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

A Pavement Design Submission is not required for short‑section projects that fall below the length thresholds that trigger a DARWin analysis—specifically, when the pavement length is less than 500 continuous feet on divided roadways or less than 1,000 continuous feet on undivided roadways—and where the proposed pavement can be matched to the existing structure while meeting the minimum thickness tables; in those cases the District Executive’s approval recorded on the project file suffices. If the existing composite pavement far exceeds the minimum thicknesses or contains elements that are not reasonable to replicate, a DARWin analysis may be directed by the District Pavement Management Engineer as an alternative approach. When the characteristics that define a pavement design differ between adjacent sections—total 18‑kip ESALs, subgrade reaction or resilient modulus, initial and terminal serviceability, reliability level, estimated unit costs of materials, or the existing pavement section—a single Pavement Design Submission is not appropriate; each project must be submitted separately to reflect those changes. Any design alteration after the original submission shall be resubmitted to the approving authority rather than relying on the initial package. A full Pavement Design Submission is also not required for Pavement Preservation projects because the preservation guidelines replace a design analysis, so the work proceeds under the preservation program instead of a traditional submittal; similarly, PennDOT Oversight projects that involve less than 30,000 square yards of mainline pavement surface can forego the type‑determination procedure and rely on documented engineering justification while still using a certification process for review. When only a STAMPP Treatment recommendation is available it is not appropriate to use that treatment as the basis for a Pavement Design Submission because the program’s recommended treatments are intended for maintenance analysis and are normally less than the design strategy required for contract rehabilitation; in those situations a full rehabilitation design—such as an asphalt overlay developed through a detailed design project—should be prepared instead of relying on the STAMPP treatment summary. Information needs

The cover letter that accompanies a Pavement Design Submission must identify the MPMS project number, county, state route and segment, indicate oversight status, complexity, the design field view or environmental approval date, anticipated schedule dates, the estimated project cost, pavement length and total length, and note any special circumstances or exceptions [1]. Site information required includes subgrade composition and condition, adjacent pavement types and performance, local material availability, right‑of‑way constraints, and any maintenance or traffic protection impacts; a detailed soils report is mandatory for all reconstruction projects as outlined in Section 2.2, and the submission must contain a CBR summary table together with all Falling Weight Deflectometer data, soils testing results and frost‑heave probability signed by the district geotechnical engineer [1]. Condition data also comprise STAMPP condition data obtained from the Roadway Management System, with documentation of where and how those input values were sourced, and may be supplemented by a field view performed by the District PME/PM to verify or refine existing‑condition variables before they are entered into the Condition Survey Method [1]. The submission must further include a complete set of existing pavement data that captures all maintenance surface treatments, measured International Roughness Index and friction values, and any recorded distresses for asphalt and jointed concrete surfaces as defined by Pennsylvania’s Location Reference System [1]. Traffic information required consists of a summary table of average daily traffic and truck percentage for each design, the source and date of those data, and the total 18‑kip ESALs representing traffic loading; additional details such as the full traffic breakdowns must be provided [1]. The cost component must present the current discount rate, Asphalt Adjustment Multiplier, quantity estimates for each alternative based on the actual typical cross section (wearing, binder, base, subbase), and unit costs for pavement‑related items, earthwork quantities, resurfacing thicknesses, shoulder construction, drainage or utility relocations; Life‑Cycle Cost Analysis printouts must show quantity and unit costs together with supporting documentation, and the LCCA shall account for shoulder construction and maintenance [1]. For life‑cycle cost comparison a difference of 10 % or more is required when only agency costs are considered, while a difference of 20 % or more is required when user delay costs are included [1]. Subgrade strength must be expressed by the effective modulus of subgrade reaction and/or roadbed soil resilient modulus, and any CBR value greater than 10 shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer; no CBR value greater than 20 may be used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation [1]. Design inputs also require initial and terminal serviceability values, a reliability level, estimated contract unit costs of pavement materials, and, when an asphalt wearing course is specified, the appropriate Skid Resistance Level designation on Form D‑4332; any existing pavement section to be rehabilitated must be described [1]. The Pavement Design Submission must contain site‑specific condition data such as the ambient average daily temperature and exposure conditions used in the mix design for thermal expansion, the locations of all cores to be taken from existing pavement, and surveyed elevations and dimensions for slab layout and joint placement [2]. Detailed shop and working drawings together with the manufacturer’s quality‑control plan must be submitted at least 21 calendar days before the pre‑production meeting, and concrete mix designs with trial batch test data must be provided at least 28 days before production [2]. A comprehensive installation plan that specifies equipment size, load capacity, delivery routes, traffic‑protection measures and the grout strength required before opening to traffic is also required [2]. Stakeholder input is captured through a series of mandated meetings—planning, pre‑fabrication and pre‑placement—where the Representative, inspection personnel, CQAD and SMS representatives, project management staff, surveyors, grout installers, sawcutters and the technical representative of the precast system all review and approve the submitted information [2].

The pavement design submission must be a complete package that contains every item enumerated in Section 6.1.C, including the cover letter, Form D‑4332, typical section drawing, location map, full DARWin printouts, all structural calculations, the traffic summary with its source and date, the geotechnical report signed by the District Geotechnical Engineer, LCCA printouts, and any special provisions, so that no required document is omitted. All quantitative inputs must be current; for example the traffic data must include its source and date, and if the design year differs by more than three years a new design and LCCA shall be performed. Accuracy is ensured by providing the original DARWin screens, signed geotechnical data, and complete calculations supporting the design. The submission must also include a full documented evaluation of all laboratory testing results, the selected CBR value and the resulting resilient modulus, and it must incorporate any required special provisions for subgrade improvement. If considerable uncertainty exists regarding the validity or representativeness of a CBR result, additional laboratory or field testing must be performed so that only accurate, representative values are used, and outlier values that do not appear to be representative of the soil type should be disregarded. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, concrete mix designs with trial batch test data have to be submitted to the Representative at least twenty‑eight days before precast slab production begins. Shop drawings, working drawings and the manufacturer’s quality‑control plan must reach the Representative no later than twenty‑one calendar days prior to the pre‑production meeting, after which the Representative has twenty‑one days to review them. The submission is required to be complete, containing detailed working drawings that show core locations, slab layout, grout reservoir placement, installation plans and all items listed in Section 105.02(c), together with shop drawings specifying reinforcement, dowel and tie‑bar details, sacrificial concrete thickness, prestressing information and material data. The survey layout of proposed longitudinal joints must be accurate before any sawcutting is performed and every dimension must fall within the tolerances shown in Table A. For asphalt concrete pavement covered by the 60‑month warranty, the Pavement Design Submission must use the most current TR‑448A form, be signed by a Certified Asphalt Level 2 Plant Technician, and must reach the DME/DMM at least seven calendar days before the material is used. The submission must be complete by including for each mix design all required data—dates, project identifiers, producer details, volumetric properties, test results and the designer’s signature—so that the information on component materials is representative of the actual materials to be placed. Any change in source or adjustment during construction requires a written notification and a revised JMF. [1][2]

Alternatives

Under the life‑cycle cost analysis guidelines (section 3.2), a Pavement Design Submission should compare alternative pavement designs evaluated through life‑cycle cost analysis; a difference of at least 10 % in agency‑only costs or a difference of at least 20 % when user delay costs are included is sufficient to select a pavement type, and the District Pavement Type Selection Committee may justify a single choice based on regional factors such as subgrade condition, local materials, and right‑of‑way constraints. [1] When Alternate Pavement Type Bidding is used (section 3.6), the district must compare at least three equivalent alternatives—a concrete pavement paired with an asphalt‑treated permeable base course, a concrete pavement paired with a cement‑treated permeable base course, and a full Superpave asphalt system—each presented with complete plans, typical sections, item numbers, quantities, and traffic control plans while meeting RC standards, DM‑2, and Publication 242, and the comparison relies on life‑cycle cost analysis using the appropriate C‑Factor added to the construction bid amount. [1] For PennDOT Oversight projects (section 6.1), the feasible options that should be compared are a rigid pavement design alternative and a flexible pavement design alternative; for Federal Oversight projects the alternatives will be defined during the Scoping Field View. [1] When deciding whether a single pavement design analysis can serve two or more adjacent projects (section 6.1 D. Procedures), the options that must be compared include total 18‑kip ESALs, effective modulus of subgrade reaction and/or roadbed soil resilient modulus, initial and terminal serviceability, reliability level, estimated contract unit costs of the pavement materials, and the existing pavement section for rehabilitation. [1] In preparing a Pavement Design Submission (section 6.1 Pavement Type Definitions), the alternatives to evaluate are reconstruction, rehabilitation, and pavement preservation; reconstruction involves complete removal and replacement of the existing structure, rehabilitation adds structural overlays or partial recycling, and preservation uses low‑intensity treatments such as micro‑surfacing or thin bonded concrete. [1] The submission may also consider baseline subgrade condition together with strengthening techniques such as cement or lime stabilization (Structural Coefficient = 0.12), rock capping modeled with CBR = 20, and geogrids or geotextiles for reinforcement, and it may evaluate whether a single uniform pavement design is appropriate or divide the project into a limited number of subsections each having its own pavement design based on differing soil types and CBR values. [1] For an AC overlay of jointed plain concrete pavement (section 10.4), the feasible options to compare are the Condition Survey Method and the Remaining Life Method, and the guidance directs the designer to use the Condition Survey Method, which requires obtaining STAMPP condition data from the Roadway Management System. Decision criteria

In the Pavement Policy Manual, the decision to prepare a Pavement Design Submission is principally governed by cost considerations. A difference of at least ten percent in agency‑only life‑cycle costs is deemed sufficient to select the lower‑cost pavement type, and when user delay costs are incorporated a difference of twenty percent in total life‑cycle costs justifies an alternative design [1]. The manual further requires that any alternate pavement be “equivalent,” meaning it must provide comparable levels of service and performance over the same analysis period, reinforcing that cost savings may not come at the expense of performance equivalence [1]. Schedule timing also influences the submission decision: for moderate‑complexity projects approval must be obtained within one year after Design Field View (or environmental) approval but no later than six months before the Final Design Office Meeting, while the most complex projects have a two‑year window with the same six‑month pre‑meeting requirement [1]. The submission package must contain life‑cycle cost analysis printouts and supporting unit‑cost calculations, underscoring that cost efficiency is an essential control factor [1]. Additionally, engineering parameters such as total 18‑kip ESALs, the effective modulus of subgrade reaction or roadbed soil resilient modulus, initial and terminal serviceability values, reliability level, estimated contract unit costs of pavement materials, and the condition of any existing pavement section for rehabilitation must remain substantially unchanged when a single design is applied to adjacent projects; these parameters are used to determine whether a separate submission is required [1]. The manual does not identify safety, durability, sustainability, accessibility or broader public impact as primary controls in this context [1]. Conversely, the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that the Pavement Design Submission must be driven primarily by the need to satisfy the warranty performance criteria. The mix design and material selections are required to ensure that the asphalt concrete pavement meets those performance standards, and the submission does not relieve the contractor from meeting the specified construction end‑result or warranty performance criteria [2]. While the specifications also impose a schedule requirement—mix designs must be submitted at least seven calendar days before use and work may not begin until the quality‑control plan and joint mix formula are submitted—the controlling factor for the decision remains the performance guarantee stipulated in the warranty [2].

When competing alternatives are evaluated in a Pavement Design Submission, the primary weighting is placed on life‑cycle cost differentials: an alternative that demonstrates at least 10 % lower agency‑only LCC or at least 20 % lower total LCC including user delay costs shall be deemed sufficient to select its pavement type. If neither of those percentage thresholds is achieved, the District Pavement Type Selection Committee may provide justification after weighing regional factors such as engineering judgment, adjacent pavement performance, subgrade condition, local materials, right‑of‑way constraints and traffic or utility impacts, with engineering judgment required to be fact‑based and extraordinary. In parallel, competing criteria related to subgrade soils are prioritized by first applying the most controlling subgrade condition; when low CBR values are widespread across the project, the design shall select the soil type with the lowest CBR so that the pavement section is adequate for all areas, whereas isolated low‑CBR zones may be improved to achieve CBR values representative of the overall project. Analysis methods and tools

In the Pavement Design Submission process, the primary evaluation method is a life‑cycle cost analysis (LCCA) performed according to Chapter 3 Section 3.2; a difference of 10% or more in life‑cycle costs, excluding user delay costs, is sufficient to select the preferred pavement type, and a difference of 20% or more when user delay costs are included also suffices, and these analyses must be recorded for HDTD approval. For Alternate Pavement Type Bidding, evaluation relies on an LCCA that incorporates a C‑Factor representing the present worth of future maintenance costs, and the low bid is calculated by adding this C‑Factor to the construction bid amount; no other tools such as condition ratings, decision trees, risk assessment, or benefit‑cost analysis are mentioned in that section. The design analysis must be performed with AASHTOWare® DARWin® 3.01 software, and both DARWin output printouts and LCCA printouts with supporting cost calculations are required components of the submission package; a preliminary design must include sufficient data to generate an LCCA for review, and any change in the project’s design year of more than 3 years triggers a new design and accompanying LCCA. The DARWin program also evaluates the design against quantitative criteria such as total 18‑kip ESALs, effective subgrade reaction or resilient modulus, initial and terminal serviceability, reliability level, estimated unit costs of pavement materials, and the condition of any existing pavement section, with supporting project cost data supplied to the Pavement Management Engineer/Manager for inclusion in the evaluation package; this section does not mention condition‑rating scales, decision trees, life‑cycle cost analysis, risk assessment, or benefit‑cost analysis as part of the submission process. For Federal Oversight projects, approval may be based on a risk assessment and FHWA can waive full review if the project is judged low‑risk; PennDOT Oversight designs must include an LCCA before BODD review. When a subgrade‑strengthening option is proposed for pavement quantities of at least 25,000 SY on Traffic Pattern Groups 1 to 4, the submission must be supported by a cost‑benefit analysis presented to the PennDOT project manager, together with a complete evaluation of laboratory testing results, selected CBR value, and derived resilient modulus, with additional field testing such as FWD or DCP when uncertainty exists in the CBR data. Existing‑pavement evaluation uses the Condition Survey Method in DARWin, which requires STAMPP condition data from the Roadway Management System and may be verified by a field view; the submission must document where and how those condition inputs were obtained. The STAMPP pavement condition survey records distresses for asphalt and concrete surfaces across the National Highway System and generates recommended treatments; the submission must include the STAMPP Treatment Summary with Form D‑4332, and Microsoft Access tools are used to produce summary reports on specific distress types such as shoulder drop‑off, rutting and fatigue cracking. Stakeholders and constraints

The District Pavement Type Selection Committee supplies the technical input for a pavement design submission and ultimately selects the pavement type after weighing regional factors such as engineering judgment, adjacent pavement types and performance, subgrade composition and condition, local materials, right‑of‑way constraints, maintenance considerations and traffic or utility impacts; the composition of that committee is at the discretion of the District Executive. For alternate pavement designs the district provides the design input by completing the alternate designs and submitting the pavement designs together with the life‑cycle cost analysis to BODD for review; after the HDTD Chief gives a preliminary approval, the package is posted for a three‑week comment period and the HDTD Chief then issues the final approval for PennDOT‑oversight projects or forwards it to FHWA for Federal‑oversight final approval. The District Geotechnical Engineer supplies the required geotechnical data, must sign that data, discusses subgrade improvement options, selects an improvement strategy suitable for the soil type present, and signs off on the roadbed soil resilient modulus; any CBR value greater than 10 to be used in the design shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer. The District Pavement Management Engineer/Pavement Manager must be consulted when a pavement design or any change to it is being considered, providing the necessary documentation and cost data; the initial pavement design package is then submitted to the District Executive for approval and, once approved, proceeds to the Bureau of Design and Delivery, while any subsequent changes are submitted to the original approving authority—either FHWA, BODD, or the District. Certified quality‑control test results are also sent to the Representative within 48 hours of sampling, but the Representative’s role is limited to receiving information rather than approving the design. The decision on a Pavement Design Submission should be taken after the life‑cycle cost analysis has been approved and the pavement type has been selected; at that point the chosen pavement type is recorded in the design package for HDTD approval and shall not be changed during either the design or construction phases unless further HDTD approval is obtained. Outcomes and follow-up

Success for a Pavement Design Submission is defined by meeting both approval timing and documentation completeness criteria established in the report. For moderate‑complexity projects, success is achieved when formal design approval is obtained within 1 year after the Design Field View (or environmental) approval and no later than 6 months before the Final Design Office Meeting. For the most complex projects, success is achieved when formal design approval is obtained within 2 years after the Design Field View (or environmental) approval and at least 6 months before the Final Design Office Meeting. Pavement Preservation projects must be let within 15 months of the Pavement Type Selection approval date for the submission to be considered successful. Success is also measured by confirming that the submission package contains every required element—cover letter with project identifiers and justification, Form D‑4332, typical section drawings, location map, DARWin printouts, structural calculations, traffic data, geotechnical report, temporary pavement plans, LCCA documentation, existing pavement history, any special provisions, and for Federal Oversight projects the STAMPP Treatment Summary—and that the correct number of copies (one for PennDOT Oversight, two for Federal Oversight) are provided; reviewers gauge compliance by checking deadline adherence and completeness against the checklist in Section 6.1.C. Success is further achieved when the package receives formal approval from the appropriate oversight authority—BODD for PennDOT‑Oversight projects and FHWA for Federal‑Oversight projects—and all required documentation is complete and retained. An individual PME/PM must perform and submit five pavement designs using DARWin that include at least one design of each specified type (full‑depth asphalt, full‑depth concrete, full‑depth reclamation, structural asphalt overlay, and a concrete overlay) and obtain written BODD certification after those designs are approved. Ongoing success is measured by maintaining an approved copy of Form D‑4332 for a minimum period of 7 years, documenting justification when projects are less than 30 000 square yards, meeting the 15‑month let requirement for preservation projects, and passing the annual PennDOT Oversight QA review. Success is also confirmed when the package includes a complete evaluation of laboratory testing results, the selected CBR value and derived resilient modulus, and any required special provisions, with all values satisfying prescribed thresholds—any CBR value greater than 10 must be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and no CBR value greater than 20 may be used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation; reviewers verify that out‑of‑range CBR results have been either disregarded or supported with additional testing when uncertainty exists, and that any back‑calculated Mr from field tests has been verified against laboratory values before inclusion. Success for a Pavement Type Selection submission is achieved when the package contains every minimum required item—scoping field view documentation, existing pavement data, International Roughness Index and friction data, a full description of all planned work, falling‑weight deflectometer test results where appropriate, any needed geotechnical data, a copy of Form D‑4332, and any special provisions—and is submitted by the District Executive or Assistant District Executive with a formal PennDOT Pavement Type Selection Memo to HDTS; approval confirms that the submission meets all criteria. Finally, for Federal Oversight submissions, success is confirmed when a copy of the STAMPP Treatment Summary is attached to each Form D‑4332, and checklist verification shows that the required summary is present in the packet. Success for a Pavement Design Submission in the full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete slab scope is achieved when the contractor delivers complete fabrication submittals—including shop drawings, working drawings and the manufacturer’s quality‑control plan—to the Representative at least 21 calendar days before the pre‑production meeting, allowing the Representative a further 21 calendar day review period to issue an acceptance or rejection; the submitted concrete mix designs must be approved with trial‑batch test data that demonstrate a compressive strength of at least 4,925 psi and a flexural strength of at least 650 psi, the fabricated slabs must conform to the dimensions and tolerances shown in the authorized shop drawings, with transverse joint widths not exceeding 1/2 inch, longitudinal joints not exceeding 3/4 inch, vertical differentials no greater than 1/4 inch, and all dowel‑slot and bedding grout must reach a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is allowed. Success is measured by the Representative’s formal acceptance notice after the review period and by field verification that every dimensional tolerance, strength threshold and joint criterion is met during construction inspections. Success for a Pavement Design Submission in the asphalt‑concrete pavement scope with a 60‑month warranty is achieved when the contractor delivers a complete mix‑design package on the most current version of the TR‑448A form, signed by a Certified Asphalt Level 2 Plant Technician, to the DME/DMM at least seven calendar days before its use in the work and includes all required items such as project identifiers, producer information, bulk specific gravities, volumetric properties, moisture‑sensitivity data and gradation chart; the QC plan must be prepared as specified in Section 106 and submitted at the start of the project, all certified QC test results must reach the Representative within forty‑eight hours of material sampling, any change in material source must be accompanied by a written notification and a revised JMF, and a Warranty Bond equal to fifty percent of the total contract amount must be furnished as required. Success is measured by confirming that each submission meets the timing thresholds (≥ 7 calendar days for the mix design and ≤ 48 hours for test results), contains all specified fields, bears the required signature, and that the warranty bond and insurance provisions are in place according to the contract terms. Results from a Pavement Design Submission are first tracked through the life‑cycle cost analysis (LCCA) that quantifies cost differences between alternatives, and the LCCA together with meeting minutes and conclusions shall be recorded in the design package submitted for HDTD approval; decision thresholds of at least a 10 % cost difference when only agency costs are considered or at least a 20 % difference when user delay costs are included guide pavement‑type selection, and once an LCCA and pavement type are approved the chosen type shall not be changed unless further HDTD approval is obtained [1]. After the district completes alternate pavement designs and the associated LCCA, they submit them to BODD and, once preliminarily approved by the HDTD Chief, post the design package in ECMS as an unofficial plan set for a three‑week review and comment period during which corrections may be made; the project is then flagged in ECMS by selecting “Yes” for “Alternative Pavement (C‑Factor)” under Innovative Bidding Types, causing it to appear on the Planned 6‑Month Letting Schedule that is refreshed each month [1]. The HDTS monitors and tracks these submissions and their outcomes so that the collected performance and cost data can be used to refine future alternate‑pavement bidding processes and improve later pavement‑type decisions [1]. All pavement design changes made following the original pavement design submission shall be submitted to the original approving authority (FHWA, BODD, or the District), with supporting cost data provided so that the change can be evaluated as part of an updated package; the District Pavement Management Engineer/Pavement Manager is consulted when a change is contemplated and ensures that appropriate documentation reaches BODD [1]. Field verification of estimated rehabilitation items during the final design phase, performed within the year before project advertisement, supplies additional performance data that can be incorporated into future design decisions [1]. Results from each Pavement Design Submission are monitored through an annual PennDOT Oversight QA visit performed by a PDAU staff review team, and the district PME keeps a list of DARWin designs for PDAU review; findings are captured in a detailed report that is sent to the DE and copied to FHWA, the Deputy Secretary, BODD leadership, the Pavement Testing and Asset Management Section Chief, and the responsible PME/PM, while the approved Form D‑4332 documenting the submission must be maintained for a minimum period of 7 years, providing a permanent record that can be referenced in future design decisions [1]. By circulating the QA reports and preserving the formal approval forms, the agency creates a documented feedback loop that informs subsequent approvals, certifications, and any needed waivers or revisions [1]. Results from subgrade testing are monitored by checking laboratory CBR values against the design criteria and by confirming any outliers with additional lab or field tests such as FWD or DCP; all CBR values greater than 10 for use in pavement designs shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, no CBR value greater than 20 will be allowed to be used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation, and back‑calculated Mr above 15 000 psi must be verified with another test; the complete evaluation of the laboratory testing results, the selected CBR, and the resulting Mr are documented and submitted with the pavement design submission, and this documentation is then used to guide subgrade‑improvement decisions, determine whether multiple design sections are feasible, and trigger additional testing when uncertainty exists, thereby providing feedback that improves later design choices [1]. Results for a Pavement Design Submission are also monitored through the STAMPP program’s annual condition survey that covers 100 % of NHS roadways and 50 % of non‑NHS roadways, with the full non‑NHS network surveyed every two years; the collected data are entered into PennDOT’s RMS database, documentation is achieved by attaching the STAMPP Treatment Summary to each Form D‑4332 submission and by generating report summaries via Access tools that feed the ROAD application on the PDIF website, providing searchable inventories of pavement distresses, guide rail, and drainage conditions; these documented treatment recommendations are used as a baseline for later decisions such as selecting an asphalt overlay when a surface‑treatment recommendation indicates surfacing is needed [1]. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529) results from a Pavement Design Submission are monitored by requiring the contractor to submit shop drawings, working drawings, and a quality‑control plan at least 21 calendar days before production; the Representative then reviews the submission within another 21 calendar days for acceptance or rejection [2]. Concrete mix designs must be accompanied by trial batch test data that demonstrate compressive and flexural strengths meet minimum values, and grout strength is continuously checked during installation to ensure it reaches a minimum compressive strength of 2 500 pounds per square inch before traffic is allowed [2]. Dimensional tolerances and joint vertical differentials are measured on‑site with the requirement that finished vertical differential across any joint does not exceed 1/4 inch, and recurring damage or distress requires discontinuing installation until the cause is determined and mitigated [2]. All submittals, test results, survey measurements, inspection findings, and meeting minutes constitute the documented record that informs corrective actions or redesign for future submissions [2]. For asphalt concrete pavement (Section 496) results are monitored by delivering certified quality‑control test data to the Representative within 48 hours after each material sample is taken, while mix designs must be submitted on the current TR‑448A form at least 7 calendar days before any use; the submission package documents date, project identifiers, producer information, aggregate and binder properties, volumetric parameters, and any additional test results, and together with the QC plan these become part of the as‑built project files [2]. If a material source or mix changes during construction, the contractor must notify the DME/DMM in writing and submit a revised JMF, ensuring that the record reflects actual field conditions; because the submissions are retained for information only, they provide a documented baseline that can be reviewed to evaluate warranty performance and guide future design adjustments [2].
