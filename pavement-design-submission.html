Pavement Design Submission

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Pavement Design Submission is a topic under Pavement Design.
pavement-design-submissionpavement-design-submissionspavement-design-packagepavement-design-analysispavement-design-approval
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

The Pavement Design Submission is a formal, documented pathway that ties a project’s design package to the engineering, economic and condition‑based data required before any pavement type may be approved, presenting life‑cycle cost analysis results and supporting documentation so reviewers can verify that an alternative meets defined cost thresholds (≥10 % agency‑only or ≥20 % total including user delay) and is equivalent in service performance [1]. It functions by assembling a complete set of items—cover letter, Form D‑4332, typical section drawings, location map, DARWin structural calculations, traffic and geotechnical reports, LCCA printouts, and, where required, STAMPP Treatment Summaries—and routing the package through district and oversight approvals (District Executive, HDTD Chief, BODD or FHWA) with any post‑approval changes requiring resubmission [1][2]. The submission’s defining characteristics are its reliance on AASHTOWare® DARWin® analysis, strict subgrade CBR approval limits, prescribed lead‑times for mix‑design and drawing reviews, and retention of an approved Form D‑4332 copy for at least seven years to ensure traceability and compliance [1][2].

Purpose and decision context

The Pavement Design Submission is intended to support the decision to select a project’s pavement type by providing life‑cycle cost analysis results, and when a difference of 10 % or more in life‑cycle costs excluding user delay costs, or a difference of 20 % or more including user delay costs, is identified, the District Pavement Type Selection Committee may use engineering judgment and regional factors to justify the choice, with the LCCA, meeting minutes and conclusions recorded in the design package submitted for HDTD approval. In the context of alternate‑pavement‑type bidding, the submission supports the decision to approve a project’s pavement design and its associated life‑cycle cost analysis, because after the alternate designs are completed the District submits the designs and LCCA to BODD for review, the HDTD Chief provides preliminary approval before a three‑week comment period, and final approval of the design and LCCA is granted by the HDTD Chief or forwarded to FHWA for Federal Oversight. For moderate‑complexity projects the submission furnishes the documentation that allows the District Executive to decide whether to approve a proposed pavement structure, with completed forms submitted to the DE for approval; upon DE approval the package is forwarded to BODD, and any later design changes must be resubmitted to the original approving authority—DE, BODD or FHWA—for evaluation and concurrence before work orders can proceed. For PennDOT Oversight projects that require an LCCA, the submission supports the authority’s decision to review and approve the design by sending the package to BODD, where certified PME/PMs recommend approval using Form D‑4332 based on sound engineering principles and conformance to Publication 242, and the DE’s approval is final. The submission also supplies the Chief Geotechnical Engineer with the full evaluation of laboratory CBR results, the selected resilient‑modulus and any special subgrade improvement provisions so that a decision can be made to approve CBR values greater than 10 and to verify that no CBR value greater than 20 is used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation. For preservation projects the submission supports the decision to approve a pavement‑type selection by assembling existing condition data, IRI, friction values, FWD results, geotechnical information and special provisions, and forwarding this package with a formal PennDOT Pavement Type Selection Memo from the District Executive or Assistant District Executive of Design to HDTS for approval. In the full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slab specification, the Pavement Design Submission process exists so that the Representative can make an acceptance or rejection decision on the contractor’s shop drawings, working drawings and manufacturer quality‑control plan; the contractor must deliver these documents at least 21 calendar days before the pre‑production meeting, after which the Representative has 21 calendar days to review them and issue a notice of acceptance or rejection. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 65–79), Ch. 6 (pp. 134–150), Ch. 12 (p. 206); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

The Pavement Design Submission process exists to meet the need for a controlled, documented pathway that links design packages to the engineering, economic and condition‑based information required before any pavement type may be approved. It provides the mechanism by which a life‑cycle cost analysis (LCCA) and its supporting documentation are formally presented for review so that reviewers can verify that an alternative meets the defined thresholds—at least a 10 % difference in agency‑only life‑cycle costs or a 20 % difference when user delay costs are included—and thereby prevent unapproved changes after approval. The process also ensures that alternate pavement designs satisfy PennDOT’s engineering and life‑cycle cost requirements, that they are “equivalent,” meaning they provide comparable levels of service and performance over the same analysis period, and that all required data—traffic information, geotechnical reports, subgrade CBR values, resilient modulus, International Roughness Index, friction data, existing pavement condition and maintenance history—are included in the submission. By requiring the inclusion of the STAMPP Treatment Summary with each Form D‑4332 for Federal Oversight projects, the process anchors the design package to the condition‑based treatment information generated by PennDOT’s STAMPP program, ensuring that designs are consistent with observed pavement distresses and meet federal oversight requirements for informed rehabilitation planning. The submission must be clear to facilitate review, retain an approved copy of Form D‑4332 for at least seven years, and any deviations from the manual must be submitted as a waiver, thereby addressing regulatory compliance and providing justification for the proposed work. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529), the need is addressed by requiring that concrete mix designs be provided to the Representative at least 28 days before slab production, that shop drawings, working drawings and the manufacturer’s quality‑control plan be delivered at least 21 calendar days before the pre‑production meeting, and that the Representative have a 21‑day review period to accept or reject the submission; these steps are intended to verify that the slabs will achieve a minimum concrete compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a minimum flexural strength of 650 psi and remain within specified dimensional tolerances. For asphalt concrete pavement covered by a 60‑month warranty (Section 496), the need is satisfied by requiring a complete, signed mix‑design package on the current TR‑448A form prepared by a Certified Asphalt Level 2 Plant Technician to be submitted to the DME/DMM at least 7 calendar days before its use, together with a quality‑control plan submitted at the start of the project as described in Section 106, so that material selections, proportions and test results can be verified and ongoing compliance monitored. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

The Pavement Design Submission process is appropriate for any new‑construction, reconstruction, or rehabilitation project, and it may also be employed for pavement preservation projects when those projects involve geometric or bridge issues that require a design analysis; short pavement‑section projects that do not meet the length thresholds are required to submit a design only if directed by the District Pavement Management Engineer [1]. Projects that exceed the length thresholds—more than 500 continuous feet on divided roadways, more than 1,000 continuous feet on undivided roadways, or bridge approaches where the total paving of both sides exceeds 1,000 feet—must undergo a DARWin pavement design analysis and submit the resulting package for District Executive approval before it is forwarded to the Bureau of Design and Delivery [1]. The submission is also required for any PennDOT Oversight project that involves a life‑cycle cost analysis and for all Federal Oversight projects, including preservation work, unless FHWA waives the requirement based on risk; in these cases the design must be reviewed and approved by BODD or FHWA respectively [1]. For PennDOT Oversight projects whose mainline pavement surface area is less than 30,000 square yards, a formal type‑determination is not required, but sufficient justification for the selected pavement type and proposed work must be documented in the project file [1]. Pervious pavement designs for trails and parking lots follow Appendix K and are submitted to the District PME for review and approval, while pervious pavement designs for other applications shall follow the same submission process as standard pavements [1]. In addition, asphalt‑concrete pavement projects governed by Section 496 of the specifications (the 60‑month warranty) must complete a Pavement Design Submission in the pre‑construction phase; the mix design(s) are to be submitted on the current TR‑448A form signed by a Certified Asphalt Level 2 Plant Technician at least seven calendar days before use, and a quality‑control plan must be submitted before any construction begins [2]. A preliminary pavement design may be submitted prior to Design Field View approval so that recommended changes can be incorporated without adversely affecting the schedule, with the complete pavement design reviewed and approved in final design [1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 77–79), Ch. 6 (pp. 134–139), Ch. 12 (p. 206); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 344–345)

A Pavement Design Submission is not required for short‑section projects that fall below the length thresholds that trigger a DARWin analysis—specifically, when the pavement length is less than 500 continuous feet on divided roadways or less than 1,000 continuous feet on undivided roadways—and where the proposed pavement can be matched to the existing structure while meeting the minimum thickness tables; in those cases the District Executive’s approval recorded on the project file suffices. If the existing composite pavement far exceeds the minimum thicknesses or contains elements that are not reasonable to replicate, a DARWin analysis may be directed by the District Pavement Management Engineer as an alternative approach. When the characteristics that define a pavement design differ between adjacent sections—total 18‑kip ESALs, subgrade reaction or resilient modulus, initial and terminal serviceability, reliability level, estimated unit costs of materials, or the existing pavement section—a single Pavement Design Submission is not appropriate; each project must be submitted separately to reflect those changes. Any design alteration after the original submission shall be resubmitted to the approving authority rather than relying on the initial package. A full Pavement Design Submission is also not required for Pavement Preservation projects because the preservation guidelines replace a design analysis, so the work proceeds under the preservation program instead of a traditional submittal; similarly, PennDOT Oversight projects that involve less than 30,000 square yards of mainline pavement surface can forego the type‑determination procedure and rely on documented engineering justification while still using a certification process for review. When only a STAMPP Treatment recommendation is available it is not appropriate to use that treatment as the basis for a Pavement Design Submission because the program’s recommended treatments are intended for maintenance analysis and are normally less than the design strategy required for contract rehabilitation; in those situations a full rehabilitation design—such as an asphalt overlay developed through a detailed design project—should be prepared instead of relying on the STAMPP treatment summary. Information needs

A Pavement Design Submission is accompanied by a cover letter that must identify the MPMS project number, county, state route and segment, indicate oversight status, complexity, the design field view or environmental approval date, anticipated schedule dates, the estimated project cost, pavement length and total length, and note any special circumstances or exceptions. Site information required includes subgrade composition and condition, adjacent pavement types and performance, local material availability, right‑of‑way constraints, and any maintenance or traffic protection impacts; a detailed soils report is mandatory for all reconstruction projects as outlined in Section 2.2, and the submission must contain a CBR summary table together with all Falling Weight Deflectometer data, soils testing results and frost‑heave probability signed by the district geotechnical engineer. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, the submission must also contain site‑specific thermal data because the mix design for continuous prestressed precast systems has to account for ambient average daily temperatures and exposure conditions at the project site in order to size panels and locate expansion joints. It must include detailed survey information of existing pavement elevations so that theoretical slab elevations can be established before fabrication, and it must provide a resource‑loaded schedule together with an inventory of equipment and materials. Condition data also comprise STAMPP condition data obtained from the Roadway Management System, with documentation of where and how those input values were sourced, and may be supplemented by a field view performed by the District PME/PM to verify or refine existing‑condition variables before they are entered into the Condition Survey Method; the submission must further include a complete set of existing pavement data that captures all maintenance surface treatments, measured International Roughness Index and friction values, and any recorded distresses for asphalt and jointed concrete surfaces as defined by Pennsylvania’s Location Reference System. Traffic information required consists of a summary table of average daily traffic and truck percentage for each design, the source and date of those data, and the total 18‑kip ESALs representing traffic loading; additional details such as the full traffic breakdowns must be provided. A traffic protection plan is required, specifying delivery truck routes and measures for maintaining traffic safety during installation. The cost component must present the current discount rate, Asphalt Adjustment Multiplier, quantity estimates for each alternative based on the actual typical cross section (wearing, binder, base, subbase), unit costs for pavement‑related items, earthwork quantities, resurfacing thicknesses, shoulder construction, drainage or utility relocations; Life‑Cycle Cost Analysis printouts must show quantity and unit costs together with supporting documentation, and the LCCA shall account for shoulder construction and maintenance. For life‑cycle cost comparison a difference of 10 % or more is required when only agency costs are considered, while a difference of 20 % or more is required when user delay costs are included. Subgrade strength must be expressed by the effective modulus of subgrade reaction and/or roadbed soil resilient modulus, and any CBR value greater than 10 shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer; no CBR value greater than 20 may be used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation. Cost data for any selected subgrade strengthening technique must use current cost information for all components and be consistent with the proposed construction, such as distinguishing rock available on site from borrow material, and a cost‑benefit analysis shall be provided to the PennDOT project manager after discussion of improvement options with the District Geotechnical Engineer. Design inputs also require initial and terminal serviceability values, a reliability level, estimated contract unit costs of pavement materials, and when an asphalt wearing course is specified, the appropriate Skid Resistance Level designation on Form D‑4332; any existing pavement section to be rehabilitated must be described. Stakeholder input is documented through precast pavement planning, pre‑fabrication and pre‑placement meetings that involve the Representative, inspection personnel, CQAS and SMS representatives, project superintendent, foreman, surveyor, grout installers, sawcutters, the technical representative of the PCPS system, the fabricator and all subcontractors. The District Pavement Management Engineer or Pavement Manager must be consulted whenever design changes are contemplated and must receive all supporting project cost data for inclusion in the evaluation package, and field verification of estimated rehabilitation items is required during the final design phase within the year preceding project advertisement. For PennDOT Oversight projects whose mainline pavement surface area is less than 30,000 square yards, the type‑determination step may be omitted, but sufficient justification for the selected pavement type must be documented in the project file, drawing on engineering judgment, experience, existing conditions and adjacent pavement type; the District Pavement Type Selection Committee may provide justification after consideration of regional factors such as utility impacts and other stakeholder concerns. For asphalt concrete pavement with a 60‑Month Warranty, the submission must contain the basic site identification for the pavement work – specifically the State Route, the project section and the County together with the contract number and date of submission. [1][2]

The pavement design submission must be a complete package that includes every item enumerated in Section 6.1.C, such as the cover letter, Form D‑4332, typical section drawing, location map, full DARWin printouts, all structural calculations, the traffic summary with its source and date, the geotechnical report signed by the District Geotechnical Engineer, LCCA printouts, and any special provisions, so that no required document is omitted. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, the submission shall contain the concrete mix designs together with trial‑batch test data, all shop and working drawings that show slab dimensions, dowel and tie‑bar locations, utility blockouts, leveling system details, and the manufacturer’s quality‑control plan. For asphalt concrete pavement covered by the 60‑month warranty, the submission must be prepared on the most current TR‑448A form, signed by a Certified Asphalt Level 2 Plant Technician, and must include every item enumerated for each mix design—dates, project identifiers, producer details, aggregate and binder specifications, volumetric properties, test results, and the designer’s signature. All quantitative inputs must be current; the traffic data must include its source and date, and if the design year differs by more than three years a new design and LCCA shall be performed. The mix designs must reach the Representative no later than 28 calendar days prior to slab production so that they are current for review, and all required items must be submitted at least 21 calendar days before the pre‑production meeting. The asphalt submission must be delivered to the DME/DMM at least seven calendar days before any material is used, ensuring the information reflects the most current mix design data. Accuracy is ensured by providing the original DARWin screens, signed geotechnical data, and complete calculations supporting the design; the submission must also include a full documented evaluation of all laboratory testing results, the selected CBR value and the resulting resilient modulus, and any required special provisions for subgrade improvement. The information must be accurate to the tolerances prescribed in the section: slab dimensions must match the authorized shop drawings and remain within the tolerance table, dowel and tie‑bar locations must be within ±1/8 inch of the drawing, and the survey layout of longitudinal joints must reflect the latest field measurements. All component materials shown in the mix design are required to be representative of the materials that will be used on the project. If considerable uncertainty exists regarding the validity or representativeness of a CBR result, additional laboratory or field testing must be performed so that only accurate, representative values are used, and outlier values that do not appear to be representative of the soil type should be disregarded. If a material source or any other adjustment changes during construction, a written notice together with a revised JMF must be submitted before the new material is used. [1][2]

Alternatives

Under the life‑cycle cost analysis guidelines (section 3.2) a Pavement Design Submission should compare alternative pavement designs using life‑cycle cost analysis, and a difference of at least 10 % in agency‑only costs or at least 20 % when user delay costs are included is sufficient to select a pavement type. [1] When Alternate Pavement Type Bidding is employed (section 3.6) the district must compare at least three equivalent alternatives—a concrete pavement paired with an asphalt‑treated permeable base course, a concrete pavement paired with a cement‑treated permeable base course, and a full Superpave asphalt system—each presented with complete plans, typical sections, item numbers, quantities, and traffic control plans while meeting RC standards, DM‑2, and Publication 242. [1] For PennDOT Oversight projects the feasible options to be compared are a rigid pavement design alternative and a flexible pavement design alternative; for Federal Oversight projects the alternatives will be defined during the Scoping Field View. [1] When deciding whether a single pavement design analysis can serve two or more adjacent projects (section 6.1 D. Procedures) the options that must be compared include total 18‑kip ESALs, effective modulus of subgrade reaction and/or roadbed soil resilient modulus, initial and terminal serviceability, reliability level, estimated contract unit costs of the pavement materials, and the existing pavement section for rehabilitation. [1] The Pavement Type Definitions require evaluation of three pavement type definitions—reconstruction, rehabilitation (the 3R process), and pavement preservation—each representing a different scope of work and material approach. [1] Subgrade strengthening alternatives such as cement or lime stabilization (Structural Coefficient = 0.12), rock capping modeled with CBR = 20, and the use of geogrids or geotextiles for reinforcement may also be evaluated within the submission. [1] For an AC overlay of jointed plain concrete pavement the feasible options to compare are the Condition Survey Method and the Remaining Life Method, with guidance directing designers to use the Condition Survey Method and to obtain STAMPP condition data from the Roadway Management System. [1] For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slab projects the design team should compare load‑transfer configurations permitted by the shop drawings, including slabs with dowel slots at the slab bottom on one transverse edge together with embedded dowels on the opposite edge; slabs that employ conventional surface dowel slots paired with embedded dowels; and slabs that use narrow‑mouth surface dowel slots. [2] The submission should also evaluate slab‑leveling alternatives described in Section 529.3(f), namely setting the slab at the desired elevation with four symmetrically placed threaded setting bolts, injecting polyurethane material beneath the slab, or supporting the slab with two strongback beams extending beyond the repair area. [2] For continuous paving sections longer than 65 feet the design team must consider tie‑bar installation alternatives, either drilling and installing tie bars in existing pavement or applying a cross‑stitching technique. [2] All of these options must satisfy joint‑gap limits of at least ¼ inch and not exceed ½ inch for transverse joints, and the vertical differential across any joint may not exceed ¼ inch. [2] When preparing a Pavement Design Submission for asphalt concrete pavement covered by the 60‑month warranty, the contractor should compare alternative types and classes of asphalt material, the various aggregate options—including the SRL‑designated aggregate or equivalent blends that are 50 % by weight—and any filler required for the mix. [2] Stone Matrix Asphalt (SMA) wearing‑course mixes may be submitted for consideration, with each alternative represented in the volumetric mix design and documented on the TR‑448A form. [2]

Decision criteria

In the Pavement Policy Manual the primary control on whether a Pavement Design Submission is required is cost, with life‑cycle cost analysis used to compare alternatives. The manual specifies that a difference of at least ten percent in agency‑only life‑cycle costs is sufficient to select the lower‑cost pavement type, while a difference of twenty percent in total life‑cycle costs—including user delay impacts—justifies an alternative. Cost analysis must be performed with C‑Factors that add future maintenance costs (excluding user delay) to the construction price so that the low bid reflects total cost. The Alternate Pavement Type Bidding process also requires that any alternate be “equivalent,” meaning it must provide comparable levels of service and performance over the same analysis period. Schedule timing further governs the decision, because for moderate‑complexity projects the design must obtain approval within one year after Design Field View (or environmental) approval but no later than six months before the Final Design Office Meeting, and for the most complex projects the window extends to two years with the same six‑month pre‑meeting requirement. Each submission must include life‑cycle cost analysis printouts together with supporting unit‑cost calculations. Engineering parameters that must remain substantially unchanged when a single design is applied to adjacent projects include total 18‑kip ESALs, the effective modulus of subgrade reaction or roadbed soil resilient modulus, initial and terminal serviceability values, the reliability level, estimated contract unit costs of the pavement materials, and the condition of any existing pavement section for rehabilitation. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that the decision to submit a design is governed by the need to meet warranty performance criteria, so the material and mix‑design package must be demonstrated to satisfy those performance requirements. To demonstrate compliance, the contractor must submit a complete Job Mix Formula on the current TR‑448A form, signed by a Certified Asphalt Level 2 Plant Technician, at least seven calendar days before its use, and must also provide an approved quality‑control plan before work starts. Consequently, the controlling criteria identified across the guidance are cost (including life‑cycle and user delay considerations), schedule timing, performance equivalence and warranty performance, together with the listed engineering parameters. When competing alternatives are evaluated in a Pavement Design Submission, the primary weighting is placed on life‑cycle cost differentials: an alternative that demonstrates at least 10 % lower agency‑only LCC or at least 20 % lower total LCC including user delay costs shall be deemed sufficient to select its pavement type. If neither of those percentage thresholds is achieved, the District Pavement Type Selection Committee may provide justification after weighing regional factors such as engineering judgment, adjacent pavement performance, subgrade condition, local materials, right‑of‑way constraints and traffic or utility impacts, with engineering judgment required to be fact‑based and extraordinary. In parallel, competing criteria related to subgrade soils are prioritized by first applying the most controlling subgrade condition; when low CBR values are widespread across the project, the design shall select the soil type with the lowest CBR so that the pavement section is adequate for all areas, whereas isolated low‑CBR zones may be improved to achieve CBR values representative of the overall project. All CBR values greater than 10 intended for use in pavement designs shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, and no CBR value greater than 20 shall be allowed in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation. The project should be divided into only a limited number of subsections to avoid short lengths with differing thicknesses that would burden contractors and inspection forces, and all decisions, improvements and justifications must be fully documented in the pavement design submission. Analysis methods and tools

In the Pavement Design Submission process, the primary evaluation method is a life‑cycle cost analysis (LCCA) performed according to Chapter 3 Section 3.2; a difference of 10% or more in life‑cycle costs, excluding user delay costs, is sufficient to select the preferred pavement type, and a difference of 20% or more when user delay costs are included also suffices, and these analyses must be recorded for HDTD approval. For Alternate Pavement Type Bidding, evaluation relies on an LCCA that incorporates a C‑Factor representing the present worth of future maintenance costs, and the low bid is calculated by adding this C‑Factor to the construction bid amount; no other tools such as condition ratings, decision trees, risk assessment, or benefit‑cost analysis are mentioned in that section. The design analysis must be performed with AASHTOWare® DARWin® 3.01 software, and both DARWin output printouts and LCCA printouts with supporting cost calculations are required components of the submission package; a preliminary design must include sufficient data to generate an LCCA for review, and any change in the project’s design year of more than 3 years triggers a new design and accompanying LCCA. The DARWin program also evaluates the design against quantitative criteria such as total 18‑kip ESALs, effective subgrade reaction or resilient modulus, initial and terminal serviceability, reliability level, estimated unit costs of pavement materials, and the condition of any existing pavement section, with supporting project cost data supplied to the Pavement Management Engineer/Manager for inclusion in the evaluation package; this section does not mention condition‑rating scales, decision trees, life‑cycle cost analysis, risk assessment, or benefit‑cost analysis as part of the submission process. For Federal Oversight projects, approval may be based on a risk assessment and FHWA can waive full review if the project is judged low‑risk; PennDOT Oversight designs must include an LCCA before BODD review. When a subgrade‑strengthening option is proposed for pavement quantities of at least 25,000 SY on Traffic Pattern Groups 1 to 4, the submission must be supported by a cost‑benefit analysis presented to the PennDOT project manager, together with a complete evaluation of laboratory testing results, selected CBR value, and derived resilient modulus, with additional field testing such as FWD or DCP when uncertainty exists in the CBR data. Existing‑pavement evaluation uses the Condition Survey Method in DARWin, which requires STAMPP condition data from the Roadway Management System and may be verified by a field view; the submission must document where and how those condition inputs were obtained. The STAMPP pavement condition survey records distresses for asphalt and concrete surfaces across the National Highway System and generates recommended treatments; the submission must include the STAMPP Treatment Summary with Form D‑4332, and Microsoft Access tools are used to produce summary reports on specific distress types such as shoulder drop‑off, rutting and fatigue cracking. The life‑cycle cost analysis that underlies a Pavement Design Submission assumes the discount rate is taken from the ECMS File Cabinet and that asphalt costs are adjusted with an Asphalt Adjustment Multiplier calculated semiannually from the total of asphalt payments and adjustments. Resurfacing thicknesses are assumed to be four inches for a nine‑point‑five mm Wearing Course mix on rigid pavements and one point five inches for flexible pavements, with alternative values applied only for other mixes. Engineering and mobilization costs must not be included in the LCCA. A pavement type may be selected when there is a difference of ten percent or more in life‑cycle costs excluding user delay costs, or a difference of twenty percent or more when user delay costs are included. In Alternate Pavement Type Bidding the alternates are assumed to be “equivalent,” meaning they provide comparable levels of service and performance over the same analysis period, and the C‑Factor applied to bids is calculated only from the present worth of future maintenance costs, deliberately excluding user delay costs. Material cost fluctuations that cannot be predicted during the preconstruction phase introduce uncertainty into the low‑bid determination. The impact of constructability, phasing or traffic‑protection issues is expected to be reflected by varying initial construction, user‑delay, or maintenance‑and‑traffic‑protection cost items rather than being treated as separate factors. A preliminary pavement design may be developed prior to receiving the geotechnical report by assuming a resilient modulus, which introduces an assumption about subgrade stiffness. If a new project field view is required because the pavement condition has changed, updated traffic data shall be obtained and a new pavement design performed, reflecting uncertainty in existing conditions and traffic volumes. If the design year of the project changes by more than three years from the design year used in the approved pavement design and LCCA, a new design and LCCA shall be performed, acknowledging uncertainty in future loading and cost parameters. The analysis further assumes a specific total eighteen‑kip ESAL count, an effective modulus of subgrade reaction or roadbed soil resilient modulus, defined initial and terminal serviceability levels, a chosen reliability level, estimated contract unit costs for the pavement materials, and the condition of any existing pavement when rehabilitation is involved; changes in these factors across adjacent projects may cause differing design outcomes. The CBR value employed is assumed to reflect the soil type that will control the design, and isolated low‑CBR areas should be improved to achieve a representative value, while any laboratory result that does not appear representative must be disregarded. All CBR values greater than ten for use in designs shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and no CBR greater than twenty may be used in the Heukelom‑and‑Klomp correlation; when considerable uncertainty exists regarding the validity of a laboratory CBR, additional laboratory or field testing such as FWD or DCP must be performed, and because back‑calculated subgrade resilient modulus from FWD is not an exact science, any Mr value above fifteen thousand psi must be verified by another form of testing. For AC overlay design the Condition Survey Method is assumed to be used, STAMPP condition data from the Roadway Management System must be obtained, and a field view by the District PME/PM may also be required to determine accurate input values for some existing‑condition variables, so the accuracy of the effective existing thickness—and thus the calculated overlay thickness—depends on how well these data are sourced and documented. The Pavement Design Submission assumes that the STAMPP treatment recommendation for a segment will be somewhat less extensive than the design strategy ultimately prepared for contract rehabilitation and treats those recommendations only as an analytical guide rather than a definitive design basis, creating uncertainty because the actual rehabilitation design may differ in scope—typically involving an asphalt overlay when surfacing is indicated—and because the underlying STAMP data are derived from surveys not intended to directly dictate design decisions. The Pavement Design Submission assumes that the mix designs submitted are fully representative of the materials that will actually be placed. However, the submission is only for information and does not relieve the Contractor from meeting the specified construction end‑result or warranty performance criteria, introducing uncertainty regarding compliance with performance requirements. If a change in material source or other adjustments are necessary during construction, written notification to the DME/DMM must be provided, which introduces further variability into the project. Moisture sensitivity data such as tensile strength ratio or Hamburg Wheel Tracking results are required to address potential performance uncertainties. [1][2]

Stakeholders and constraints

The District Pavement Type Selection Committee supplies the technical input for a pavement design submission and ultimately selects the pavement type after weighing regional factors such as engineering judgment, adjacent pavement types and performance, subgrade composition and condition, local materials, right‑of‑way constraints, maintenance considerations and traffic or utility impacts; the composition of that committee is at the discretion of the District Executive. For alternate pavement designs the district provides the design input by completing the alternate designs and submitting the pavement designs together with the life‑cycle cost analysis to BODD for review; after the HDTD Chief gives a preliminary approval, the package is posted for a three‑week comment period and the HDTD Chief then issues the final approval for PennDOT‑oversight projects or forwards it to FHWA for Federal‑oversight final approval. The District Geotechnical Engineer supplies the required geotechnical data, must sign that data, and any CBR value greater than 10 to be used in the design shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer; for all projects the District Geotechnical Engineer shall verify, by signature, the appropriate roadbed soil resilient modulus value for use in the pavement design. The District Executive records approval for short‑section pavement designs and, for all other submissions, the HDD Chief first gives a preliminary approval; after posting the design package for comment the HDD Chief grants final approval for PennDOT‑oversight projects or forwards the submission to FHWA for Federal‑oversight final approval. The District Pavement Management Engineer/Pavement Manager must be consulted when a pavement design or any change to it is being considered, providing the necessary documentation and cost data; all subsequent changes are submitted to the original approving authority—either FHWA, BODD, or the District. Certified pavement engineers or managers supply the technical recommendation on a design and forward it to the District Engineer, whose approval is the final decision for PennDOT‑oversight submissions; for Federal‑Oversight projects the Bureau of Design Development performs a quality‑control review and forwards the package to FHWA, whose review and approval completes the process. The required documentation for a pavement type selection is assembled and submitted by either the District Executive or the Assistant District Executive of Design, who package the scoping view, existing data, IRI, friction, work description, FWD results, geotechnical information, Form D‑4332 and any special provisions into a formal PennDOT Pavement Type Selection memo that is sent to HDTS for review and approval. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs the contractor or its fabricator must submit the concrete mix designs at least twenty‑eight days before slab production and must also provide shop drawings, working drawings and the manufacturer’s quality‑control plan to the Representative at least twenty‑one calendar days prior to the pre‑production meeting. The Representative then has up to twenty‑one calendar days to review those submissions and to issue either acceptance or rejection. Input on the submission is provided during the precast pavement planning meetings by a wide range of project participants, including inspection personnel, CQAS and SMS representatives, the superintendent, foreman, surveyor, grout installers, sawcutters, the technical representative of the precast concrete slab system and the fabricator. For asphalt‑concrete pavements covered by the sixty‑month warranty the mix design that forms the core of a Pavement Design Submission must be prepared by the contractor, signed by a Certified Asphalt Level 2 Plant Technician, and sent to the District Maintenance Engineer/District Materials Manager at least seven calendar days before it is used; the Designer also signs the JMF. The DME/DMM reviews the submission and, together with the Designer’s signature, provides the authority needed before work can begin. Certified results of quality‑control testing are then provided to the Representative for record‑keeping. [1][2]

Outcomes and follow-up

Success for a Pavement Design Submission is defined by meeting both timing and documentation completeness criteria established in the Pavement Policy Manual. For moderate‑complexity projects, success is achieved when formal design approval is obtained within one year after the Design Field View (or environmental) approval and no later than six months before the Final Design Office Meeting, while for the most complex projects the same approval must be secured within two years after the Design Field View (or environmental) approval and at least six months before that meeting [1]. Pavement Preservation projects are considered successful only if they are let within fifteen months of the Pavement Type Selection approval date [1]. A submission is also judged successful when the package contains every required element—cover letter with project identifiers and justification, Form D‑4332, typical section drawings, location map, DARWin printouts, structural calculations, traffic data, geotechnical report, temporary pavement plans, life‑cycle cost analysis documentation, existing pavement history, any special provisions, and for Federal Oversight projects the STAMPP Treatment Summary—and when the correct number of copies (one for PennDOT Oversight, two for Federal Oversight) are provided, as verified against the checklist in Section 6.1.C [1]. Formal approval from the appropriate oversight authority—BODD for PennDOT‑Oversight projects and FHWA for Federal‑Oversight projects—confirms that all criteria have been satisfied [1]. An individual PME/PM must perform and submit five pavement designs using DARWin, including at least one design of each specified type (full‑depth asphalt, full‑depth concrete, full‑depth reclamation, structural asphalt overlay, and a concrete overlay), and obtain written BODD certification after those designs are approved; ongoing success is measured by maintaining an approved copy of Form D‑4332 for a minimum period of seven years, documenting justification when projects are less than thirty thousand square yards, meeting the fifteen‑month let requirement for preservation projects, and passing the annual PennDOT Oversight QA review [1]. Success also requires that the submission include a complete evaluation of laboratory testing results, the selected CBR value and derived resilient modulus, and any required special provisions, with all values satisfying prescribed thresholds—any CBR value greater than ten must be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, no CBR value greater than twenty may be used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation, and out‑of‑range CBR results must either be disregarded or supported with additional testing when uncertainty exists; back‑calculated resilient modulus values must be verified against laboratory values before inclusion [1]. For Pavement Type Selection submissions, success is achieved when the package contains every minimum required item—scoping field view documentation, existing pavement data, International Roughness Index and friction data, a full description of all planned work, falling‑weight deflectometer test results where appropriate, any needed geotechnical data, a copy of Form D‑4332, and any special provisions—and is submitted by the District Executive or Assistant District Executive with a formal PennDOT Pavement Type Selection Memo to HDTS; approval confirms that the submission meets all criteria [1]. For Federal Oversight submissions, success is confirmed when a copy of the STAMPP Treatment Summary is attached to each Form D‑4332 and checklist verification shows that the required summary is present in the packet [1]. Under SECTION 529—Full Depth Precast/Prestressed Concrete Pavement Slabs, success is achieved when all required shop drawings, working drawings and the manufacturer’s quality‑control plan are delivered to the Representative at least twenty‑one calendar days before the pre‑production meeting and the Representative issues an acceptance notice within its own twenty‑one‑day review period; measurement of success includes confirming that fabricated slab dimensions fall inside the tolerances of Table A, that the concrete mix attains a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a flexural strength of 650 psi, and that joint widths and vertical differentials comply with the limits of half‑inch for transverse joints, three‑quarters inch for longitudinal joints, and quarter‑inch for vertical differential, any deviation triggering rejection [2]. Under SECTION 496—Asphalt Concrete Pavement, 60‑Month Warranty, success is achieved when the contractor delivers a complete, signed TR‑448A mix design package at least seven calendar days before material use, includes all required data fields and representative material information, and submits an approved quality‑control plan before any work begins; the submission is considered successful only if any material changes are promptly reported in writing with a revised JMF, certified QC test results are provided to the Representative within forty‑eight hours of sampling to demonstrate that production meets specified tolerances, and the contractor furnishes a Warranty Bond equal to fifty percent of the total contract amount for the pavements covered by the warranty, which becomes effective on the date of physical work completion and remains in force for the required period [2].

Results from a Pavement Design Submission shall be tracked through the life‑cycle cost analysis (LCCA) that quantifies cost differences between alternatives, and the LCCA together with meeting minutes and conclusions shall be recorded in the design package submitted for HDTD approval. The documented LCCA is used to apply decision thresholds of at least a 10 % cost difference when only agency costs are considered or at least a 20 % difference when user delay costs are included, guiding pavement‑type selection. Once an LCCA and pavement type are approved the chosen type shall not be changed unless further HDTD approval is obtained. After the district completes alternate pavement designs and the associated LCCA, they submit them to BODD and, once preliminarily approved by the HDTD Chief, post the design package in ECMS as an unofficial plan set for a three‑week review and comment period during which corrections may be made. The project is then flagged in ECMS by selecting “Yes” for “Alternative Pavement (C‑Factor)” under Innovative Bidding Types, causing it to appear on the Planned 6‑Month Letting Schedule that is refreshed each month. The HDTS monitors and tracks these submissions and their outcomes so that the collected performance and cost data can be used to refine future alternate‑pavement bidding processes and improve later pavement‑type decisions. All pavement design changes made following the original submission shall be submitted to the original approving authority (FHWA, BODD, or the District), and supporting cost data shall be provided so that the change can be evaluated as part of an updated package. The District Pavement Management Engineer/Pavement Manager shall be consulted when a change is contemplated and is tasked with ensuring that appropriate documentation reaches BODD, which keeps the record clear and speeds review. Field verification of estimated rehabilitation items during the final design phase, performed within the year before project advertisement, supplies additional performance data that can be incorporated into future design decisions. Results from each Pavement Design Submission are monitored through an annual PennDOT Oversight QA visit performed by a PDAU staff review team, and the district PME keeps a list of DARWin designs for PDAU review; findings are captured in a detailed report that is sent to the DE and copied to FHWA, the Deputy Secretary, BODD leadership, the Pavement Testing and Asset Management Section Chief, and the responsible PME/PM. The approved Form D‑4332 documenting the submission must be maintained for a minimum period of 7 years, providing a permanent record that can be referenced in future design decisions. Results from subgrade testing are monitored by checking laboratory CBR values against the design criteria and by confirming any outliers with additional lab or field tests such as FWD or DCP; all CBR values greater than 10 for use in pavement designs shall be approved by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, and in no case will a CBR value greater than 20 be allowed to be used in the Heukelom and Klomp correlation, while back‑calculated Mr above 15 000 psi must be verified with another test. The complete evaluation of the laboratory testing results, the selected CBR, and the resulting Mr are documented and submitted with the pavement design submission, and this documentation is then used to guide subgrade‑improvement decisions, determine whether multiple design sections are feasible, and trigger additional testing when uncertainty exists, thereby providing feedback that improves later design choices. Results for a Pavement Design Submission are monitored through the STAMPP program’s annual condition survey that covers 100 % of NHS roadways and 50 % of non‑NHS roadways, with the full non‑NHS network surveyed every 2 years, and the collected data are entered into PennDOT’s RMS database. Documentation is achieved by attaching the STAMPP Treatment Summary to each Form D‑4332 submission and by generating report summaries via Access tools that feed the ROAD application on the PDIF website, providing searchable inventories of pavement distresses, guide rail, and drainage conditions; these documented treatment recommendations are used as a baseline for later decisions, such as selecting an asphalt overlay when a surface‑treatment recommendation indicates surfacing is needed. For precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs the results of a Pavement Design Submission are monitored by requiring the contractor to submit shop drawings, working drawings, and the manufacturer’s quality‑control plan at least 21 calendar days before the pre‑production meeting, after which the Representative has 21 calendar days to review the package and issue acceptance or rejection. During construction the Representative records key performance data such as grout compressive strength, which shall not be less than 2 500 pounds per square inch, and slab dimensions compared with the tolerances in Table A, noting any deviations in field logs. Any slab that exceeds a finished vertical differential of 1/4 inch across a joint or exhibits recurring damage triggers a stop‑work directive until the cause is identified and corrective actions are documented, and those findings are fed back into future mix designs, tolerance criteria, and installation procedures. For asphalt concrete pavement covered by the 60‑month warranty, results are monitored by providing certified quality‑control test results to the Representative within 48 hours of material sampling and by notifying the DME/DMM in writing if any material source changes occur during construction. Documentation is achieved through a mix‑design submission on the current TR‑448A form signed by a Certified Asphalt Level 2 Plant Technician at least 7 calendar days before use, together with a QC plan submitted before work begins; work shall not start until the QC plan and the JMF have been submitted. All such submissions become part of the as‑built project files and may be referenced for future design decisions. [1][2]
