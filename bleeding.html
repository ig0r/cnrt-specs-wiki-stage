Bleeding

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Bleeding is a topic under Distress.
bleedingflushingbleed-through
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Bleeding in micro‑surfacing is the presence of excess emulsified asphalt that migrates to the pavement surface, producing a smooth, shiny and possibly tacky film, and it may also appear at longitudinal joints where bleeding is prohibited [2]. It occurs on asphalt‑type surfaces such as hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) or polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing, confined to the surface layer and joint locations rather than wheel paths, cracks, edges, lifts, interfaces, base or subgrade [1][2]. The distress is caused by over‑application of material—specifically when more than 5 % of the finished surface contains excess emulsified asphalt—or by practices such as over‑filling or excessive crowning that accelerate binder migration to the surface [2]. When bleeding exceeds the 5 % limit or is present at longitudinal joints, the pavement is classified as defective work under Section 483.3(l) and requires corrective action before acceptance [2].

Description and identification

Bleeding in micro‑surfacing is the presence of excess emulsified asphalt that migrates to the pavement surface, creating a smooth, shiny area that may feel tacky, and it can also appear at longitudinal joints where bleeding is prohibited. According to the Finished Surface requirements in Section 483.3(l), excess emulsified asphalt must not exceed 5% of the finished surface area and any bleeding at joints is not allowed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341)

Bleeding is recognized when binder rises to the surface at longitudinal joints, which the specification expressly prohibits (Bleeding at joints is not allowed). It is differentiated from excess emulsified‑asphalt flushing, which the same section defines as a smooth, shiny, possibly tacky surface limited to no more than 5 % of the finished surface area; therefore any glossy, sticky film occurring at joints exceeds the allowable flushing limit and is treated as bleeding. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341)

Where it occurs

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), bleeding is mentioned only in the context of hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) asphalt treatments and may be present as minor bleeding on candidate HIR asphalt surfaces when it occurs in isolated areas [1]. Because HIR can be applied to full‑depth asphalt or to an asphalt layer over a stable concrete deck, the distress may appear on either of those asphalt‑based structures, but the source does not cite bleeding on plain concrete decks, separate overlays, or shoulders [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification 408/2026 addresses bleeding only for flexible pavements treated with polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing and requires that no bleeding occur at longitudinal joints [2]. It also limits excess emulsified asphalt, described as a smooth, shiny, possibly tacky surface, to not exceed 5 % of the finished surface area, thereby confining the distress to asphalt‑type surfaces and making no reference to concrete, composite, overlay or shoulder structures [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341)

The specification defines bleeding as excess emulsified asphalt that produces a smooth, shiny and possibly tacky surface; such flushing must not exceed 5 % of the finished surface area and any occurrence at joints—including longitudinal joints—is expressly prohibited, indicating that the distress is confined to the surface layer and joint locations rather than wheel paths, cracks, edges, lifts, interfaces, base or subgrade. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341)

Causes and mechanisms

Bleeding in micro‑surfacing occurs when excess emulsified asphalt remains on the pavement surface, allowing binder to rise and form a smooth, shiny, tacky film that can spread across joints; it is triggered by over‑application of material—specifically when more than 5 % of the finished surface contains excess emulsified asphalt—and by practices such as over‑filling or excessive crowning of rutted areas during spreading and finishing, which accelerate binder migration. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341)

Severity levels and rating

For HIR asphalt candidate pavements the source allows only minor bleeding, which is deemed acceptable provided it occurs in isolated areas; unlike rutting or cracking, no numerical depth or width limit is given for bleeding but its occurrence must be limited to small, isolated spots. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133)

The specification uses the Defective Work criteria in Section 483.3(l) of the flexible‑pavement manual as the procedure that rates bleeding severity; under the Finished Surface requirements any occurrence of bleeding at joints is deemed unacceptable, i.e., a defect. Consequently the source treats bleeding as a binary condition – if present it fails the specification. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341)

Measurement, survey and extent

Bleeding is measured as the percentage of the finished surface area that shows excess emulsified asphalt (flushing); the specification limits this to not exceed 5% of the surface, and the assessment is made by identifying smooth, shiny areas that may be tacky to the touch and determining what portion of the total pavement they occupy. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341)

Effects on performance

When bleeding produces excess emulsified asphalt that exceeds five percent of the finished surface area, or appears at longitudinal joints, the pavement is classified as defective work under Section 483‑l (Finished Surface); this triggers a requirement for surface correction before the pavement can be accepted. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341)

Repairs and treatments

Hot In‑Place Recycled (HIR) asphalt is cited as a treatment for bleeding, but it may be used only when the bleeding is minor or confined to isolated spots; minor bleeding and low friction numbers are acceptable for HIR candidate pavements, and its application is limited to cases where bleeding occurs in isolated areas while other surface‑only distress criteria are met (source: Pavement Policy Manual, section 5.19 C) [1]. The polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt paving system, commonly applied as micro‑surfacing, mandates correction of any work that exhibits excess emulsified asphalt (flushing) or bleeding; the specification prohibits bleeding at joints and requires that flushing not exceed 5 % of the finished surface area, with remedial action required when these conditions are observed (source: Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026, section 483.3(g) and subsection (l)) [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341)

Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) asphalt may be used only when bleeding is confined to isolated areas; the guidelines also note that minor bleeding and low friction numbers are acceptable as part of the surface‑distress criteria for HIR candidate projects, implying that widespread bleeding would rule out this treatment. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133)

Prevention in design and construction

The construction specifications require that during spreading and finishing (483.3(g)) crews avoid excess crowning of rutted areas to keep binder from accumulating and causing bleeding; they also demand a finished surface with no more than 5 % excess emulsified asphalt (flushing), which is described as a smooth, shiny, possibly tacky film that constitutes bleeding, and they explicitly prohibit bleeding at longitudinal joints, all of which are enforced under the Defective Work “Finished Surface” criteria in section 483.3(l). [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341)

The source requires that the finished micro‑surfacing surface contain no excess emulsified asphalt (flushing) greater than 5% of the surface area and that bleeding at longitudinal joints be prohibited; compliance with these limits is a condition for acceptable work under the Defective Work provisions of Section 483.3(l). [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341)

Specification limits and acceptance

Under Section 483 (Polymer‑Modified Emulsified Asphalt Paving System – Micro Surfacing), subsection l “Defective Work” the finished surface requirement specifies that bleeding at joints shall not be present, which means a zero tolerance; the presence of any shiny or tacky excess asphalt at joint locations is considered defective. This condition is judged by visual inspection for bleeding at joints and is enforced through the defect correction provisions in Section 483.3(l)(2). [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341)
