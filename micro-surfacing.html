Micro Surfacing

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Micro Surfacing is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
micro-surfacingmicro-surfacingmicro-surfacepolymer-modified-emulsified-paving-systempolymer-modified-emulsified-asphalt-paving-system
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Micro Surfacing is a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt thin‑film treatment applied as a scratch or leveling course to structurally sound pavements that exhibit only surface‑level distress such as low‑severity cracking, raveling, friction loss, moisture infiltration, or shallow rutting [1] [2]. Its purpose is to preserve the existing pavement by sealing cracks, filling voids and shallow to moderate ruts (from less than ½ in. up to about 2 in.), restoring a uniform skid‑resistant texture and ride quality without adding structural capacity [1] [2]. The system employs three mix types—Type A for cracks and ruts < ½ in., Type B for ruts ½–1¼ in., and a Rut‑Fill mix for ruts up to 2 in.—which cure rapidly (set within one hour) and are placed only when ambient temperature is at least 50 °F, rain is not imminent and freezing conditions are absent, allowing traffic to reopen shortly after placement [1] [2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Micro Surfacing is intended for structurally sound asphalt pavements that exhibit surface‑level distress such as ruts not caused by base failure, low severity cracking, low to medium severity raveling/weathering, friction loss, moisture infiltration, and minor surface irregularities. [1] It can fill and re‑profile rutted travel lanes, seal cracks, and restore adequate skid resistance without requiring a structural overlay. [1] The treatment is applied as a scratch or leveling course to treat surface defects such as scratches, unevenness that requires leveling, and ruts that need filling prior to an overlay. [1] It addresses surface conditions that compromise safety and comfort—specifically low skid resistance, poor ride quality, and existing rutting—and should be applied early in the maintenance cycle before more advanced distresses develop. [1] Under preservation guidelines it is classified as a preventive‑maintenance treatment for pavements with significant remaining service life, applied before serious damage begins to preserve the existing pavement and maintain safety. [1] It is listed among preservation treatments for sections showing functional distress—including cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling, and joint faulting—with the goal of retarding further deterioration and improving the functional condition of the highway. [1] Micro Surfacing is also applied to existing asphalt pavements primarily to remediate surface distress such as ruts, cracked surfaces and voids. [2] For the Type A mix it seals cracks, fills voids and shallow ruts less than 1/2 inch. [2] The Type B mix addresses moderate ruts ranging from 1/2 inch to 1 1⁄4 inch and can serve as a scratch, leveling or seal‑coat course. [2] The Rut Fill (RF) mix is specified for deep ruts of 2 inch in a single pass, thereby restoring ride quality. [2] The treatment is further used on pavement sections that exhibit signs of fatigue or excessive rutting, with the objective of leaving a uniform, skid‑resistant surface texture. [2] Excess emulsified asphalt (flushing) greater than 5% of the finished surface area must be avoided because such flushing would otherwise cause deterioration and loss of skid resistance. [2]

Micro Surfacing is applied to structurally sound asphalt pavements that exhibit low‑severity cracking, low to medium severity raveling or weathering, friction loss, moisture infiltration, or shallow (less than ½ inch) ruts. Its primary purpose is to fill existing ruts—shallow (less than ½ inch) or moderate (½ inch to 1 ¼ inch)—and resurface the pavement, sealing cracks and filling voids while leaving a uniform surface that restores the designed cross‑section profile. By sealing cracks, filling voids, and providing a scratch or seal coat, the treatment directly repairs damage and restores functional riding performance, including skid resistance and ride quality. Because it is classified as a pavement preservation treatment, the objective also includes extending the pavement’s service life by preserving the existing surface and retarding future deterioration. The guidance states that such treatments must occur early in the maintenance cycle before advanced distresses emerge, allowing the method to slow the progression of distress while maintaining or improving the functional condition of the section. Distress characterization and causes

When a pavement is selected for micro surfacing, the typical distress includes low severity cracking and low to medium severity raveling or weathering, friction loss, and moisture infiltration, which indicates water entering the surface layer and accelerating deterioration [1]. Moisture infiltration represents a drainage failure that allows water to penetrate the surface, promoting further distress [1]. Raveling and weathering reflect material aging and environmental exposure that weaken the aggregate matrix [1]. Structural deficiencies such as inadequate subbase material can cause premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements, contributing to accelerated distress [1]. Aggregate‑related problems, including Vanport Limestone concerns and Alkali Silica Reaction, may produce skid resistance issues and other surface deterioration even though they may not be detectable with STAMPP data [1]. Heavy traffic loading expressed as Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) data is considered when prioritizing micro‑surfacing treatments because higher loads can exacerbate existing distress [1]. Micro‑surfacing must be placed only when the surface can achieve satisfactory penetration and adhesion and the atmospheric temperature remains at least 50 °F for the entire placement, otherwise inadequate bonding can accelerate deterioration [2]. Rain or freezing temperatures expected within 24 hours after application are prohibited because water infiltration would prevent proper bonding and thus increase distress [2]. At the pre‑placement meeting any areas that show signs of fatigue or excessive rutting should be identified, as these indicate underlying structural deficiencies such as inadequate capacity or weak support that contribute to accelerated deterioration [2]. If the polymer‑modified emulsion mix takes longer than one hour and twenty minutes to set, placement must be halted because delayed setting promotes flushing and loss of bond, accelerating pavement degradation [2]. Excess emulsified asphalt greater than 5 % of the finished surface creates a smooth, shiny surface that can bleed and reduce skid resistance, thereby accelerating distress [2]. Improper spreading and compaction that produce tear‑or‑drag marks, clumps larger than 1½ in., washboard or ripple patterns beyond 100 linear feet, or longitudinal ridges exceeding a ¼‑inch bump are considered defective work and accelerate pavement deterioration [2]. Traffic must not be allowed on the newly completed surface until the mix has set sufficiently to prevent pick‑up, because premature loading can cause loss of bond and further distress [2].

Applicability and treatment selection

Micro Surfacing is appropriate for pavements that are structurally sound and do not require extensive base repair, such as those verified by FWD testing with total patching less than 10 % of the area[1]. It can also be used on flexible pavements where low‑severity cracking, low to medium severity raveling or weathering, friction loss, moisture infiltration, or minor surface irregularities are present, and when the goal is to improve skid resistance, ride quality, or fill ruts early in the maintenance cycle[1]. Rigid pavements that are structurally sound as confirmed by FWD testing may be treated provided the total patching does not exceed less than 10 % of the pavement area[1]. The treatment is especially cost‑effective on four‑lane roadways where only the travel lane is rutted, and it may be employed for rut fill when the distress is not related to base failure, including sound jointed plain or reinforced concrete pavements[1]. For traffic considerations, Micro Surfacing cures quickly and performs well under traffic provided proper controls are maintained during placement[1]. Application may be made to existing asphalt surfaces that exhibit signs of fatigue or excessive rutting identified at the pre‑placement meeting[2]. The mixture must be placed when ambient temperature is at least 50 °F for the entire placement and rain must not be imminent, nor should freezing temperatures be expected within 24 hours after application[2]. Seasonal restrictions prohibit placement from September 16 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0, 2‑0, 3‑0, 4‑0, 10‑0 and 5‑0 (Monroe, Carbon, Schuylkill Counties) and from October 1 to April 30 in Districts 6‑0, 8‑0, 9‑0, 11‑0, 12‑0 and 5‑0 (Berks, Lehigh, Northampton Counties), unless a written waiver is issued by the District Executive[2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 104–105), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 338)

Micro Surfacing should not be used when the pavement is not structurally sound or when a significant amount of base repair is required; in those cases a structural overlay or another rehabilitation method must be chosen. It is also unsuitable if the rutting or distress originates from base failure because Micro Surfacing may be used for rut fill only when the pavement distress is not related to base failure. When a Superpave scratch or leveling course is required on Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement (JPCP) or Jointed Reinforced Concrete Pavement (JRCP), another treatment should be selected instead of Micro Surfacing. Section 6.1 states that when a pavement has failed or become functionally obsolete, Micro‑surfacing—being a preservation treatment—is not appropriate and reconstruction must be pursued; similarly, if the distress calls for structural enhancement beyond what a thin surface layer can provide, rehabilitation such as a structural overlay should replace Micro‑surfacing. Section 12.3 requires that Micro Surfacing be applied early in the maintenance cycle before advanced distresses emerge; it is unsuitable when the pavement has progressed beyond those early signs, when the structure is not sound as verified by FWD testing (which requires total patching percentages of less than 10%), or when an overlay on a flexible pavement would exceed 2 inches. Section 13.7 limits Micro Surfacing to sections that have not yet reached extensive deterioration; patching greater than 10% of the pavement area is generally not cost‑effective or permitted for Pavement Preservation projects, and when distress exceeds those limits—such as large‑area patching, a need to increase structural capacity, or condition data indicating that rehabilitation or reconstruction thresholds have been met—a more extensive rehabilitation with deeper base repair or overlays, or full reconstruction, should be pursued instead of Micro Surfacing. Micro‑surfacing is not appropriate when the pavement distress exceeds the rut depths addressed by the three mix types; Type A is limited to sealing cracks, filling voids and shallow ruts that are less than ½ inch deep, Type B is used for moderate ruts ranging from ½ inch to 1 ¼ inch, and the Rut Fill (RF) mix is intended for deep ruts of about 2 inches in a single pass, so pavements with ruts deeper than these limits or defects not covered by those mixes fall outside the scope of micro‑surfacing. Micro‑surfacing is unsuitable whenever the existing surface cannot receive satisfactory penetration and adhesion, which the specification limits to periods when the atmospheric temperature is 50 °F minimum, when rain is imminent or freezing temperatures are expected within 24 hours after placement, and during the seasonal prohibitions that prohibit application from September 16 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0, 2‑0, 3‑0, 4‑0, 10‑0, 5‑0 (Monroe, Carbon, Schuylkill) and from October 1 to April 30 in Districts 6‑0, 8‑0, 9‑0, 11‑0, 12‑0, 5‑0 (Berks, Lehigh, Northampton). A pre‑placement meeting must identify any pavement areas showing signs of fatigue or excessive rutting; if such distress is present the project should consider an alternative maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair method. Condition assessment and timing

To begin the determination of micro‑surfacing need, a visual inspection of the existing surface is performed to confirm that only low‑severity cracking, low‑to‑medium raveling or weathering, friction loss, moisture infiltration, and any minor surface irregularities that can be filled are present; when those distresses are observed the pavement is considered a candidate for micro surfacing. In parallel, districts conduct automated pavement condition surveys (videologging) that record distress such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and faulting, and enter those data into treatment‑cycle matrices which flag preservation treatments. If the survey indicates a skid‑resistance deficiency, a Skid Friction test performed by BOMO must be requested and its results used to prioritize micro surfacing. Falling Weight Deflectometer testing is also employed to characterize pavement and subgrade stiffness and to quantify any required concrete patching, noting that patching greater than 10 % of the pavement area is generally not cost‑effective for preservation projects such as micro surfacing. Equivalent Single Axle Load traffic loading data may be considered when ranking candidates, but the primary triggers remain the condition survey, skid‑friction test, and FWD results combined with the treatment matrices. Prior to any placement, a preplacement meeting is held with Contractor and PennDOT personnel to visually inspect the pavement and identify any areas of concern that show signs of fatigue or excessive rutting; this inspection determines where treatment is needed. The crew must also measure ambient conditions, confirming that the atmospheric temperature is at least 50°F for the entire placement and that no rain is imminent nor freezing temperatures are expected within 24 hours after application. Finally, the schedule is checked against district‑specific seasonal prohibitions (for example, no application from September 16 to April 30 in certain districts) to ensure the treatment can proceed. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (p. 105), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (p. 338)

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, Micro Surfacing is scheduled as a seal‑coat alternative on pavement shoulders at five‑year intervals (5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40 and 45 years) when the maintenance plan calls for “Seal Coat or Micro Surface shoulders” as part of Clean and Seal or Full Depth Patching activities [1]. The Manual states that the treatment should be applied to pavements that remain structurally sound, do not require significant base repair, and exhibit only surface‑level distress such as low severity cracking, low to medium severity raveling or weathering, friction loss, moisture infiltration, or rutting that is unrelated to base failure [1]. It further notes that Micro Surfacing may be used to restore skid resistance, improve ride quality, and fill minor surface irregularities, and it should be placed early in the maintenance cycle before any advanced distresses develop, serving as a preventive‑maintenance measure for pavements still in good condition with significant remaining service life [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specification indicates that Micro‑surfacing is applied to existing pavements that have cracking, voids, or rutting, and that the specific mix type determines the depth of distress it can address: a Type A mix seals cracks, fills voids and shallow ruts less than ½ inch deep; a Type B mix fills moderate ruts ranging from ½ inch to 1 ¼ inch; and a Rut Fill (RF) mix is used to fill deep ruts up to 2 inches in a single pass [2]. The specification characterizes this range of applications as addressing early‑to‑moderate distress before further deterioration occurs [2].

Materials and repair details

Micro‑surfacing is applied as a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt paving system that is supplied in three distinct mix types. Type A is used to seal cracks, fill voids, and address shallow ruts less than ½ inch deep; Type B is employed for moderate ruts ranging from ½ inch to 1¼ inch and can serve as a scratch, leveling, seal‑coat, or surface treatment; and the Rut Fill (RF) mix is intended for deep ruts of about 2 inches in a single pass [1][2]. The mixes require a Class CQS‑1hPM polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt obtained from an approved producer and certified per the applicable sections, together with a latex‑based polymer modifier that enables rapid cure within one hour without surface damage [2]. Fine aggregate of Type B or better and coarse aggregate meeting the quality tables in Section 703 are blended to the final gradation shown in Table A, with wearing‑course aggregates required to meet at least the specified SRL and may be combined in a 50 % by weight ratio when needed [2]. When filler is necessary to maintain the percent passing the No. 200 (75 µm) sieve, approved non‑air‑entrained Type 1 Portland cement or hydrated lime is supplied, and water free of harmful soluble salts is used along with any manufacturer‑supplied additives for set‑time control, all in the weight percentages specified in Table A [2]. Production must be carried out in a self‑propelled front‑feed continuous‑loading mixing machine equipped with a chain‑dragged conveyor belt and a positive‑displacement water‑jacketed gear pump to proportion ingredients accurately; a spray bar fully wets the aggregate before it enters a continuous‑flow twin‑shaft, multi‑bladed pug mill at least 50 inches long, where aggregates are screened through a scalping screen limited to a 3/8‑inch maximum square opening for Type A material and a 1/2‑inch maximum square opening for Types B and RF, with polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt introduced beyond the first quarter point of the pug mill [2]. The spreading unit consists of a mechanical squeegee box attached to the mixer, equipped with adjustable‑shaft paddles, flexible front and rear seals, and a movable steel strike‑off bar to ensure uniform placement without skips or lumps; water may be sprayed into the spreader box to facilitate spreading, and hand spreading is performed in a small windrow with a squeegee or lute while excess material is removed at each run end [2]. The placed layer must be compacted before traffic using a pneumatic‑tire roller set to 40 psi–60 psi over the entire area, and for double applications the surface is rolled after each pass; a test strip one lane wide and between 100 ft and 550 ft long must demonstrate a set time not exceeding ten minutes and be opened to traffic within one hour [2]. For projects where micro‑surfacing replaces conventional scratch courses, Type A or Type B materials may be used instead of a 4.75 mm Superpave scratch layer on structurally sound pavements that do not require major base repair, and no Superpave scratch or leveling course should be placed before the micro‑surfacing application on jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) or jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) [1]. Micro‑surfacing scratch or leveling courses require approximately 30 % of the Superpave leveling quantity; for example, a Superpave rate of 90 lb/yd² would require 27 lb/yd² of micro‑surfacing [1]. The design guidelines call for placement at either 1½ inches of a Superpave 9.5 mm mix design or 2 inches of a Superpave 12.5 mm mix design, and the system may be used together with a 1.0‑inch 6.3 mm thin asphalt overlay to improve skid resistance, ride quality, and rut filling early in the maintenance cycle [1]. A double application is generally recommended unless the road surface is in excellent condition, and on Interstates a double application of Type A SRL‑E per Publication 408 Section 483 is advised; the specification may also require a double application to achieve the total design pounds per square yard for the surface course when called for [1][2]. Defects such as excess emulsified asphalt covering more than five percent of the area, tear or drag marks larger than specified dimensions, clumps over 1½ in., washboard ripples longer than one hundred linear feet, longitudinal seams exceeding a quarter‑inch bump, edge placement variance beyond ±3 in. per 100 ft, or unfilled ruts measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge are considered defective and must be corrected before acceptance [2]. An asphalt tack coat as referenced in Section 460.4 is also required as part of the treatment.

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 104–106), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 336–341)

Micro Surfacing delivers optimal performance when the existing pavement is structurally sound, a condition that must be verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing and limited to total patching of less than ten percent of the area [1]. The treatment requires a stable substrate for adhesion and durability and therefore should not be applied where extensive base repair or severe rutting exists, nor where fatigue cracking is evident in the pavement surface [1][2]. Prior to placement a pre‑placement meeting must confirm that the surface is sufficiently dry to allow satisfactory penetration and bonding and that ambient temperature will remain at least fifty degrees Fahrenheit for the entire operation while rain is not imminent and freezing temperatures are not expected within twenty‑four hours [2]. Existing pavements may exhibit low‑severity cracking, low to medium severity raveling or weathering, friction loss, moisture infiltration, or minor surface irregularities, all of which can be accommodated by the Micro Surfacing layer; however, advanced distresses such as fatigue cracking are prohibited [1][2]. On jointed plain concrete pavement and jointed reinforced concrete pavement no Superpave scratch or leveling course shall be placed before applying the Micro Surfacing layer [1]. The mix type must correspond to the measured rut depth, with Type A specified for shallow ruts less than one‑half inch, Type B for moderate ruts ranging from one‑half inch to one‑and‑one‑quarter inches, and the Rut Fill variant for deep ruts of approximately two inches; a double application is generally recommended unless the surface is in excellent condition, and Interstate projects may require a double application of Type A SRL‑E even on excellent surfaces [1][2]. The emulsified asphalt component shall be a Class CQS‑1hPM product supplied by an approved manufacturer, and the polymer modifier must be latex‑based and capable of curing fast enough to permit traffic within one hour without damaging the surface [2]. Fine aggregate must meet Type B or better requirements as defined in Table A, Section 703.1(c), while coarse aggregate must satisfy the quality criteria of Table B, Section 703.2(a); the final gradation shall conform to Table A and may consist of blends up to fifty percent by weight of each aggregate, and all aggregates must possess the SRL designation required for wearing courses [2]. The filler shall maintain the percent by weight passing the 75‑µm (No. 200) sieve, be a non‑air‑entrained Type 1 Portland cement or hydrated lime as specified, and water used in the mix shall be free of harmful soluble salts [2]. Any additional additives supplied by the manufacturer may be employed to control set time under varying ambient conditions provided that all materials are combined in the weight percentages prescribed in Table A and documented through a certified mix design, quality‑control plan, and JMF before placement [2]. Type A aggregates shall pass a three‑eighth‑inch scalping screen, whereas Type B or Rut Fill aggregates may be up to one‑half inch, ensuring that the polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt can adequately coat the particles; the filler must fully coat the aggregate before entering the mixer and the polymer‑modified emulsion shall be introduced only after the first quarter point of a continuous‑flow pug mill that is at least fifty inches long [2]. Mixing and spreading equipment shall be calibrated to deliver a uniform, turbulence‑maintained mix with sealed hoppers; any inconsistency in proportioning or distribution can reduce skid resistance and durability, and aggregate acceptance test results must be passed with at least ninety percent within limits before placement [2]. The mixture shall set within one hour and shall not exceed one hour and twenty minutes; if the set time exceeds these limits a warning may be issued and placement must be stopped when the upper limit is reached [2]. Proper spreading shall seal cracks, fill voids, and restore the designed rut profile without excess crowning, producing a uniform surface that contains no flushing greater than five percent of the finished area, no clumps larger than one‑and‑a‑half inches, no ripple patterns exceeding one hundred linear feet, and no longitudinal ridges, bumps or depressions greater than a quarter‑inch; seams where longitudinal passes join shall be neat with no bump, ridge, or depression greater than a quarter‑inch as measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge, and edge alignment shall remain within plus‑or‑minus three inches per one hundred feet of roadway [2]. Compliance with the specified material proportions and quality‑control measures is essential because the polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt system cures quickly and can accommodate traffic only when those controls are maintained [1].

Treatment procedures

Before work begins a Representative‑approved test strip must be built to prove the micro‑surfacing mix can be opened to traffic within one hour and achieve a maximum set time of ten minutes; during placement the mixture is spread from the box to seal cracks, fill voids and create a uniform surface, using squeegees or lutes where the spreader cannot reach, keeping material continuously in the box, optionally spraying water into it, and avoiding any lumps, balls or unmixed aggregate while making neat seams at pass joins; the mix must set within one hour of placement—if it exceeds one hour a warning is issued and if it exceeds one hour and twenty minutes placement must be stopped—and after spreading the entire area is compacted with a pneumatic‑tire roller set to 40–60 psi before traffic is allowed, which may not occur until the Representative confirms sufficient cure. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341)

The mixing plant shall be calibrated and a certified calibration sheet submitted for each mix design no later than 60 days before its use, with daily meter readings recorded on a run sheet. The contractor must submit a certified mix design at least two weeks prior to placement and obtain approval of the QC plan and JMF before any work begins; if conditions change, a revised JMF is required before proceeding, and manufacturers supply additives to control set time while the latex‑based polymer modifier shall cure fast enough to permit traffic on the treated surface within one hour without damage. Implementation begins with a pre‑placement meeting to review the specification, QC plan, aggregate delivery plan and any pavement concerns before any material is placed. The mix shall be produced in a self‑propelled front‑feed continuous‑loading mixing machine equipped with a chain‑dragged conveyor belt, a positive‑displacement water‑jacketed gear pump and a twin‑shafted pug mill at least 50 inches long; truck‑mounted units may be used only on projects less than 20,000 square yards except for limited‑access highways or municipal jobs. Placement may occur only when the entire surface is dry, the ambient temperature remains at a minimum of 50°F for the whole operation, rain is not imminent and no freezing temperatures are expected within 24 hours, and it must be avoided from September 16 to April 30 in Districts 1‑0 through 5‑0 and from October 1 to April 30 in Districts 6‑0, 8‑0, 9‑0, 11‑0, 12‑0 and 5‑0. Aggregate quantity shall be obtained from a calibrated dry weight provided by an aggregate control device with moisture deductions applied using PTM No. 513; filler shall be measured through a fines feeder control device and the polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt shall be accounted for by its weight used. A spreader box that continuously carries material shall be used, water may be sprayed into it, and squeegees or lutes shall be employed in zones the box cannot reach; when hand spreading is required a small windrow shall be laid and the mix spread with a hand squeegee. The mixture must be placed so that each pass joins in a neat seam, excess material shall be removed promptly, and the set time shall not exceed one hour—if it exceeds one hour twenty minutes placement must stop. Before traffic is permitted the surface shall be compacted using a pneumatic‑tire roller inflated to 40–60 psi, with a second roll for a double application. Traffic may not be allowed on the fresh surface until the mix has set sufficiently and the Representative authorizes opening, and test strips are prohibited on limited‑access highways. [2]

Quality and workmanship

Section 483.2 requires the inspector to verify that the emulsified asphalt is Class CQS‑1hPM from an approved producer and that the latex polymer modifier can cure fast enough to permit traffic placement within 1 hour, while confirming that fine and coarse aggregates satisfy the quality requirements of Table A and Table B and that filler, water and any other additives conform to their specified limits. The mix design—including percentages of aggregate, filler, polymer‑modified emulsion and cure time—must be submitted on a certified form at least 2 weeks before use together with an approved quality‑control plan, and each mixing unit must have a calibrated sheet dated within 60 days prior to use with daily run‑sheet meter readings recorded to calculate the percent cement or hydrated lime, total emulsion, asphalt residue and yield per square yard; mixture composition, application rate and every shipment of material must be certified on the basis of the quality‑control tests and sent to the Inspector‑In‑Charge within 1 working day after testing. Section 483.4 specifies that inspection of a micro‑surfacing job shall verify the quantities actually placed by measuring aggregate with the calibrated dry weight from the aggregate control device and correcting for naturally occurring moisture using PTM No. 513, determining filler quantity from the fines feeder control device, and computing polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt quantity by the weight used. Section 483.3 mandates a preplacement meeting with the contractor and PennDOT to review the specification, quality‑control plan, source of supply list and aggregate delivery plan while noting any pavement fatigue or excessive rutting, and placement may proceed only when the surface temperature is at least 50 °F for the entire operation and rain or freezing conditions are not expected within the next 24 hours. It further requires that all aggregates be delivered to the mixing machine screened directly into the truck using a scalping screen that does not exceed a 3/8‑inch opening for Type A material or a 1/2‑inch opening for Types B and RF, and that the mixing equipment be a self‑propelled continuous‑loading unit equipped with calibrated material‑control devices, proper filler feed placement, a spray bar, and a twin‑shaft pug mill at least 50 inches long that introduces polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt beyond the first quarter of the mixer. The spreading apparatus shall consist of a mechanical squeegee box with adjustable paddles, flexible front and rear seals, sufficient turbulence to prevent setting or side build‑up, and a movable steel strike‑off bar so that the finished surface is uniformly skid‑resistant and free of skips, lumps or tears. Section 483.3(g) requires aggregate samples taken at least two weeks before work, with a minimum three‑pound sample from each lot or sublot tested per PTM No. 616/100, and acceptance is granted only when the percent within limits is at least 90, using the single‑sample or three‑sample procedures defined for quantities under or over 500 tons; written approval of any on‑site blending must be obtained, and a day‑one test strip must set in no more than ten minutes and be open to traffic within one hour. During spreading the inspector shall verify that the surface is uniform, free of lumps, balls, unmixed aggregate, excess crowning, and that sufficient material remains in the spreader box, and must ensure that the mixture sets within one hour, with immediate cessation of placement if setting exceeds one hour and twenty minutes. The entire area shall be compacted with a pneumatic‑tire roller at 40–60 psi. Seams and edges must be within ±3 in per 100 ft and the transverse profile shall show no depressions. Final acceptance evaluates the application rate against Table C and limits defects such that flushing greater than 5 % of the finished surface, clumps larger than 1½ in, washboard or ripple patterns extending over 100 linear feet, longitudinal streaks exceeding a ¼‑inch ridge, joint bumps over a ¼‑inch, or tear/drag marks beyond specified dimensions are deemed defective work. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 336–341)

A satisfactory micro‑surfacing treatment is defined by strict material compliance as required in Section 483.2: the emulsified asphalt shall be Class CQS‑1hPM, the polymer modifier must cure fast enough to permit traffic within 1 hour without damaging the surface, aggregates must meet the quality requirements and final gradation shown in Table A (and satisfy at least the specified SRL), and filler must keep the percent by weight passing the 75 µm sieve as required. A certified mix design prepared according to TR‑483 shall be submitted at least 2 weeks before use, a calibrated mixing‑equipment sheet dated within 60 days of deployment shall accompany each mix design, daily meter readings shall be recorded on a Run Sheet to calculate material percentages and yield, and the mixture composition and application rate shall be certified based on those quality‑control tests with certifications sent to the Inspector‑In‑Charge within 1 working day after testing while every shipment shall also be certified per Section 106.03(b)3. During placement, as required in Section 483.3, the ambient temperature must be at least 50°F for the entire placement and rain or freezing conditions may not be expected within 24 hours; the prescribed seasonal restrictions for each district shall be observed; aggregates shall be screened through a scalping screen with a maximum square opening of 3/8‑inch for Type A or 1/2‑inch for Types B/RF; the mixer shall include a continuous‑flow, twin‑shafted pug mill at least 50 inches long and shall be calibrated before each mix; and the spreading equipment shall lay the material uniformly, producing a surface free of skips, lumps, tears or excessive side build‑up. The finished micro‑surfacing must be spread uniformly with no lumps, balls or unmixed aggregate and must set within one hour of placement (any set time longer than one hour and twenty minutes requires stopping placement); the surface may not contain excess emulsified asphalt flushing greater than 5 % of the area, tear or drag marks larger than 1 in by 3 in, more than twelve marks larger than ½ in by 4 in per ten feet, clumps over 1½ in diameter, washboard ripples longer than 100 ft, or longitudinal streaks exceeding a ¼‑inch ridge, bump or depression measured with a ten‑foot straightedge. Longitudinal joints shall be neat with seams no greater than a ¼‑inch bump, ridge or depression measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge and overlap not more than four inches; edges shall be placed within ±3 in horizontal variance in any hundred feet, and ruts shall be filled so that no depressions are visible on a twelve‑foot straightedge. Compaction shall be performed over the entire area using a pneumatic‑tire roller at 40–60 psi, and a test strip shall demonstrate a maximum set time of ten minutes and be open to traffic within one hour. Performance and service life

Micro Surfacing is applied to structurally sound pavements as a preservation treatment that may be considered for standard usage as an alternative to slurry seals or seal coats. When proper controls are maintained it cures quickly and performs well under traffic, restoring skid resistance, improving ride quality and filling ruts, thereby enhancing the functional performance of the surface. Because it is intended to be placed early in the maintenance cycle—before advanced distresses appear—it preserves the existing surface, retards further deterioration, and maintains or improves the functional condition without substantially increasing structural capacity. By restoring the pavement almost to its original condition, the treatment can postpone costly rehabilitation and reconstruction, which extends the expected service life of the roadway. The cost‑effective preservation strategy thus enhances durability by allowing the pavement to remain in good condition for a longer period before reconstruction is required. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (p. 104), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–213), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Typical micro‑surfacing defects arise when the finished surface does not meet the uniformity limits described in Section 483.3(l): excess emulsified asphalt (“flushing”) greater than five percent of the area, tear or drag marks larger than one inch wide and three inches long (or more than twelve marks exceeding one‑half inch wide and four inches long per ten feet), clumps or foreign objects over 1½ inches in diameter, washboard or ripple patterns longer than one hundred linear feet, and longitudinal streaks with ridges, bumps or depressions greater than a quarter‑inch measured on a ten‑foot straightedge. These distresses are usually caused by improper spreading and finishing—such as over‑filling ruts, allowing lumps or unmixed aggregate, using an incorrect application rate, or failing to control set time—because the specification requires that the mixture be spread uniformly, excess material be removed at run ends, and the mix set within one hour (or be stopped if it exceeds one hour twenty minutes). Additional failures such as mis‑aligned longitudinal joints (>¼‑inch bump) or edge placement variance beyond ±3 inches per 100 ft also stem from poor seam making and edge control during the spreading operation. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341)

Monitoring and follow-up

After micro‑surfacing is placed, the Representative must visually inspect the entire treated area to confirm that a uniform surface texture meets the finished‑surface criteria listed in Section 483.3(l), such as no excess emulsified asphalt greater than 5 % of the surface, no tear or drag marks exceeding the size limits, no clumps larger than 1½ in., and no washboard or ripple patterns longer than 100 linear feet. The mix must also be verified to have set within one hour before any traffic is allowed, and traffic may only resume when the Representative gives direction. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 339–341)

When annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (STAMPP) begin to show distress such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling or weathering, joint spalling or faulting on a section that received micro‑surfacing, the condition matrices will trigger a move from preservation to routine maintenance, rehabilitation or reconstruction.[1] In addition, if skid‑friction testing performed by BOMO confirms a verified skid resistance problem, corrective action must be addressed in the district plan.[1] A further indicator is when required patching exceeds ten percent of the pavement area, which is generally not cost‑effective for a micro‑surfacing preservation project and signals that more extensive repair or rehabilitation is needed.[1] Under the Defective Work provisions, a micro‑surfaced area signals the need for further maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation when excess emulsified asphalt (flushing) exceeds five percent of the finished surface.[2] It also indicates a problem when tear or drag marks larger than one inch wide and three inches long are observed.[2] The presence of clumps or foreign objects greater than 1½ inches in diameter likewise requires corrective action.[2] Washboard or ripple patterns that extend more than one hundred linear feet are another condition that triggers additional work.[2] Longitudinal streaks, seams, ridges, bumps or depressions that exceed a quarter‑inch tolerance measured with a straightedge also indicate that the micro‑surfacing has not met performance criteria and corrective action is required.[2] Edge placement variations of more than plus or minus three inches over any one hundred feet constitute a deficiency requiring further maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation.[2] Finally, ruts that still show measurable depressions are considered a surface deficiency that signals the need for additional work.[2]
