Pavement Preservation

Part of: PennDOT Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242) is-subtopic-of
Pavement Preservation is a topic under PennDOT Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242).
pavement-preservation
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-30

Pavement preservation is a preventive‑maintenance strategy applied to pavements that retain adequate structural capacity but are beginning to show surface‑level deficiencies such as cracking, rutting or loss of texture, with the goal of restoring functional condition while delaying more extensive rehabilitation [1][2]. It operates by intervening early in the maintenance cycle and applying thin, high‑friction or rejuvenating treatments—such as micro‑surfacing or diamond grinding—to improve ride quality, skid resistance and profile uniformity without substantially increasing structural depth [1][2]. By preserving the existing pavement’s performance rather than adding structural capacity, these actions extend its useful service life and postpone costly reconstruction [1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Pavement Preservation” also include the following subtopics: Asphalt Patching, Base Repair, Concrete Patching, Corrective Maintenance, Crack Sealing, Cross-Stitching, Diamond Grinding, Dowel Bar Retrofit, High Friction Surface Treatment, Joint Rehabilitation, Joint Sealing, Maintenance Cycle, Micro Surfacing, Preventive Maintenance, Rut Filling, Seal Coat, Slab Stabilization, Slurry Seal, and Surface Treatment.

The Maintenance Cycle orchestrates these interventions by matching route conditions to prescribed treatments such as Crack Sealing, Joint Sealing, and Micro Surfacing to retard deterioration without adding structural capacity. [1][2] Preventive Maintenance programs deploy these surface-level actions alongside Diamond Grinding and Slurry Seal on sound pavements, while Corrective Maintenance addresses isolated defects like those repaired by Asphalt Patching or Concrete Patching that threaten immediate safety. [1][2] When base support fails, Base Repair restores load distribution, whereas Slab Stabilization fills subgrade voids to eliminate rocking; for rigid pavements with specific joint issues, Joint Rehabilitation and Cross-Stitching restore continuity, and Dowel Bar Retrofit recovers transverse load transfer. [1][2] High Friction Surface Treatment provides specialized skid resistance on polished surfaces, and Rut Filling reprofiles depressed lanes using polymer-modified emulsions within the defined structural limits. [1][2] The Maintenance Cycle coordinates these interventions by matching route characteristics and condition indicators to specific preservation treatments such as Micro Surfacing, Slurry Seal, or Seal Coat for surface-level distress. [1][2] Surface Treatment serves as the general category for applying thin protective layers to sound pavements, with High Friction Surface Treatment providing specialized skid resistance where aggregate polishing is a concern. [1][2]

Purpose and treatment objectives

Pavement preservation programs are directed at surface‑level deficiencies that erode the functional condition of a roadway while the underlying structural capacity remains adequate [1][2]. The treatments target early‑stage distresses such as surface cracking—including non‑working cracks—edge spalling, corner breaks, loss of texture, rutting, raveling or weathering, and concrete joint spalling or faulting that can admit water and accelerate failure [1]. For rigid pavements the preservation effort is initiated when longitudinal roughness exceeds a maximum International Roughness Index of 70 inches per mile on facilities with posted speeds above 45 mph (or 90 inches per mile where lower speeds apply) [2]. Additional performance problems include deviations greater than one‑quarter inch over twelve feet, ridges exceeding one‑eighth inch, and side‑to‑side differentials beyond one‑sixteenth inch, all of which are corrected to restore a uniform riding surface [2]. Settled depressions or low spots that cause the profile to fall below design grade are also addressed; grout stabilization and jacking are employed so that the surface lies within ±0.02 ft of the intended grade and upward movement is limited to 0.05 inch, with any lift exceeding 0.10 ft prompting slab removal and replacement [2]. Longitudinal cracks or joint separations that permit water ingress are routed to a width of three‑quarters inch and a depth of one inch before being sealed or cross‑stitched to reestablish structural continuity, while flexible‑pavement cracks from one‑quarter to one inch are cleaned, dried and filled level with asphalt sealant [2]. The guides also specify that crack sealing on the resurfacing network be performed on a three‑to‑five‑year cycle and concrete joint sealing on a five‑year cycle to maintain safety, ride quality, and accessibility [1]. Micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, mill‑and‑overlay, chip sealing, diamond grinding, dowel‑bar retrofits, base repair, and thin overlays are employed to fill ruts, improve skid resistance, and restore smoothness before the listed distresses progress to advanced cracking or structural collapse [1]. By intervening early in the maintenance cycle, these preventive measures aim to preserve the existing pavement’s functional condition, retard further deterioration, and extend service life without substantially increasing structural capacity [1][2].

Across the guides, pavement preservation is described as a preventive‑maintenance strategy whose primary aim is to extend the useful service life of existing roadways while maintaining or restoring their functional condition [1]. The approach works by preventing new damage and retarding the progression of existing distress through coordinated, cost‑effective treatments such as surface rejuvenation, micro‑surfacing, milling, leveling, diamond grinding, base repair or concrete patching [1]. When localized failures such as unsound or delaminated concrete are encountered, preservation actions specifically call for removal of the defective material and installation of new pavement to restore structural integrity [2]. Consequently, the combined objective includes preventing deterioration, slowing its progression, repairing damage, restoring functionality, and extending the pavement’s service life [1][2].

Distress characterization and causes

The distress that initiates pavement preservation is observed within a State Route segment defined by the department as roughly a half‑mile stretch bounded by specific termini. Within this corridor, surveys quantify the extent of deterioration as a proportion of the segment’s area; for instance, when the most recent performance survey indicates that about thirty percent of the segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses, microsurfacing is required. In addition to surface‑wide distress, the treatment targets transverse joints and cracks distributed across the slab surface. Any joint or crack exhibiting a loaded‑slab corner deflection of at least 0.02 inch together with a joint efficiency at the 65 percent threshold (or higher when approved) is classified for stabilization. Severity is further gauged by the magnitude of vertical movement; upward displacement exceeding 0.05 inch is deemed unacceptable and may trigger work stoppage, slab replacement, or emergency repair. Progression of these conditions is tracked annually through performance surveys and repeated deflection testing, comparing current values against established limits; continued deterioration that reaches a harmful or unsafe level authorizes immediate emergency actions. The operational impact includes the possibility of road closures, traffic halts during grouting operations, and a mandatory minimum twelve‑hour cure period before reopening to traffic, as well as liability for liquidated damages until the pavement is restored. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

The distress that pavement preservation aims to mitigate originates from several interrelated mechanisms that degrade both rigid and flexible surfaces. [1][2] Moisture entering the structure through non‑working cracks, poorly cured joints, or inadequately finished surfaces penetrates the surface layers, accelerating deterioration and producing cracking, honey‑combing, edge slumping, and other defects that cannot be remedied by simple patching. [1][2] When slab thickness falls more than half an inch below the design value or when compressive strength and plastic air content fall below 55 % of specified limits, the structural capacity is insufficient to resist traffic stresses, leading to premature mid‑slab cracking, excessive slab movement, and vertical lifts that exceed acceptable thresholds. [2] Age‑related loss of binder flexibility in flexible pavements reduces their ability to accommodate repeated loading, promoting top‑down surface cracks that are further intensified by environmental exposure. [1] Certain aggregates, such as Vanport Limestone or those susceptible to alkali‑silica reaction, generate internal stresses that manifest as cracking, spalling, and loss of cohesion. [1] Drainage deficiencies—particularly the lack of a feather pass extending at least two feet into adjacent shoulders or gore areas—allow slurry and water to infiltrate the pavement structure, compounding moisture‑related distress. [2] Deficient subbase support beneath jointed concrete slabs reduces load‑bearing capacity and contributes to premature cracking. [1] Environmental loading, including temperature cycles and moisture, compounds these effects, producing raveling, edge weathering, rutting, and joint faulting observed in condition surveys. [1] Heavy traffic loads induce loaded‑corner deflections of 0.02 inch or greater and vertical movements exceeding 0.05 inch, which accelerate distress when structural capacity is inadequate. [2] Localized ground loss from subsidence or edge slump creates depressed zones that concentrate stresses and hasten deterioration. [2] Collectively, water infiltration, drainage failure, insufficient structural capacity, weak support, material aging, reactive aggregates, poor construction practices, environmental exposure, and heavy loads accelerate pavement degradation, necessitating preservation treatments such as crack sealing, chip seals, micro‑surfacing, thin overlays, diamond grinding, grooving, or cement‑grout stabilization. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 389–452), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Applicability and treatment selection

Preservation is considered suitable when the existing pavement still possesses adequate structural integrity despite surface distress, allowing it to be scarified and mixed in‑place or to receive a thin high‑friction overlay.[1][2] For hot‑in‑place recycling the pavement must be an asphalt layer that can be scarified to at least 1½ in. depth, while for high‑friction surface treatment the substrate may be either asphalt or concrete provided it has cured for a minimum of thirty days or twenty‑eight days respectively.[1][2] The decision also depends on average daily traffic; higher traffic volumes require careful evaluation before applying hot‑in‑place recycling, whereas high‑friction treatments demand that the work zone be kept clear of equipment and vehicles until the surface has cured.[1][2] Cold‑weather protection is required for recycled material, so an overlay must be placed before winter and any subsequent chip seal or micro‑surfacing postponed at least four weeks, while high‑friction applications are limited to wind speeds below fifteen miles per hour with gusts under twenty miles per hour during placement.[1][2] The surface to receive high‑friction treatment must be thoroughly cleaned and prepared, and any cracks wider than a quarter inch must be pretreated before the application.[2] Traffic must be managed to maintain an unobstructed passage through the work zone while the treatment cures.[2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (p. 131); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 581–583)

Pavement preservation treatments are intended for pavements that remain in good condition and retain sufficient structural capacity, and they must be applied early in the maintenance cycle before advanced distresses emerge [1]. When a pavement has failed or become functionally obsolete—evidenced by extensive age‑related cracking, advanced distresses, loss of remaining service life, or subbase deficiencies—it cannot be restored to required performance with thin overlays, seal coats, or crack sealing, and reconstruction or a more substantial rehabilitation that adds structural depth must be selected [1]. Projects that require increasing the pavement’s structural thickness, providing new travel lanes, or altering existing safety features such as lowering guide‑rail height below minimum criteria also fall outside the preservation scope and call for alternative maintenance or rehabilitation strategies [1]. The preservation matrix imposes strict thickness limits; flexible‑pavement overlays greater than two inches and concrete overlays exceeding four and a half inches when a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing course is used—or five inches with a 12.5 mm mix—are excluded from preservation and must follow the 3R criteria [1]. Patching operations that would cover more than ten percent of the surface area are generally not permitted for preservation projects unless a cost‑effectiveness justification can be demonstrated, and any concrete patching exceeding this threshold requires another treatment approach [1]. Operational enhancements such as auxiliary or weaving lanes are permissible only when they are no longer than three thousand feet and the adjacent travel lanes being preserved extend for at least ten thousand contiguous feet (excluding bridges); larger additions exceed preservation limits [1]. Full‑depth repairs are required for conditions like premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements, aggregate problems such as Vanport limestone or alkali‑silica reaction, bridge constraints, or other geometric issues, which lie beyond the scope of preservation and necessitate rehabilitation or reconstruction [1]. In addition, preservation is deemed unsuitable when performance criteria indicate that distress levels have surpassed warranty thresholds—for example, a year‑5 survey showing that thirty percent of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses—prompting remedial actions such as microsurfacing rather than routine preservation treatments [2]. If individual repair patches would be spaced less than one hundred feet apart, the guidance requires combining them into a single continuous repair, indicating that isolated preservation work is not appropriate under those conditions [2]. Moreover, during the warranty period the department does not perform routine maintenance activities such as crack sealing or base repairs; any condition requiring those actions must be addressed by other remediation methods outside the preservation program [2].

Condition assessment and timing

Determining whether pavement preservation is required starts with a full‑length walking field inspection of the entire segment, during which the inspector records drainage conditions, shoulder widths, cross‑slope, geometric features and any crash‑cluster information. The visual survey documents surface distress such as unstable concrete slabs that rock under traffic, delaminations detected by sounding with a steel bar or drag chain, flushing, potholes, fatigue cracking, cracks larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch, joint offsets, edge deterioration, raveling, spalling, faulting and rutting, and quantifies their severity. Cross‑slope uniformity is checked at 100‑foot intervals and any deviation greater than a quarter‑inch over a twelve‑foot length is noted as a defect. Ride quality is measured with the International Roughness Index and friction testing, and when a skid‑resistance deficiency is suspected a formal skid‑friction test is performed. The guides specify maximum IRI values of 70 inches per mile for facilities with posted speeds above 45 mph and 90 inches per mile where speeds are 45 mph or lower, and the Modified Roughness Index is compared to prescribed limits, with verification testing on at least ten percent of lane miles using an inertial lightweight profiler. Rut depth is verified with a twelve‑foot straightedge across the lane width, and individual bumps or irregularities greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch (straightedge) or ridges/valleys exceeding one‑eighth of an inch measured with a transverse‑profile gauge are flagged for grinding or resurfacing. Structural capacity is evaluated through Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, which provides pavement and subgrade modulus data and indicates the percentage of area that would require patching; preservation work is generally limited to ten percent of the surface unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is shown. Deflection testing of every joint or crack is performed according to ASTM D4695 when ambient temperature is between 40 °F and 70 °F, and a loaded slab‑corner deflection of at least 0.02 inch together with a joint efficiency of 65 percent or higher (or less than 65 percent for patching) designates the location for stabilization; if temperature limits prevent testing, visual verification of pumping or poor joint performance is used as an alternative screening method. When joint faulting exceeds one‑quarter inch it is measured and milled, and if faulting exceeds one‑half inch a scratch course is prescribed before preservation work proceeds. Core samples are extracted—typically six‑inch diameter cores—from areas showing fatigue cracking or other signs of base failure, and the cores are tested for void content, asphalt percentage and gradation according to PTM 715, PTM 702 (or approved methods) and PTM 739 in accordance with AASHTO T209 to verify existing layer thickness, integrity and joint condition before any milling depth is set. Traffic loading information, including average daily traffic, truck proportion and calculated equivalent single axle loads, is incorporated together with the collected condition data into Pavement Condition/Treatment matrices that are used for project prioritization and to confirm that ESAL estimates remain within contract values. Existing pavement history, prior surface treatments and, where slab stabilization is contemplated, relevant geotechnical data are also gathered to complete the baseline supporting selection and extent of preservation treatment. Annual performance surveys conducted in accordance with Publication 336 automatically identify and measure distresses; manual supplemental surveys address flushing and potholes, and if a five‑year survey shows that at least thirty percent of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial preservation such as microsurfacing is triggered. A field quality‑control plan documents the existing roadway profile, including depressions and the intended grade line, and a Roadway Profile Report comparing current and design grades is prepared; significant deviations may require additional grout injection or other preservation actions before work acceptance. [1][2]

Pavement preservation is initiated while the roadway remains functionally serviceable and in generally good condition but has begun to exhibit early‑stage distress such as minor rutting, loss of skid resistance, increased surface roughness, incipient cracking or edge deterioration, with no advanced cracking or structural failure present [1]. The pavement must still possess a substantial amount of remaining service life, and for rigid sections it should be confirmed structurally sound through falling‑weight deflectometer testing, while any required patching should be confined to less than ten percent of the surface area [1]. Across the guides, preservation treatments are triggered when measurable deterioration thresholds are reached; for example, a five‑year performance survey that identifies at least thirty percent of a segment’s area containing two or more low‑severity distresses mandates the application of microsurfacing or another approved preservation method [2]. Additional trigger points include joints or cracks whose loaded slab corner deflection equals or exceeds 0.02 inch, with joint efficiency of sixty‑five percent or higher requiring full cement‑grout stabilization and lower efficiencies permitting patching and stabilization when authorized [2]. Any crack or joint that has widened beyond one‑eighth inch should be sealed, and any pothole or gouge deeper than one inch must be repaired prior to placing an overlay [2]. The preservation treatments themselves are limited in thickness so that micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, or thin mill‑and‑overlay applications typically range from one and a half to two inches, and overlays exceeding two inches on flexible pavements are not classified as preservation interventions [1].

Materials and repair details

Pavement preservation relies on a defined inventory of surface‑treatment materials that are identified by product codes, including a micro‑surfacing mix used for slurry seals and ultrathin friction courses (code 711‑7134‑01), a seal‑coat material (code 711‑7124‑01), an asphalt crack‑sealing product (code 711‑7128‑01), a concrete joint‑sealing product (code 711‑7147‑01), and paver finishers specified as either one‑inch thick (code 711‑7125‑01) or thicker than one inch (code 711‑7135‑01) [1]. Ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses are limited to three‑quarters of an inch, thin overlays and mill‑and‑resurface layers are generally no more than two inches in thickness, and mill‑and‑overlay treatments range from one‑and‑a‑half to two inches [1].

Crack sealing begins with cleaning the surface using a mild detergent, rinsing, drying, and removing loose material, old sealant, crack filler or thermoplastic markings; debris is cleared with compressed air at a minimum of 100 psi [2]. Cracks that are at least one‑quarter inch wide are cleaned, then a mixed binder proportioned within ±2 % of the manufacturer’s volume specifications is spread to a uniform film thickness of roughly fifty to sixty‑five mils using a serrated squeegee; while the binder remains wet, aggregate is placed at a rate of twelve to fifteen pounds per square yard and worked into a smooth layer, with any overfilled cracks scarified flush after cure [2]. For openings from one‑quarter inch up to one inch an asphalt sealant is applied level with the pavement surface, producing a film thickness between one‑thirty‑second and one‑sixteenth inch and spanning roughly one to three inches in width; wider openings receive Asphalt Wearing Course 4.75 mm [2]. Seal‑coat applications employ product code 711‑7124‑01 and are prohibited when ambient temperature falls below forty degrees Fahrenheit or exceeds ninety degrees unless written approval is obtained, with all heating and mixing staying within the manufacturer’s time limits [2][1].

Joint sealing uses low‑modulus, non‑sag silicone sealants that meet ASTM criteria for tensile strength, elongation, extrusion rate, specific gravity, durometer hardness, shelf life, ozone/UV resistance and bond strength to cement mortar, as well as rubberized joint sealants conforming to ASTM D6690 (Type I, II or IV) [2]. Pre‑formed neoprene compression seals must stay within tight dimensional tolerances, while closed‑cell polyethylene fillers are required to compress twenty‑five to eighty‑five percent under loads of five to thirty psi; polyurethane foam joint fillers are supplied in minimum eight‑foot lengths, widths of approximately four and a half to four and three‑quarters inches, depths ranging from seven and three‑quarters to thirteen inches, densities of six to ten pounds per cubic inch, and must recover at least sixty‑five percent after compression [2].

Dowel‑bar retrofits require slots filled with a quarter‑inch thick pre‑formed cellular polystyrene filler cut to match the slot cross‑section, a one‑and‑a‑half inch non‑metallic expansion cap positioned with a quarter‑inch clearance beneath it, and plastic or non‑metallic chairs that maintain at least a half‑inch gap from all slot surfaces; vertical tilt or horizontal skew of each dowel end is limited to a maximum of a quarter inch and longitudinal translation may not exceed one inch, while Type B coated dowels receive a bond‑breaker lubricant an hour before concrete placement followed by epoxy applied per the manufacturer’s instructions [2].

Full‑depth concrete patches—either normal or accelerated strength cement concrete—are limited to lengths between six and twenty feet (or longer when required) but must never be less than one lane width; each patch is laid out as a rectangle extending approximately three inches beyond delaminated edges, with minimum dimensions of fifteen by ten inches and a depth of at least two inches, not exceeding half the slab thickness [2]. Full‑depth saw cuts are made at the existing pavement/patch interface, transverse load‑transfer dowels are installed in the faces of the existing slab, a sealant reservoir is created and the joint sealed; prior to placing concrete, two layers of minimum four‑mil polyethylene sheeting serve as bond breakers, while longitudinal joints receive a quarter‑inch thick polystyrene board as a full‑depth bond breaker and transverse joints receive three‑quarters inch expansion joint material (half‑inch when abutting an existing joint) [2]. Edge spall repairs are not accepted as satisfactory remedies for edge slumping or spalling adjacent to lanes [2].

Saw‑cutting of existing slabs is limited to a maximum length of twenty feet across the lane width, a depth of approximately one inch (about two‑thirds of slab thickness), and a width no greater than one‑half inch; cuts must be full depth and are performed with a diamond‑tipped blade equipped with a guard, alignment guides, water cooling and cut‑depth control [2][1]. A second transverse saw cut at least one inch deep is made at each end of a patch area, wheel saws may use carbide steel tips but must not penetrate the subbase more than half an inch, and jackhammers used for removal are limited to thirty pounds in weight [2].

After fracturing, a minimum fifty‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller with four to seven rubber wheels inflated to at least one hundred psi makes three passes for the crack‑and‑seat method or five passes for break‑and‑seat; the void beneath each fractured slab is backfilled with Subbase No. 2A and capped with Flexible Base Replacement, after which the surface is swept, tack‑coated as required, and overlaid within one week with plant‑mixed asphalt concrete unless another material receives written approval [2][1].

Diamond grinding employs a self‑propelled machine weighing at least forty thousand pounds and equipped with a grinding head no narrower than four feet; the diamond blade stack consists of one‑eighth inch blades spaced by one‑thirty‑second inch spacers, producing parallel grooves between 0.09 and 0.13 inch wide with land areas between 0.060 and 0.110 inch and a groove depth of about one‑sixteenth inch; the finished surface must achieve an International Roughness Index of no more than seventy inches per mile where posted speeds exceed forty‑five miles per hour, or ninety inches per mile where speeds are forty‑five miles per hour or less, with ridges not exceeding one‑eighth inch and texture coverage at least ninety‑eight percent; a feather pass extending at least two feet onto the shoulders must continue for a minimum of one hundred feet [2].

Grout stabilization may use cement grout or high‑density polyurethane grout; injection holes are limited to five‑eighths inch for polyurethane and one‑and‑a‑half inch for cement, pressures may reach up to two hundred psi, slab lift tolerances are plus or minus 0.02 foot for reinforced concrete (plus or minus 0.03 foot for jointed slabs), upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited, and traffic may not be reopened until at least thirty minutes after completion [2].

When asphalt grout stabilization is specified, a fully insulated asphalt tanker equipped with a removable access hatch and a minimum two‑inch overflow pipe supplies heated asphalt (380 °F–450 °F) pumped at 25 to 45 psi through vertical holes up to one‑and‑a‑half inch in diameter; tapered cylindrical plugs are inserted about two inches deep, a splash shield of at least fifteen square feet protects passing traffic, and slab lift must remain below five‑hundredths of an inch before traffic resumes [2].

If a geotextile interlayer is required, all pits larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch are filled with approved patch material and compacted; the fabric’s edges overlap by eight inches plus or minus two inches, extend at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, and no more than three layers may be overlapped in any location [2].

All vertical elements that will contact new asphalt—curbs, gutters and similar structures—receive a uniform coating of emulsified asphalt meeting PennDOT Material Class TACK (or an equivalent hot binder), applied in at least two layers; prior to any overlay a tack coat of the same material is applied to the cleaned surface, allowing full break‑through time before placement of new pavement [2][1].

Hot‑in‑place recycling begins by heating the pavement surface with propane‑fired firebrick heaters to approximately 300 °F ±25 °F, then scarifying the existing layer to a minimum depth of one‑and‑a‑half inches; a predetermined quantity of recycling agent is blended into the reclaimed material and redistributed by a heated screed, with equipment capable of treating lane widths from ten feet to fourteen‑and‑a‑half feet; after HIR a four‑week cure period precedes any chip seal or similar surface treatment, although hot‑mix overlays may be placed immediately [1].

Cold in‑place recycling of the base is permitted to a maximum depth of four inches, followed by an asphalt overlay not exceeding one and a half inches and a 60 lb/yd² scratch course [1].

For concrete decks, first‑time overlays must meet minimum depths—typically four‑and‑a‑half inches with a Superpave 9.5 mm mix or five inches with a 12.5 mm mix—and ultra‑thin bonded concrete overlays of two inches serve as non‑structural wearing layers [1].

Preventive repair actions across the preservation program include asphalt crack sealing, chip sealing, slurry or micro‑surfacing, thin and ultrathin hot‑mix overlays, diamond grinding, dowel‑bar retrofits, localized base repairs, and isolated, partial, or full‑depth concrete patching; full‑depth concrete patching and joint rehabilitation are limited to no more than ten percent of the pavement area [1][2].

Project documentation must detail the extent and type of patching, milling depths, joint repairs and sealing, shoulder work, subsurface drainage, any special material provisions, and may be supported by Falling Weight Deflectometer results and geotechnical data for slab stabilization where necessary [1].

The condition of the existing pavement—captured through field measurements such as International Roughness Index, surface friction, cross‑slope, and a survey of distress types and severity—is the primary determinant of which preservation technique is suitable [1]. Traffic exposure, expressed by average daily traffic and the proportion of trucks, further influences selection because higher loads require treatments with greater structural capacity [1]. Structural adequacy is verified either by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing or by core inspections to confirm that the pavement can support the intended preservation without premature failure [1]. For flexible pavements, preservation overlays are limited to a maximum thickness of two inches unless milling is performed to remove defective layers; exceeding this limit moves the work out of the preservation category [1]. Rigid concrete pavements may receive preservation overlays up to four and one‑half inches when a 9.5 mm mix is used, or up to five inches with a 12.5 mm mix, provided other criteria are met [1]. Early intervention—applying treatments while the pavement remains in good condition and retains significant remaining service life—produces the greatest bond durability and overall performance [1][2]. The slab itself must meet minimum material properties, including a compressive strength of at least 4 925 psi, a flexural strength not less than 650 psi, and percent‑within‑limits values of at least 55 % for both compressive strength and plastic air content [2]. Slab thickness must be within one‑half inch of the design value, joint and crack differentials cannot exceed one‑sixteenth inch, and vertical joint misalignment is limited to a quarter‑inch before traffic may resume [2]. Surface defects such as rain damage, improper joint construction, curing flaws, honeycombing, spalling, depressions or high points beyond specified limits must be repaired prior to any preservation work [2][1]. Cracks up to 0.007 in are sealed with a high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate after cleaning, followed by a dry‑blast sand broadcast about twenty minutes later; wider cracks receive pretreatment but are not overfilled, using appropriate cellular polystyrene or expansion‑joint material depending on width [2]. All longitudinal joints and cracks must be cleaned immediately before sealant placement to ensure proper bonding [2]. When an overlay is placed, only plant‑mixed asphalt concrete may be used unless another product receives written approval, and the underlying base must achieve greater than 92 % of its theoretical maximum density with percent‑asphalt and gradation values within the mix‑design tolerances [2]. Hot‑in‑place sealant binders are mixed to within ±2 % of the manufacturer’s volume specifications and applied at a uniform thickness of 50–65 mils together with an aggregate broadcast rate of 12–15 lb per square yard after the surface has been shot‑blasted clean of oils, debris, curing compounds, carbonation, and loose material [2]. Sealant materials must satisfy performance criteria that include a tensile strength measured at 150 % elongation under ASTM D412 after a seven‑day cure at 77 °F ± 3 °F and 45–55 % relative humidity, appropriate durometer hardness, ozone and ultraviolet resistance per ASTM C793, sufficient flow, and the ability to develop the prescribed bond strength to cement mortar, often requiring priming [2]. Polymeric polyurethane systems require precise control of mixing temperature and component ratios because any deviation alters flow and strength; injection is placed through vertical holes no larger than 5⁄8 in drilled to a depth that fully penetrates any stabilized base, and pavement uplift greater than 0.05 in during placement is prohibited [2]. High‑density asphalt grout must be heated between 380 °F and 450 °F before pumping; material hotter than about 500 °F is rejected, and it is pumped at pressures generally ranging from 25 to 45 psi with a continuous injection pressure limit of 200 psi, short‑term peaks up to 300 psi permitted only as directed, and brief spikes to 600 psi allowed when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade [2]. Proper equipment—including insulated tankers, reversible pumps, three‑way nozzles and appropriately sized hoses—must be compatible with the hot mix to prevent premature cooling or leakage [2]. For grout‑based slab stabilization, pressure control is critical; continuous pressures may not exceed 200 psi, short‑term pressures up to 300 psi are allowed only as directed, and brief spikes to 600 psi are permissible when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade [2]. Diamond‑grinding preservation requires equipment that does not cause spalling at joints or fracture surface aggregates, limits blade‑pass overlap to one inch, and produces a texture free of depressions greater than one‑eighth inch over a twelve‑foot straightedge while maintaining the prescribed cross‑slope and drainage tolerances [2]. Subbase deficiencies that lead to premature mid‑slab cracking in jointed concrete sections reduce the effectiveness of sealants and overlays, so subgrade support must be confirmed through geotechnical data before slab stabilization is contemplated [1][2]. Aggregate characteristics such as Vanport limestone or susceptibility to alkali‑silica reaction can create skid‑resistance problems, especially when processes like diamond grinding are used, and these issues must be identified even if they are not captured by standard surface distress monitoring [1]. In hot‑in‑place recycling, sufficient heat penetration into the underlying layer enhances interlayer bonding, which is a prerequisite for long‑term performance [1]. When geotextile interlayers are employed on rigid pavements, the slab must first be stabilized; any unstable slabs or fragments that rock under traffic require repair, and surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be patched before the fabric is laid flat on a clean surface [2]. The geotextile fabric is overlapped by eight ± 2 in, no more than three layers may be stacked, and its temperature must remain below 120 °F prior to overlay placement [2]. Patching activities should not exceed ten percent of the surface area unless a cost‑effective justification is provided; for concrete slabs, cracks wider than one‑eighth inch and potholes deeper than one inch are routed to a half‑inch depth, blown dry with air at least 100 psi, and filled with specified fillers or sealants [1][2]. Ambient temperature constraints require that sealant placement be prohibited when the air temperature is below 40 °F or above 90 °F unless written approval is obtained; grout work may not commence if daytime temperatures are under 40 °F or if the subgrade or base course is frozen, and all work must be performed only when both ambient and pavement surface temperatures exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit [2][1]. Finally, performance monitoring after five years dictates that if at least thirty percent of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action is required, underscoring the need for proper material selection and compatibility from the outset [2].

Treatment procedures

Preparation begins with a field quality‑control plan prepared and submitted at least fourteen days before work, identifying qualified personnel, equipment, saw‑cut locations and traffic protection measures. Existing markings, rumble strips and nearby utility openings are covered, the pavement is washed with a mild detergent, rinsed, and dried with hot compressed air while ambient and surface temperatures are verified to be no lower than 40 °F (and for flexible sealants not above 90 °F unless an exception is granted). The existing surface condition is then evaluated by coring and inspecting the cores to determine pavement depth and integrity, which establishes the required milling or scarifying depth—generally between one‑and‑a‑half and two inches for flexible pavements. Deflection testing under the same temperature limits is performed to locate joints or cracks that require stabilization, and any unstable slabs, slab fragments or faulting joints exceeding a quarter‑inch are repaired or milled before preservation treatment proceeds. For milling or scarifying, material is removed either by conventional milling or by hot‑in‑place recycling scarification; when the latter is used the surface is heated with propane‑fired firebrick trucks until it reaches roughly 300 °F (±25 °F) through radiant heat and then scarified to at least a 1½‑inch depth. A measured quantity of recycling agent is introduced, blended while being laid by a heated screed, and the freshly placed recycled mix is compacted to achieve target density; similar compacting is performed for micro‑surfacing coats, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, Superpave overlays or cold‑in‑place recycle capped with a 1½‑inch asphalt cover. When an asphalt overlay is applied over concrete, transverse joints are sawed and sealed to preserve cross‑slope continuity, and any full‑depth joint repairs on concrete are verified by falling‑weight deflectometer testing and may be finished with diamond grinding for smoothness. Diamond grinding of concrete surfaces employs a self‑propelled machine weighing at least 40 000 lb with a minimum four‑foot grinding head; a feather pass is made along shoulders for drainage, then the entire surface is ground longitudinally with overlap limited to two inches (one inch where specified), staying at least two feet from curbs, producing parallel grooves 0.09–0.13 in wide, lands 0.060–0.110 in, and a depth of about 1/16 in; slurry is removed continuously and the pavement is cleaned before traffic returns. For concrete patching or spall repair, full‑depth longitudinal saw cuts are made first with a diamond‑tipped blade along the perimeter of the intended patch, followed by transverse cuts; deteriorated concrete is then removed in one or more pieces while protecting adjacent subbase and pavement, unsuitable subbase material is replaced, and formwork is set on all sides not in contact with remaining pavement. Grout, polyurethane or high‑density asphalt stabilization requires vertical round holes drilled to specified diameters (no larger than 5/8 in for polyurethane, up to 3/8 in larger than a tie‑bar for epoxy, and not larger than 1½ in for cement or asphalt grout) and to a depth that penetrates any stabilized base; as each drill is withdrawn compressed air is blown into the hole for five to fifteen seconds to clear debris. Grout components are mixed on site within ±2 % of manufacturer volume specifications, heated (asphalt grout to 380–450 °F, rejecting material above 500 °F), and pumped at pressures not exceeding 25–45 psi for asphalt, 200 psi for cement, while monitoring slab lift and stopping injection if upward movement exceeds 0.05 in; excess material is removed, wooden plugs withdrawn and holes patched flush with rapid‑set non‑shrink concrete. Seal‑coat or hot‑mix surface treatments use a calibrated binder mixed within ±2 % by volume and immediately spread with a serrated squeegee to achieve a uniform film of 50–65 mil; while the binder remains wet, aggregate is placed at 12–15 lb per square yard to ensure full coverage without exposing the binder. If an asphalt interlayer is required, a nominal one‑inch depth of Superpave mix is placed, keeping surface temperature below 120 °F by misting or using white‑pigmented curing compounds; when a geotextile fabric is used as a separation layer it is rolled flat on the cleaned pavement, overlapped 8 ± 2 in, extended at least four inches beyond the edge, kept free of folds, dampened but not saturated, and secured according to manufacturer instructions. After placement, surface tolerances are checked with a straightedge and any deviation over 3/16 in is corrected by grinding; ride quality is verified requiring an IRI not greater than 70 in/mile for speeds above 45 mph or 90 in/mile at lower speeds. Standard curing practices apply, but a four‑week waiting period is required before surface treatments such as chip seal, micro‑surfacing, or slurry seal can be installed; hot‑mix asphalt overlays do not require this delay. Traffic restrictions are enforced for at least thirty minutes after grout or polyurethane injection, thirty minutes after asphalt grout, and twelve hours after cement grout before reopening the pavement, and concrete repairs are opened to traffic only after cylinder tests confirm a minimum compressive strength of 1 000 psi. Implementation of pavement preservation begins with assembling the prescribed plant‑maintenance inventory, which across the guides includes a seal‑coat unit (code 711‑7124‑01), a crack‑sealing unit for asphalt surfaces (code 711‑7128‑01), and a machine capable of applying slurry seal, micro‑surfacing or an ultrathin friction course (code 711‑7134‑01) together with mixers, paver finishers, level and scratch machines, recycling gear and shoulder‑upgrading tools. In addition, the guides require impact devices that can deliver a controlled variable force without spalling, sonic vibratory tools limited to conditions where subgrade liquefaction will not occur, and a fifty‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller for seating fractured slabs. When hot‑in‑place recycling is selected, the required mobile heating train consists of two to three propane‑heated trucks that first radiantly heat the pavement to approximately 300 °F (± 25 °F), followed by a scarifier that removes at least one and a half inches of surface, a metered addition of recycling agent, blending equipment, and finally a heated screed that places and compacts the rejuvenated layer. The preservation sequence for hot‑in‑place treatment must follow the strict heating‑scarify‑add‑mix‑place‑compact order without deviation, and it is limited to lane widths between ten and fourteen‑and‑a‑half feet. Across the guides a detailed quality‑control plan must be submitted at least fourteen days before work begins, after which existing markings are covered, the pavement is mechanically swept or shot‑blasted, washed with detergent, rinsed and dried using hot compressed air delivered at a minimum of one hundred pounds per square inch. For flexible pavements receiving hot‑mix seal coat, the binder may be mixed by hand‑driven low‑speed drills with helical stirrers, semi‑mechanized mixers equipped with heated metering pumps, or fully automated continuous applicators capable of laying binder and aggregate at rates up to two thousand square yards per hour. Full‑depth longitudinal cuts are placed before any transverse cuts, and all perimeter cuts are completed prior to joint repairs; the wheel saw employed must be self‑propelled, power‑driven, equipped with a diamond‑tipped blade, blade guard, alignment guides, water‑cooling system and depth controls, and penetration into the subbase is limited to one‑half inch. When required, the saw can make two parallel cuts per dowel‑bar slot, and concrete between cuts is removed with lifting devices or a jackhammer not exceeding thirty pounds (or fifteen pounds if damage has occurred). All concrete repairs—including patching, spall repair, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization and slab jacking—must be completed and ride‑quality measurements taken before any diamond grinding operation. Diamond grinding is performed with a self‑propelled machine weighing at least forty thousand pounds when fitted with a grinding head no less than four feet wide; the operation may be executed in a single or two‑pass sequence with each pass overlapping only one to two inches, followed by a feather pass for drainage, and the grinding head must remain at least two feet from curbs, bridge barriers or other roadway edges. Localized corrections up to fifteen feet in length are addressed with walk‑behind carbide grinders that have locking depth controls. When grout injection is required, vertical round holes are drilled with diamond‑tipped core drills whose diameters are limited to five‑eighths inch for high‑density polyurethane and up to one‑and‑a‑half inches for asphalt grout; after blowing oil‑free compressed air at a minimum of one hundred psi through each hole, the appropriate calibrated pump injects the material, with high‑density polyurethane injected under pressure gauges capable of detecting slab lift as small as 0.001 inch. All preservation activities are governed by ambient and surface temperature limits; no work may be performed when either the air or pavement temperature falls below forty degrees Fahrenheit, and sealant placement is restricted to air temperatures roughly between forty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit. The guides further require a minimum four‑week waiting period after a hot‑in‑place treatment before any chip seal, micro‑surfacing or slurry‑seal coating may be applied, and an overlay must be placed before the onset of winter conditions. Hot‑mix seal coat applications must be halted if wind speed exceeds fifteen miles per hour or gusts reach twenty miles per hour. Silicone joint‑sealing materials are required to cure at seventy‑seven ± three degrees Fahrenheit with relative humidity between forty‑five and fifty‑five percent, although they remain functional from minus twenty to plus one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit. Falling‑weight deflectometer testing is prohibited when the pavement surface is frozen or when temperatures are below forty‑five degrees or above ninety degrees Fahrenheit. Binder and aggregate for hot‑mix seal coat must be heated to within tight tolerances, typically between three hundred eighty and four hundred fifty degrees Fahrenheit; material exceeding five hundred degrees is rejected, and asphalt used in grout or overlay operations follows the same 380 °F to 450 °F range with any batch above five hundred degrees discarded. After the pavement has been fractured and seated, the lane remains closed until an asphalt overlay seals the surface, and no equipment or traffic may occupy the treated lane during the required curing periods—thirty days for new asphalt before hot‑mix seal coat and twenty‑eight days for concrete surfaces. Saw‑cut areas may be reopened to traffic for a maximum of seventy‑two hours, and any excavated patch must be repaired within forty‑eight hours, including weekends and holidays; failure to do so requires work to halt. Drop‑off hazards are protected as prescribed, and repairs spaced less than one hundred feet apart are combined into a single continuous patch to minimize lane closures. Heavy equipment is prohibited on fresh rigid slabs until the concrete attains a minimum compressive strength of three thousand pounds per square inch, and all movement onto or off the slab must be via constructed ramps. Joints must be sealed—or an approved joint‑protecting method employed—before any traffic is allowed, and construction vehicles are kept off fresh interlayers; sharp turns and braking are minimized, with installation over high‑traffic areas deferred until just before concrete placement. Following grout injection, pavement must be closed to traffic for at least thirty minutes for polyurethane and asphalt treatments and for twelve hours for cement grout unless a field‑QC plan permits earlier reopening. Open flames are forbidden during any heating operation, and the pneumatic roller cannot be used on wet pavement. [1][2]

Quality and workmanship

Before any preservation work the crew must verify that the existing pavement material is suitable by coring representative sections and inspecting each core for depth and structural integrity, with joint repairs later confirmed by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing. All materials to be used are required to meet their approved specifications, and concrete mix designs must be supported by trial‑batch test data demonstrating that the minimum compressive and, where applicable, flexural strengths will be achieved. Fabricated slabs are accepted only when dimensions fall within the tolerances shown on shop drawings, while binder volumes must be held within plus or minus two percent of the manufacturer’s recommendation, producing a uniform film thickness of fifty to sixty‑five mils and an aggregate spread of twelve to fifteen pounds per square yard over the wet film. Minimum compressive strengths are strictly enforced: grout must reach at least 2 500 psi before traffic is permitted, concrete cylinders must achieve one 000 psi for patch work and three 000 psi for new pavement, and the overall percent‑within‑limits for compressive strength may not fall below fifty‑five percent. Sealant products must be sourced from approved bulletins and their physical properties—including tensile stress at 150 % elongation, specific gravity, durometer hardness, shelf life, ozone and ultraviolet resistance, flow, bond strength to cement mortar, tack‑free time, movement capability, and functional temperature range from –20 °F to +140 °F—must be verified by the appropriate ASTM methods. Sealants are heated within the temperature range indicated on the shipping label and must not remain at pouring temperature for more than six hours; all equipment used for mixing, heating or injecting materials must possess a calibration certificate dated within the previous twelve months, and polyurethane components must be mixed in exact manufacturer‑specified proportions. Prior to mixing the surface is heated to approximately 300 °F with an allowable tolerance of plus or minus 25 °F, and scarified so that the cut penetrates at least one‑and‑a‑half inches; a predetermined amount of recycling agent is then added and blended uniformly throughout the rejuvenated mix. The mixed material must be placed and compacted to meet the required density criteria, and the thicknesses of any milling, overlay, or patching operations are checked against design limits, ensuring that the total patched area does not exceed the allowable percentage. After placement a minimum four‑week curing period is observed before any chip seal, micro‑surfacing, or slurry seal can be applied, and an overlay must be installed before winter conditions arrive. During preparation the existing pavement surface is inspected for cleanliness; loose particles, foreign debris, excess joint sealer and crack filler are removed, cracks wider than one‑eighth inch are filled and sealed, and a uniform tack coat is applied to promote bonding of any overlay. Subbase placement and compaction must achieve the design elevation within plus or minus half an inch, while fine grading of bedding material may not vary more than one‑eighth inch over each slab length; existing markings are either protected or removed, and surfaces are cleaned with detergent, swept, air‑washed and blasted to meet ICRI CSP 5 roughness before protective mats are laid. Ambient and surface temperatures must be at least forty degrees Fahrenheit; for geotextile interlayers the fabric temperature may not exceed one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit, the material must remain damp but never saturated, lie flat on the cleaned substrate, overlap edges by eight inches plus or minus two inches, extend at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, and be limited to three layers in any location. Asphalt interlayers require a nominal one‑inch depth, joint faulting not exceeding a quarter inch before placement and a half inch after, with surface temperature also capped at one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit. Drilling for tie bars or injection holes must produce smooth vertical openings; hole diameters may not exceed three‑eighths of an inch larger than the bar for concrete work, five‑eighths of an inch for grout injection, and up to one and a half inches for asphalt injection, each filled with epoxy providing at least one inch of cover after rotation to purge air. Installation inspections verify that slabs are placed so vertical differentials across joints do not exceed a quarter inch and joint gaps remain within prescribed limits—half an inch transversely and three‑quarters inch longitudinally; dowel and tie bars, along with bedding grouts, must be installed with no more than a quarter‑inch tilt or skew at either end and no greater than one inch of longitudinal translation, chairs must provide at least half an inch clearance, and lubrication or polystyrene fillers are applied as required. Grout and anchoring materials must achieve their specified compressive strengths before traffic is allowed, and full curing must follow manufacturer recommendations; when a fractured layer is present it is compacted with a minimum fifty‑ton pneumatic roller without vertical deflection, and its elastic modulus is later verified by falling‑weight deflectometer testing. Sealants are inspected for the absence of pinholes or uncured areas, uniformity in all dimensions, and proper application flush with the pavement, producing a film thickness between one‑thirty‑second and one‑sixteenth inch over a width of one to three inches; wall misalignment in compressed seals may not exceed one‑eighth inch. During grout or asphalt injection the pressure is maintained between twenty‑five and forty‑five psi (with an upper limit of two hundred psi for certain operations), slab lift is continuously monitored with equipment capable of detecting movement to a thousandth of an inch, and upward movement is limited to five‑hundredths of an inch; work must be halted if this limit is reached. Rolling operations employ steel‑wheeled rollers of ten tons or greater (or variable‑force impact devices) following prescribed rolling patterns, moisture levels on wheels, and observation for aggregate breakage. The completed‑work inspection checks that edge slumping, depressions and high points are within the allowable tolerances specified in Section 506.3(o), and that any surface defects—rain damage, improper joint construction, curing errors, poor final finish, cracking or honeycombing—have been satisfactorily repaired; the final pavement is re‑sounded in the presence of the representative to confirm removal of all delaminated concrete, and a comprehensive sampling and testing package is prepared before opening to traffic. A quality‑control plan covering material mixing, curing, joint sawing, sealing and cleaning (the latter completed within thirty minutes prior to placement) must be approved and documented; traffic may only resume after grout has attained the required compressive strength, anchoring materials are fully cured, all joints have been sealed as specified, and a calibrated skid test—conducted no earlier than thirty‑one days after application of any hot‑mix thin‑film surfacing—has confirmed acceptable performance. Performance and service life

Pavement preservation is a planned, cost‑effective preventive‑maintenance strategy that treats structurally sound pavements before serious distress appears. By applying surface or near‑surface treatments—such as crack sealing, chip sealing, slurry or micro‑surfacing, thin overlays, mill‑and‑overlay, Concrete Pavement Restoration, thin bonded concrete on asphalt, diamond grinding, base repair and concrete patching—the existing function of the roadway is restored and its condition is kept sound. The use of Hot In‑Place Recycled (HIR) asphalt further rejuvenates the surface; scarifying, mixing a recycling agent and hot re‑compaction remove surface distress, improve joint compaction and allow heat to penetrate the underlying layer for better interlayer bonding. These actions produce smoother rides, enhance safety and mobility, and reduce congestion. Because preservation focuses on maintaining rather than adding structural capacity, it retards future deterioration, bolsters durability, and maintains or improves functional performance. The cumulative effect of systematic, successive preservation treatments postpones costly rehabilitation, extends the pavement’s useful life, and lengthens the expected service life until a more extensive overlay or reconstruction is required. Pavement preservation treatments are vulnerable to a suite of surface and structural distresses that can compromise the intended life‑extension benefits [1][2]. The most frequently observed surface failures include rutting, various cracking patterns (longitudinal, transverse, and mid‑slab), edge deterioration, raveling or weathering, joint spalling, faulting, transverse and longitudinal spalls, and the loss of integrity in existing asphalt or concrete patches [1][2]. Spalling is identified as the predominant failure on rigid pavements and can be induced by excessive impact forces from equipment, fracture of aggregate during diamond grinding, insufficient curing, or exposure to rain before the surface has set [2]. Premature mid‑slab cracking in jointed‑concrete sections often signals a deficient supporting subbase, while aggregate‑related problems such as high Vanport Limestone content or alkali‑silica reaction can diminish skid resistance and become more evident after diamond grinding [1]. The development of these distresses is closely linked to underlying material conditions that fall below the thresholds established for preservation work, such as when required milling or overlay depths exceed prescribed limits or when more than ten percent of a concrete surface must be patched [1]. Structural inadequacies revealed by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, subbase insufficiency, reactive aggregates, or slabs that are thinner than the design thickness by more than 0.5 inch also increase the likelihood of treatment failure [1][2]. Joint performance issues arise when transverse clearances are less than ¼ inch, vertical differentials exceed ¼ inch, or joint faulting surpasses ¼ inch (requiring a geotextile interlayer) or half an inch (necessitating a scratch course or milling) [2]. Cracks wider than 0.007 inches or deeper than one‑third of the slab thickness must be replaced, whereas narrower cracks are only sealed after thorough cleaning at temperatures above 40 °F and overfilled sealants are scarified to achieve a flush surface [2]. Additional performance failures such as flushing, potholes, fatigue cracking, and other low‑severity defects become warranty concerns when, after five years, thirty percent of a segment exhibits two or more of these distresses, prompting microsurfacing or another approved treatment within sixty days [2]. Contractor‑controlled factors that influence distress development include selection of equipment that limits impact forces to avoid spalling, operation of rollers only when ambient and pavement temperatures are above 40 °F and the surface is dry, adherence to mixing tolerances for binders and aggregates, prevention of aggregate contamination in high‑friction treatments, maintenance of proper clearance from utilities, regulation of grout injection pressures to a maximum of 200 psi, and compliance with overjacking limits of 0.02 ft (continuously reinforced) or 0.03 ft (jointed) with an absolute ceiling of 0.10 ft [2]. Temperature constraints also govern preservation work; placement must not occur when ambient temperature is below 40 °F, surface temperatures must remain below 120 °F before overlay placement, and curing periods must be protected from rain to prevent premature cracking or loss of bond [1][2]. When any of the specified thresholds are exceeded—such as compressive strength falling below 55 % of the specified value or less than 2,500 psi, slab thickness deficiency, excessive joint faulting, surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch, or vertical movement greater than 0.05 inch—the prescribed remedial actions range from localized drilling, patching, resealing, or grinding to full‑depth slab replacement at no additional cost to the agency [2][1]. Early intervention through mill‑and‑overlay, micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded courses, or timely crack sealing before advanced distresses develop is repeatedly emphasized as a means to reduce the incidence of these failures [1][2].

Monitoring and follow-up

After a preservation treatment is finished, the segment is first inspected visually to confirm that the material was placed correctly and that no obvious defects such as missed spots or surface blemishes are present [1]. A quantitative verification of the patching quality follows by measuring the treated area with Falling Weight Deflectometer testing and recording the resulting patching percentage, both of which become part of the acceptance documentation [1]. The completed work is then entered into the Roadway Management System’s Pavement History database and incorporated into the district’s overall interstate/freeway program to provide a permanent record for future tracking [1]. Thereafter the segment is folded into the regular condition‑monitoring cycle, beginning with an annual automated pavement condition survey conducted through videologging under the STAMPP system that captures data on cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling, joint spalling and faulting [1]. In parallel, the Department carries out a structured post‑treatment inspection program in accordance with Publication 336, which includes yearly automated surveys to locate and measure distresses and manual surveys when flushing or potholes are observed; rut depth is measured with a twelve‑foot straight edge during these inspections [2]. Survey results are compared against the performance limits set out in Table A, and if at the fifth‑year survey thirty percent of the segment exhibits two or more low‑severity distresses, remedial action such as microsurfacing or another approved treatment is required [2]. During the warranty period the contractor may also perform nondestructive monitoring at any time provided a seven‑day notice is given to the District Executive [2]. If skid‑resistance concerns arise, especially on concrete sections, a calibrated skid‑friction test in accordance with ASTM E274/E274M (AASHTO T 242) is requested from the Bureau of Materials Operations and performed no earlier than thirty‑one days after the surface has been reopened [1][2]. For treatments that employ a binder and high‑friction surface treatment, the first aggregate cleanup is performed once the binder has cured and traffic is restored, followed by a second vacuum‑sweeping of the surface between fifteen and thirty days later [2]. At the time of the second sweep the surface is examined for exposed bare pavement, inadequate aggregate retention, delamination or discoloration, and any deficient locations are prepared and retreated with HFST [2]. When cement‑grout stabilization is used, a post‑treatment inspection is required at least twenty‑four hours before final acceptance; each treated joint or crack is retested using the same deflection procedures and checked for vertical movement [2]. Any slab that shows upward movement greater than 0.05 inch or a loaded corner deflection exceeding 0.02 inch must be re‑grouted and retested until it meets the specified limits [2]. Traffic may not be opened to the repaired area until a minimum of twelve hours has elapsed after grouting, unless an approved field quality‑control plan or manufacturer’s guidance permits earlier opening [2].

After a preservation treatment the pavement must be monitored for time‑based cycles, and the guides specify that resurfaced asphalt sections should receive crack sealing on a three‑to‑five‑year cycle while concrete networks require joint sealing every five years; if a segment has reached five years of service without the scheduled seal work, additional maintenance or rehabilitation is indicated [1]. Any isolated or obvious deficiency that compromises safety or accessibility must be evaluated and, when cost‑effective, corrected to preserve the current level of travelway safety [1]. Condition surveys that reveal a trend of increasing distress—such as widening or propagating cracks, growing rut depth, edge deterioration, raveling or weathering of the surface, joint spalling, or faulting—signal that the existing preservation treatment is no longer sufficient and further intervention should be considered [1]. Early warning signs including premature mid‑slab cracking in jointed concrete, often linked to inadequate subbase material, and aggregate‑related problems such as Vanport Limestone concerns or alkali‑silica reaction that can reduce skid resistance also trigger the need for additional work before the next scheduled treatment point is reached [1]. Geometric tolerances provide performance thresholds; cross‑slope deviations greater than one‑quarter inch over any twelve‑foot segment, loaded slab‑corner deflections of at least 0.02 inch, surface lifts exceeding 0.05 inch after treatment, or overjacking that raises the pavement more than one‑tenth foot above the target grade each require stabilization, grinding, resealing, or removal and replacement [2]. Ride‑quality limits are breached when the International Roughness Index exceeds 70 inches per mile on roads with posted speeds above forty‑five miles per hour or exceeds 90 inches per mile where the speed limit is forty‑five mph or less, when any individual bump measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge is larger than three‑sixteenths of an inch, when ridges or valleys between adjacent lanes exceed one‑eighth of an inch, or when the Mean Roughness Index surpasses the maximum value specified in the applicable tables, all of which mandate remedial action [2]. Structural surveys that find at least thirty percent of a segment containing two or more low‑severity distresses, or any distress type exceeding the limits set out in the prescribed tables, call for corrective treatments such as microsurfacing or other approved preservation methods, and repairs spaced less than one hundred feet apart must be combined into a continuous patch [2]. Thickness and material quality criteria also define deficiency; loss of pavement depth greater than half an inch below the design thickness, Percent Within Limits for compressive strength or air content falling below fifty‑five percent, poor aggregate retention, signs of delamination or discoloration, exposed underlying asphalt or concrete, and surface defects that cannot be satisfactorily repaired—including cracking, honeycombing, rain damage, improperly constructed or cured joints, unsatisfactory final finish, depressions, high points, or edge slumping—render the pavement defective and require additional maintenance or rehabilitation [2]. Surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be filled with patching material, and joint faulting exceeding one‑quarter inch for geotextile interlayers (or one‑half inch for asphalt interlayers) similarly mandates a scratch course, milling, or grinding to reduce the fault [2]. Joint performance thresholds state that when loaded slab‑corner deflection reaches at least 0.02 inch and joint efficiency is sixty‑five percent or higher, stabilization of all joints or cracks is required; if joint efficiency falls below sixty‑five percent under the same deflection condition, both patching and stabilization are mandated [2]. The appearance of radial cracks radiating from grout injection holes or transverse cracks developing between adjacent injection holes signals poor‑quality preservation and necessitates full‑slab replacement [2]. Grade profiling must remain within plus‑or‑minus 0.02 foot for continuously reinforced concrete slabs and plus‑or‑minus 0.03 foot for jointed slabs; any deviation beyond these limits, as well as unstable slabs that move or rock under normal traffic loads, requires stabilization or replacement before further overlay work can proceed [2].
