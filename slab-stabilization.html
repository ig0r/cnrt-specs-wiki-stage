Slab Stabilization

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Slab Stabilization is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
slab-stabilization
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-27

Slab stabilization is a preservation technique applied to Portland‑cement concrete pavements that have lost uniform bearing because voids have formed beneath the slab‑subbase interface, typically due to pumping action, subgrade consolidation, settlement or water infiltration [1][2]. The method restores full support by drilling vertical holes at joints or cracks and injecting a fill material—commonly cementitious grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane foam—and may be followed by a thin (≈1 in) asphalt overlay to regain ride quality and skid resistance [1][2]. Success is judged by eliminating visible movement such as rocking, pumping or faulting and by achieving a loaded slab‑corner deflection of less than 0.02 inch with joint efficiency at least 65 percent, thereby reducing excessive deflection, improving structural integrity and extending the pavement’s service life without increasing its original design capacity [1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Slab Stabilization” also include the following subtopics: Slabjacking.

Slabjacking serves as a specific remedial application of slab stabilization by using controlled injection to raise faulted or sunken concrete slabs back to their design elevation while filling underlying voids with cementitious grout, hot asphalt, or polyurethane foam. [1]

Purpose and treatment objectives

Slab stabilization is applied when a Portland‑cement concrete pavement exhibits loss or uneven bearing caused by subsurface voids that develop beneath the slab or its base, which may arise from pumping action, subgrade consolidation, settlement, or damage near transverse joints, cracks and slab corners [1]. These voids manifest as rocking, pumping, faulting, transverse‑joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking, small depressions (blow‑holes), excessive deflection and differential settlement that degrade ride quality and skid resistance [1]. The guides also identify visible uplift or depression of the slab under normal traffic loads and a measured vertical movement at a loaded slab corner of 0.02 inch (0.02 ft) or greater as criteria for stabilization [2]. When such movement occurs, joint efficiency can fall to about 65 percent, producing non‑uniform support that threatens serviceability and the ability to place interlayer material for overlays [2]. In both sources the underlying performance problem is the loss of full, uniform support beneath the rigid pavement, which if left unaddressed leads to continued slab movement, joint deterioration and eventual breakup of the slab [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–98), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–598)

Slab stabilization is applied when voids or movement exist beneath concrete slabs, creating loss of uniform support at the slab‑subbase interface [1][2]. The treatment typically involves filling those voids and may include placing a thin asphalt overlay roughly one inch (≈6.3 mm) thick to re‑establish full and even bearing for the slab [1]. By providing this restored support, the structural integrity and load‑bearing capacity of the pavement are recovered, which returns the surface to its intended functional performance such as improved skid resistance, ride quality, and reduced deflection [1]. Consequently, the primary objective of slab stabilization is to restore functionality by repairing the damage that caused instability or voids [1][2]. Because the repaired support prevents further movement of slabs under traffic, the treatment also serves to extend the pavement’s service life as a secondary benefit [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462)

Distress characterization and causes

The distress targeted by slab stabilization consists of small voids that develop beneath concrete pavements, most commonly at transverse joints, cracks and the outer corners of slabs, and may appear as blow‑hole depressions in the shoulder region. These voids are identified when a loaded slab corner deflection reaches or exceeds 0.02 inch at any transverse joint or crack, and their severity is further evaluated by joint efficiency, with values of 65 % or higher indicating that full stabilization is required, while lower efficiencies call for additional patching. The loss of subgrade support caused by the voids produces excessive slab deflection and stress, which manifest as joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and can ultimately lead to complete slab breakup if left untreated. Although the guides do not specify a precise progression rate, the observed sequence of increasing faulting, cracking and eventual slab fracture implies that the condition can deteriorate progressively over time without remedial action. Operationally, the distress degrades ride quality and structural integrity, and it imposes safety constraints such as the need to stop pumping operations whenever any vertical movement is detected, the requirement to halt work if radial cracks emanate from grout injection holes, and the possibility that transverse cracks between grout holes may necessitate full slab replacement. Standard pressure‑pump precautions and protective equipment for workers handling hot asphalt are also mandated to mitigate hazards associated with the repair process. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Slab stabilization is required when concrete pavements display movement such as rocking, pumping, or faulting, indicating that the slab no longer rests on uniform support [1][2]. The primary mechanisms generating this distress are inadequate drainage of surface and subsurface water combined with insufficient structural capacity in the underlying base or sub‑base [1]. Water infiltrates through joints or cracks, initiating a pumping action that, together with subgrade consolidation and settlement, creates voids beneath the slab and removes bearing support [1]. The loss of support leads to excessive slab deflection, which manifests as transverse joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately slab breakup [1]. Heavy traffic loads exacerbate the problem when the underlying support is weak, while climate‑related durability issues—particularly drainage failures—accelerate water‑induced deterioration [1]. The visible rocking or movement of slabs or fragments under normal traffic loads reflects this non‑uniform support and must be corrected before any overlay or interlayer material can be placed [2]. Effective remediation therefore involves filling the voids, improving subgrade structural capacity, sealing joints and cracks, and maintaining functional subsurface drains to prevent recurrence of the distress [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–98); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462)

Applicability and treatment selection

Slab stabilization is indicated for Portland‑cement concrete pavements that show clear evidence of lost subgrade support, such as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines at joints or cracks, small blow‑hole depressions near joints, and corner breaks, which signal voids created by pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or water infiltration; the treatment restores uniform support without increasing design capacity and is most effective when joint and crack sealing can be performed simultaneously and subsurface drainage remains functional [1]. The technique is also warranted whenever measurable instability is observed under normal traffic loads, specifically when a loaded slab‑corner deflection of at least 0.02 inch is recorded and the joint‑efficiency rating reaches 65 percent or higher (lower efficiencies being permissible only if combined with patching), or when slabs or fragments visibly move or rock under regular traffic [2]. Environmental constraints require daytime air temperatures of 40 °F or above but not exceeding 70 °F for testing, and the subgrade or base course must be free of frost before work can commence [2]. Additionally, the site must be staffed by qualified personnel with documented experience and a quality‑control plan, and the overall conditions must allow concurrent sealing and drainage functionality as described in the guidance [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Slab stabilization is appropriate only for structurally sound pavements as verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing and when the required patching does not exceed roughly ten percent of the slab area, excluding existing good patches [1]. If the percentage of needed patching exceeds this ten‑percent threshold or a cost‑effectiveness analysis shows that exceeding it would be uneconomical, alternative treatments such as full‑depth patching, overlay, or replacement should be pursued [1]. The method is limited to situations where distress originates from loss of support beneath the slab, manifested by excessive deflection; it does not address surface depressions, blow‑holes, corner breaks, diagonal cracking, faulted joints, or any need to increase structural capacity [1]. When visible surface defects, faulting, or a requirement for additional structural strength are present, another maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or reconstruction approach must be selected [1]. Slab stabilization is also unsuitable when the primary cause of failure is climate‑or material‑related durability problems such as frost damage or aggregate polishing, or when load‑related structural deficiencies require dowel retrofits or other structural improvements; in those cases a more extensive repair is required [1]. Environmental conditions that prevent reliable execution must be avoided; work should not commence if daytime temperatures are below 40 °F or if the base course or subgrade is frozen, and deflection testing cannot be performed when air temperature is below 40 °F, above 70 °F, or during spring‑thaw periods [2]. The technique is further limited to joints or cracks that exhibit a loaded slab corner deflection of at least 0.02 inches and a joint efficiency of 65 % or greater; joints below these values fall outside the scope of stabilization and require an alternative repair method [2]. During grout jacking, lifts must remain within small‑lift tolerances—approximately 0.02 ft from string‑line grade for continuously reinforced slabs and about 0.03 ft for jointed slabs—and if achieving the target profile would demand a raise greater than roughly one‑tenth of a foot, the operation is classified as overjacking and the pavement should be removed and replaced according to prescribed rehabilitation procedures [2]. If vertical movement occurs while pumping or if further material placement becomes economically infeasible, work should be halted and another maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair approach selected [2].

Condition assessment and timing

Determining whether slab stabilization is required begins with a systematic visual inspection performed under normal traffic conditions, during which the inspector quantifies all visible distress such as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines at joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, small blow‑hole depressions in shoulders adjacent to joints or cracks, corner breaks and general slab settlement [1][2]. Core samples are then extracted both near deteriorated joints and at randomly selected locations so that subbase or subgrade soil conditions can be evaluated and hidden deterioration beneath the surface can be identified [1]. Structural testing follows, with a Falling Weight Deflectometer test run on the existing pavement to assess overall structural capacity before any overlay design, and a comprehensive deflection survey conducted on every transverse joint and crack in accordance with ASTM D4695 that records unloaded and loaded slab‑corner deflections [1][2]. From the deflection data joint efficiency is calculated, and joints or cracks that exhibit a loaded corner deflection of at least 0.02 in and a joint efficiency of 65 % or greater (or less than 65 % when a patch‑and‑stabilize approach is specified) are identified for stabilization [2]. Non‑destructive techniques such as ground‑penetrating radar may be employed to pinpoint joints that require replacement and to delineate zones where voids exist beneath the slab [1]. Where visual signs of instability are not evident, a vertical‑movement test is required using calibrated equipment capable of simultaneously measuring lift at the pavement edge or at two opposite slab corners adjacent to a joint with a resolution of 0.001 in, and calibration certificates must be current within the previous twelve months [2]. A field inspection for movement signs—rocking, pumping, or faulted slabs—is also conducted; the presence of any of these conditions confirms the need for slab stabilization at those locations [1][2]. Finally, existing roadway‑profile surveys are prepared to document depressions and compare the existing grade with the proposed design grade, and discrepancies revealed in the profile report may trigger additional grout injection as directed by the Representative [2]. Together, these inspections, measurements, and tests determine both the necessity of slab stabilization and the precise locations where remedial treatment should be applied.

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–98); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–598)

Slab stabilization should be applied when the concrete slab has lost uniform support, which is evident through voids, instability, rocking, pumping, faulting, transverse‑joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks, small depressions (blow‑holes) at joints or cracks, corner breaks, or overall settlement.[1] These conditions are confirmed by diagnostic testing such as deflection tests, faulting measurements, Falling Weight Deflectometer results, or other non‑destructive methods that reveal excessive movement or voids under specific joints.[1][2] A quantitative trigger is a loaded slab corner deflection of at least 0.02 in measured at a joint or crack; when this threshold is met, stabilization is required for joints whose efficiency is 65 % or greater, while joints below that efficiency should be patched first and then stabilized.[2] If temperature limits prevent deflection testing, visual evidence of pumping problems or poor joint performance serves as an acceptable basis to specify slab stabilization.[2] The treatment is appropriate when the loss of support is localized to less than about ten percent of the pavement surface on a structurally sound slab, as verified by FWD testing, ensuring that the slab can be restored before any overlay, patching, or further rehabilitation.[1] In all cases where slabs or fragments move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads, creating non‑uniform support for an intended overlay, stabilization (or repair) must precede placement of interlayer material.[2][1]

Materials and repair details

Slab stabilization restores support beneath concrete pavements by filling voids with one of three grout systems—a cement‑based fluid grout, a hot‑asphalt undersealing, or a high‑density polyurethane foam. The cement‑based grout is produced from cement mixed with water and supplementary cementitious material in a volumetric ratio of one part cement to three parts SCM, optionally incorporating admixtures such as a multiphase wetting agent, expansive agent, or accelerator; the water content is adjusted so that the efflux time measured according to ASTM C939 falls between 16 and 26 seconds, although a more fluid mix with an efflux of 10 to 15 seconds may be used for the first injection, and the grout must be capable of expanding up to ten percent, achieve an initial set within one to six hours, limit bleed loss to no more than 2.5 % of its volume, and develop a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi based on five cylinders [1][2]. Typically about one cubic foot of this grout—approximately a quarter bag of cement—is pumped into each drilled hole [1]. The hot‑asphalt undersealing uses ASTM D 3141 asphalt cement supplied by an approved manufacturer, heated to a temperature between 380 °F and 450 °F before pumping; each hole receives between thirty and fifty gallons of liquid asphalt, which is allowed to cool for at least thirty minutes before traffic resumes [1][2]. The high‑density polyurethane option consists of a two‑component low‑viscosity foam that expands to fill voids at roughly 0.4 lb per square foot, hardens in about half an hour, and must be a certified product from an approved source [1][2]. Vertical, smooth‑sided injection holes are drilled through the slab following the joint or crack pattern; for polyurethane grout the hole diameter may not exceed five‑eighths of an inch, whereas cement and asphalt grouts permit diameters up to one‑and‑half inches, and in all cases the depth must be sufficient to penetrate the underlying stabilized base [1][2]. After drilling a tapered cylindrical plug is driven no more than two inches into each hole to provide a seal before grout injection [2]. The equipment roster required for cement‑based work includes a positive‑displacement grout pump, a high‑speed mixing unit capable of 800–2000 rpm, supply hoses, valves, flow meters and pressure gauges, as well as a slab‑lift measurement system able to detect movement as small as one thousandth of an inch [2]. For polyurethane injection a mounted pump unit with a controlled‑rate display that records material usage in pounds, together with pressure and temperature control devices, is mandated [2]. Asphalt operations demand a fully insulated transport tanker equipped with a removable access hatch and a minimum two‑inch overflow pipe, an underseal pump fitted with at least a one‑and‑half‑inch flexible metal hose and quick‑cutoff valve, a pressure water distributor trailer holding at least fifty gallons, and compressor units delivering no less than one hundred cubic feet per minute of air [2]. During injection the rise of the slab is continuously monitored; cement grout pressures are limited to 200 psi with short spikes permitted up to 300 psi and brief peaks of 600 psi when the pavement is bonded, asphalt pumping pressures are maintained between 25 and 45 psi, and polyurethane injection rates are adjusted to control slab lift within allowable tolerances [2]. Upward movement may not exceed 0.05 in for cement grout, while continuous reinforced concrete pavements must be raised within 0.02 ft (≈0.24 in), jointed slabs or bridge‑end slabs within 0.03 ft (≈0.36 in), and asphalt operations keep rise below 0.05 in [2][1]. After each hole is filled, the wooden plug is removed and the opening is patched full depth with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete material that is struck flush with the surrounding pavement; any panel that exceeds the allowable lift tolerance is ground back to grade, and if post‑grout deflection exceeds 0.02 in additional grout may be placed followed by re‑testing before acceptance [1][2]. All joints and cracks are subsequently sealed with appropriate joint sealants, drainage provisions are verified, and a thin asphalt overlay up to one inch thick using a 6.3 mm mix design may be applied as a non‑structural wearing course to improve skid resistance and ride quality when desired [1][2]. Full‑depth patches typically employ a two‑hole layout, and when no testing is performed at least twenty‑five percent of transverse joints must be treated [1]. Traffic restrictions differ by material: a minimum thirty‑minute closure is imposed for hot‑asphalt work, whereas cement‑based stabilization requires at least twelve hours before the slab can be reopened [1][2].

The performance of slab stabilization depends first on the condition of the existing concrete slab and its supporting structure, which must be structurally sound as confirmed by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing and exhibit compressive strength and air content that meet standard specifications; the amount of existing patchwork should remain below roughly ten percent of the surface area, otherwise a cost‑effectiveness analysis is required [1]. In addition, the slab must display adequate friction, acceptable ride quality, and aggregate that is not prone to polishing, while joints or cracks selected for treatment need to show a loaded slab‑corner deflection of at least 0.02 inch; joints with an efficiency of 65 percent or greater can be fully stabilized, whereas lower efficiencies call for combined patch‑and‑stabilize actions [1][2]. Daytime temperatures must exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit and the subgrade or base course must be unfrozen before any injection work is undertaken, because low temperatures impair grout flow and set times [2]. The fill material chosen to fill voids must satisfy engineered property limits: cement‑based grout, hot‑mix asphalt grout, or high‑density polyurethane grout each must have an expansion limited to between zero and ten percent, an initial setting time of one to six hours, bleed loss not greater than 2.5 percent of the volume, and a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi measured on at least five cylinders [2]. For cement grout the water content is adjusted so that the efflux time measured by ASTM C939 lies between sixteen and twenty‑six seconds (a more fluid ten‑ to fifteen‑second mix may be used for the first injection), and the volumetric composition must be one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material [2]. Asphalt grout must be heated to a temperature range of 380 °F to 450 °F—any material above five hundred degrees Fahrenheit is rejected—and pumped at pressures between twenty‑five and forty‑five pounds per square inch, with injection line pressure not exceeding two hundred psi continuously (short‑term spikes to three hundred psi are permitted, and brief spikes to six hundred psi only when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade) [2]. The polyurethane system must be a low‑viscosity two‑component mix that can be injected at a controlled flow rate while limiting slab rise; it expands into a closed‑cell foam that remains lightweight yet rigid, providing resistance to water ingress and chemical attack [1][2]. All grout types must be placed within one hour of mixing, and flowability measurements are required at least twice during each work shift; the mixed material may not be held in the mixer or pump sump for more than one hour after mixing [2]. Hole preparation is critical to compatibility: drill holes must be vertical, round, and sized no larger than five‑eighths inch in diameter for polyurethane or one and a half inches for cement and asphalt grout, deep enough to penetrate any existing stabilized base without breaking through the slab bottom, and water or thin mud encountered in voids must be cleared with compressed air before injection [2]. After grouting, wooden plugs are removed and the holes are sealed full depth with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete patching material applied within its shelf life and temperature limits, ensuring a proper seal that reduces water entry and supports overlay bonding [1][2]. Compatibility with the surrounding pavement system is achieved by comprehensive joint and crack sealing to limit water infiltration, by maintaining functional subsurface drainage, and by selecting a fill material that can bond effectively with any preservation layer such as micro‑surfacing or an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course; typically a one‑inch (6.3 mm) thin asphalt overlay is placed over the stabilized slab [1]. When the subgrade or subbase is sound and drainage operates properly, bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays may be used; if the underlying support is compromised or drainage deficient, unbonded solutions may be required, but performance will be limited if slab movement such as rocking, pumping, or faulting is not addressed before overlay placement [1]. During injection, upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited, and overjacking exceeding 0.10 foot mandates removal and replacement of the slab; these limits protect against excessive lift that could damage the structure or impair ride quality [2]. Finally, the timing of each step—allowing cement grout to cure for about twelve hours before traffic, permitting hot asphalt to cool sufficiently after pumping, and ensuring polyurethane foam completes its expansion—must be observed before reopening the lane to traffic to achieve durable performance [1].

Treatment procedures

Before any stabilization work begins, the contractor must provide written documentation of qualified personnel—each with at least five years of relevant experience—to the Representative at least ten working days in advance and submit a field‑QC plan that details hole spacing, depth, diameter, drilling method, injection sequence, equipment list, line and grade control, waste disposal, and curing period. The guides also require that all joints and cracks be sealed and subsurface drains kept functional so that water ingress is limited prior to any drilling or grout placement. Deflection testing of each joint or crack is performed in ambient temperatures between 40 °F and 70 °F using ASTM D4695, and any location showing a loaded‑slab corner deflection of 0.02 inches or more with joint efficiency of 65 % or higher is slated for stabilization. Traffic control devices are installed to protect both the drilling crew and the stabilization crew while work proceeds. The crew then assembles and calibrates all required equipment, which includes a positive‑displacement pump or high‑density polyurethane injection unit with calibrated flow meter, pressure and temperature controls, a core drill equipped with diamond‑tip barrels, appropriate hoses, valves, plugs, lift‑monitoring devices, a water tanker, and—when hot asphalt is used—a splash shield of at least 15 sq ft. Dry grout ingredients are accurately weighed by mass, mixed with measured water in a mixer for less than one hour while flowability is checked. When hot asphalt is the selected material, it is heated to a temperature between 380 °F and 450 °F and circulated prior to pumping. Vertical grout‑injection holes are then drilled at the locations indicated on the design drawings; hole diameters must not exceed 5/8 in when high‑density polyurethane is used or 1½ in for cement‑based or asphalt treatments, and each hole is drilled deep enough to penetrate the underlying base. As the drill is withdrawn, compressed air is blown for several seconds to clear debris from each bore. The selected stabilizing material—cement‑based grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane—is then injected under controlled conditions, with continuous pressures limited to 200 psi unless otherwise directed and with upward slab movement monitored so that uplift does not exceed 0.05 in. For hot asphalt the pump pressure is maintained between 25 psi and 45 psi, while for high‑density polyurethane the injection rate is regulated to keep slab uplift within the 0.05 in limit; cement grout follows the same 200 psi pressure ceiling. Material is pumped in a pattern until the pavement surface is raised to within 0.02 ft of the established string‑line grade for monolithic slabs or within 0.03 ft for jointed slabs, and any overjacking beyond this tolerance is corrected by grinding; if the lift exceeds 0.10 ft the slab must be removed and replaced. When the nozzle is withdrawn from each hole, any excess material at the surface is removed promptly. After grouting, wooden plugs are extracted and the drill holes are patched full‑depth with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete material that is flush with the pavement surface. If directed, the stabilized joints are retested after 24 hours and re‑grouted if deflection still exceeds 0.02 in. Curing requirements depend on the stabilizing material; hot asphalt must be allowed to set for at least thirty minutes before traffic is reopened, while cement‑based grout requires a minimum of twelve hours of closed‑to‑traffic curing. Final acceptance is based on a surveyed profile report confirming that the as‑built grade meets the specified tolerances and on submission of the profile drawings to the Representative. [1][2]

Slab stabilization projects require a suite of specialized equipment that varies with the selected grout type, including a core drill equipped with diamond‑tip barrels capable of producing vertical holes no larger than five‑eighths inch (or up to one‑and‑half inches where specifications allow) and a calibrated pump unit able to deliver material at controlled rates and pressures [2]. For cementitious or polyurethane systems the equipment list is expanded to include high‑speed mixers operating between 800 rpm and 2000 rpm, proportioning devices for the two‑component polymer, cylindrical plugs, hoses, valves, manifolds, lift‑measurement devices accurate to 0.001 inch, and a compressor capable of supplying at least 100 cfm of air for blowing compressed air through the drilled cavities [2]. Asphalt‑based stabilization adds an insulated transport tanker, a reversible under‑seal pump, a pressure‑water distributor trailer, a tapered nozzle with a three‑way valve, and splash shields to contain hot material, while also requiring safety gear to protect crews from the hot liquid asphalt that may squirt during injection [1][2]. The work is sequenced so that joint and crack sealing and the drilling of access holes are performed immediately before grout injection, allowing both the drilling crew and the stabilizing crew to share a single set of Maintenance and Protection of Traffic devices and to operate within a single lane at any one time [1]. After the holes are drilled to the approved pattern and depth that penetrates the underlying base, they are cleared, plugged, and compressed air is blown while the drill is withdrawn before the grout or hot asphalt is pumped into the cavities [2]. When an asphalt grout is used the material must be heated to a temperature between 380 °F and 450 °F (never exceeding 500 °F and kept away from open flame) prior to pumping at pressures of 25 to 45 psi, whereas cement or polyurethane mixes are prepared on‑site, placed within one hour of mixing, and injected under a maximum pressure of 200 psi with occasional short spikes permitted up to 300 psi and rare peaks to 600 psi under special conditions [2]. Throughout injection the rise of the pavement is continuously monitored and must not exceed 0.05 inch; any upward movement beyond this tolerance requires an immediate stop, removal of excess material or grinding as dictated by the quality‑control plan [2]. Environmental constraints limit work to daytime temperatures of at least 40 °F with the subgrade and base course unfrozen, and deflection testing further restricts air temperature to a range of 40 °F to 70 °F while prohibiting spring‑thaw conditions [2]. Traffic restrictions mandate that appropriate traffic control remain in place for the entire curing period, that splash shields of minimum size be installed during injection, and that the lane under repair not be reopened until the injected material has cured or cooled—up to about twelve hours for cement grout or at least thirty minutes for asphalt or polyurethane systems [1][2]. The combined use of shared MPT devices, single‑lane confinement, and post‑injection monitoring ensures that traffic safety is maintained while allowing the slab stabilization process to proceed efficiently [1].

Quality and workmanship

Inspection begins by confirming the presence of loss‑of‑support indicators such as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines or debris in joints and cracks, small blow‑hole depressions near shoulders, corner breaks, and overall slab settlement. Prior to any grout placement all joints and cracks must be sealed and subsurface drainage verified so that water infiltration is minimized. The material to be injected must be the specified grout—cementitious, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane—and the contractor must provide current certification and an approved mix design that has been reviewed before work commences. For cementitious mixes the volumetric ratio of one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material is required, with water added so that the efflux time measured according to ASTM C939 falls between sixteen and twenty‑six seconds; a more fluid mix with an efflux of ten to fifteen seconds may be used for the first injection, and a stiffer grout must be employed if the initial mix fails to raise the slab. The accompanying laboratory test package must demonstrate expansion limited to zero‑to‑ten percent, an initial set time of one to six hours, bleed not exceeding 2.5 % of the volume, and a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi based on five cylinders, together with documented flowability, shrinkage/expansion behavior and the required 1‑, 3‑ and 7‑day strengths. Preparation also includes verification that all pumping equipment is functional and calibrated, with calibration certificates dated within the preceding twelve months, and each unit equipped with a display capable of recording material volume in pounds. Pressure‑and‑temperature control devices for polyurethane grout must be inspected to confirm they maintain proper mix temperature and proportionate component mixing, and the drilling rig must be checked that it can produce smooth, vertical holes whose diameters do not exceed five‑eighths inch where specified and never more than one‑and‑a‑half inches overall, reaching a depth sufficient to penetrate any stabilized base. Cylindrical plugs used in the holes must match the required length and tapered design to seat up to two inches deep, and lift‑measurement equipment must be capable of detecting slab movement with a resolution of 0.001 inch. During installation the field quality‑control plan is reviewed for hole spacing, depth, diameter, drilling method and injection sequence, and each drilled hole is cleared with compressed air before grout placement. When asphalt grout is used it must be heated to a temperature between 380 °F and 450 °F; any material exceeding 500 °F is rejected. Injection pressure is continuously monitored, being limited to 25‑45 psi for asphalt and not to exceed 200 psi for other grouts, with short‑term allowances up to 300 psi or brief spikes to 600 psi when bonded; the rate of injection is controlled so that upward movement greater than 0.05 inch triggers an immediate stop. Elevation after jacking must fall within prescribed tolerances—plus or minus 0.02 foot for continuously reinforced concrete slabs and plus or minus 0.03 foot for jointed slabs—relative to the string‑line grade. Deflection testing of every joint or crack is performed in accordance with ASTM D4695 when ambient temperature lies between 40 °F and 70 °F, using a loaded slab corner deflection threshold of at least 0.02 inch and requiring a joint efficiency of 65 percent; if the temperature falls outside this window visual verification of pumping quality or joint performance is used instead. After grouting the wooden plugs are removed and all drill holes are patched full‑depth with rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete flush with the surface, and the stabilized joints are retested after twenty‑four hours; any joint that still exceeds the deflection limit is re‑grouted. Traffic may not be reopened until a minimum of thirty minutes has elapsed for asphalt operations or twelve hours for cementitious or polyurethane grout unless the approved QC plan specifies a different interval, and a final roadway profile report documenting achieved elevations must be submitted; overjacking beyond 0.10 foot triggers grinding or full removal and replacement as prescribed. The completed work is finally inspected to confirm that voids are fully filled, no new surface depressions exist, and the slab can carry load without excessive deflection, thereby verifying restored support. Slab stabilization is considered satisfactory when all observable indications of lost slab support have been eliminated, including transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines at joints or cracks, small depressions (blow‑holes) in shoulders, corner breaks, and any measurable settlement, thereby restoring full subgrade support beneath the concrete. The overarching objective is to fill underlying voids sufficiently so that slab deflections are reduced and further pumping, faulting, or cracking become unlikely. Acceptance of a stabilization job requires that the equipment used for grout placement be current, with a calibration certificate dated within the previous twelve months and pump flow readouts capable of being manually reset. Drill holes must be vertical, round, and their diameter may not exceed five‑eighths of an inch unless a larger size is expressly authorized, in which case the absolute maximum permitted is one‑and‑a‑half inches, and each hole must penetrate through any unstable base without breaking out the slab. During pumping the operator must stop immediately if any vertical movement of the pavement is observed, and no radial cracks may emanate from the injection holes or develop as transverse cracks between holes. The grout must conform to an approved mix design, which may be cement‑based, asphalt‑based, or high‑density polyurethane, and any change in material source triggers a new approved design. For cement‑based grout the prescribed proportions are one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, with a water efflux time of ten to fifteen seconds for the initial injection and sixteen to twenty‑six seconds for subsequent placements as required by ASTM C939. The cement grout may expand only between zero and ten percent, set initially within one to six hours, bleed no more than 2.5 % of its volume, and must achieve a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi based on the average of five cylinders. When asphalt grout is used it must be heated to a temperature between 380 °F and 450 °F; material above 500 °F is rejected, and it is pumped at a pressure of twenty‑five to forty‑five psi, never exceeding two hundred psi unless otherwise directed. Injection pressure is monitored with equipment capable of detecting slab lift to a resolution of one‑thousandth of an inch. The primary geometric acceptance criterion focuses on slab movement; at each treated joint or crack the loaded corner deflection must not exceed 0.02 inch, and joint efficiency must be at least sixty‑five percent for full stabilization, with lower efficiency permissible only when a patching procedure has been approved. After grouting a retest performed no sooner than twenty‑four hours later must confirm that the deflection remains below this limit, and if measurable movement persists after two proper groutings the slab is rejected. The overall uplift of any panel may not exceed 0.05 inch; any excess material must be ground back to grade before final acceptance. For continuously reinforced concrete pavements the final post‑jacking elevation must fall within plus or minus 0.02 foot of the string‑line reference, while jointed slabs and bridge end sections may deviate up to plus or minus 0.03 foot; over‑jacking greater than 0.10 foot requires grinding down or full removal and replacement at no additional cost. Operational timing limits are enforced: the contractor must wait at least thirty minutes after grout placement before removing equipment, and the pavement may not be opened to traffic for less than twelve hours following the grouting operation. Acceptance is completed only after a complete roadway profile report showing existing, proposed, and achieved grades has been submitted and reviewed by the responsible representative. [1][2]

Performance and service life

Slab stabilization is employed wherever the concrete slab lacks uniform bearing, such as around patches or in areas where voids have formed beneath the slab due to pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or settlement. By drilling holes and injecting a suitable fill material—commonly cement grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane—the voids are eliminated and full support is re‑established. This restoration of bearing reduces slab deflection, curtails transverse joint faulting, corner breaks, and further cracking, thereby improving the pavement’s structural integrity and ride quality. When the stabilization is incorporated into a thin‑asphalt preservation overlay—typically a 1.0‑inch (6.3 mm) layer—the surface condition is enhanced through better skid resistance and smoother riding characteristics. Although the treatment does not increase the original design capacity or remove existing surface depressions, it nonetheless upgrades functional performance, prolongs durability, and extends the expected service life of the repaired section without requiring a full structural redesign. Because PennDOT classifies slab stabilization among its cost‑effective preservation measures, it is recognized as a practical means to maintain pavement condition and longevity. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Slab stabilization treatments can still exhibit a suite of recurring distresses that indicate incomplete remediation of underlying voids. Typical surface manifestations include transverse joint faulting, corner breaks, slab settlement, blow‑hole depressions and the buildup of fines at joints or cracks, all of which signal that voids beneath the concrete remain. These failures are fundamentally linked to the pumping action that creates or enlarges voids, subgrade consolidation under load, and water infiltration that compromises support. When joint and crack sealing or subsurface drainage are insufficient, moisture continues to enter the pavement and exacerbate the loss of support. In addition to these underlying mechanisms, improper control of grout placement and temperature during installation introduces distinct distresses. Radial cracks radiating from injection holes and transverse cracks that develop between adjacent holes are clear indicators of poor quality or incorrect installation methods. Vertical movement of the slab during pumping is another common problem; lifts greater than the allowable 0.05 inch tolerance constitute a violation, with upward movements exceeding one‑tenth of a foot typically requiring full slab removal and replacement, while smaller excesses are corrected by grinding to restore grade. Excessive uplift is usually caused by injecting grout at pressures outside the specified range of 25 to 45 psi or by delivering an excessive volume that raises the panel beyond the permissible limits. Overheating the asphalt binder above roughly 500 °F also reduces durability, and uncontrolled seepage from joints or surface cracks before the material has fully congealed further compromises performance. These installation‑related failures are driven primarily by deviations from prescribed pressure, temperature, grout volume, and quality‑control procedures such as proper hole cleaning and timely cessation of pumping when movement is detected. Because slab stabilization does not increase the pavement’s structural capacity or fully correct surface depressions, any remaining moisture or insufficient support can cause the same deflection‑induced cracking to reappear after the grout has cured. Consequently, successful treatment requires adequate joint and crack sealing, effective subsurface drainage, strict adherence to pressure (25–45 psi), temperature (<500 °F), lift tolerances (≤0.05 inch), and vigilant monitoring of movement and cracking during and after placement. Monitoring and follow-up

After slab stabilization is completed, the contractor must conduct a post‑grout inspection that includes retesting the deflection of each treated joint or crack twenty‑four hours after the grout has set, as directed by the Representative. Any joint that still exhibits a loaded‑slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more must be re‑grouted and retested, and acceptance is permitted only after two successful groutings; otherwise full‑depth replacement of the slab is required. Visual examination for radial or transverse cracks and verification that patched holes are flush with the pavement surface also form part of the acceptance criteria. At the same time, the contractor must submit a roadway‑profile quality‑control package containing surveyed profile drawings that display the original depression, the design grade, and the as‑built grade achieved by grout jacking. The Representative reviews these drawings and may require additional injection if the as‑built profile does not meet the specified grades. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–598)

When a slab that has been treated continues to exhibit transverse joint faulting, the presence of fines accumulating at joints or cracks on either the traffic lane or shoulder, and small blow‑hole depressions in the shoulder adjacent to those joints or cracks, these signs reveal that voids remain untreated. Corner breaks and any overall settlement of the slab are likewise indicators that the original stabilization was inadequate. Persistent slab movement—including rocking, pumping, or continued faulting—also signals that further maintenance is required before additional work such as an overlay can be performed. Measured loaded corner deflection values equal to or exceeding 0.02 inch, especially when combined with joint efficiency falling below roughly 65 percent, are taken as quantitative thresholds indicating insufficient treatment. The appearance of radial cracks radiating from grout injection holes, transverse cracking between adjacent injection holes, and any vertical movement observed during pumping all point to poor quality or improper execution of the stabilization and therefore call for re‑grouting or full‑depth replacement. Profile surveys that still show depressions or a grade that cannot be raised within ±0.02 foot of the designed string line trigger another injection cycle, while jacking that lifts the slab more than 0.10 foot beyond the target elevation requires removal of excess material or complete pavement replacement. In summary, the combination of these distress patterns and measured performance limits across the guides defines when additional maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation is necessary after slab stabilization.
