Slab Stabilization

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Slab Stabilization is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
slab-stabilization
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-30

Slab stabilization is a preservation technique applied to Portland‑cement concrete pavements that have lost uniform bearing because voids have developed beneath the slab or its underlying base, often as a result of pumping action, subgrade consolidation, settlement, or damage near joints and corners [1][2]. The treatment fills those subgrade cavities—commonly with a thin (~1 in) asphalt overlay, cementitious grout, or high‑density polyurethane—and stabilizes any rocking slabs so that full, even support is re‑established at the slab/subbase interface, thereby restoring load‑bearing capacity, ride quality and skid resistance [1][2]. Acceptance criteria require measurable distress such as a loaded corner deflection of 0.02 in or greater, joint efficiency of about 65 % or less, or visible rocking before work begins, and post‑treatment limits on uplift (≤0.05 in) and grade tolerance (±0.02–0.03 ft) to ensure the slab can safely receive an overlay [2].

PennDOT publications covering “Slab Stabilization” also include the following subtopics: Asphalt Undersealing, Cement Grout, and Slabjacking.

Slab Stabilization employs Asphalt Undersealing as a specialized treatment that injects hot liquid asphalt into voids beneath distressed slabs to restore support while strictly monitoring lift limits. [1][2] The procedure utilizes Cement Grout as the primary fluid mixture, proportioned with supplementary cementitious materials and admixtures to fill the slab-subbase interface and reduce deflection without increasing design capacity. [1][2] Slabjacking serves as a method within this framework that restores elevation by injecting lift material into drilled holes along joints or cracks to eliminate faulting and corner breaks and regain uniform load distribution. [1][2]

Purpose and treatment objectives

Slab stabilization is employed when a Portland‑cement concrete pavement exhibits loss or uneven bearing caused by subsurface voids beneath the slab or its underlying base [1]. These voids may arise from pumping action, subgrade consolidation, settlement, or damage near transverse joints, cracks and slab corners, producing symptoms such as rocking, pumping, faulting, transverse‑joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking, small depressions (blow‑holes), excessive deflection and differential settlement that degrade ride quality and skid resistance [1]. The guides also define the distress in quantitative terms, specifying that loaded slab‑corner deflections of 0.02 inch or greater, joint efficiencies at about 65 % or less, visible depressions requiring the slab to be raised within ±0.02 foot of the design grade line, or upward lifts exceeding 0.05 inch constitute unacceptable vertical movement [2]. In addition, any slabs or fragments that rock visibly under normal traffic loads are identified as having non‑uniform bearing that must be corrected before an overlay can be placed [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–98), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–612)

Slab stabilization is employed when voids or movement exist beneath a rigid‑base or concrete pavement that would otherwise cause non‑uniform support for an overlay. [1][2] The treatment fills those voids, often with a thin asphalt overlay about one inch (≈6.3 mm) thick, and stabilizes any rocking slabs so that full and uniform support is re‑established at the slab/subbase interface. [1][2] By repairing the loss of underlying support and fixing unstable sections, the primary objective is to restore the pavement’s intended functional performance—improving load‑bearing capacity, skid resistance, ride quality, and reducing deflection. [1][2] Any extension of service life or slowing of further deterioration is a secondary benefit that follows from achieving this restored functionality. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Distress characterization and causes

Slab stabilization is applied to address voids that develop beneath concrete pavements, most commonly at transverse joints, cracks and the outer corners of slabs, and may appear as blow‑hole depressions in shoulders. [1] The distress also includes areas where whole slab sections or fragments move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads, indicating a loss of uniform support for any subsequent overlay. [2] These locations represent the extent of the problem, ranging from isolated sub‑grade voids to larger unstable slab fragments that compromise the continuity of the pavement surface. [1][2] The lack of subgrade support caused by the voids produces excessive slab deflection and stress, which manifest as joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and, in advanced stages, complete slab breakup. [1] When slabs or fragments rock under traffic, the resulting non‑uniform support can precipitate premature failure of an overlay, further reducing structural integrity. [2] The severity is therefore high, as both conditions degrade the pavement’s structural capacity and ride quality, creating safety concerns such as increased faulting, potential pumping, and risk of slab collapse. [1][2] Although the guides do not provide a quantified progression rate, they describe a typical sequence in which small voids evolve into faulted joints and eventually full‑depth breakup if left untreated, while visible slab movement is treated as an immediate safety and operational issue. [1][2] The operational consequences include reduced ride smoothness, increased maintenance needs, and the possibility that an overlay placed over unstable areas will fail prematurely, necessitating additional repairs. [2] Overall, the distress addressed by slab stabilization poses significant safety and serviceability risks that must be remedied before further pavement rehabilitation. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Slab stabilization is required when concrete pavements exhibit movement such as rocking, pumping or faulting, which signals that the slab no longer rests on uniform support. The distress originates from a combination of inadequate drainage of surface and subsurface water and insufficient structural capacity in the underlying base or sub‑base. Water infiltrating through joints or cracks initiates a pumping action; together with subgrade consolidation and settlement this creates voids beneath the slab, removing bearing support. The loss of support produces excessive deflection that manifests as transverse joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and ultimately slab breakup. Heavy traffic loads can further stress the slab when the underlying support is weak, while climate‑related durability problems—particularly drainage failures—accelerate water‑induced deterioration. Effective remediation therefore incorporates filling voids, improving subgrade structural capacity, sealing joints and cracks, and maintaining functional subsurface drains to prevent recurrence of the distress. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–98)

Applicability and treatment selection

Slab stabilization is indicated for Portland‑cement concrete (rigid) pavements that show loss of subgrade support manifested by transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines at joints or cracks, small blow‑hole depressions near joints, corner breaks, and overall slab settlement. [1][2] The method is also appropriate when measurable loss of support is confirmed by a loaded‑slab corner deflection at a joint or crack of 0.02 inch (0.5 mm) or greater together with a joint efficiency of approximately 65 percent or higher, unless an approved patching plan permits lower efficiency. [2] Visible instability such as slabs or slab fragments that move or rock under normal traffic loads likewise qualifies the pavement for stabilization. [2] The treatment is intended for situations where joint and crack sealing can be performed concurrently and functional subsurface drainage exists, ensuring that voids beneath the slab caused by pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or water infiltration are addressed without increasing the original design capacity. [1] Traffic considerations require that stabilization be completed before any overlay or interlayer material is placed, so that the underlying support is restored prior to additional pavement layers. [2] Environmental constraints dictate that work be performed when daytime air temperatures are between about 40 °F and 70 °F, the subgrade or base course is not frozen, and spring‑thaw conditions are avoided; testing and grout placement should not occur outside this temperature window. [2] Qualified personnel with documented experience in slab stabilization must be present, and grout injection must be controlled so that upward movement of the slab does not exceed roughly 0.05 inch. [2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Slab stabilization is only appropriate for pavements that are structurally sound, a condition that must be verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, and where the required patching does not exceed roughly ten percent of the slab area, excluding any existing patches already in good condition; if this patch‑area threshold is surpassed or a cost‑effectiveness analysis shows it would be uneconomical, an alternative such as full‑depth patching, overlay, or replacement should be pursued [1]. The technique is intended to address loss of support caused by voids beneath the slab that produce excessive deflection, and it does not remedy surface depressions, blow‑holes, corner breaks, diagonal cracking, faulted joints, nor does it increase the pavement’s design structural capacity; therefore, when such visible defects or a need for additional structural strength are present, another maintenance or rehabilitation method must be selected [1]. Slab stabilization is also unsuitable when the underlying distress is dominated by climate‑or material‑related durability problems—such as frost damage or aggregate polishing—or by load‑related structural deficiencies that require dowel retrofits or other structural improvements, in which case a more extensive repair approach is required [1]. Environmental and performance limits further restrict the use of slab stabilization: work cannot commence if daytime air temperature is below 40 °F, if the subgrade or base course material is frozen, or during spring‑thaw conditions, and deflection testing is prohibited when temperatures are under 40 °F or above 70 °F [2]. The treatment should be avoided when visual inspection reveals poor joint performance that cannot be verified by testing, when anticipated upward movement of the pavement exceeds 0.05 in., or when the representative determines that continued grout placement is not economically feasible at a given location [2]. Additionally, if the lift required for stabilization would cause over‑jacking greater than about 0.10 ft, the specification mandates removal and replacement of the slab rather than continuing stabilization [2]. Under any of these circumstances—excessive patch area, unsuitable structural or distress conditions, adverse temperature or subgrade states, excessive upward movement, over‑jacking beyond limits, or economic infeasibility—a different repair method such as full‑depth patching, slab replacement, or another preservation technique should be selected [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–612)

Condition assessment and timing

Determining the need for slab stabilization begins with a systematic condition assessment that first requires a thorough visual inspection of the concrete pavement, during which all visible distress such as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines at joints or cracks on traffic lanes and shoulders, small blow‑hole depressions adjacent to joints, corner breaks and any general slab settlement are quantified and recorded. The visual survey is also used to identify slabs or concrete fragments that move, rock, pump, or otherwise appear unstable under normal traffic loads, because the presence of such movement confirms that stabilization (or jacking) is required before overlay placement. When ambient temperature permits testing, a field crew conducts deflection testing in accordance with ASTM D4695 on every transverse joint and crack, measuring both loaded and unloaded corner deflections so that joint efficiency can be calculated. Any joint or crack that exhibits a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 inch or greater triggers stabilization, provided the computed joint efficiency meets the minimum threshold of 65 percent unless a lower value has been expressly approved by the project representative. If temperature conditions fall outside the allowable range for deflection testing, the crew relies on visual verification for pumping or other poor joint performance before specifying slab stabilization. In addition to visual and deflection assessments, core samples are extracted both near deteriorated joints and at randomly selected locations so that subbase or subgrade soil conditions can be evaluated and hidden deterioration beneath the surface can be identified. A Falling Weight Deflectometer test is then run on the existing pavement to assess its overall structural capacity prior to any overlay design, with the resulting deflection measurements used together with faulting measurements to locate zones that lack adequate support. Non‑destructive techniques such as ground‑penetrating radar may be employed to pinpoint joints that require replacement and to delineate voids beneath the slab, further refining the areas where stabilization is needed. A roadway profile survey is also performed; existing surface elevations are recorded, depressions are highlighted, and the as‑built grade line is plotted against the proposed design grade, with any discrepancy indicating that additional grout injection or other stabilization actions are required to achieve the target elevation. Together, these inspections—visual quantification of distress, temperature‑qualified deflection testing, core sampling, FWD and other non‑destructive measurements, and detailed profile surveying—provide the comprehensive set of observations, measurements, and tests needed to determine both where and whether slab stabilization is required. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–98); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–612)

Slab stabilization is indicated whenever a concrete slab has lost its uniform support, which can be recognized by observable distress such as voids beneath the slab, rocking, pumping, faulting, transverse‑joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks, small depressions (blow‑holes), corner breaks, or overall settlement. These conditions are typically confirmed through diagnostic testing—including deflection tests, faulting measurements, Falling Weight Deflectometer results, or other nondestructive methods—that reveal excessive movement or voids under specific joints or slab areas. Quantitatively, the guides require stabilization when loaded slab‑corner deflection reaches at least 0.02 inch and the joint efficiency is 65 percent or greater, a threshold that signals measurable instability likely to affect overlay performance. If the joint efficiency falls below this level, stabilization may still be mandated provided patching or repair is approved by the agency representative. Profile surveys that identify depressions preventing the slab from attaining the required grade also trigger stabilization, with jacking prescribed until the surface lies within approximately 0.02 foot of a string‑line reference for continuously reinforced concrete slabs or about 0.03 foot for jointed slabs, as directed by the representative. The treatment is generally applied to localized areas that affect less than roughly ten percent of the pavement surface on a structurally sound slab verified by FWD testing, and it should be performed before any overlay, interlayer placement, or further rehabilitation to re‑establish full, even support for the slab. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–98), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–612)

Materials and repair details

Slab stabilization restores support beneath concrete pavements by filling underlying voids with one of three grout systems— a cement‑based fluid grout, a hot asphalt undersealing, or a high‑density polyurethane foam that expands to occupy the cavity[1][2]. The cement grout is produced from a mixture of one part cement by volume combined with three parts supplementary cementitious material, any approved admixtures, and sufficient water to achieve an efflux time of roughly sixteen to twenty‑six seconds, although a more fluid batch targeting ten to fifteen seconds may be used for the initial injection[2]. The hot asphalt grout must meet ASTM D3141, possess a flash point of at least five hundred degrees Fahrenheit, and be heated to between three‑hundred eighty and four‑hundred fifty degrees before pumping; each hole receives between thirty and fifty gallons of liquid asphalt which is allowed to cool for a minimum of thirty minutes prior to traffic reopening[1][2]. The polyurethane option consists of a two‑component low‑viscosity foam mixed according to the manufacturer’s specifications, expanding at approximately 0.4 lb per square foot and hardening in roughly half an hour[1][2].

All three methods require vertical, round drill holes that penetrate the slab and reach the supporting base; for cement and asphalt applications the hole diameter may not exceed one‑and‑a‑half inches, while the polyurethane technique calls for holes no larger than five‑eighths of an inch[2]. Full‑depth patches typically employ a two‑hole layout aligned with the joint or crack pattern, and when testing is omitted at least twenty‑five percent of transverse joints must be treated; the drilled apertures are sealed after placement using either expanding rubber packers or hoses for cement work, or cylindrical tapered plugs driven about two inches into the hole for polyurethane and asphalt operations[1][2].

The injection equipment must include a positive‑displacement pump; cement grout delivery is paired with a high‑speed mixer capable of eight hundred to two thousand revolutions per minute, whereas the polyurethane system uses a calibrated pump that records pressure, temperature, and weight during discharge[2]. Asphalt work additionally requires a fully insulated transport tanker equipped with a removable hatch and a minimum two‑inch overflow pipe, a pressure‑water distributor trailer of at least fifty gallons capacity, and a compressor delivering no less than one hundred cubic feet per minute to purge voids with air before injection[2].

Injection pressures are limited to two hundred pounds per square inch for cement and polyurethane grout, while asphalt is placed at twenty‑five to forty‑five psi, with any brief excursions above two hundred psi permitted only under project direction[2]. The volume of material injected per hole is roughly one cubic foot of cement grout (about a quarter bag of cement), thirty to fifty gallons of hot asphalt, or the amount of polyurethane foam required to fill the void as dictated by its expansion rate[1][2].

Lift monitoring equipment capable of detecting movement as small as one‑thousandth of an inch is employed to ensure that slab rise does not exceed five hundredths of an inch; for continuously reinforced pavements the target lift is within plus or minus two hundredths of a foot of the design grade, and for jointed slabs within three hundredths of a foot, with any over‑jacking greater than one tenth of a foot requiring grinding or removal of the affected section[2].

After grouting, excess material is removed and each drilled hole is patched flush with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete patching material; protective splash shields of at least fifteen square feet are installed for asphalt operations, all joints and cracks are sealed, and drainage provisions are maintained to prevent future void formation[1][2]. An optional thin asphalt overlay may be placed in conjunction with slab stabilization, limited to one inch thickness and using a 6.3 mm mix design, to improve skid resistance and ride quality as a non‑structural wearing course[1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 600–612)

Slab stabilization performance depends first on the condition of the existing concrete slab and its supporting structure, including compressive strength, air content, and overall structural soundness verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing. [1][2] The amount of existing patchwork must remain below roughly ten percent of the total surface area; projects exceeding this threshold require an additional cost‑effectiveness analysis before proceeding. [1] Surface characteristics such as adequate friction, acceptable ride quality, and the absence of polishing‑susceptible aggregate also influence the long‑term success of the treatment. [1] Void locations are typically found near transverse joints, cracks, or at outside slab corners, manifesting as joint faulting, fines, blow‑holes, corner breaks, or settlement, and accurate identification of these distress types guides the selection of fill material. [1] For cement‑based grout the prescribed mix is one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, with water added to achieve an efflux time of ten to fifteen seconds for the initial injection and sixteen to twenty‑six seconds for subsequent placement. [2] The grout must expand no more than ten percent, set within one to six hours, bleed no more than 2.5 % of its volume, and attain a minimum seven‑day compressive strength of 700 psi. [2] High‑density polyurethane is supplied as a low‑viscosity two‑component system; proper temperature control and proportionate mixing are required using pressure‑ and temperature‑control devices that have been calibrated within the past twelve months, and injection holes must be vertical, smooth, and no larger than five‑eighths of an inch. [2][1] The polyurethane expands into a closed‑cell foam that is lightweight yet rigid, providing resistance to water ingress and chemical attack while maintaining durability. [1] Asphalt grout must be heated to a temperature between 380 °F and 450 °F (materials exceeding 500 °F are rejected) and pumped at pressures of 25 to 45 psi; continuous pressure may not exceed 200 psi, with short‑duration increases to 300 psi permitted under specific directives and brief spikes to 600 psi allowed only when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade. [2] All grout or asphalt materials must meet relevant specifications—ASTM D3141 for asphalt, ASTM C939 for cement efflux time, ASTM C940 for expansion, bleed, and setting criteria—and be sourced from approved manufacturers within their shelf life. [2][1] Compatibility with the existing pavement requires comprehensive joint and crack sealing to limit water entry that could reactivate pumping, and the selected fill material must be compatible with any preservation layer such as a one‑inch (6.3 mm) thin asphalt overlay or micro‑surfacing so that bonding is achieved without compromising ride quality. [1][2] The overlay thickness commonly applied in slab stabilization is a one‑inch thin asphalt wearing course, which must bond effectively to the stabilized slab and any underlying preservation layer. [1] Subgrade or subbase stability and functional drainage are essential; when these conditions are met bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays can be used, whereas compromised support may necessitate unbonded solutions with reduced performance. [1][2] Ambient conditions must allow work only when daytime temperatures are above forty degrees Fahrenheit and the subgrade or base course is unfrozen; otherwise the treatment should be postponed. [2] Joints or cracks to be treated must exhibit a loaded corner deflection of at least 0.02 inches and a joint efficiency of 65 % or greater before stabilization can proceed. [2] During jacking, upward movement of the slab may not exceed five‑hundredths of an inch; any lift beyond this indicates incompatibility with the existing concrete and requires reassessment. [1][2] For continuously reinforced slabs the target elevation after jacking is within ±0.02 ft of the string line grade, while jointed slabs and bridge‑end sections may be raised within ±0.03 ft; lifts exceeding a tenth of a foot mandate removal and replacement of the slab. [2] All equipment used for material preparation and injection must be calibrated—pumps tested within the previous twelve months and pressure devices capable of maintaining the specified limits—to ensure consistent performance. [2][1] Finally, any water or thin mud present in voids must be cleared by air before grout placement to prevent interference with material expansion and bonding. [2]

Treatment procedures

Before any slab‑stabilization work begins, all joints and cracks are sealed and subsurface drains are kept functional to limit water ingress. The owner must assign a qualified crew supervisor with at least five years of comparable experience and prepare a detailed field quality‑control plan ten working days in advance, documenting line‑and‑grade control, hole spacing, drilling methods, equipment specifications, waste‑disposal procedures, required curing periods, traffic opening times, and existing roadway profiles that show present depressions and the proposed grade line. Deflection testing in accordance with ASTM D4695 is performed when ambient temperature lies between 40 °F and 70 °F, and any joint or crack exhibiting a loaded slab‑corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more with a joint efficiency of at least 65 % (or as otherwise approved) is marked for treatment. Traffic control devices are installed to protect both the drilling crew and the stabilization crew while drilling is carried out at locations and in patterns prescribed by construction standards, typically just prior to grout placement. Vertical grout‑injection holes are drilled with a diameter not exceeding one‑and‑a‑half inches, or 5/8 inch when high‑density polyurethane is specified, to a depth sufficient to penetrate any previously stabilized base. As the drill is withdrawn, compressed air is blown through each bore for five to fifteen seconds to clear debris. The stabilizing material—either cement‑based grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane—is mixed exactly as the manufacturer’s specification dictates; cement grout must be placed within one hour of preparation and asphalt is heated to a temperature between 380 °F and 450 °F before pumping. An expanding rubber packer attached to a calibrated pump hose is lowered into each hole so that the discharge end rests just above the concrete surface and never extends below the lower face of the slab. The material is injected under continuous pressure not exceeding 200 psi (25‑45 psi for asphalt unless otherwise directed) until the pavement lift reaches within 0.02 ft of the target grade for continuously reinforced slabs or within 0.03 ft for jointed slabs, and upward movement greater than 0.05 in is prohibited. When the desired elevation is achieved, the packer is withdrawn, any excess material on the surface is removed, wooden plugs used during drilling are extracted, and each hole is patched with rapid‑set non‑shrink concrete flush with the surrounding pavement. For asphalt work a water ring and splash shield protect traffic while the material congeals, and any seepage is halted until solidification. The repaired area must be kept free of traffic for at least thirty minutes after grouting operations, or twelve hours when cement grout is used, unless the QC plan and manufacturer guidance permit earlier opening; traffic may be restored only after a retest confirms no excessive movement occurred during curing. All waste material generated during drilling and injection is disposed of in accordance with the approved waste‑disposal plan, and final line‑and‑grade control is verified before acceptance. [1][2]

The implementation of slab stabilization begins with a documented field quality‑control plan that records the existing pavement profile and defines the hole pattern and spacing required for the repair [2]. The equipment suite must include drilling rigs capable of creating access holes, core drills fitted with diamond‑tip barrels (hole diameters limited to 5/8 inch for polyurethane or up to 1½ inch when using asphalt), pumping units for cement grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane, and personal protective equipment to guard crews from hot liquids [1][2]. Additional required hardware comprises a positive‑displacement cement injection pump paired with a mixing machine that can operate between 800 rpm and 2000 rpm, a grout plant for cementitious mixes, a calibrated high‑density polyurethane pump equipped with pressure and temperature control devices, an insulated asphalt transport tanker with a temperature readout, tapered injection nozzles with three‑way valves, cylindrical plugs and other sealing accessories, a water tanker of sufficient capacity, a pressure‑water distributor trailer, an underseal pump with reversible hose, and a compressor capable of delivering at least 100 cfm for air‑blowing the cavities; precision lift‑measurement devices able to detect movements as small as 0.001 inch are also required to monitor slab rise during injection [2]. Sequencing starts by drilling the prescribed vertical holes and immediately clearing them with compressed air, after which water is applied around each hole and a splash shield erected before mixing the selected grout according to manufacturer specifications [2]. When asphalt grout is used, the material must be heated to between 380 °F and 450 °F prior to circulation, while joint and crack sealing are performed just before grout pumping so that both crews can share a single set of Maintenance and Protection of Traffic devices and work within one lane at a time [1][2]. Injection pressures are controlled for each material: continuous pressures may reach 200 psi for cement grout with short‑term elevations to 300 psi (and up to 600 psi when the pavement is bonded to the subgrade), asphalt is pumped at 25–45 psi, and polyurethane injection rates are regulated to keep slab lift below 0.05 inch, halting further injection if upward movement exceeds that limit [2]. The rise of the pavement is continuously monitored with the precision devices, and any over‑jacked areas identified after grouting are corrected by grinding or replacement before traffic is restored [2]. Environmental constraints require that work not commence when daytime temperatures fall below 40 °F, when the subgrade or base course is frozen, or during periods of spring thaw; deflection testing is limited to air temperatures between 40 °F and 70 °F, asphalt must be maintained within its temperature window, and open flames are prohibited during heating operations [2]. Traffic restrictions mandate that only a single lane be closed at any one time while drilling and stabilization occur, that the same MPT devices protect both crews, and that the slab remain closed for a minimum of thirty minutes after polyurethane or asphalt injection and for at least twelve hours after cement grout injection—or longer if specified in the approved QC plan—before reopening; final traffic reopening is permitted only after all jacking corrections are completed and the prescribed curing period has been verified [1][2].

Quality and workmanship

Inspection of slab stabilization begins by confirming that loss‑of‑support symptoms such as transverse joint faulting, fines or debris at joints and cracks, blow‑hole depressions near shoulders, corner breaks, and overall slab settlement are present before work proceeds. The first element inspected is the grout material itself, requiring a pre‑approved mix design that is accompanied by independent laboratory test results showing early compressive strengths at 1, 3 and 7 days, flowability, shrinkage or expansion, initial set time and bleed limits. The mix must achieve an expansion between 0 % and 10 % (ASTM C940), an initial setting window of 1 to 6 hours (AASHTO T 131), a bleed limit not exceeding 2.5 % of the volume, and a 7‑day compressive strength averaging at least 700 psi based on five cylinders. For cement‑based grout the prescribed proportion is one part cement to three parts supplementary cementitious material by volume, with any approved admixtures, and water content controlled so that the efflux time falls between 16 and 26 seconds (ASTM C939), with an initial injection efflux of ten to fifteen seconds if required. When asphalt grout is used it must be heated to a temperature range of 380 °F to 450 °F, any material above 500 °F being rejected, and the injection pressure limited to 25‑45 psi. High‑density polyurethane or other specialty grouts may be injected at continuous pressures not exceeding 200 psi, with short‑term peaks permitted up to 300 psi and brief bonded spikes allowed to 600 psi. Prior to placement the crew must verify that all transverse joints and longitudinal cracks have been properly sealed and that subsurface drainage functions are intact, actions intended to reduce water infiltration that contributes to pumping. Existing roadway profiles and the proposed grade line for the treatment area are documented, providing a baseline for post‑stabilization comparison. The work package must detail hole spacing, depth, diameter and drilling method; drilled holes are required to be vertical, round, and sized no larger than 5/8 in. when using cement or polyurethane grout and up to 1½ in. for asphalt grout, with each hole cleared of debris by compressed air and wetted before injection. The pump unit used for material placement must possess a current calibration certificate dated within the preceding twelve months and be equipped with a calibrated weight‑tracking meter; flowability of the mixed grout is checked twice each shift and the mix may not remain in the mixer or pump sump longer than one hour after mixing. During injection the prescribed hole pattern and volume are followed so that the slab is raised within the tolerance of ±0.02 ft for continuously reinforced pavement or ±0.03 ft for jointed slabs, with any over‑jacking beyond 0.10 ft requiring removal and replacement of material. Lift is monitored with equipment capable of detecting movement as small as 0.001 in.; an upward movement greater than 0.05 in. triggers an immediate stop to pumping. Pressure is continuously observed to remain within the allowable limits for the selected grout type, and excess material is removed from each hole before traffic is permitted. The crew must observe the required curing or cooling period—approximately twelve hours for cement‑based grout, at least thirty minutes for hot asphalt or polyurethane—before reopening the slab to traffic. After the material has cured, wooden plugs are extracted and the drill holes are patched full depth with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete patching material. A post‑stabilization profile survey is submitted for acceptance, confirming that voids have been filled, no new surface depressions exist, and the slab can carry load without excessive deflection; a deflection test showing an improvement of at least 0.02 in. may be required. Traffic may not be opened until a minimum of thirty minutes has elapsed for asphalt or polyurethane work, while cement‑based repairs require waiting until the twelve‑hour cure is achieved. The completed work is finally accepted when all material properties, installation tolerances and post‑work measurements meet the specified criteria. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 600–612)

A satisfactory slab‑stabilization treatment is one that fully restores subgrade support beneath the concrete and eliminates every observable sign of lost slab support, including transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines at joints or cracks, small depressions or blow‑holes in shoulders, corner breaks, and any measurable settlement.[1] The guides further require that the equipment used to place grout be demonstrably reliable, with the injection pump calibrated within the preceding twelve months and a certification on file, and all monitoring devices capable of detecting uplift at a resolution of one‑thousandth of an inch.[2] When hot‑asphalt grout is specified, it must be heated to a temperature between 380 °F and 450 °F before pumping and delivered at a pressure ranging from 25 to 45 psi.[2] If cementitious grout is employed, the mix design must receive approval, the water content adjusted so that the initial efflux time falls between ten and fifteen seconds for the first injection and sixteen to twenty‑six seconds for subsequent injections, the expansion limited to no more than ten percent, the initial set time constrained to one through six hours, bleeding restricted to 2.5 % of the volume, and a verified seven‑day compressive strength of at least 700 psi based on five cylinders.[2] All drill holes must be vertical, round, no larger than five‑eighths inch in diameter, and properly plugged before grout placement to ensure containment of the material.[2] During injection the pavement surface may not rise more than five hundredths of an inch; any upward movement beyond this limit mandates immediate cessation of pumping.[2] After grouting each treated joint or crack is load‑tested and must exhibit a slab‑corner deflection less than 0.02 inch together with a joint efficiency of at least sixty‑five percent, and if the deflection remains at or above 0.02 inch after a twenty‑four‑hour retest the hole must be re‑grouted, with acceptance granted only after two successful groutings without excess movement.[2] The final roadway profile is required to be raised within the prescribed grade tolerance—plus or minus 0.02 foot for continuously reinforced concrete slabs and plus or minus 0.03 foot for jointed or bridge‑end slabs—and any overjacking exceeding 0.10 foot obliges removal and replacement of the slab.[2] Surveyed profile drawings that document existing, proposed, and achieved grades must be submitted before acceptance can be granted.[2] Traffic may not be permitted onto the repaired area until at least thirty minutes have elapsed after grouting unless a field quality‑control plan or manufacturer recommendation authorizes an earlier opening.[2] The overarching objective is to fill the voids sufficiently so that slab deflections are reduced and subsequent pumping, faulting, or cracking become unlikely.[1]

Performance and service life

Slab stabilization is employed wherever the concrete slab lacks uniform bearing, such as around patches or in areas where voids have formed beneath the slab due to pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or settlement. By drilling holes and injecting a suitable fill material—commonly cement grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane—the voids are eliminated and full support is re‑established. This restoration of bearing reduces slab deflection, curtails transverse joint faulting, corner breaks, and further cracking, thereby improving the pavement’s structural integrity and ride quality. When the stabilization is incorporated into a thin‑asphalt preservation overlay—typically a 1.0‑inch (6.3 mm) layer—the surface condition is enhanced through better skid resistance and smoother riding characteristics. Although the treatment does not increase the original design capacity or remove existing surface depressions, it nonetheless upgrades functional performance, prolongs durability, and extends the expected service life of the repaired section without requiring a full structural redesign. Because PennDOT classifies slab stabilization among its cost‑effective preservation measures, it is recognized as a practical means to maintain pavement condition and longevity. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Slab stabilization treatments can be undermined by a suite of recurring distresses that indicate the underlying voids have not been fully eliminated, including transverse joint faulting, corner breaks, slab settlement, blow‑hole depressions and the buildup of fines near joints or cracks. [1] These symptoms arise from mechanisms such as pumping action, subgrade consolidation and water infiltration that generate voids beneath the concrete, and they are intensified when joint and crack sealing or subsurface drainage are insufficient. [1] In addition to these surface‑related failures, cracking patterns that appear during the injection process provide early warning of poor execution, with radial cracks radiating from grout holes and transverse cracks forming between adjacent holes. [2] Excessive upward movement of the slab is another frequent dimensional failure; lifts greater than 0.05 in are prohibited and any raise exceeding 0.10 ft requires abandonment of the work and replacement of the slab. [2] Conversely, insufficient lift is identified when the final profile falls outside the allowable tolerances of ±0.02 ft for continuously reinforced slabs or ±0.03 ft for jointed slabs, prompting additional grout injection to achieve the target grade. [2] Premature leakage of asphalt from joints or cracks before the undersealing material has set can also occur, necessitating a halt in pumping until the asphalt congeals. [2] The development of these failures is governed by several controllable variables, notably temperature constraints that forbid work below 40 °F, frozen subgrade conditions, and testing outside the 40‑70 °F window because low temperatures impair grout flow and bonding. [2] Proper material temperatures are required— asphalt must be heated to between 380 °F and 450 °F before pumping, while grout injection pressures must remain within prescribed limits (continuous ≤200 psi, short‑duration up to 300 psi, and up to 600 psi only when the slab is bonded to the subgrade); exceeding these pressures can force material into unintended cracks or damage the slab and underlying layers. [2] Adequate hole preparation, including thorough cleaning with compressed air and adherence to a mixing‑time limit that prevents grout from being held more than one hour after mixing, together with real‑time monitoring of vertical movement during pumping, are essential to avoid overjacking or under‑lifting that would otherwise produce the cracking and lift‑tolerance violations described. [2] Finally, because slab stabilization does not increase the pavement’s structural capacity or correct existing surface depressions, any remaining moisture or insufficient support after treatment can cause deflection‑induced cracking to reappear once the grout has cured. [1]

Monitoring and follow-up

After slab stabilization is finished, the contractor must perform a post‑grouting inspection about twenty‑four hours later and before final acceptance. Each treated joint or crack is retested with the same deflection test procedure (ASTM D4695) used originally, and the measured lift is compared to the allowable limit. If any slab shows a deflection of 0.02 inch or more it must be re‑grouted and retested; after two properly performed groutings the Representative may either accept continued movement or require full‑depth replacement with a concrete patch. In parallel, the contractor submits a roadway profile report that includes surveyed drawings of the existing grade, the proposed grade, and the grade actually achieved after jacking. The Representative checks that the final elevation is within .02 foot for continuously reinforced concrete slabs or .03 foot for jointed slabs, and may order additional injection if those tolerances are exceeded. Once the acceptance criteria are satisfied, a final visual check is performed: wooden plugs are removed and all drill holes are filled with rapid‑set non‑shrink concrete patching material, the patches being struck flush with the surrounding pavement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–612)

After slab stabilization, the presence of transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines at joints or cracks on either traffic lane or shoulder, small blow‑hole depressions in the shoulder adjacent to those joints or cracks, corner breaks, overall settlement of the slab, or ongoing slab movement such as rocking, pumping, or further faulting indicates that underlying voids have not been fully mitigated and additional work is required [1]. Likewise, a joint or crack that continues to show a loaded corner deflection of at least 0.02 in together with a joint efficiency falling below roughly 65 % signals insufficient treatment and calls for further grouting or patching [2]. The emergence of radial cracks radiating from grout injection holes, as well as transverse cracks forming between adjacent holes, is interpreted as poor quality that may necessitate corrective action up to full‑depth slab replacement [2]. During the jacking phase any upward movement of the slab exceeding 0.05 in must be stopped and reassessed because such excess lift indicates a failure of the stabilization effort [2]. Profile measurements that exceed the allowable grade tolerances—±0.02 ft from the string line for continuously reinforced concrete slabs or ±0.03 ft for jointed slabs—also reveal over‑jacking; when the lift surpasses 0.10 ft the pavement must be removed and replaced, while smaller excesses require grinding [2]. Persistent depressions or excessive rises identified in the grade report therefore serve as additional evidence that further maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation is needed before any overlay can be placed [1][2].
