Ride Quality

Part of: Pavement Condition Assessment is-subtopic-of
Ride Quality is a topic under Pavement Condition Assessment.
ride-quality
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-28

Ride Quality is a performance metric that quantifies the smoothness of a pavement surface by measuring its longitudinal roughness, most commonly expressed as the International Roughness Index (IRI) and the derived Mean Roughness Index (MRI) [1][2]. It is used to decide whether a pavement segment satisfies contract‑specified smoothness limits—no more than 70 inches per mile where posted speeds exceed 45 mph and no more than 90 inches per mile at 45 mph or less—and to trigger acceptance, corrective grinding, or incentive payments based on those values [2]. Because excessive roughness degrades occupant comfort and skid resistance, the measured ride quality directs the choice of preservation or rehabilitation treatments (e.g., diamond grinding, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses) and influences material decisions such as avoiding roller‑compacted concrete where IRI incentives apply [1].

Purpose and decision context

Ride Quality measurements are employed to decide whether a pavement segment meets the smoothness criteria required for contract acceptance and any associated payment incentives. The decision hinges on comparing International Roughness Index (IRI) or derived Mean Roughness Index (MRI) values against prescribed limits; for posted speeds above 45 mph the IRI must not exceed 70 in/mile, while at 45 mph or less it may not exceed 90 in/mile, and the MRI must fall within the maximum listed in Table A, with additional checks on individual bumps and verification tolerances [2]. In the preliminary engineering stage the same smoothness data are used to determine whether an existing concrete pavement can be rehabilitated with conventional Concrete Pavement Restoration; if ride quality is judged inadequate, restoration is deemed infeasible [1]. During rehabilitation the measured ride quality directs the selection of surface treatments: pavements lacking Vanport Limestone aggregate are steered toward diamond grinding, whereas those containing aggregates prone to rapid polishing are directed to an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or high‑friction surface treatment [1]. The decision also influences material choice at design when incentive payments depend on IRI performance; roller‑compacted concrete is excluded because its placement variability can prevent meeting the required smoothness thresholds [1]. After construction or resurfacing, ride‑quality data are used to verify that the finished lot satisfies the acceptance standards, to trigger corrective actions such as additional grinding or replacement if it does not, and ultimately to authorize payment of the contract amount and any ride‑quality incentive [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–99), Ch. 11 (p. 197); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 232–235), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 385–463)

Ride Quality addresses the problem of excessive pavement roughness that compromises occupant comfort and safety, recognizing that rough or uneven concrete surfaces—particularly those containing aggregates such as Vanport Limestone that polish rapidly—reduce ride comfort and can diminish skid resistance [1][2]. The need is to provide a smooth, low‑roughness surface for both concrete and asphalt pavements, eliminating uncomfortable or unsafe vehicle motion while meeting contract compliance requirements [1][2]. The performance goal is to achieve an acceptable International Roughness Index (IRI) level, with specific thresholds of no more than 70 inches per mile on roads where the posted speed limit exceeds 45 mph and no more than 90 inches per mile where speeds are 45 mph or less [2]. In addition, the Mean Roughness Index (MRI) must remain within the maximum values set out in Table A, not exceeding the acceptance value by more than 6.1 inches per mile on at least ninety percent of each 528‑foot lot [2]. Ride Quality also requires removal of localized surface defects such as bumps larger than three‑sixteenths of an inch (or one‑quarter inch for rigid pavements) and ridges or valleys greater than one‑eighth of an inch, typically through grinding or replacement [2][1]. To meet these goals, appropriate preservation or rehabilitation treatments—including diamond grinding, micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, high‑friction surface treatments, or suitable overlays—are applied early in the maintenance cycle before advanced distresses develop, restoring profile, cross‑slope, and friction while preserving long‑term pavement life [1]. These treatments must satisfy minimum ride‑quality thresholds specified in Publication 408 (Section 404.4 or 507.4) and are prohibited on roller‑compacted concrete where IRI will be used for performance incentives because such pavements typically have a rougher surface [1]. Accurate assessment depends on timely collection of Pavement History data and IRI testing within three months after reopening to traffic, enabling reliable asset‑management decisions and incentive payments based on measured smoothness; raw profile data are captured and submitted promptly after construction or rehabilitation for review [1][2]. Meeting the specified IRI and MRI criteria triggers incentive payments, while non‑conforming sections receive reduced compensation until corrective action restores required smoothness [2]. The guides also note that using flexible base replacements on rigid pavements can compromise long‑term rideability, so short‑ and long‑term impacts on ride quality must be considered before such actions [1]. Overall, Ride Quality seeks to eliminate uncomfortable driving experiences caused by surface roughness, structural deficiencies, or inadequate measurement, thereby ensuring a uniform, comfortable riding experience throughout the pavement network [1][2].

Scope and applicability

Ride Quality is employed as a performance criterion across a spectrum of pavement projects that either create a new riding surface or preserve an existing one. [1][2] In the preliminary‑engineering phase of concrete‑pavement rehabilitation, including CPR studies, ride‑quality considerations are evaluated together with subgrade/subbase condition and friction adequacy to decide whether repair is feasible. [1] During the design stage, specifications may require diamond grinding to raise ride quality on rigid pavements, except where the aggregate is Vanport Limestone, in which case an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or a high‑friction surface treatment is prescribed. [1] The metric governs all construction, reconstruction and resurfacing efforts; International Roughness Index (IRI) data are collected on the newly placed pavement within three months of entering Pavement History information and subsequently monitored annually for NHS routes or biennially for non‑NHS routes. [1] For preservation‑type treatments—micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, and diamond grinding applied to both flexible and rigid pavements—the objective is to improve ride quality early in the maintenance cycle before advanced distresses such as rutting develop; these thin‑layer interventions must meet a minimum ride‑quality level defined in Publication 408 and are exempt from a full pavement design. [1] Ride Quality assessment is also required for any project that delivers a finished surface, whether rigid concrete or flexible asphalt, when the agency needs to verify smoothness at acceptance or post‑construction, particularly where a performance incentive is tied to the result. [2] For concrete work this includes new ground slabs and rehabilitation operations such as diamond grinding or grooving; measurements are taken before any grinding and again after the operation to confirm that IRI does not exceed 70 in/mi on facilities with posted speeds above 45 mph and does not exceed 90 in/mi where speeds are 45 mph or less. [2] The testing is performed on pavement lots at least 528 ft long by 12 ft wide, excluding bridge decks, ramps shorter than 1,500 ft, tapered sections, shoulders, medians and approach slabs that are under separate contracts. [2] For asphalt projects ride‑quality evaluation is required when the work results in a full‑depth overlay—such as an overlaid bridge approach or deck—that involves at least two construction operations (for example profile milling followed by placement of base, binder or wearing courses); the assessment occurs after placement and calibration in the presence of an inspector, and roads with posted speeds of 40 mph or lower are excluded. [2] In federally funded contracts the department must conduct smoothness verification on a minimum of ten percent of lane miles using an inertial lightweight profiler; a lot is deemed defective if its Modified Ride Index exceeds the maximum value in Table A, if any individual bump measured with a 12‑foot straightedge exceeds 3/16 in (or 1/4 in for certain rigid‑pavement specifications), or if ridges or valleys exceed 1/8 in. Defective lots must be corrected by grinding before any pavement‑depth testing and then re‑tested for compliance. [2] Ride Quality also serves as the basis for incentive payments: non‑defective concrete or asphalt lots receive a payment calculated from Table A and the measured MRI, while lots that required corrective action are paid after the corrected measurement; partial lots earn a proportional share of the full‑lot incentive. However, when the contract scope consists solely of diamond‑grinding a concrete rehabilitation, no ride‑quality incentive is provided and the metric should not be applied for incentive purposes. [2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 11 (p. 197), Ch. 12 (pp. 203–205); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 232–235), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 385–463)

Ride‑quality improvement or assessment methods become inappropriate when the pavement material or construction type compromises the intended performance, and a different approach must be selected [1][2]. For concrete pavements that incorporate aggregates such as Vanport Limestone, diamond grinding—normally prescribed for ride‑quality enhancement—is unsuitable because the process removes the mortar layer and rapidly reduces skid resistance; in those cases an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or a high‑friction surface treatment should be used instead [1]. Likewise, roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements generate inherently rougher textures that produce International Roughness Index values higher than those of conventional Portland cement concrete or asphalt, making compliance with IRI‑based specifications or payment incentives unrealistic, so projects requiring IRI verification should specify a conventional concrete or asphalt pavement rather than RCC [1]. The use of the International Roughness Index as the ride‑quality metric is also limited to standard concrete lane sections that satisfy full‑depth overlay definitions and meet specific lot‑size and speed criteria; any segment that fails these conditions must be excluded from the ride‑quality lot and evaluated with an alternative profiling or measurement approach [2]. Exclusions include bridge decks that have not been overlaid, ramps shorter than 1 500 ft, tapered pavements narrower than 12 ft, shoulders, medians, any pavement within five feet of appurtenances such as manholes, water boxes or railroad tracks, partial lots under 100 ft in length, and roadways whose posted speed limit is 40 mph or less [2]. Localized depressions caused by subsidence or edge slump are likewise omitted unless a waiver is granted, and these areas are measured separately from the ride‑quality profile [2]. Diamond‑grinding projects and NGCS texturing operations on concrete rehabilitation contracts do not receive ride‑quality incentives; instead they are compensated based on quantity of material processed or surface area treated, indicating that a different performance metric is preferable for those work types [1][2]. Consequently, whenever the pavement type, aggregate susceptibility, structural configuration, lot dimensions, speed limit, proximity to appurtenances, or specific rehabilitation method falls within any of these excluded categories, a separate evaluation—such as conventional profiling, square‑yard quantity measurement, or surface‑area payment—replaces the standard IRI‑based ride‑quality assessment [2].

Information needs

Ride Quality assessment depends on accurate condition and site information, beginning with a complete Pavement History that records the dates and types of construction, reconstruction or resurfacing for each pavement segment, and this history must be entered promptly—within four weeks for agency‑initiated projects and within six weeks for contractor‑executed work—to allow IRI testing to be scheduled no later than three months after entry [1]. The condition data required are high‑resolution surface profiles obtained with the PTM No. 428 profiler while an inspector is present, accompanied by daily calibration results supplied before each run, and both the raw unfiltered profile files and a summary printout of International Roughness Index (IRI) and Mean Roughness Index (MRI) values must be turned over to the responsible representative within twenty‑four hours [2]. Individual surface irregularities are identified with a twelve‑foot straightedge; any bump exceeding three‑sixteenths of an inch (the specification also permits one‑quarter inch as the trigger) or any ridge or valley greater than one‑eighth inch is flagged for corrective grinding, and every irregularity over one‑quarter inch must be ground [2]. The overall roughness of each defined lot is expressed by the MRI, which is compared with a maximum acceptable value tabulated in Table A; verification MRI may not exceed the acceptance value by more than 6.1 inches per mile on at least ninety percent of the 528‑foot lots and the average difference must meet the same tolerance while lot‑length variance stays below 0.2 percent [2]. Site information defines a lot as a continuous 528‑ft segment of a single lane that is at least twelve feet wide, with the start and end of each lot designated after excluding bridge decks, ramps shorter than 1,500 ft, tapered sections narrower than twelve feet, shoulders, medians, partial lots under one hundred feet, areas within five feet of appurtenances, and any other surfaces specified as non‑eligible; contractors must also record which construction operations are present, with a minimum of two milling or asphalt layers noted for each lot [2]. Traffic data are limited to the posted speed limit, which classifies a lot as Type 1 when the limit exceeds 45 mph and as Type 2 when it is 45 mph or lower; this classification sets the allowable IRI ceiling at seventy inches per mile for Type 1 facilities and ninety inches per mile for Type 2 facilities and also drives the incentive calculation [2]. Cost information is embedded in a single unit price used to calculate incentive payments when a lot meets acceptance criteria, with the MRI and Table A values determining the payment; if corrective grinding has been performed a partial‑lot payment is computed as a percentage of a full lot, and if a contractor wishes to retain a defective lot after repair written approval from the district executive triggers a four‑thousand‑dollar rebate, while no separate fee is paid for performing the ride‑quality evaluation itself [2]. Environmental inputs are not prescribed for the ride‑quality assessment, indicating that no specific environmental data are required [2]. Stakeholder involvement includes provision of daily calibration data, submission of raw profile files and IRI/MRI summaries within the stipulated timeframe, presence of an inspector during measurement, coordination with the Department’s Roadway Inventory and Testing Unit at least fourteen days in advance and a virtual meeting to verify testing procedures, documentation of cross‑slope tests, depressions and slope misalignments, and final approval by the district executive for any deviation such as leaving a defective lot in place; the representative uses the MRI data to determine incentive payments and maintains all related documentation [2].

Information on ride quality must be entered promptly after a project reopens to traffic, with a deadline of four weeks for Department‑run work and six weeks for contractor‑performed work [1]. The International Roughness Index measurements that support the entry must then be obtained within three months of that data entry to ensure the values reflect the current pavement condition [1]. Accuracy of the ride‑quality data is achieved by using the approved pavement surface‑profile equipment at every test location and by performing a daily calibration of the instrument, with the calibration results submitted before any measurements are taken [2]. The calibrated status report, together with the raw unfiltered profile files and a summary printout of both IRI and Mean Roughness Index values, must be included in the submission to guarantee completeness for incentive calculations [2]. Current information requires that the raw data package and the summarized roughness indices be delivered to the Representative within twenty‑four hours of collection for each lot [2]. Completeness also depends on having a full Pavement History record that documents every resurfacing, reconstruction, or other major treatment so that the IRI values can be interpreted correctly; incomplete or outdated history would undermine reliability [1]. The reported IRI values must satisfy the maximum limits of 70 in/mile where posted speeds exceed 45 mph and 90 in/mile where posted speeds are 45 mph or less [2].

Alternatives

When ride quality is the primary performance metric, agencies must compare a suite of preservation and rehabilitation treatments matched to pavement type and condition. For rigid pavements the baseline smoothness‑restoring treatment is diamond grinding, while ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses or high‑friction surface treatments are considered when the aggregate polish rapidly. In addition, short‑length corrections may employ carbide grinding limited to sections fifteen feet long or shorter. Full‑width diamond grinding of the lane is another feasible alternative that can be evaluated before and after treatment using the same profilometer. Where surface texture loss is severe, removal of the entire surface course by milling followed by repaving to at least the minimum specified layer depth—or a minimum ten‑foot concrete segment between joints—is prescribed as a corrective option. Micro‑surfacing and ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses are also listed as viable thin‑layer treatments that can improve skid resistance, ride quality, and rut filling. A 6.3 mm (≈¼‑inch) Superpave asphalt overlay may be compared with the aforementioned thin‑layer options, provided it meets the minimum 1½‑inch thickness requirement on sound structures with adequate drainage. For larger rehabilitation projects the feasible choices narrow to concrete pavement restoration, a new concrete overlay, or an asphalt overlay, each assessed against existing distress, drainage, rehabilitatability and life‑cycle cost. Rapid‑setting accelerated concrete units and precast concrete paving systems constitute repair alternatives that differ in opening time, shrinkage behavior, and ability to achieve target IRI after any post‑install grinding. Profile correction techniques such as heater planing or milling can be contrasted with grinding strategies when transverse or longitudinal profile adjustments are needed. When grooves are required the two feasible grinding‑and‑grooving strategies—single‑pass operation that produces a flush surface with longitudinal grooves, and a two‑pass operation that first grinds the slab then adds the grooves—should be compared. All ride‑quality evaluations must use the same pavement surface profile measuring equipment before any treatment and after repair, and the resulting IRI values are checked against the applicable ceilings of 70 in/mi for posted speeds above 45 mph and 90 in/mi for speeds at or below 45 mph. Roller‑compacted concrete is excluded from comparisons where IRI will be measured because its texture is typically rougher than conventional Portland‑cement concrete and hot‑mix asphalt. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–99), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 232–235), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 385–396)

Both the single‑pass and two‑pass NGCS options rely on a self‑propelled diamond‑grinding machine weighing at least 40,000 pounds and fitted with a minimum four‑foot grinding head; the single‑pass option adds a stacked set of grooving blades that are one‑quarter to three‑eighths inch taller than the grinding blades, while the two‑pass option uses an initial pass with only flat grinding blades followed by a second pass that installs the longitudinal grooves. Because the two‑pass method requires an additional grinding run, it generally consumes more crew time and extends lane closures compared with the single‑pass alternative, but both procedures demand careful control of joint differentials (no more than 1/16‑inch) and strict slurry removal to avoid traffic contamination, thereby limiting disruption risk. The specifications prohibit equipment that spalls joints or fractures aggregates, which reduces the risk of surface damage, and any area that fails the smoothness or IRI criteria must be reground, establishing a maintenance loop for future ride‑quality compliance. [2]

Decision criteria

The decision for ride‑quality should be governed primarily by performance, meaning the pavement’s ability to meet prescribed smoothness criteria.[1][2] Performance is quantified through measured roughness using the International Roughness Index (IRI) and the derived Mean Roughness Index (MRI), with explicit thresholds that depend on posted speed limits.[2] For roadways where the posted speed exceeds 45 mph, the maximum allowable IRI is 70 in/mi, while for speeds of 45 mph or less the limit is 90 in/mi.[2] Acceptance also requires that the MRI not exceed the specified acceptance value by more than 6.1 in/mi for at least ninety percent of the tested 528‑ft sections and that the overall average MRI stay within the same tolerance.[2] Payment incentives are tied directly to compliance with these performance thresholds, with lots deemed defective when their MRI exceeds the maximum allowable value and incentive payments withheld unless all IRI lots meet the speed‑related limits.[2] Because ride‑quality performance dominates, other considerations such as cost, schedule, durability, sustainability, accessibility or broader public impact are subordinate in the selection of materials or construction methods.[1][2] Accordingly, alternatives that could degrade smoothness—such as using flexible‑base replacements beneath concrete slabs or employing roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) in sections subject to IRI‑based incentive payments—are prohibited unless analysis shows that any short‑term or long‑term loss of ride quality is acceptable.[1] The evaluation process requires submission of raw unfiltered IRI data and a summary printout of MRI values within 24 hours of measurement, reinforcing the primacy of performance data in decision making.[2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 232–233), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 385–396)

Competing ride‑quality criteria are first ordered by applying the project‑specific Ride Specification and confirming that the pavement attains the minimum ride quality required by Publication 408. [1] The foremost quantitative gate is compliance with International Roughness Index limits that are tied to posted speed, where facilities serving speeds greater than 45 mph must not exceed 70 inches per mile and those at 45 mph or less may not exceed 90 inches per mile. [2] Only after the IRI threshold is satisfied do the guides move to geometric standards, requiring correction of any transverse cross‑slope deviation greater than a quarter inch over a twelve‑foot segment and verification of longitudinal roughness both before and after diamond grinding. [1][2] Additional hard thresholds must also be met independently: the maximum allowable Mean Roughness Index as defined in Table A, any individual bump larger than three‑sixteenths (or one‑quarter) of an inch measured with a 12‑ft straightedge, and adjacent ridges or valleys exceeding one‑eighth of an inch. [2] Failure to meet any of these geometric or roughness limits triggers repair and retesting before the pavement can be considered acceptable. [2] For final incentive determination, the Mean Roughness Index carries the decisive weight; payment is based on the MRI provided that the verification MRI does not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches per mile for at least ninety percent of the tested lots. [2] Consequently, the weighting across sources follows a hierarchical structure: IRI and speed‑related limits act as prerequisite gates, all geometric tolerances must be satisfied equally thereafter, and the MRI alone drives payment decisions, while safety or ancillary items are addressed only after these ride‑quality criteria have been fulfilled. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 203–204); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 232–234), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 385–396)

Analysis methods and tools

Ride quality is assessed through direct roughness measurements combined with a suite of nondestructive tests such as deflection testing, faulting measurement, friction testing, ground‑penetrating radar, MIRA and MIT Scan systems [1]. The roughness data are captured using the Department’s inertial lightweight profiler calibrated in accordance with PTM No. 428, which records longitudinal surface profiles for each pavement lot [2]. Processed profile data yield both an International Roughness Index (IRI) and a Mean Roughness Index (MRI), the former being limited to 70 inches per mile where posted speeds exceed 45 mph and to 90 inches per mile where speeds are 45 mph or less, while the MRI is subject to verification tolerances of no more than 6.1 inches per mile excess on at least 90 percent of the 528‑ft lots and an overall average excess also limited to 6.1 inches per mile, with total length discrepancy kept under 0.2 percent [2]. Verification testing must be performed on a minimum of one day covering at least ten percent of the project’s lane miles, after which the MRI for each lot is entered into Table A and used to calculate incentive payments, including proportional adjustments for partial lots and post‑correction values when corrective work is required [2]. The collected roughness and nondestructive‑test data are incorporated into life‑cycle cost analysis to compare rehabilitation alternatives, although the guides do not prescribe condition ratings, decision trees, risk assessment, or benefit‑cost analysis specifically for ride‑quality evaluation [1].

Ride‑quality assessments rely on several core assumptions that, if not met, introduce uncertainty into International Roughness Index (IRI) and Mean Roughness Index values. The methodology assumes that all surface profiling is performed with the same inertial lightweight profiler, which must be certified, calibrated each day, and have its calibration record submitted before use. For Roller Compacted Concrete pavements it is further assumed that the paver and roller equipment dominate the final texture, making equipment performance a direct source of variability, and because RCC inherently yields a rougher finish than conventional concrete or asphalt, sections where IRI drives payment incentives are excluded. The process also presumes that pavement‑history data are entered promptly—within four weeks for department‑managed projects and six weeks for contractor‑managed projects—so that IRI measurements reflect the current condition of the surface. Measurements are taken only where at least two specified construction activities have been performed and an inspector is present, ensuring that the profile reflects post‑construction conditions. It is assumed that any slurry or grinding residue is completely removed and that geometric deviations greater than a quarter‑inch over 12 ft are corrected before profiling. The analysis further assumes that IRI limits of 70 in/mi for posted speeds above 45 mph and 90 in/mi for speeds at or below 45 mph are appropriate, and that results are calculated on full or partial 528‑ft lots while excluding bridge decks, short ramps, shoulders, appurtenances, and roadways with speed limits of 40 mph or lower. Uncertainty arises because even with daily calibration equipment condition or sensor drift can cause variation in measured IRI values, and for RCC the variability in paver/roller performance adds further uncertainty to ride‑quality results. Delays beyond the prescribed data‑entry windows, testing before a pavement has reopened to traffic, or reliance on biennial surveys for non‑National Highway System routes generate uncertainty about whether recorded IRI truly reflects current smoothness. Differences in how operators use the profiler or follow procedures can affect measured values, contributing to measurement error. Any remaining grinding residue or surface deviation exceeding the tolerance introduces additional error into the IRI calculation. The requirement that verification MRI not exceed acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 in/mi for at least ninety percent of the lots, and that average differences stay within this limit, creates a tolerance band; exceeding it triggers review of testing methods, equipment, and personnel, reflecting inherent uncertainty in meeting thresholds, while the exclusion criteria and limited lot length can affect how representative the sampled data are of overall pavement condition. [1][2]

Stakeholders and constraints

Input for ride‑quality assessment originates from two primary sources: the Districts and the contractor’s measurement crew working with an Inspector. The Districts are required to enter Pavement History data for every maintenance or construction project promptly, providing the foundational record that triggers later testing [1][2]. Simultaneously, on each contract, the contractor’s measurement crew records longitudinal roughness, cross‑slope, and other profile data in the presence of the Inspector, submits daily calibration results, and forwards raw IRI/MRI files and summary printouts to the project Representative within twenty‑four hours [2]. The Agency’s Asset Management Division, specifically the Pavement Testing and Asset Management Section, uses the District‑provided history entries to schedule system‑wide International Roughness Index (IRI) testing, conduct quality assurance, store and retrieve data, and generate reports that inform when and how ride quality is measured and documented [1]. The project Representative receives the contractor’s raw IRI/MRI data and, using that input, designates lot boundaries from the beginning to the ending ride‑quality limits for each lane or ramp, then compares each lot’s Mean Roughness Index (MRI) against the acceptance values set out in Table A of the relevant sections to decide whether corrective action is required and to calculate any incentive or rebate payment [2]. For federally funded projects, the Department of Transportation acts as the ultimate decision‑maker on overall acceptance; it conducts smoothness verification testing with an inertial lightweight profiler for at least one day covering a minimum of ten percent of the project’s lane miles, coordinating the effort with the Representative at least fourteen days in advance and involving the Roadway Inventory and Testing Unit to verify procedures [2]. The Department reviews both the contractor‑submitted data and its own verification results; if tolerances are not met it may reject the results or require retesting, thereby exercising final authority over acceptance of ride quality for those projects [2]. When a lot is found defective but corrective work has been performed, the District Executive must provide written approval before the contractor can leave the repaired lot in place and receive any downward rebate adjustment, such as a $4,000 reduction, completing the approval chain [2]. Thus input comes from Districts and contractor crews; analysis and scheduling are performed by the Asset Management Division’s Pavement Testing Section; primary ride‑quality decisions and incentive approvals rest with the project Representative; federal‑level acceptance is validated by the Department of Transportation through verification testing; and final written consent for retaining corrected defective lots rests with the District Executive [1][2].

The selection of any ride‑quality treatment is tightly bounded by a network of PennDOT specifications, design manuals and funding policies that prescribe both the materials and the procedural steps required for compliance. For concrete pavements the default rehabilitation method is diamond grinding as stipulated in Publication 408 Section 514, but an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or a High Friction Surface Treatment may be used when Vanport Limestone aggregate is employed or when the aggregate tends to polish rapidly; full‑depth slab patching must follow Publication 72M and Roadway Construction Standards RC‑26M, with precast units placed at least one‑quarter inch above the surrounding surface so that a final diamond grind can restore cross‑slope, profile and IRI [1]. Roller‑compacted concrete is expressly prohibited on projects where ride quality will be measured for payment incentives, and any exposed RCC pavement on routes with posted speeds of 35 mph or higher must receive diamond grinding to provide the required texture [1]. Overlay choices are limited by Chapter 5 and Publication 408 Section 413.3(b); on Interstate highways a Superpave wearing‑course overlay cannot be less than 1½ inches thick without prior PDAU approval, thin asphalt overlays must respect the depth limits in Table 9.5 and applicable weather restrictions, and flexible‑pavement preservation projects may not use overlays thicker than 2 inches while a standard 6.3‑mm Superpave wearing course must employ PG 64E‑22 binder [1]. Early‑cycle applications that aim to improve skid resistance and ride quality are required to use micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses [1]. Procedurally, districts must enter construction data within four weeks of a department‑initiated reopening (six weeks for contractor‑initiated work) and the Pavement Testing and Asset Management staff must conduct IRI testing on the newly opened surface within three months of that entry; every preservation project must apply the appropriate Ride Specification and achieve at least the minimum ride‑quality thresholds set out in Publication 408 Sections 404.4 or 507.4, while patching is limited to ten percent of the pavement area unless a cost‑effectiveness analysis justifies a larger extent and all repairs must be verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing before proceeding [1]. Design exceptions must follow the documentation and approval procedures outlined in Publication 10C and Design Manual Part 1C, with funding allocated only to actions deemed cost‑effective by PennDOT’s Pavement Management System [1]. Contractual provisions further require that IRI be measured both before any diamond grinding and after work using the same profilometer, with a maximum IRI of 70 in/mi on facilities whose posted speed exceeds 45 mph and 90 in/mi where the speed limit is 45 mph or less; these limits must be satisfied before any payment is issued [2]. Surface irregularities are limited to three‑sixteenths of an inch on a twelve‑foot straightedge, ridges or valleys may not exceed one‑eighth inch, and any bump greater than a quarter inch is deemed defective and must be ground out; the Mean Roughness Index for each lot must remain within the maximum values listed in Table A of Section 404.4 (or Section 507.4), with exceeding values triggering corrective grinding, milling or replacement [2]. For federally funded work a verification test using an inertial lightweight profiler must cover at least ten percent of lane miles, and the verification MRI may not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 in/mi for at least ninety percent of the tested 528‑foot lots, with overall averages also staying within that tolerance; lot‑length differences are limited to two‑tenths of a percent and certain features—bridge decks, ramps shorter than 1,500 ft, tapered sections narrower than twelve feet, shoulders, medians, zones near appurtenances, short partial lots under one hundred feet, and any roadway with a posted speed of forty miles per hour or lower—are expressly excluded from ride‑quality calculations [2]. Incentive payment is structured as a one‑dollar‑per‑unit bonus based on the lot’s MRI; because this payment is classified as a bonus, Section 110.02(d) does not apply and costs for evaluating ride quality are not reimbursed separately, although a defective lot may be left in place with written approval from the District Executive and a $4,000 rebate to the contractor [2]. Each measured lot must contain at least two asphalt construction layers, surface profiles must be recorded in accordance with PTM No. 428 under inspector supervision, daily calibration data must be submitted before testing, and raw IRI and MRI results are required within twenty‑four hours for payment determination [2]. For rigid pavements Section 514.4 treats longitudinal roughness and ride quality as incidental to the unit‑price item and expressly forbids any incentive for diamond‑grinding rehabilitation; the measurement apparatus must be included in the unit price and remains the contractor’s property after closeout, while funding policy limits incentives to payment for the textured surface installed and provides no incentive for NGCS texturing on concrete rehabilitation projects [2]. Section 507.4 defines performance criteria, payment triggers and requires comparison of a lot’s MRI against Table A; any irregularity exceeding a quarter inch measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge must be ground out, longitudinal joints must comply with Section 501.3(o)1, and the department may order grinding of excluded areas provided they meet the straight‑edge tolerances specified in Section 507.3(c) [2].

Timing and implementation

The decision regarding ride quality is required at multiple points in a pavement project, beginning during the preliminary engineering phase when the existing surface is evaluated for rehabilitation and when any substitution of a concrete slab with a flexible base is contemplated. At that stage the engineer must consider both short‑term and long‑term impacts on ride quality before committing to the material change. After a new surface has been placed, the decision is refined once an initial International Roughness Index (IRI) measurement has been collected and analyzed; project data are to be entered within four to six weeks of reopening to traffic and the IRI test must be performed no later than three months after that entry. The guides also require a definitive ride‑quality decision at the acceptance stage, when the measured IRI or Mean Roughness Index (MRI) values are submitted to the Representative for review. For concrete rehabilitation or new grooved concrete surfacing, the measurement must be taken immediately after the work is finished—before any diamond grinding in the case of rehabilitation and after grinding and grooving for NGCS—and the raw unfiltered IRI/MRI files together with a summary printout are to be delivered within twenty‑four hours of taking the measurements. The Representative then compares the MRI (or IRI) against the maximum values prescribed in Table A (or Section 507.4) and checks for any individual bump exceeding the allowable straightedge tolerance; if the indices are within limits and no excessive bumps exist, the lot is deemed non‑defective and payment can be determined immediately. If the MRI exceeds the limit or an oversized bump is identified, the lot is classified as defective, corrective grinding must be performed before any pavement‑depth testing, a new measurement is taken, and the decision is finalized only after the re‑measurement confirms compliance with the posted speed and Table A criteria. Finally, the guides advise that ride‑quality treatments such as micro‑surfacing or an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course be applied early in the maintenance cycle—before advanced distresses develop—to improve ride quality while the pavement condition remains relatively good. Action to protect ride quality begins as soon as a pavement segment is slated for International Roughness Index (IRI) measurement; the guides prohibit the use of roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) in any location where IRI will be collected, and they require that an exposed RCC surface on roadways with posted speeds of 35 mph or higher be diamond‑ground before the segment is opened to traffic. When a concrete rehabilitation project includes diamond grinding, the guides treat the work as non‑eligible for ride‑quality incentives and therefore trigger corrective procedures rather than bonus payment. The primary quantitative triggers are IRI thresholds: any section where the posted speed exceeds 45 mph must not exceed 70 inches per mile, and sections with speeds of 45 mph or less must stay below 90 inches per mile; exceeding these limits obliges re‑grinding before payment can be issued. In addition, longitudinal roughness results that fall outside the tolerances of Section 507.3(d) also require corrective grinding. Mean Roughness Index (MRI) values constitute a further trigger; when the MRI for a lot surpasses the maximum value listed in Table A of Sections 404.4 or 507.4, corrective work must be performed, and if the MRI exceeds the acceptance limit by more than 6.1 inches per mile on at least ninety percent of the lots, the department will review testing procedures and may suspend the contractor’s profiler certification until recertification is obtained within ten business days. Physical irregularities activate action as well: any individual bump measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge that exceeds three‑sixteenths of an inch (or one‑quarter inch where a stricter limit applies) must be ground, and any ridge or valley between adjacent ride‑quality lots larger than one‑eighth of an inch also mandates grinding; longitudinal joints that fail the requirements of Section 501.3(o)(1) trigger the same response. Procedural deadlines accompany these thresholds: contractors must coordinate with the Representative at least fourteen days before smoothness verification testing, provide daily calibration results prior to any profiling, and submit raw unfiltered IRI/MRI data files together with a summary printout within twenty‑four hours of each measurement; a 300‑foot test section must be approved before full construction proceeds, and a ride‑quality lot becomes eligible for measurement only after at least two construction operations have been completed and profiling is performed under inspector supervision in accordance with PTM No. 428. When any pavement segment is resurfaced, reconstructed or otherwise altered, its Pavement History must be entered into the Roadway Management System within four weeks for Department‑managed projects or six weeks for contractor‑managed projects, after which Pavement Testing staff are required to complete an IRI measurement on the new surface within three months. Annual IRI collection is performed on all National Highway System routes and a two‑year cycle covers non‑NHS roads to keep ride‑quality data current for asset‑management decisions. Preservation projects that fail to meet the minimum ride‑quality level defined in Publication 408 (Sections 404.4 or 507.4) must have the appropriate Ride Specification applied, and early signs of distress such as emerging rutting, loss of smoothness, or other identifiable surface problems prompt timely preservation treatments like micro‑surfacing or an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course before those distresses advance to later stages in the maintenance cycle. The implementation of the ride‑quality option relies on coordinated resources from the agency’s Asset Management Division and from the contractor’s grinding crew. Agency personnel from the Pavement Testing and Asset Management Section are tasked with system‑wide International Roughness Index data collection, quality assurance, storage, retrieval and reporting, using dedicated IRI measurement equipment and, when needed, a Videologging contractor that accesses pavement‑history records maintained in the Roadway Management System. For force projects the new pavement history must be entered into the system within four weeks of reopening to traffic, while for contractor‑executed projects the entry deadline is six weeks; thereafter the testing unit runs a weekly search for these entries and must complete IRI testing on the newly opened surfaces within three months of data entry. Routine IRI collection is performed annually on the National Highway System and on a two‑year cycle for non‑NHS routes, providing both scheduled and post‑construction ride‑quality data. The contractor must first finish all required concrete rehabilitation actions—including patching, spall repair, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization and jacking—before any grinding can commence. Grinding equipment consists of a power‑driven self‑propelled diamond‑grinding machine that weighs at least 40 000 lb when equipped with a minimum four‑foot grinding head and the specified blade stack; for localized corrections of fifteen feet or less, walk‑behind carbide‑tipped grinders with locking depth control may be employed. Prior to grinding, baseline ride‑quality is determined using a PTM‑428 compliant profilometer together with calibration kits and data‑processing software capable of generating International Roughness Index and Mean Roughness Index values; the equipment is calibrated each day and the calibration report submitted before any profiling run. An inspector must be present during all measurements, and the raw unfiltered files plus a summary printout are turned over to the project Representative within twenty‑four hours of each survey. The grinding operation proceeds lane by lane while checking cross‑slope every one hundred feet and correcting any deviation greater than a quarter‑inch in twelve feet; after completing the full‑width grind, feather‑passing, grooving and slurry removal are performed. A three‑hundred‑foot test section is constructed and must be approved before the crew continues with full‑width grinding. The contractor coordinates with the Representative at least fourteen days in advance to schedule the Department’s inertial lightweight profiler and to hold a virtual meeting with the Roadway Inventory and Testing Unit for verification of smoothness testing procedures. For federally funded projects the Department conducts smoothness verification testing on a minimum of one day covering at least ten percent of the project’s lane miles, using its inertial lightweight profiler concurrently with the contractor’s acceptance testing. Measured IRI values must not exceed seventy inches per mile for facilities with posted speeds above forty‑five miles per hour or ninety inches per mile for speeds of forty‑five miles per hour or less; if these limits are exceeded, the Department reviews procedures and equipment and may require profiler recertification within ten business days. The overall schedule therefore follows a sequential path: concrete rehabilitation, baseline IRI measurement, construction and approval of the test section, full‑width grinding with cross‑slope checks, post‑grind profiling, Department verification testing, and final acceptance based on the specified IRI thresholds. Outcomes and follow-up

Success for Ride Quality is declared when the finished pavement satisfies the smoothness criteria set out in the applicable Ride Specification and attains at least the minimum ride‑quality value prescribed by Publication 408 (Section 404.4 or Section 507.4) [1]. For concrete pavements, those minimum values are expressed as speed‑dependent International Roughness Index limits: no more than 70 inches per mile where posted speeds exceed 45 mph and no more than 90 inches per mile where speeds are 45 mph or less, together with Mean Roughness Index thresholds defined in Table A of the specification [2]. In addition to the IRI and MRI caps, each lot must present a uniformly smooth surface free of isolated bumps larger than three‑sixteenths of an inch when measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge, and any irregularity exceeding one‑quarter inch must be ground away; edge ridges or valleys may not exceed one‑eighth of an inch [2]. The Department verifies these conditions by conducting longitudinal roughness surveys before any rehabilitation work and repeating the surveys after diamond grinding using an inertial lightweight profiler that follows PTM No. 428 and operates in parallel with the contractor’s acceptance profiling [1][2]. All measurements are taken in the presence of an inspector, preceded by daily calibration results submitted to the Representative, and the raw unfiltered profile data together with summary printouts of IRI and MRI values must be provided within twenty‑four hours of each test [2]. Verification of the post‑construction ride quality requires that the measured verification MRI not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches per mile for at least ninety percent of the 528‑foot lots, and that the lane‑average verification MRI stay within the same margin; the total length of all tested lots in a lane may differ by less than 0.2 percent [2]. To ensure the IRI reflects current conditions, project pavement history must be entered into the database within four weeks for Department‑managed projects and within six weeks for contractor‑performed projects after traffic reopens, after which an IRI test is required no later than three months to provide a current rideability value [1]. When both the specification‑based minimum values and the up‑to‑date IRI/MRI results satisfy these tolerances, the pavement lot is considered fully compliant and eligible for full incentive payment; partially compliant lots receive proportional incentives, while defective lots may be left in place only with written approval and a rebate adjustment [2].

Ride‑quality results are monitored through a coordinated program that combines periodic network‑wide roughness surveys with targeted surface‑profile measurements at key points in the construction sequence. For the national highway system, International Roughness Index (IRI) values are collected each year, while routes outside the NHS are surveyed on a two‑year cycle, and any newly resurfaced or reconstructed segment is tested within three months after its Pavement History record is entered. In addition, pavement surface profiles are measured immediately after concrete rehabilitation, again before and after any diamond grinding, and finally after the completion of the next generation concrete surfacing (NGCS). The same inertial lightweight profiler specified in PTM No. 428 is used for all measurements; it must be calibrated daily and operated under an inspector’s supervision to ensure data integrity. For federally funded projects the department conducts a concurrent smoothness verification test on at least one day covering no less than ten percent of lane miles, using its own profiler alongside the contractor’s acceptance testing. All raw, unfiltered profile files together with summary printouts of IRI and Mean Roughness Index (MRI) values are transmitted to the designated Representative within twenty‑four hours of collection, accompanied by the daily calibration record. District staff must enter Pavement History information promptly—within four weeks for department‑managed projects and six weeks for contractor‑performed work—so that the central Pavement Testing and Asset Management team can retrieve, quality‑assure, store, and report the data. The documented IRI values are linked to construction dates in the Roadway Management System, allowing analysts to evaluate initial smoothness, monitor trends or anomalies, and assess the effectiveness of recent work. Each 528‑foot lot’s results are recorded against contractual limits; IRI may not exceed seventy inches per mile where posted speeds exceed forty‑five miles per hour and ninety inches per mile where speeds are forty‑five mph or less, while individual bumps larger than three‑sixteenths of an inch must be ground before further testing. MRI values are compared to the acceptance MRI, with verification MRI required not to exceed the acceptance value by more than six point one inches per mile on at least ninety percent of lots and with overall variance kept below zero point two percent. These documented outcomes directly drive payment and incentive decisions, with lots meeting thresholds generating incentive payments calculated from MRI according to Table A, whereas non‑conforming lots may be rejected for payment, require corrective grinding, or receive a rebate such as a four‑thousand‑dollar reduction. When verification tolerances are not met, the department reviews testing procedures, equipment, and personnel, may reject the contractor’s results, apply its own verification values for payment, and suspend profiler certification until recertified. The performance record thus provides a feedback loop that informs future construction and maintenance planning, refines quality‑control targets, and shapes specifications for subsequent grinding or rehabilitation activities.
