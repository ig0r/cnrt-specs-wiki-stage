Ride Quality

Part of: Pavement Condition Assessment is-subtopic-of
Ride Quality is a topic under Pavement Condition Assessment.
ride-quality
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-29

Ride Quality is a performance metric that quantifies the smoothness of concrete (and flexible) pavements, primarily expressed through the International Roughness Index (IRI) or Mean Roughness Index (MRI) [1][2]. It is used throughout the pavement life cycle—from preliminary‑engineering feasibility studies to post‑construction acceptance—to decide whether restoration methods are appropriate, to trigger corrective grinding, and to determine incentive payments tied to meeting prescribed smoothness thresholds [1][2]. The metric is obtained by profiling standard 528‑ft lane segments with calibrated inertial profilers (or a 12‑foot straightedge for localized bumps), and compliance is judged against speed‑dependent limits of 70 in/mi for speeds >45 mph or 90 in/mi for ≤45 mph, MRI not exceeding the acceptance value by more than 6.1 in/mi on at least 90 % of lots, and individual bumps or ridges limited to three‑sixteenths–one‑quarter inch and one‑eighth inch respectively [1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Ride Quality” also include the following subtopics: Pavement Profile.

Pavement Profile supplies the longitudinal roughness and transverse cross-slope measurements that quantify the elevation deviations defining Ride Quality. [2] The procedure uses gauge blocks and calibrated profiling equipment to record IRI and MRI values at regular intervals along 528-ft segments. [2]

Pavement Profile

Purpose and decision context

Ride Quality, most often expressed through International Roughness Index (IRI) or Mean Roughness Index (MRI), serves as a primary metric used to support several pivotal decisions across the concrete pavement life cycle [1][2]. In the preliminary engineering phase the measured ride quality of an existing pavement is compared against project criteria to decide whether conventional Concrete Pavement Restoration can be successfully applied; if the ride‑quality result is deemed inadequate, restoration is concluded infeasible [1]. The same ride‑quality information also influences the choice of surface treatment during rehabilitation: when improvement is required and the pavement does not contain Vanport Limestone aggregate, diamond grinding is prescribed, whereas pavements constructed with aggregates that polish rapidly are directed toward an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or a high‑friction surface treatment [1]. At the design stage ride‑quality considerations guide material selection, for example excluding roller‑compacted concrete from projects where IRI performance will be tied to incentive payments because its surface roughness can vary with placement equipment and may fail to meet smoothness criteria [1]. After construction or rehabilitation the measured ride quality is used to determine whether a pavement lot satisfies the smoothness acceptance criteria set out in contract specifications, thereby supporting the decision to accept the work for payment or to classify it as defective and require corrective action [2]. For concrete sections that have been diamond‑grinded, the post‑grind IRI must not exceed 70 in/mi on roads with posted speeds above 45 mph or 90 in/mi where the speed limit is 45 mph or less; meeting these thresholds authorizes payment [2]. For both concrete and asphalt lots the MRI is compared with the maximum values listed in Table A of the applicable specification, and any individual bump greater than 3/16 inch measured with a 12‑foot straightedge also renders the lot defective; exceeding these limits triggers corrective grinding or other remedial measures [2]. When ride quality meets the prescribed thresholds the contractor receives incentive payments as defined in Section 507.4, whereas failure to meet them results in only the base contract amount being paid [2]. The Representative may also require corrective grinding of excluded areas at the department’s expense when straightedge testing reveals irregularities exceeding one‑quarter inch, reinforcing the role of ride‑quality data in deciding whether additional work is necessary [2]. Finally, post‑construction ride‑quality data are used to verify that the finished pavement satisfies smoothness standards required for effective asset management, to evaluate the success of the work, and to forecast service life before further treatment becomes necessary [1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–99), Ch. 11 (p. 197); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 237–240), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 390–474)

Ride Quality is introduced to remedy the problem of excessive surface irregularities on newly constructed or rehabilitated pavements that reduce occupant comfort and safety [1][2]. The need therefore is to provide a smooth, low‑roughness pavement surface that delivers a uniform riding experience across the network [1][2]. To meet this need the specifications set measurable performance goals based on roughness indices such as the International Roughness Index (IRI) and the Mean Roughness Index (MRI) [1][2]. For facilities with posted speeds greater than 45 mph the IRI must not exceed 70 in/mi, while for speeds of 45 mph or less the limit is 90 in/mi [2]. In addition to profile‑based limits, any individual bump measured with a 12‑foot straightedge must be below the allowable tolerance—generally one‑quarter inch, though some provisions permit three‑sixteenths inch—and ridges or valleys relative to adjacent pavement may not exceed one‑eighth inch [2]. The MRI for each lot, defined as a minimum of 528 ft of a single lane, must stay within the maximum values set out in Table A and cannot surpass the acceptance value by more than 6.1 in/mi on at least 90 % of the lots [2]. Meeting these thresholds triggers full incentive payments to contractors, while failure to meet them requires corrective actions such as carbide or diamond grinding, removal and replacement, micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, high‑friction surface treatments, or other overlays before payment is released [1][2]. Early‑stage preservation actions are required before advanced distresses develop so that rideability can be maintained without compromising long‑term pavement life [1]. The guides also caution against using flexible base replacements on rigid pavements because such patches can degrade future smoothness and reduce the effectiveness of ride‑quality improvements [1]. Accurate assessment depends on timely collection of Pavement History data and IRI testing within three months after reopening to traffic, which makes the roughness measurements more valuable for asset‑management decisions [1]. In sum, Ride Quality addresses the problem of uncomfortable driving experiences caused by surface roughness, structural deficiencies, or inadequate measurement by establishing quantifiable smoothness criteria and prescribing preservation and rehabilitation treatments that achieve a uniform, comfortable riding experience throughout the pavement network [1][2].

Scope and applicability

Ride Quality is employed as a performance criterion across a broad spectrum of pavement projects, including the construction of new surfaces, the rehabilitation of existing rigid and flexible pavements, and thin‑layer preservation treatments aimed at extending service life [1][2]. For both concrete (rigid) and asphalt (flexible) work the requirement is triggered whenever the finished surface must satisfy prescribed smoothness limits, and compliance is verified during the acceptance or close‑out stage of the project [2]. In new concrete construction and overlay projects the Representative inspects each lot with a twelve‑foot straightedge and any irregularity exceeding one‑quarter inch, as well as longitudinal joints that exceed this tolerance, must be corrected by grinding before the surface can be accepted [2]. New flexible‑pavement work that includes at least two asphalt layers—such as base, scratch, leveling, binder or wearing courses—or full‑depth overlays of bridge approach slabs and decks is similarly evaluated after construction on lots that are a minimum of 528 ft long and 12 ft wide located on roadways with posted speeds above forty miles per hour; individual bumps greater than three‑sixteenths inch also require corrective grinding [2]. The International Roughness Index may not exceed seventy inches per mile where the posted speed limit is greater than forty‑five miles per hour and may not exceed ninety inches per mile where the speed limit is forty‑five miles per hour or less, with additional defect criteria that any ridge or valley larger than one‑eighth inch renders a lot defective [2]. Rehabilitation projects involving concrete repairs—such as patching, spall repair, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization or slab jacking—must have ride‑quality measurements taken before any diamond‑grinding is performed to establish a baseline and again after grinding to verify that the surface meets the allowable limits [2]. Flexible‑pavement rehabilitation that satisfies the layer, length, width and speed requirements is likewise measured after the repair work, and corrective grinding is mandated when the measured bump or ridge/valley thresholds are exceeded [2]. During the preliminary‑engineering phase of concrete‑pavement rehabilitation, ride quality is evaluated together with subgrade/subbase condition and friction adequacy to determine whether the structure can be successfully repaired, and at the design stage specifications may call for diamond grinding to raise ride quality unless the pavement contains Vanport Limestone aggregate, whose rapid loss of skid resistance after grinding necessitates an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or a high‑friction surface treatment instead [1]. Preservation‑type thin‑layer interventions such as micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses and diamond grinding on both flexible and rigid pavements are intended to improve ride quality early in the maintenance cycle before advanced distresses like rutting develop, and they must achieve a minimum ride‑quality level defined in Publication 408, which exempts them from a full pavement design [1]. For all new‑surface projects and for rehabilitation or preservation work, International Roughness Index data are collected on the “new” pavement within three months of entry into the Pavement History system and subsequently monitored annually on National Highway System facilities or biennially on non‑NHS assets to verify post‑construction performance [1]. Federally funded projects require an additional smoothness‑verification test performed by the Department using an inertial lightweight profiler for at least one day and covering a minimum of ten percent of the project’s lane miles concurrent with acceptance testing, and the resulting ride‑quality values are used to determine incentive or bonus payments after the post‑construction or post‑repair stage [2]. No separate incentive is provided for diamond‑grinding rehabilitation projects, although longitudinal roughness may be measured incidentally as part of the overall ride‑quality assessment [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 11 (p. 197), Ch. 12 (pp. 203–205); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 237–240), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 394–474)

Ride‑quality assessment becomes inappropriate when the pavement material or construction type compromises the intended performance measures [1][2]. For concrete pavements that use Vanport Limestone aggregate, diamond grinding is unsuitable because it removes the mortar layer and causes a rapid decline in skid resistance; an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or a high‑friction surface treatment should be selected instead [1]. Roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements also preclude the use of the International Roughness Index, as their inherent texture produces IRI values that are typically higher than those of conventional Portland cement concrete or asphalt, making compliance with IRI‑based specifications unrealistic; projects requiring IRI verification should therefore specify a conventional concrete or asphalt pavement [1]. Ride‑quality measurement is omitted on any pavement segment that does not meet full‑depth overlay criteria, such as vertical transition zones at paving limits and bridge approaches, and it is excluded on bridge decks that have not been overlaid [2]. Short ramps and tapered sections—specifically those shorter than 1,500 feet in length or narrower than 12 feet—are also excluded from ride‑quality evaluation, as are shoulders, medians, surfaces within five feet of appurtenances, and partial lots under 100 feet [2]. Roadways with a posted speed limit of forty miles per hour or lower are considered unsuitable for ride‑quality assessment [2]. Approach slabs or relief joints placed under another contract fall outside the scope of ride‑quality incentives, prompting the use of alternative criteria rather than IRI or MRI values [2]. When contracts pay by square‑yard quantities for rigid pavements, roughness and ride‑quality determinations become incidental and are not used as primary performance measures [2]. Concrete rehabilitation projects that employ diamond grinding receive no incentive tied to ride‑quality results, and projects that apply NGCS texturing also exclude ride‑quality incentives, relying instead on area‑based payment for the textured surface [2]. In all of these circumstances a different evaluation approach—such as area‑based measurements or other performance metrics—is preferred over ride‑quality indices [1][2].

Information needs

Accurate ride‑quality evaluation requires that each pavement segment be linked to its construction chronology, with dates and types of original build, reconstruction or resurfacing recorded in a Pavement History database; these records must be entered within four weeks for agency‑initiated openings to traffic and within six weeks for contractor‑performed projects so that longitudinal roughness testing can be scheduled no later than three months after entry. The primary condition data are longitudinal roughness indices, namely the International Roughness Index (IRI) and the Mean Roughness Index (MRI), which are measured on standard 528‑foot single‑lane lots while excluding bridge decks, short ramps, tapered sections, partial lots under 100 ft, shoulders and medians. For facilities with posted speeds greater than 45 mph the IRI may not exceed approximately 70 in/mi, whereas for speed limits of 45 mph or less the allowable IRI rises to about 90 in/mi; these thresholds are applied on a lot‑by‑lot basis. The MRI for each lot must stay within six‑point‑one inches per mile of the acceptance value on at least ninety percent of the evaluated lots, providing a tolerance check that complements the IRI limit. Individual surface irregularities are screened with a 12‑foot straightedge; any bump larger than three‑sixteenths of an inch must be ground, although some specifications permit up to one‑quarter inch before grinding is required, reflecting a minor divergence among guidelines. Transverse profiling also limits the height difference between adjacent lots to no more than one‑eighth of an inch, preventing ridges or valleys that would degrade ride comfort. Cost considerations are embedded in the incentive structure, where contracts include a dollar‑priced item for evaluating concrete and asphalt ride quality; after corrective work the MRI is compared to Table A to determine any payment adjustment, and if a defective lot is retained with written approval a flat rebate of $4,000 may be applied. The contractor must provide daily calibration results before taking measurements and submit raw unfiltered IRI and MRI data files together with summary printouts to the agency Representative within twenty‑four hours, ensuring timely stakeholder review. All measurements are performed in the presence of the Representative, who also reviews cross‑slope test locations and maximum depression depths, and whose written approval is required for any deviation from corrective action plans. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 11 (p. 197); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 237–240), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 394–405)

Ride‑quality information must be entered promptly after a project reopens to traffic—within four weeks for Department‑run work and within six weeks for contractor work—and the associated IRI testing must be performed no later than three months after that entry, because only current and complete Pavement History records allow the IRI values to represent the true smoothness experienced by vehicle occupants. [1] Accuracy of the measurements is ensured by using the pavement surface profile measuring equipment specified in the relevant sections and by employing the same device for both the pre‑grinding assessment after concrete rehabilitation and the post‑diamond‑grinding reassessment; the equipment must be calibrated each day and calibration results submitted to the Representative before any profiling begins. [2] To keep the data current, all raw, unfiltered profile files together with a summary printout of the International Roughness Index (IRI) and Mean Roughness Index (MRI) are required to be turned over to the Representative within twenty‑four hours of collection, or immediately after measurement. [2] Completeness is achieved by providing the full calibrated dataset for every ride‑quality lot defined in the contract, including all longitudinal roughness tests, calculated IRI values, and confirmation that at least two specified construction operations are present in the measured segment; the Pavement History must also be complete, covering every resurfacing, reconstruction, or other relevant activity. [1][2] The reported IRI values must meet the contractual maximum thresholds of 70 in/mile where posted speeds exceed 45 mph and 90 in/mile where posted speeds are 45 mph or less, and payment is contingent on compliance, so the information must be complete for every applicable lane segment. [2]

Alternatives

When ride quality is the primary performance metric, agencies compare a set of preservation and rehabilitation treatments that suit the pavement type and condition [1]. For rigid pavements diamond grinding serves as the baseline treatment for restoring smoothness, but when the aggregate tends to polish rapidly the alternatives shift to an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or a high‑friction surface treatment [1]. Full‑depth PCC patching, accelerated rapid‑setting concrete, and precast concrete paving systems are also viable repair options because they differ in opening time, shrinkage behavior, and ability to achieve the target IRI after any post‑install grinding [1]. In larger rehabilitation projects the feasible choices narrow to three alternatives—concrete pavement restoration, a new concrete overlay, or an asphalt overlay—each evaluated against existing distress, drainage, rehabilitatability and life‑cycle cost considerations [1]. When a thin surface layer is preferred a 6.3 mm (≈¼‑inch) Superpave asphalt overlay can be compared with micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, and profile‑correction techniques such as heater planing or milling, provided the overlay meets the minimum 1½‑inch thickness requirement on sound structures with adequate drainage [1]. Roller‑compacted concrete should be excluded from projects where IRI will be measured because its texture is typically rougher than conventional Portland‑cement concrete and hot‑mix asphalt [1]. The comparison framework begins with a baseline International Roughness Index measurement taken before any smoothing treatment and repeats the same measurement after the selected corrective or construction scenario using identical equipment so that IRI values can be directly compared [2]. Post‑treatment IRI must not exceed 70 in/mi where the posted speed limit is greater than 45 mph, or 90 in/mi where it is 45 mph or less [2]. Feasible options to compare therefore include a non‑defective lot evaluated against benchmark values in Table A, a defective lot that may be left in place with written approval in exchange for a $4,000 rebate, and a partial lot whose payment is prorated as a percentage of a full lot [2]. The corrective actions themselves constitute comparison alternatives such as carbide grinding limited to problem zones fifteen feet long, diamond grinding applied across the full lane width, removal of the surface course by milling followed by replacement of at least the minimum specified layer depth, and more extensive removal of a minimum ten‑foot segment between transverse joints [2]. When noise‑reducing grooved concrete surfaces are used two construction alternatives must be compared—a single‑pass grind that incorporates longitudinal grooves in one pass versus a two‑pass sequence that first grinds and then adds the grooves—because each produces a different texture that influences IRI while still observing the speed‑dependent thresholds [2]. The comparison also distinguishes lot types based on posted speed, defining Type 1 lots for speeds greater than 45 mph and Type 2 lots for speeds of 45 mph or less, each measured over a consistent full‑lot length of 528 ft [2]. In addition to IRI, individual surface irregularities are evaluated with a 12‑foot straightedge; any bump exceeding a quarter‑inch triggers grinding even on excluded areas that otherwise meet the straightedge tolerance [2]. At least two defined construction operations such as profile milling together with any combination of asphalt layers (base, scratch, leveling, binder or wearing courses) should be included in the comparison to assess their effect on smoothness [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–99), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 237–240), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 394–405)

Ride‑quality improvement offers four distinct corrective options, each with its own material, time, disruption, risk and maintenance profile. The carbide‑grinding alternative relies on walk‑behind machines fitted with carbide‑tipped heads; it is limited to patches no longer than fifteen feet and a single‑pass depth of one‑eighth inch. The process creates parallel grooves whose width ranges from three‑thirty‑seconds to one‑quarter inch, leaving a land area between one‑sixteenth and three‑sixteenths inch. Because the equipment is portable and the work zone small, installation proceeds quickly and traffic can be reopened after a brief period of slurry removal; the principal risk is exceeding the prescribed depth, which would generate surface ridges that could compromise the International Roughness Index (IRI) target. Future maintenance centers on preserving the groove geometry that underpins the IRI performance.

The diamond‑grinding single‑pass option employs a power‑driven, self‑propelled grinder weighing at least forty thousand pounds and equipped with a grinding head of minimum four‑foot width. The head carries a stacked set of one‑eighth‑inch grinding blades together with taller grooving blades, allowing the entire lane width to be ground and longitudinal grooves cut in a single pass. This combined operation shortens field time relative to multi‑pass methods, but it demands precise blade alignment, a feather pass for drainage, and continuous removal of slurry before traffic is restored, thereby limiting disruption while mitigating the risk of joint spalling or ridges beyond one‑eighth inch. The finished surface must meet IRI limits of seventy inches per mile for speeds above forty‑five miles per hour (or ninety inches per mile at lower speeds) and maintain cross‑slope tolerances; future maintenance is therefore tied to preserving those smoothness thresholds.

The two‑pass diamond‑grinding alternative uses the same equipment but first makes a grinding pass with the one‑eighth‑inch blade stack, then follows with a second pass to cut the longitudinal grooves. The additional pass extends construction time and requires an extra alignment check between operations, increasing traffic disruption compared with the single‑pass method. Nevertheless it provides comparable control over groove depth and spacing and is subject to the same IRI and cross‑slope requirements, so future maintenance remains focused on retaining the established roughness limits.

Finally, the removal‑and‑replacement option calls for cutting out a minimum of ten feet of reinforced cement concrete between transverse joints—or an entire panel of plain cement concrete—and installing new joint work in accordance with standard drawings. This approach introduces fresh concrete and joint sealants as material inputs and entails substantially longer demolition and placement periods, resulting in greater traffic disruption than grinding alternatives. Risks include maintaining structural integrity during removal and ensuring proper curing of the replacement slab. Once placed, the new pavement segment follows normal maintenance cycles, with its own IRI performance to be monitored over time. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 395–399)

Decision criteria

The guides agree that performance, expressed through measured pavement roughness, should be the controlling criterion for ride‑quality decisions, outweighing safety, cost, durability, schedule, sustainability, accessibility and public impact. Performance is quantified primarily by the International Roughness Index, with acceptance limits set at 70 in/mi where posted speeds exceed 45 mph and 90 in/mi where they are 45 mph or less. In addition to IRI, verification testing employs a Mean Roughness Index, requiring that the verification MRI not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 in/mi for at least ninety percent of the 528‑ft lots in each lane. Because incentive payments are tied directly to compliance with these IRI and MRI thresholds, corrective action is triggered solely on performance shortfalls, without reference to other project criteria. The same performance emphasis mandates that engineers evaluate both short‑term and long‑term loss of ride quality before approving alternatives such as flexible‑base replacements in concrete repairs, ensuring any degradation is acceptable. Likewise, the guidance explicitly prohibits the use of roller‑compacted concrete in sections where IRI will be measured for incentive payments, since its inherently rougher surface can produce inconsistent IRI values that would jeopardize performance compliance. Consequently, across the sources, ride‑quality decisions are governed by quantitative performance metrics, with all other considerations taking a secondary or non‑applicable role. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 237–238), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 395–405)

Across the guides, competing ride‑quality criteria are organized in a strict hierarchy rather than being assigned proportional numerical weights. The first step is to apply the project‑specific Ride Specification and verify that the pavement meets the minimum ride‑quality level required by Publication 408 before any other design or preservation standards are considered. That primary threshold is expressed as a speed‑dependent roughness ceiling, where facilities with posted speeds greater than 45 mph must not exceed an International Roughness Index of 70 in/mi and those with speeds of 45 mph or less must not exceed 90 in/mi; the same IRI (or Mean Roughness Index where specified) functions as the gatekeeper for acceptance and payment. Only after the IRI/MRI limit is satisfied does the pavement become eligible for further evaluation against secondary ride‑quality items. The secondary items are enforced as hard thresholds but carry lower priority; at least 98 % of the surface must be textured with the specified aggregate system, any cross‑slope deviation greater than one quarter inch over a twelve‑foot run must be corrected, individual bumps larger than three‑sixteenths of an inch and ridges or valleys exceeding one eighth of an inch are prohibited. These supporting checks ensure uniformity, drainage, and compliance with geometric preservation criteria, but they do not influence the payment formula; they simply trigger corrective work if violated. Consequently, the weighting of competing criteria places the speed‑dependent roughness limit at the top, followed by texture coverage, cross‑slope control, and localized surface irregularities, with all remaining pavement preservation and safety standards considered only after these ride‑quality priorities have been satisfied. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 203–204); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 237–239), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 398–405)

Analysis methods and tools

Ride quality assessment relies on direct roughness measurements obtained with standardized inertial lightweight profilers that record the longitudinal surface profile and generate International Roughness Index values, which serve as the primary compliance metric [2]. The IRI thresholds are set at a maximum of 70 in/mile for facilities with posted speeds above 45 mph and 90 in/mile where speeds are 45 mph or less, and all measured lots must satisfy these limits to qualify for payment [2]. In addition to IRI, the Mean Roughness Index is calculated from the same raw profile data and compared against a tabular reference to determine incentive payments, with partial lots receiving proportional shares based on their MRI values [2]. The measurement process requires daily calibration of the profiler, submission of calibration results to the Representative, and delivery of unfiltered data files and summary printouts within 24 hours of collection [2]. Verification of the contractor’s results is performed by the Department on at least ten percent of lane miles using its own profiler, and the verification MRI may not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 inches per mile for at least ninety percent of the 528‑foot lots, nor may the average verification MRI differ from the average acceptance MRI by more than the same margin across a lane [2]. Beyond roughness profiling, ride‑quality evaluation incorporates a suite of non‑destructive tests—including deflection testing, faulting measurement, friction testing, ground‑penetrating radar, and specialized systems such as MIRA and MIT Scan—to characterize structural condition and surface characteristics [1]. The results from these measurements are fed into life‑cycle cost analysis to compare rehabilitation alternatives and support decision making, although the guides do not prescribe separate use of condition ratings, decision trees, risk assessment, or benefit‑cost analysis specifically for ride quality [1][2].

The overall ride‑quality result is predicated on a set of interrelated assumptions that together define how pavement smoothness is quantified and interpreted. For Roller Compacted Concrete sections the guides assume that the texture produced by the paver and roller during placement dominates the final surface, so any variation in equipment performance directly influences the International Roughness Index value and therefore such sections are excluded from payment‑linked IRI programs. A parallel assumption for all pavement types is that the measured Mean Roughness Index (MRI), obtained with PTM No. 428 or an inertial lightweight profiler, serves as a reliable surrogate for occupant comfort and can be compared against a prescribed acceptance MRI using a strict verification tolerance of no more than 6.1 inches per mile on at least ninety percent of the defined lots. The measurement protocol further assumes that each lot consists of a continuous 528‑foot lane segment, that only fully constructed portions after at least two specified construction operations are evaluated, and that any excluded features such as vertical transitions, bridge decks not overlaid, short ramps, shoulders, medians, appurtenances, partial lots under 100 feet, or roadways with posted speeds of forty miles per hour or lower are measured separately and omitted from the lot calculation. Accurate MRI values also depend on the assumption that profiling equipment is calibrated daily, that calibration records are submitted to the Representative before any data collection, and that the operators conducting the survey hold current certification and demonstrate competence. In addition, the guides assume that Pavement History information—recording construction, reconstruction, resurfacing dates, etc.—is entered promptly (within four weeks for department‑managed projects and six weeks for contractor‑managed projects) so that IRI testing can be scheduled within three months of data entry and reflect current surface conditions. When the RCC equipment assumption is violated, the inherent roughness of RCC combined with paver or roller variability introduces notable uncertainty into IRI values, prompting the recommendation to avoid using RCC for incentive‑based ride‑quality measurement. Delays beyond the prescribed entry windows, testing performed before a pavement has reopened to traffic, or reliance on biennial surveys for non‑NHS routes generate uncertainty about whether recorded IRI truly represents present smoothness. Calibration drift, errors in daily equipment checks, or inadequately certified operators create variability in the recorded profile and therefore affect MRI outcomes. Choices made during profiling—such as using raw versus filtered data, submitting profiles within twenty‑four hours, and how residual ridges, valleys, or bumps are handled after grinding—add further sources of uncertainty to the final ride‑quality value. The exclusion rules intended to isolate representative pavement sections can reduce overall representativeness if features are misidentified or applied inconsistently, thereby increasing uncertainty in the aggregated ride‑quality result. Finally, uncertainties arise when tolerances for transverse deviations (no more than one‑eighth inch), individual bumps (exceeding three‑sixteenths or one‑quarter inch depending on measurement method), or overall lot compliance are not met, triggering departmental reviews of testing procedures, equipment performance, and personnel competence. Stakeholders and constraints

Input for ride‑quality assessment is supplied by the local Districts, which must enter Pavement History data for every maintenance or construction activity promptly [1]. In addition, the contractor performing the work records cross‑slope locations, conducts longitudinal roughness tests, and generates raw unfiltered IRI and MRI files that are submitted to the district Representative within 24 hours of each survey [2]. The Asset Management Division of the Bureau of Maintenance and Operations, specifically its Pavement Testing and Asset Management Section, uses the Districts’ history entries to schedule system‑wide IRI testing, conduct the measurements, analyze the results, and produce reports that determine when and how ride quality is measured and documented [1]. The district Representative receives the contractor’s calibration data and roughness values, designates measurement lots, compares each lot’s MRI to the prescribed tables, and initially decides whether corrective grinding is required and which lots qualify for payment [2]. When the measured IRI exceeds the allowable limits—70 in/mi where posted speeds are greater than 45 mph and 90 in/mi where speeds are 45 mph or less—the Representative may order grinding and holds authority to approve that work [2]. For federally funded projects, the State Department’s Roadway Inventory and Testing Unit conducts a smoothness verification test covering at least ten percent of the lane miles on a scheduled day, and its results constitute the final compliance decision and the basis for incentive‑payment adjustments if the contractor’s acceptance test is unsatisfactory [2]. After any corrective action, the District Executive must provide written approval before a defective lot can remain in place or before a testing waiver is issued, and also authorizes any rebate adjustments such as a downward reduction of the lot payment [2]. Thus, ride‑quality input originates from both the Districts’ pavement history records and the contractor’s field measurements; the decision hierarchy proceeds through the Asset Management Section for system‑wide scheduling, the district Representative for initial adjudication, the State Department testing unit for ultimate compliance on federal projects, and the District Executive for final waivers and rebate approvals [1][2].

The selection of ride‑quality treatments is governed by a suite of PennDOT specifications, design manuals, and funding policies that set permissible methods and materials. For concrete pavements the default restoration method is diamond grinding to meet profile and IRI targets unless the pavement uses Vanport Limestone aggregate or an aggregate prone to rapid polishing, in which case an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course or a High Friction Surface Treatment may be employed. Full‑depth slab patching must conform to Publication 72M and Roadway Construction Standards RC‑26M, with precast units installed at least one‑quarter inch above the surrounding surface so that a final diamond grind can reestablish cross‑slope, profile, and IRI. Roller‑compacted concrete is barred from projects where ride quality will be measured for payment incentives, and any exposed RCC pavement on routes with speed limits of 35 mph or higher must receive diamond grinding to provide the required texture. Overlay designs are limited by Chapter 5 and Publication 408 Section 413.3(b); on Interstate highways a Superpave wearing‑course overlay cannot be less than 1½ inches thick without prior PDAU approval, while thin asphalt overlays must obey depth limits in Table 9.5 and weather restrictions. Flexible‑pavement preservation projects may not use overlays thicker than 2 inches, and a standard 6.3‑mm Superpave wearing course must employ PG 64E‑22 binder; micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses are prescribed for early‑cycle applications aimed at improving skid resistance and ride quality. All ride‑quality projects must apply the appropriate Ride Specification and achieve the minimum thresholds set out in Publication 408 Sections 404.4 or 507.4. Districts are required to enter construction data within four weeks of a department‑initiated reopening (six weeks for contractor‑initiated work), and Pavement Testing and Asset Management staff must conduct IRI testing on the newly opened surface within three months of that entry. Patching is limited to ten percent of the pavement area unless a cost‑effectiveness analysis justifies a larger extent, and all repairs must be verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing before proceeding; design exceptions must follow the documentation and approval procedures in Publication 10C and Design Manual Part 1C, with funding allocated only to actions deemed cost‑effective by PennDOT’s Pavement Management System. Prior to any grinding or surface work the existing pavement must be profiled using the same inertial profiler that will later verify post‑treatment results, and the equipment must be calibrated each day in accordance with PTM No. 428, with calibration records supplied to the Representative before measurements are taken. Raw IRI and MRI data must be turned over within twenty‑four hours of collection, and a “full lot” is defined as 528 ft of a single lane that meets geometric criteria—minimum width twelve feet, posted speed above forty mph, at least two construction layers, and exact lot length; partial lots are paid proportionally. Facilities with posted speeds greater than forty‑five mph must achieve an IRI not exceeding seventy inches per mile, while those with speeds of forty‑five mph or less may not exceed ninety inches per mile, and the MRI for each lot must stay within the maximum values listed in Table A of Section 404.4 for flexible pavement or Section 507.4 for rigid pavement. Individual surface irregularities are capped at three‑sixteenths of an inch on flexible pavements and one‑quarter inch on rigid pavements when measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge; ridges or valleys may not exceed one‑eighth inch in the transverse profile, and longitudinal joints must meet Section 501.3(o)(1) or be corrected. Certain facility elements—bridge decks, short ramps, shoulders, zones within five feet of appurtenances, narrow tapered sections, medians and very short partial lots—are excluded from ride‑quality lot calculations. For federally funded projects smoothness verification testing must cover at least ten percent of lane miles, and the verification MRI may not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than six point one inches per mile on at least ninety percent of the 528‑ft lots, with an overall average difference limited to the same amount and a length‑difference tolerance of two‑tenths of one percent. Payment for ride‑quality performance is governed by the incentive policy in Section 507.4; contractors receive a dollar‑based item calculated from Table A together with the measured MRI for each lot, and because this payment is classified as an incentive the normal provisions of Section 110.02(d) do not apply. No ride‑quality incentive is available for diamond grinding on concrete rehabilitation projects nor for NGCS texturing on concrete projects; textured surface work is compensated only by the square‑yard quantity completed. If a lot remains defective after corrective actions, it may be left in place only with written approval from the District Executive and the contractor’s acceptance of a fixed rebate of four thousand dollars against the amount paid for that lot. Costs associated with the ride‑quality evaluation itself are excluded from separate reimbursement. [1][2]

Timing and implementation

The timing for a ride‑quality decision is anchored at several points throughout the life of a pavement project. The guides agree that an initial judgment must be made during preliminary engineering when the existing surface is evaluated for rehabilitation, and it must also be taken before any substitution of a concrete slab with a flexible base so that short‑ and long‑term impacts on ride quality can be assessed [1]. Once a new surface has been placed, the decision is refined after an initial International Roughness Index measurement has been collected and analyzed; project data are required to be entered within four to six weeks of reopening to traffic and the IRI test must be performed no later than three months after that entry, providing timely information for the ride‑quality judgment [1]. In addition, the guides stress that treatments such as micro‑surfacing or an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course should be applied early in the maintenance cycle—before advanced distresses develop—to improve ride quality while pavement condition remains relatively good [1]. The final acceptance of ride quality occurs during the acceptance phase of a rigid‑pavement contract, immediately after the surface profile has been measured and IRI or Mean Roughness Index data have been submitted to the Representative [2]. An initial evaluation is required before any diamond grinding, following prescribed profile‑measuring procedures, and after the grooved concrete surface is finished a second measurement is compared with allowable IRI limits—70 in/mile for posted speeds above 45 mph and 90 in/mile for speeds at or below 45 mph—as well as with bump‑size criteria (no individual bump larger than 3/16 inch and no irregularity exceeding ¼ inch when checked with a 12‑foot straightedge) [2]. If those thresholds are met, the lot is deemed non‑defective and incentive payment is calculated immediately; if limits are exceeded or prohibited bumps are found, the decision is deferred until corrective grinding is performed and a re‑measurement confirms compliance before final acceptance [2]. Across the sources, therefore, the decision for ride quality should be made early—during preliminary engineering and prior to major maintenance actions—and then confirmed at project acceptance after measured IRI/MRI values satisfy prescribed limits, with any necessary corrections addressed before final acceptance.

Action is triggered whenever a project requires an International Roughness Index (IRI) measurement for ride‑quality assessment or payment incentives, and in such cases roller‑compacted concrete pavements are excluded. If an RCC surface will be exposed to traffic traveling at 35 mph or higher, the surface must be diamond‑ground before opening to traffic. After any concrete rehabilitation or diamond‑grinding operation, the newly finished pavement is profiled and its IRI compared with speed‑dependent limits of 70 in/mi for posted speeds above 45 mph and 90 in/mi for posted speeds of 45 mph or less. Deterioration in rideability on existing pavements also initiates corrective action; on Interstates overlays thinner than 1½ inches require prior approval, while thin overlays elsewhere are allowed only when the surface is asphalt, the underlying structure is sound with less than two percent patching, and drainage is adequate or will be upgraded. Early signs of distress such as emerging rutting, loss of smoothness, or other identifiable surface problems prompt preservation treatments like micro‑surfacing or an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course before the distresses progress further in the maintenance cycle. Whenever a pavement segment is resurfaced, reconstructed or otherwise altered, its Pavement History must be entered into the Roadway Management System within four weeks for Department‑managed projects or six weeks for contractor‑managed projects. Following that entry, the Pavement Testing staff must complete an IRI measurement on the new surface within three months. Annual IRI collection is performed on all National Highway System routes, and a two‑year cycle covers non‑NHS roads to keep ride‑quality data current for asset‑management decisions. The guides require that each ride‑quality lot be calibrated daily before any profile measurement, and the raw unfiltered IRI and Mean Roughness Index files must be delivered to the Representative within twenty‑four hours of testing. The Representative evaluates the lot against Table A (or Section 404.4) and determines incentive payment; an MRI that exceeds the maximum listed in Table A, a bump greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge (or one‑quarter inch where the specification calls for that tolerance), or a ridge/valley exceeding one‑eighth of an inch all trigger corrective grinding, removal or replacement before further testing or payment can proceed. If the MRI exceeds its tolerance by more than 6.1 in/mi, a Department review is launched, the profiler’s certification is suspended and recertification must be arranged with the RITU within ten business days. Contractors must also coordinate smoothness verification with the Representative at least fourteen days in advance; failure to meet this coordination deadline postpones evaluation. When corrective work has been completed, a second MRI measurement is taken and incentive payment is recomputed; the contractor may retain a still‑defective lot only after obtaining written approval from the District Executive and accepting a $4,000 rebate. All preservation projects must achieve the minimum ride quality defined in Publication 408 (Sections 404.4 or 507.4), and the appropriate Ride Specification is applied to bring non‑conforming surfaces into compliance. Any change in posted speed limit that alters the lot type terminates the current lot and initiates a new evaluation under the applicable IRI threshold. [1][2]

The contractor is required to finish all concrete‑pavement rehabilitation tasks—including patching, spall repair, dowel retrofit, slab stabilization or jacking—before any grinding operations can commence. After rehabilitation, a PTM No. 428‑compliant profiling instrument must be calibrated each day and the calibration results submitted to the Representative prior to taking measurements; the surface profile is then recorded in the Representative’s presence and the raw data together with IRI and MRI summary reports are turned over within twenty‑four hours. Unless otherwise approved, the entire lane width is ground, and after each longitudinal roughness test the IRI must not exceed seventy inches per mile on facilities with posted speeds greater than forty‑five miles per hour, or ninety inches per mile where the speed limit is forty‑five miles per hour or less. If any segment fails to meet the specified IRI limits, additional grinding passes are performed on that segment before final acceptance. All activities are carried out under the Representative’s supervision and in coordination with the Roadway Inventory and Testing Unit, ensuring compliance with the established schedule and resource allocations. Outcomes and follow-up

Success for Ride Quality is defined as the newly opened pavement achieving the smoothness criteria set out in the applicable Ride Specification and meeting or exceeding the minimum ride‑quality value prescribed by Publication 408 (Section 404.4 or Section 507.4). In addition, each lot must satisfy all contract‑specified tolerances so that it qualifies for the full ride‑quality incentive, which includes speed‑dependent International Roughness Index limits of 70 in/mile where posted speeds exceed 45 mph and 90 in/mile where they are 45 mph or lower. The longitudinal roughness measured by the IRI must be obtained after the pavement has been opened to traffic, with the project’s pavement history entered into the database within four weeks for Department‑managed projects and within six weeks for contractor‑performed projects, followed by an IRI test no later than three months after that entry. Concurrently, the Mean Roughness Index derived from the Department’s inertial lightweight profiler must not exceed the acceptance MRI by more than 6.1 in/mile on at least ninety percent of the 528‑ft lots in each lane, and the average verification MRI for a lane may not be higher than the average acceptance MRI by more than the same margin. The surface must also be free of localized defects; any bump measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge that exceeds three‑sixteenths of an inch (and certainly any exceeding one‑quarter inch) must be ground, no ridge or valley on the transverse profile may exceed one‑eighth inch, and no individual irregularity greater than one‑quarter inch is permitted. All measurements are performed with calibrated pavement‑profile equipment in accordance with PTM No. 428, with daily calibration results documented and raw unfiltered data files together with summary printouts of IRI and MRI values submitted to the Representative within twenty‑four hours of measurement. The Representative reviews the MRI (and where applicable the IRI) to classify each lot as “not defective,” and lots that meet the criteria receive incentive payment according to Table A, while any lot requiring corrective grinding is re‑measured and the post‑correction MRI determines the adjusted incentive; if a defective lot is retained with written District Executive approval, the contractor accepts a $4,000 rebate. Thus, success for Ride Quality is demonstrated by compliance with both the specification‑based minimum values and the contract‑level smoothness tolerances, documented through timely data entry, calibrated profiling, prompt submission of measurement results, and formal acceptance or incentive determination by the Representative. Ride‑quality results are monitored through a coordinated program that combines system‑wide International Roughness Index (IRI) surveys with lot‑level profiling using approved inertial profilers [1][2]. For the National Highway System the IRI is collected each year, while non‑NHS routes are surveyed on a two‑year cycle, and any pavement that has been resurfaced or reconstructed is required to be tested within three months of its entry into the Pavement History database [1]. District personnel must record the new pavement history promptly—within four weeks for Department‑managed projects and within six weeks for contractor‑performed work—to enable the central Pavement Testing and Asset Management team to retrieve, quality‑assure, store and report the data [1]. At the lot level a defined 528‑foot segment of a single lane is measured longitudinally by an inertial profiler that complies with PTM No. 428, and the profiler must be calibrated before use with a daily calibration record submitted to the project Representative [2]. The raw, unfiltered profile files together with a summary printout of IRI and Mean Roughness Index (MRI) values are delivered to the Representative within twenty‑four hours of each survey, creating a permanent documented record for every lot [2]. All calibration records, cross‑slope or depression measurements, and any corrective‑action documentation are retained as part of the project file and later entered into the Roadway Management System where IRI values are linked to construction dates [1][2]. The documented IRI and MRI results are compared against speed‑limit based acceptance thresholds—no more than 70 in/mi when the posted speed exceeds 45 mph and no more than 90 in/mi when it is 45 mph or less—and lots that exceed these limits are classified as non‑payable and must be re‑ground [2]. For each lot the MRI is also checked against the values in Table A, and when the verification MRI does not exceed the allowed excess of 6.1 in/mi on at least 90 % of the lots, incentive payments are calculated; otherwise a review of testing procedures, equipment and personnel is triggered and profiler recertification may be required [2]. The Department conducts smoothness‑verification testing on at least one day covering a minimum of ten percent of lane miles, coordinated with the Representative in advance, and those verification results are cross‑checked against contractor‑submitted values before final payment decisions are made [2]. Analysts use the linked IRI trends and lot‑level MRI outcomes to evaluate initial smoothness, detect anomalies, assess the effectiveness of recent preservation or reconstruction work, and feed those insights into future design, maintenance planning, equipment selection and contract specifications [1][2].
