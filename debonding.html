Debonding

Part of: Bonded Concrete Overlay is-subtopic-of
Debonding is a topic under Bonded Concrete Overlay.
debonding
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Debonding is the loss of adhesion between an existing asphalt overlay and its underlying cement‑concrete or brick base, identified when coring performed during design reveals that a satisfactory bond does not exist [1]. The distress manifests at the layer interface—whether under composite pavements, interlayers, self‑adhesive paving grids, or bonded concrete overlays—and is linked to construction deficiencies such as improper tack‑coat temperature or rate, insufficient joint saw‑cut width, surface contamination, or milling that removes more than the preferred 1 ½ inches of asphalt [1][2]. Because a debonded overlay cannot function as a structural layer, specifications require removal of the entire depth of the existing asphalt and treatment of the area as a surface‑replacement item, with defect classification based on the bond‑strength test specified in Section 545.3(e) (ASTM C1583) performed at seven days; an average below 175 psi (or any individual result under 160 psi) triggers full‑depth removal and replacement [2].

Description and identification

Debonding is the loss of adhesion between an existing asphalt overlay and the underlying concrete or brick base, which is identified when coring performed during the design phase shows that an adequate bond does not exist; because the overlay cannot rely on a proper bond, the entire depth of that asphalt layer will require removing the entire depth of the existing asphalt overlay. The source notes that it is preferred that a minimum of 1 ½ inches of existing asphalt material be left in place to retain structural integrity and provide adequate bonding, but when debonding is discovered the full thickness must be stripped and accounted for with a surface‑replacement item in the contract. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

During the design phase, coring is performed to verify whether a satisfactory bond exists between an existing asphalt overlay and its concrete or brick base; when coring reveals an inadequate bond, the condition is classified as debonding rather than other surface defects. When debonding is confirmed by coring, repair requires removing the entire depth of the affected asphalt overlay, whereas a properly bonded layer may be retained with at least 1 ½ inches left in place to retain structural integrity. In the construction of bonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements, debonding is identified by conducting a bond‑strength test at seven days in accordance with ASTM C1583 after surface preparation but before placing the overlay. If the average strength of three tests falls below 175 psi and any individual result does not reach a minimum of 160 psi, the area is classified as defective in bond strength under Section 545.3(e). Additionally, to prevent debonding, saw‑cut widths shall be at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack, or greater, as required by Section 545.3(h). [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 466–469)

Where it occurs

Within overlay projects, debonding is identified for composite pavements in which an existing asphalt overlay rests on a cement‑concrete or brick base; the design must include coring to verify that the bond between the concrete (or brick) base and the asphalt is adequate, and if debonding is found the entire depth of the asphalt overlay will require removing. When milling a portion of an asphalt layer over such a rigid base the entire asphalt layer may be removed, but it is preferred that at least 1 ½ inches of existing asphalt material be left in place so the material retains structural integrity and remains adequately bonded. Debonding also mentioned for asphalt overlay structures where an interlayer is placed over existing asphalt pavement; it can arise when the tack coat or asphalt material is applied incorrectly, such as if the asphalt is too cold, the application rate is insufficient, or a leveling course is missing after milling. The issue likewise applies to self‑adhesive paving grid overlays that require a manufacturer‑approved tack coat. Debonding can occur on rigid concrete‑surfaced pavements that receive a concrete overlay, whether the overlay is specified as unbonded or bonded. For unbonded concrete overlays the distress is recognized at the overlay itself and particularly at transitions where a geotextile debonding layer must be placed. For bonded concrete overlays the distress may develop at transverse joints or existing cracks in the underlying pavement when the saw‑cut width does not meet the required dimension; the specification calls for the cut to be at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–121); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 466–474)

In the Pavement Policy Manual, Section 5.12 OVERLAYS > b (Interlayer Installation Guidance) identifies debonding at the layer interface between an existing asphalt overlay and its underlying concrete or brick base, where the newly placed interlayer fails to adhere to the underlying asphalt surface [1]. The same source notes that this lack of adhesion occurs specifically at the interface between the interlayer (tack coat or asphalt material) and the existing pavement layer [1]. In the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification, Section 548 (Unbonded Concrete Overlay), debonding is addressed at the limits of a concrete overlay where transitions occur, and a geotextile may be placed as a debonding layer between the overlay and any existing treated base course or concrete pavement directly below it [2]. The specification further defines the overlay panels as “unbonded concrete overlay panels” that are separated by transverse and longitudinal joints created by saw cuts, linking debonding to those joint locations within the overlay itself [2]. For bonded concrete overlay projects covered by Section 545, debonding is a concern at the interface over existing transverse joints and any cracks that lie beneath the new concrete overlay, tying the distress to pre‑existing discontinuities in the underlying pavement [2]. The document requires that the saw‑cut width be at least the width of the underlying joint or crack (or greater) to prevent debonding, directly associating potential debonding locations with transverse joints and cracks in the existing pavement [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–121); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 466–474)

Causes and mechanisms

Debonding in overlay applications is primarily linked to construction‑related deficiencies, such as applying a tack coat that is too heavy or laying asphalt when ambient temperatures are high, which can impair waterproofing performance and lead to loss of adhesion. The guidance further notes that placing the asphalt layer at temperatures that are too low or using an insufficient application rate can also cause debonding, indicating that material temperature control during construction is a critical factor. Additionally, the existing pavement condition, the type of overlay selected, and the pavement temperature at placement influence interlayer performance, implying that design choices and construction practices related to material selection and timing contribute to debonding risk. In bonded concrete overlays, debonding is addressed through specific construction requirements: transverse joint saw cuts must be cut to a width equal to or greater than the underlying joint or crack to prevent separation. The specification also mandates thorough surface cleaning, removal of loose material, and protection from oil or moisture before placement, recognizing that contamination can precipitate debonding. Bond‑strength testing in accordance with ASTM C1583 must achieve an average strength of at least 175 psi, with no individual test result falling below 160 psi; failure to meet these thresholds is considered a defect and may indicate debonding. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 120–121); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 466–469)

Debonding develops when the adhesive connection between an existing asphalt overlay and its underlying cement‑concrete or brick base is insufficient, and coring must be performed during the design phase of the project to determine if an adequate bond exists; if the bond fails, the distress progresses to the point where the entire depth of the overlay must be removed. The mechanism also includes failure of an interlayer to achieve adequate adhesion, which can occur if the asphalt material or tack coat is applied at an improper temperature, is too cold, or is applied too heavily, leading to slippage especially under high temperatures. Surface conditions such as a rough milled base may impede bonding and may require a leveling course before the interlayer is placed. Debonding initiates when the concrete overlay does not achieve a sufficient bond to the prepared existing surface, which can occur if contaminants such as oil or grease remain, standing water is present, or the surface temperature exceeds the limit before placement. Inadequate joint preparation—specifically saw cuts that are narrower than the underlying joint—also creates a path for separation. The distress progresses once measured bond strength falls below the required average of 175 psi (with each individual test needing at least 160 psi), causing the area to be classified as defective and prone to loss of adhesion. Debonding is accelerated when milling removes more than the preferred minimum of 1 ½ inches of existing asphalt, compromising structural integrity and reducing bonding capacity. It is further accelerated by exposure to high ambient temperatures, overspray of tack coat that can bleed through, and steep grades greater than 5 % that demand specially proportioned mixes for compaction and shear stability. Accelerating conditions also include insufficient surface cleaning, temperatures above 120°F at placement, moisture on the substrate, and joint saw cuts that do not meet the minimum width equal to or greater than the underlying joint. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–121); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 466–469)

Severity levels and rating

The condition rating for debonding is assigned by the bond‑strength testing procedure specified in Section 545.3(e), which requires testing according to ASTM C1583 at seven days; if the average of three test results is below 175 psi and each individual result is at least 160 psi, the area is classified as defective, triggering replacement per the specification. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 466–469)

Measurement, survey and extent

Debonding is evaluated by measuring bond strength in pounds per square inch using an ASTM C1583 pull‑off test performed on the prepared surface seven days after preparation and in the presence of the Inspector; three specimens are tested at each selected location and their average is compared to the acceptance limits, with an average below 175 psi or any individual result under 160 psi indicating a defective area. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 466–469)

Debonding is surveyed by manual bond‑strength pull‑off testing (ASTM C1583) performed in the presence of the Inspector, with the first test conducted at seven days after surface preparation as required by Section 545.3(e). For each day’s placement covering up to 3,000 square yards, five random locations are selected and tested; if the average of three tests falls below 175 psi (with each individual result not less than 160 psi) the area is deemed defective, triggering additional testing at 100‑foot intervals longitudinally from the failed spot. The extent of debonding is then quantified by representing each set of three failing tests as a defect that spans 50 feet in both directions along the lane, effectively expressing the condition as a length of affected pavement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 466–469)

Effects on performance

When an asphalt overlay loses its bond to the underlying concrete or brick base, the loss of adhesion must be addressed because the debonded section cannot function as a structural layer; the source requires that the entire depth of the existing asphalt overlay be removed and that any undetected debonding areas be accounted for with a surface replacement item in the contract. Consequently, untreated debonding escalates to full‑depth removal of the overlay and additional surface replacement work. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

Repairs and treatments

In the Pavement Policy Manual, when coring performed during design reveals that an existing asphalt overlay has lost adhesion to a concrete or brick base, debonding requires removal of the entire depth of the existing asphalt overlay and treatment of the location as a surface‑replacement item in the contract; this provision also permits inclusion of debonded areas not discovered by coring (section 5.12 OVERLAYS) [1]. The same manual further mitigates debonding during interlayer installation by specifying that if fabric pickup occurs because of high ambient temperature or tack overspray, clean blotting sand or broadcast asphalt material shall be spread over the affected area and excess removed before new asphalt placement (section 5.12 b) [1]. It also requires proper application temperature and an adequate rate of the asphalt or tack coat to prevent loss of adhesion (section 5.12 b) [1]. When milling has produced a rough surface, a leveling course may be required prior to placing the interlayer (section 5.12 b) [1]. For self‑adhesive paving grids, a manufacturer‑approved tack coat or asphalt material shall be applied unless the grid and overlay are installed on the same day as the leveling course (section 5.12 b) [1]. In the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications for unbonded concrete overlays, a geotextile debonding layer must be placed at concrete‑overlay transitions that sit on an existing treated base course or concrete pavement directly beneath the overlay, addressing the intentional lack of bond at those transition locations (Section 548.3(e)3.a) [2]. For bonded concrete overlays, transverse joints are required to be saw‑cut to a width no less than the underlying joint or crack, which directly prevents loss of adhesion at those locations and applies during the joint‑cutting step before the overlay cures (Section 545.3(h)) [2]. Additionally, bond strength must be verified by ASTM C1583 testing seven days after placement; if the average of three test results is below 175 psi or any individual result falls under 160 psi, the area is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced as a debonded section per the Defective Work provision (Section 545.3(e) and Section 545.3(n)) [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–121); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 466–474)

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) in Section 5.12 Overlays, when coring performed during design shows that the bond between an existing asphalt overlay and its concrete or brick base is inadequate—that is, debonding of the overlay from the base—the required treatment is to remove the entire depth of the existing asphalt overlay and to provide a surface‑replacement item in the contract. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications (Publication 408/2026) state in Section 548.3(u) that a geotextile debonding layer may be placed only at transition zones where the new concrete overlay rests on an existing treated base course or directly on underlying concrete pavement, and they do not impose any severity, depth, width or extent limits for the debonding condition. The same specifications in Section 545.3(h) prescribe that a transverse joint saw‑cut treatment is applicable only when the saw cuts are at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack, and when bond‑strength testing performed to ASTM C1583 yields an average of not less than 175 psi with each individual test result not below 160 psi; if these limits are not satisfied, the overlay is deemed defective in bond strength and must be removed and replaced per Section 545.4(c). [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 466–474)

Prevention in design and construction

For milling over cement concrete or brick bases the manual prefers that at least 1 ½ inches of the existing asphalt layer be retained so that the material keeps its structural integrity and remains adequately bonded to the underlying base; this preference applies specifically to milling operations on such bases (section 5.12) [1]. The manual requires coring to be performed during the design phase in order to determine whether an adequate bond exists between the concrete or brick base and the existing asphalt, thereby addressing potential debonding before construction (section 5.12) [1]. If debonding of the existing overlay is later identified—or if it was not discovered by coring—the contract should contain a surface replacement item that allows removal of the entire depth of the compromised overlay, which addresses unexpected loss of adhesion (section 5.12) [1]. When an interlayer is placed, proper application and temperature of the asphalt material together with a correct tack‑coat rate are required to ensure waterproofing performance and to prevent debonding; this measure applies whenever an interlayer is installed and must consider existing pavement condition, overlay type and pavement temperature (section 5.12 b) [1]. If milling of the existing asphalt pavement has produced a rough surface, a leveling course may be required before the interlayer is placed so that adhesion can be achieved; this requirement applies only when milling creates such a surface (section 5.12 b) [1]. For self‑adhesive paving grids the manual mandates that a manufacturer‑approved tack coat or asphalt material shall be used in accordance with manufacturer requirements unless the grid and overlay are installed on the same day as the leveling course; this addresses potential non‑bonding of the grid (section 5.12 b) [1]. When an interlayer fails to adhere, the guidance calls for checking that the asphalt is not too cold or increasing the application rate, which serves as a corrective step specific to observed lack of bonding (section 5.12 b) [1]. In transition zones where an existing treated base course or concrete pavement lies directly beneath the unbonded concrete overlay, a geotextile layer shall be placed as a debonding layer to reduce unwanted adhesion between the overlay and the underlying material; this applies only to those transition areas identified in the project plans (section 548.3 u) [2]. Transverse saw‑cuts made over existing joints shall be cut to a width that is at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack, which is intended to prevent debonding of the bonded concrete overlay from the existing pavement; this applies when the overlay has attained sufficient strength to be sawn without significant raveling and after joint widths have been measured immediately prior to placement of the overlay/inlay (section 545.3) [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–121); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 466–474)

When milling an asphalt layer over a cement‑concrete or brick base, retain at least 1 ½ inches of the existing asphalt so that the material can retain its structural integrity and remain adequately bonded to the underlying base; this practice directly addresses debonding by preserving enough overlay thickness for bond continuity. Coring must be performed during the design phase to determine if an adequate bond exists between the concrete or brick base and the existing asphalt material, thereby detecting potential debonding before construction. Any debonded areas not discovered by coring shall be accounted for with a surface‑replacement item in the contract, ensuring that unbonded sections are remedied. Proper application temperature (and rate) of the asphalt material is required for waterproofing to perform and to prevent debonding whenever an interlayer tack coat or asphalt is placed. If milling of the existing asphalt pavement has been performed, a leveling course may be required before placing the interlayer, addressing debonding caused by a rough milled surface. For self‑adhesive paving grids, a manufacturer‑approved tack coat or asphalt material shall be used on the grid according to manufacturer requirements to prevent debonding between the grid and overlay. On interlayer projects where grades are steeper than 5 %, the asphalt mix should be proportioned for compaction and shear rut stability, which the source links to preventing debonding under steep‑grade conditions. A geotextile debonding layer shall be placed at concrete‑overlay transitions where the transition zone rests on an existing treated base course or concrete pavement, preventing loss of adhesion between the new overlay and the underlying surface. Thorough surface preparation—including scarifying, shot‑blasting or water‑blasting, removal of loose material, final cleaning no more than 30 minutes prior to paving, and maintaining the surface temperature below 120 °F through fog‑misting or protective covers—is mandated to eliminate contaminants and moisture that could cause loss of adhesion. Bond strength testing in accordance with ASTM C1583 shall be performed before overlay placement, and any area where the average of three test results is less than 175 psi or any individual result is under 160 psi shall be deemed defective, ensuring adequate adhesion. Transverse joints shall be saw‑cut to a width at least equal to the underlying joint or crack (or greater) after measuring joint widths immediately prior to placement, providing sufficient mechanical interlock across the joint and preventing debonding of a bonded concrete overlay. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–121); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 466–474)

Specification limits and acceptance

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10) Section 5.12 OVERLAYS specifies that at least 1 ½ inches of existing asphalt must be left in place to retain an adequate bond with a concrete or brick base [1]. The same manual states that if debonding of the overlay occurs, the entire depth of the existing asphalt overlay must be removed and accounted for with a surface‑replacement item in the contract [1]. In the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 Specifications, Section 545.3(e) of the Rigid Pavements specifications requires a bond‑strength test performed at seven days according to ASTM C1583 on five random locations per day’s placement (up to 3,000 yd²) [2]. The area is deemed defective for debonding when the average of three test results is below 175 psi and any individual result falls under 160 psi, which serves as the acceptance limit [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 466–469)

When debonding of an existing asphalt overlay from a concrete or brick base is identified, the specification requires that the entire depth of the asphalt overlay be removed and that a surface‑replacement item be provided for those areas not discovered by coring, as stipulated in the contract; the work is to be carried out under the contract provisions, and no specific time frame for completion is given. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)
