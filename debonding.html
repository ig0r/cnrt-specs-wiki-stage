Debonding

Part of: Bonded Concrete Overlay is-subtopic-of
Debonding is a topic under Bonded Concrete Overlay.
debonding
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Debonding is the loss of adhesion between an existing asphalt overlay and its underlying cement‑concrete or brick base, identified when coring performed during the design phase shows that an adequate bond does not exist [1][2]. The distress can also arise at the interface of an interlayer (tack coat or asphalt material) when the material is applied at improper temperature, with excessive tack‑coat rate, or over a rough milled surface, and it is aggravated by moisture, oil, grease, rapid cooling, or temperatures exceeding 120 °F [1][2]. Because the overlay cannot rely on a proper bond, the entire depth of the asphalt layer must be removed and accounted for as a surface‑replacement item in the contract, reflecting its inability to function as a structural layer [1][2].

Description and identification

Debonding is the loss of adhesion between an existing asphalt overlay and the underlying concrete or brick base, which is identified when coring performed during the design phase shows that an adequate bond does not exist; because the overlay cannot rely on a proper bond, the entire depth of that asphalt layer will require removing the entire depth of the existing asphalt overlay. The source notes that it is preferred that a minimum of 1 ½ inches of existing asphalt material be left in place to retain structural integrity and provide adequate bonding, but when debonding is discovered the full thickness must be stripped and accounted for with a surface‑replacement item in the contract. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

During the design phase, coring must be performed to determine if an adequate bond exists between the concrete or brick base and the existing asphalt material, as described in the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10) section 5.12 OVERLAYS. When coring reveals an inadequate bond, the condition is classified as debonding rather than other surface defects, according to the same source. Because debonding represents loss of adhesion, the repair approach differs—removing the entire depth of the affected asphalt overlay, whereas a properly bonded layer can remain with at least 1 ½ inches left in place to retain structural integrity, as stipulated in that manual. Areas where coring does not expose debonding are accounted for by including a surface‑replacement item in the contract, per the Pavement Policy Manual. In the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2020 Specifications section 545—BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF CONCRETE‑SURFACED PAVEMENTS, debonding in a bonded concrete overlay is identified by the mandatory 7‑day bond‑strength test prescribed in Section 545.3(e), which must be performed after surface preparation and before placing the overlay using ASTM C1583. An area is classified as debonded when the average of three test results falls below 175 psi while each individual result must not drop beneath 160 psi; such values trigger a defective‑work determination distinct from other distresses, according to that specification. Additionally, the specification requires saw‑cut widths to be at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack, providing a visual and dimensional cue that separates true debonding from merely insufficient joint preparation. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 455–457)

Where it occurs

Debonding is identified for composite pavements in which an existing asphalt overlay rests on a cement‑concrete or brick base; the design must incorporate coring to verify that the bond between the concrete (or brick) base and the asphalt is adequate, and if debonding is found the entire depth of the asphalt overlay must be removed. When milling a portion of such an asphalt layer over the rigid base, it is permissible to remove the whole asphalt layer, although the guidance prefers that at least 1 ½ inches of existing asphalt material remain so that the material retains structural integrity and stays adequately bonded. Debonding also occurs in asphalt overlay structures where an interlayer is placed over existing asphalt pavement, arising when the tack coat or asphalt material is applied incorrectly—such as when the asphalt is too cold, the application rate is insufficient, or a leveling course is omitted after milling. The same concern applies to self‑adhesive paving grid overlays that require a manufacturer‑approved tack coat. For bonded concrete overlay projects applied to an asphalt‑surfaced pavement—a composite pavement composed of an asphalt base and a cement‑concrete overlay—the specification mandates the placement of a geotextile debonding layer at the limits of the overlay where the transition rests on treated base course or underlying concrete. In bonded concrete overlays, including inlays, that are placed over existing concrete‑surfaced pavements, debonding is recognized as a potential distress especially at transverse joints; to prevent it the saw‑cut width shall be at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack, and any area failing the prescribed bond‑strength criteria is treated as defective in bond strength. For unbonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements, the specifications likewise require a geotextile debonding layer at overlay transitions and allow either a geotextile or an asphalt interlayer to serve as the separation layer between the existing slab and the new concrete. Both documents indicate that debonding concerns are limited to overlay structures and are not indicated for other pavement elements such as shoulders. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–121); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 446–462)

In the context of asphalt overlays, debonding is observed at the layer interface between an existing asphalt overlay and its underlying concrete or brick base [1]. The Interlayer Installation Guidance further specifies that debonding occurs where the newly placed interlayer fails to adhere to the underlying asphalt surface, i.e., at the interface between the interlayer (tack coat or asphalt material) and the existing pavement layer [1]. For bonded concrete overlays placed over concrete‑surfaced pavements, debonding is considered primarily at existing transverse and longitudinal joints and any underlying cracks, where saw cuts shall be made with a width at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack and joint backing material shall be installed within ±1/4 inch of the surface before overlay placement [2]. In addition, debonding is addressed at the transition zones of a bonded concrete overlay where the overlay meets an existing treated base course or underlying concrete pavement; in these locations a geotextile debonding layer shall be installed between them as required by Section 212 [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements, debonding is treated as occurring at the interface between the overlay and the existing pavement specifically at slab edges or transitions and at joints that exhibit faulting greater than ¼ inch; a geotextile separation layer shall be placed flat on the cleaned surface, overlapped by about 8 ± 2 inches and extended at least 4 inches beyond the pavement edge to provide the debonding layer [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–121); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 446–462)

Causes and mechanisms

Debonding is primarily linked to construction‑related factors such as the use of an overly heavy tack coat or the placement of an asphalt layer when ambient temperatures are high; these conditions can impair waterproofing performance and lead to loss of adhesion.[1] Temperature deficiencies, where the asphalt material is applied while too cold or where the application rate is insufficient, are also identified as causes of debonding because they reduce the ability of the interlayer to bond properly.[1] The guidance further notes that the condition of the existing pavement, the type of overlay, and the temperature of the pavement at placement influence performance, implying that design or construction deficiencies in material selection and application rates can contribute to debonding.[1] In the Pennsylvania specifications debonding is treated as a construction‑related deficiency; to prevent it the transverse joint saw cuts must be cut at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack in the existing pavement.[2] The specification also requires that standing water, oil, and grease be removed from the prepared surface before placement, recognizing that moisture and contaminants can cause loss of bond.[2] Bond‑strength testing is mandated, with an average of three tests required to be at least 175 psi and each individual test not less than 160 psi; failure to meet these values classifies the work as defective, indicating insufficient bond strength as a cause of debonding.[2] Finally, temperature control during curing is prescribed—insulating blankets must be provided when forecast air temperatures are 45 °F or lower and surface temperature changes must not exceed 40 °F within any one‑hour period—to avoid rapid cooling that can induce debonding.[2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 120–121); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 455–457)

Debonding develops when the adhesive connection between an existing asphalt overlay and its underlying cement‑concrete or brick base is insufficient; coring must be performed during the design phase to determine if an adequate bond exists, and if the bond fails the distress can progress to the point where the entire depth of the overlay must be removed. [1] The mechanism also includes failure of an interlayer to achieve adequate adhesion when the asphalt material or tack coat is applied at an improper temperature, is too cold, or is applied too heavily, which may cause slippage especially under high temperatures and compromises waterproofing performance. [1] Surface conditions such as a rough milled base can impede bonding and may require a leveling course before the interlayer is placed. [1] In bonded concrete overlays, debonding develops through loss of adhesion caused by inadequate mechanical interlock at transverse joints when the saw‑cut width does not equal or exceed the underlying joint or crack, allowing the new slab to separate from the existing pavement. [2] Poor surface preparation that leaves loose debris, standing water, oil or grease, combined with placement when the substrate temperature exceeds 120 °F or experiences a rapid cooling rate greater than 40 °F in one hour, reduces bond strength and predisposes the overlay to debonding. [2] Additionally, an average pull‑off test result below 175 psi together with any individual value under 160 psi is deemed defective and further accelerates separation of the overlay. [2] In unbonded concrete overlays that employ a geotextile interlayer, debonding develops when the interlayer is placed over an existing pavement that has not been fully prepared, creating a deliberate non‑adhesive interface; unstable slabs or slab fragments that move or rock visibly must be stabilized before the interlayer to prevent uneven support that can initiate separation. [2] The progression of debonding in this case is accelerated if surface defects larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch are left unfilled, if loose debris remains on the fabric, if construction traffic is allowed on the geotextile, or if the fabric’s surface temperature exceeds 120 °F before overlay placement. [2] The development of debonding in asphalt overlays is further accelerated when milling removes more than the preferred minimum of 1 ½ inches of existing asphalt, compromising structural integrity and reducing bonding capacity; exposure to high ambient temperatures, overspray of tack that can bleed through, and steep grades greater than 5 % also increase the rate at which the distress progresses. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–121); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 455–462)

Severity levels and rating

The condition rating for debonding is assigned by the bond‑strength testing procedure referenced in Section 545.3(e), which calls for ASTM C1583 tests at seven days. When the average of three test results is below 175 psi and each individual result fails to reach a minimum of 160 psi, the area is deemed defective, representing a severe debonding condition. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 455–457)

Measurement, survey and extent

Within Section 545—Bonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfaced Pavements, debonding is quantified by measuring bond strength in pounds per square inch using an ASTM C 1583 pull‑off test. After surface preparation and before overlay placement, specimens are tested at seven days; the inspector selects five random locations from each day’s lot, conducts three pull‑off tests at each location, records the psi values, and considers the area defective when the average of the three results is below 175 psi and any individual result is less than 160 psi. Within Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfaced Pavements, debonding is evaluated by measuring joint faulting as a vertical offset. The measurement is made by inspecting each joint for vertical displacement and comparing the observed offset to the prescribed limits: joints showing more than 1/4 inch of faulting require placement of a geotextile debonding layer, and if the faulting exceeds 1/2 inch a scratch course or milling must be performed to reduce the faulting. In addition, surface defects larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch are identified during this inspection and repaired before overlay placement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 455–462)

Under Section 545.3(e) Bond Strength, debonding is surveyed by conducting ASTM C1583 pull‑off tests seven days after surface preparation; for each day’s placement not exceeding 3,000 sq yd the Inspector selects five random locations (lot testing), making it a manual nondestructive test performed once per lot with point samples. If the average of three test results is below 175 psi and each individual result is at least 160 psi, the area is deemed defective and additional pull‑off tests are taken at 100‑ft intervals longitudinally from the defect; each trio of failing tests defines a debonded segment extending 50 ft in both directions, so extent is expressed as length of affected pavement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 455–457)

Effects on performance

When an asphalt overlay loses its bond to the underlying concrete or brick base, the loss of adhesion must be addressed because the debonded section cannot function as a structural layer; the source requires that the entire depth of the existing asphalt overlay be removed and that any undetected debonding areas be accounted for with a surface replacement item in the contract. Consequently, untreated debonding escalates to full‑depth removal of the overlay and additional surface replacement work. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

Repairs and treatments

When coring during design reveals loss of adhesion between an existing asphalt overlay and a concrete or brick base, the debonded area shall be removed in its entirety and treated as a surface‑replacement item in the contract, which also permits inclusion of debonded zones not discovered by coring. If fabric pickup occurs because of high ambient temperature or tack overspray, clean blotting sand or broadcast asphalt material shall be spread over the affected spot and excess removed before new asphalt placement. Proper application temperature and an adequate rate of the asphalt or tack coat are required to prevent loss of adhesion. When milling has produced a rough surface, a leveling course may be required prior to placing the interlayer. For self‑adhesive paving grids, a manufacturer‑approved tack coat or asphalt material shall be applied unless the grid and overlay are installed on the same day as the leveling course. In bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavements, any crack in the existing asphalt greater than three‑quarters of an inch shall be cleaned and filled with non‑shrink grout or another Department‑approved material so that the repaired spot can bond to the concrete overlay before final surface cleaning. At any transition where an existing treated base course or concrete pavement lies directly beneath a new concrete overlay, a geotextile debonding layer shall be placed as specified in Section 540.3(j). For bonded concrete overlays over concrete‑surfaced pavements, proper surface preparation—including scarifying, removal of loose material and a final shot‑ or water‑blast—to create a roughened substrate is required to ensure the overlay can bond to a clean, textured base. After this preparation, bond‑strength testing shall be performed; if the average of three ASTM C1583 results is below 175 psi and each individual test has a minimum strength of 160 psi, the area is deemed defective, addressing debonding by detecting insufficient adhesion. When transverse joints are saw‑cut, the cut width shall be at least the underlying joint or crack width to prevent debonding at those locations. If bond‑strength testing identifies a defect, the overlay shall be removed and replaced as defective work. For unbonded concrete overlays over concrete‑surfaced pavements, a geotextile interlayer shall be installed as a deliberate debonding membrane when transitions sit over existing treated base or concrete pavement directly beneath the overlay and also when joints exhibit faulting greater than one‑quarter inch that have been milled. An asphalt interlayer may be used to address separation at joints whose faulting exceeds one‑half inch, provided a scratch course or milling of the joint is performed before placing a one‑inch Superpave mix. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–121); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 446–462)

For projects where coring performed during design shows that the bond between an existing asphalt overlay and its concrete or brick base is inadequate—i.e., debonding of the existing asphalt overlay from the concrete or brick base—the manual requires removing the entire depth of that asphalt overlay and allowing for the debonded areas not discovered by coring with a surface replacement item in the contract. In concrete‑overlay transitions where the transition area contains an existing treated base course or existing concrete pavement directly beneath the overlay, a geotextile debonding layer may be placed as specified in Section 212. For bonded concrete overlays, when the average result of the three 7‑day bond‑strength tests is below 175 psi and any individual test falls under the minimum of 160 psi, the area is considered defective and must be removed and replaced. To prevent debonding at transverse joints in bonded concrete overlays, the saw‑cut width shall be at least the width of the underlying joint or crack (or greater) as required by Section 545.3(h). For unbonded concrete overlays, a geotextile interlayer may be installed only after joints that exhibit faulting greater than 1/4 inch are milled and any surface defects larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch have been filled. When an asphalt interlayer is used in unbonded concrete overlays, joint faulting that exceeds 1/2 inch requires a scratch course or milling/grinding of the joints prior to placement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 446–462)

Prevention in design and construction

For milling over cement concrete or brick bases, the manual prefers retaining at least 1 ½ inches of the existing asphalt layer so that the material keeps its structural integrity and remains adequately bonded to the underlying base; this preference applies specifically to milling operations on such bases (section 5.12) [1]. The same source requires coring during the design phase to determine whether an adequate bond exists between the concrete or brick base and the existing asphalt, thereby addressing potential debonding before construction (section 5.12) [1]. If debonding of the existing overlay is later identified—or if it was not discovered by coring—the contract should contain a surface replacement item that allows removal of the entire depth of the compromised overlay, which addresses unexpected loss of adhesion (section 5.12) [1]. When an interlayer is placed, proper application and temperature of the asphalt material together with a correct tack‑coat rate are required to ensure waterproofing performance and to prevent debonding; this measure applies whenever an interlayer is installed and must consider existing pavement condition, overlay type and pavement temperature (section 5.12 b) [1]. If milling of the existing asphalt pavement has produced a rough surface, a leveling course may be required before the interlayer is placed so that adhesion can be achieved; this requirement applies only when milling creates such a surface (section 5.12 b) [1]. For self‑adhesive paving grids, a manufacturer‑approved tack coat or asphalt material shall be used in accordance with manufacturer requirements unless the grid and overlay are installed on the same day as the leveling course; this addresses potential non‑bonding of the grid (section 5.12 b) [1]. When an interlayer fails to adhere, the guidance calls for checking that the asphalt is not too cold or increasing the application rate, which serves as a corrective step specific to observed lack of bonding (section 5.12 b) [1]. For asphalt‑surfaced pavements covered by Section 540, the specification requires comprehensive surface preparation before the overlay is placed, including milling, cleaning, filling cracks larger than three‑quarters of an inch with non‑shrink grout, removal of all loose debris, oil, grease and standing water, protection from contaminants, and confirming that the pavement temperature is less than 120 °F no more than thirty minutes prior to placement; these actions are intended to preserve adhesion between the existing asphalt and the concrete overlay and thereby prevent debonding (Section 540) [2]. Section 540.3(j) also mandates the placement of a geotextile as a debonding layer at transition zones where an existing treated base course or concrete pavement lies directly beneath the new overlay, providing a controlled separation that mitigates unwanted bond‑related distress (Section 540) [2]. For concrete‑surfaced pavements addressed in Section 545, the design calls for thorough surface preparation—scarifying followed by shot‑blasting or water‑blasting to produce a roughened clean substrate—to eliminate loose debris and contaminants that could cause loss of adhesion (Section 545) [2]. After this preparation, bond‑strength testing according to ASTM C1583 must be performed before placement; an area is considered defective if the average strength of three tests is below 175 psi or any individual test falls under 160 psi, ensuring sufficient interlayer bonding and reducing the likelihood of debonding (Section 545) [2]. When transverse joints are cut over existing joints, the saw‑cut width must be at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack (or greater), a requirement intended to prevent the overlay from separating from the substrate (Section 545) [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays covered by Section 548, the specification requires installing a geotextile interlayer as a debonding layer at transition zones where an existing treated base course or concrete slab underlies the new overlay; the geotextile is placed flat on a repaired and cleaned surface and overlapped as required before concrete placement, thereby providing separation that prevents delamination of the overlay (Section 548) [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–121); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 446–462)

The Pavement Policy Manual requires that when milling an asphalt layer over a cement‑concrete or brick base, at least 1½ inches of the existing asphalt be retained so that the material retains structural integrity and remains adequately bonded to the underlying base; this practice directly addresses debonding by preserving enough overlay thickness for bond continuity (Section 5.12 b) [1]. The same manual requires coring during the design phase to verify an adequate bond exists between the concrete or brick base and the existing asphalt, thereby detecting potential debonding before construction (Section 5.12 b) [1]. Areas where debonded sections are not identified by coring shall be accounted for with a surface‑replacement item in the contract (Section 5.12 b) [1]. Proper application temperature and rate of asphalt material are required to achieve waterproofing and thus prevent debonding whenever an interlayer tack coat or asphalt is placed (Section 5.12 b) [1]. If milling of the existing asphalt pavement has been performed, a leveling course may be required before placing the interlayer, addressing debonding caused by a rough milled surface (Section 5.12 b) [1]. For self‑adhesive paving grids, a manufacturer‑approved tack coat or asphalt material shall be used on the grid according to manufacturer requirements to prevent debonding between the grid and overlay (Section 5.12 b) [1]. On interlayer projects with grades steeper than 5 %, the asphalt mix should be proportioned for compaction and shear rut stability, which the source links to preventing debonding under steep‑grade conditions (Section 5.12 b) [1]. For a bonded concrete overlay over an asphalt‑surfaced pavement, Section 540.3(e) requires that the existing asphalt be milled, cracks larger than three‑quarters inch be filled with approved grout, all standing water be eliminated, and the surface be thoroughly cleaned no more than thirty minutes before concrete placement while keeping the temperature below 120 °F; these actions are intended to secure a proper bond between the asphalt and the concrete overlay and thus prevent debonding at the interface [2]. Where a transition rests on existing treated base course or concrete pavement, Section 540.3(j) mandates installation of a geotextile debonding layer beneath the overlay, addressing potential debonding at transition zones [2]. For bonded concrete overlays over concrete‑surfaced pavements, Section 545.3(b) specifies surface preparation that includes scarifying, shot or water blasting and a final cleaning that removes all loose debris, oil, grease and other contaminants, with the pavement temperature kept below 120 °F immediately before placement; this creates a clean, roughened substrate to prevent loss of adhesion [2]. Section 545.3(h) requires transverse joints to be saw‑cut to a width no less than the underlying joint or crack, ensuring sufficient separation for bond integrity and thereby preventing debonding [2]. Section 545.3(e) calls for a bond‑strength test per ASTM C1583 after preparation and before placing the overlay, requiring an average of at least 175 psi and each individual result not less than 160 psi; any area failing these limits shall be treated as defective to prevent loss of adhesion [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays over concrete‑surfaced pavements, Section 548.3(e) requires that unstable slabs be repaired or stabilized, shallow defects larger than 4 in² × 1 in deep be filled with approved patch material, and all loose debris be swept away before any interlayer is placed, thereby ensuring a uniform, clean substrate for bonding [2]. Section 548.3(3)(a) mandates placement of a geotextile interlayer on the repaired surface with overlaps of 8 inches ± 2 inches, no more than three layers, edge extension at least 4 inches beyond the pavement, and that the fabric be kept dry and its temperature not exceed 120 °F; these conditions maintain continuous contact and drainage to avoid debonding at joints faulted greater than ¼ inch [2]. When an asphalt interlayer is used, Section 548.3(3)(b) calls for a one‑inch Superpave mix and requires that joint faulting exceeding ½ inch be reduced by a scratch course or milling, with the pavement surface temperature not exceeding 120 °F before placement; these measures are intended to secure bond at high‑fault joints [2]. At overlay transitions where existing treated base or concrete lies directly beneath, Section 548.3(u) specifies that a geotextile debonding layer shall be installed, providing a controlled separation to prevent unintended adhesion failures [2].

Specification limits and acceptance

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) Section 5.12 OVERLAYS specifies a minimum of 1 ½ inches of existing asphalt material must be left in place to retain an adequate bond with a concrete or brick base; if debonding of the overlay occurs, the entire depth of the existing asphalt overlay must be removed and accounted for with a surface‑replacement item in the contract. [1] The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2020 Specifications, Section 545—BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF CONCRETE‑SURFACED PAVEMENTS, subsection 545.3(e) requires bond strength to be evaluated by ASTM C1583 testing at seven days, and the average of three test results must not be less than 175 psi, with each individual test required to reach at least 160 psi; failure to meet these values classifies the area as defective. [2] The same subsection also mandates that saw‑cut widths be measured before overlay placement and be at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack in order to prevent debonding. [2]
