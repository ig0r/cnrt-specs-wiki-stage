Corrective Maintenance

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Corrective Maintenance is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
corrective-maintenance
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Corrective Maintenance is a reactive pavement‑preservation strategy that is invoked when a localized deficiency threatens the safe and efficient operation of a facility or jeopardizes the future integrity of the pavement section [1]. Its purpose is to return the surface to an acceptable level of service by repairing damage—such as potholes, edge failures, grade separations on flexible pavements, isolated joint problems on rigid pavements, or defects that exceed pervious‑asphalt tolerances—rather than providing a preventive or life‑extending treatment [1][2]. The process works by inspecting the pavement for conditions that surpass defined limits (for example surface irregularities greater than 3/8 inch, depth deficiencies of one‑half inch or more, or infiltration rates below 200 inches per hour) and then executing targeted repairs like patching, joint replacement, or full‑width slab removal to bring the treated area back into compliance with the construction specifications [2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Corrective Maintenance is applied when a pavement develops a deficiency that threatens safe and efficient operation or future integrity, requiring a reactive repair to bring the surface back to an acceptable level of service [1]. For flexible pavements the typical distress includes potholes and other localized deterioration such as edge failures or grade separations along shoulders [1]. For rigid pavements corrective actions may involve joint replacement or full‑width, full‑depth slab replacement at isolated locations [1]. In pervious asphalt pavement systems the treatment is triggered when measured defects exceed the tolerances set in Section 420.3, specifically surface irregularities greater than 3/8‑inch after compaction, depth deficiencies of one‑half inch or more, an average infiltration rate below 200 inches per hour, asphalt content or coated‑aggregate percentage less than 95 %, or a sieve‑size proportion exceeding 4 % passing No. 200 [2]. Under those conditions the defective area must be removed and replaced according to the specified procedures [2]. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete slabs corrective maintenance is used when a slab has become damaged or cracked, including manufacturing imperfections, handling damage, or construction‑related damage that compromises the top surface and edges; each instance shall be inspected on a case‑by‑case basis and may halt further installation until the cause of recurring distress is identified and mitigated [2].

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 302–303), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Corrective Maintenance is a reactive strategy that is triggered when a deficiency appears that threatens safe, efficient operation or the future integrity of the pavement, and its purpose is to bring the pavement back to an acceptable level of service by repairing the damage—such as potholes, localized edge failures, or joint problems—thereby restoring functionality rather than preventing further deterioration or extending overall service life. Corrective maintenance is intended to restore the pavement to an acceptable functional condition by repairing damage that exceeds the limits set in the construction specifications. For pervious‑asphalt pavements, irregularities greater than 3/8 inch are corrected, depth deficiencies of 1/2 inch or more are removed and replaced, and infiltration rates below the minimum average of 200 inches per hour are brought back into compliance, returning the surface to its required tolerance, depth, and performance criteria. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete slabs, corrective maintenance is directed toward repairing manufacturing imperfections, handling damage, or construction‑related cracks so that the slab can function as intended; each instance of damage is inspected on a case‑by‑case basis, and recurring distress may require halting installation until the underlying cause is identified and mitigated. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 302–303), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Applicability and treatment selection

Corrective maintenance is intended for reactive repairs of localized deficiencies such as potholes, edge failures, or isolated joint replacements; it is not the preferred treatment when the pavement condition requires broader or structural interventions. When a pavement is still in good condition with ample remaining service life, preventive‑maintenance actions like crack sealing or thin overlays are more appropriate, while age‑related surface cracking that is not confined to a single spot calls for minor rehabilitation techniques. If the distress involves loss of structural capacity or demands an increase in load‑carrying ability, major rehabilitation or full reconstruction should be selected instead of corrective maintenance, and catastrophic events that reduce service to a minimum level trigger catastrophic maintenance until a permanent solution can be designed. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213)

Condition assessment and timing

To decide if corrective maintenance is needed on a pervious asphalt pavement, the Representative must inspect surface tolerance with a 12‑foot straightedge and treat any irregularities that exceed three‑eighths of an inch as defective; depth is verified by drilling one six‑inch diameter test hole for each 3,000 square yards (or less) of completed base course and measuring any deficiency of one‑half inch or more; infiltration capacity is assessed on three areas per 10,000 square feet in accordance with ASTM C 1701, and any section that does not meet a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour must be removed and replaced. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 302–303)

According to section 12.7 of the Pavement Policy Manual, corrective maintenance should be applied when a deficiency has developed that negatively impacts the safe, efficient operation of the facility and threatens the future integrity of the pavement section; it is a reactive response to unforeseen conditions rather than a proactive strategy [1]. Typical triggers for flexible pavements under this guidance include observable failures such as potholes, localized deterioration such as edge failures or grade separations on shoulders, which are addressed to restore an acceptable level of service [1]. For rigid pavements the same source cites isolated joint replacement or full‑width slab replacement at specific locations as examples of corrective maintenance actions [1]. In the pervious asphalt pavement system specifications, corrective maintenance is required when measurable defects exceed defined thresholds; any surface irregularity greater than 3/8‑inch after compaction must be corrected immediately [2]. Sections that are deficient in depth by 1/2‑inch or more must be removed and replaced, and pervious pavement that does not achieve the minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour also triggers removal and replacement [2]. Additional deficiencies noted in the specification such as inadequate surface tolerance, insufficient asphalt content, coated aggregate less than 95 % or sieve passing greater than the 4 % limit are likewise considered defective and must be corrected [2].

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 302–303)

Materials and repair details

Corrective Maintenance is performed reactively to restore a pavement when deficiencies such as potholes, edge failures, or grade separations develop on flexible pavements and when isolated joint problems or slab damage occur on rigid pavements; the required repair actions include filling potholes, patching localized deterioration, replacing joints, or performing full‑width and full‑depth slab replacement at the affected locations [1]. For pervious asphalt pavement corrective maintenance, any surface irregularity exceeding three‑eighths of an inch shall be immediately corrected by loosening the surface mixture and adding or removing pervious paving material without grinding or milling [2]. Sections whose depth is deficient by one‑half inch or more shall be removed and replaced until the depth is within one‑half inch of the indicated level [2]. A test hole of six‑inch diameter shall be drilled to the full depth of the pervious course, backfilled with material acceptable to the Representative, and compacted flush with the finished course [2]. The replacement pavement must satisfy the same acceptance criteria as new work, including at least 95 % coated aggregate particles, no more than 4 % passing the No. 200 sieve, and an infiltration rate of at least 200 inches per hour [2]. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, corrective maintenance requires a concrete mix that shall meet a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a minimum flexural strength of 650 psi, and each slab shall be cast one‑quarter inch thicker than the nominal design thickness [2]. Slabs shall be limited to a maximum length of 15 feet, or 20 feet when prestressed, and a minimum length of 6 feet, with widths determined by longitudinal joint locations [2]. Transverse joints between slabs shall not exceed one‑half inch and shall be at least one‑quarter inch along their entire length; longitudinal joints shall not exceed three‑quarters inch, and vertical differentials across any joint shall not exceed one‑quarter inch (or up to one‑half inch before diamond grinding) [2]. The slabs must incorporate load‑transfer dowels, tie bars, slots, grout ports, foam gaskets or setting‑bolt hardware as shown on the shop drawings, with dowels coated with a bond breaker and lifting devices recessed at least one inch below the surface [2]. Repair of damaged or cracked slabs shall follow the prescribed patching of dowel slots, placement of rapid‑setting grout to achieve a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic, and removal or grinding of any slab that exceeds the vertical differential limits [2].

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 302–303), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

The success of corrective maintenance on pervious asphalt pavement systems is governed by the condition of the aggregate coating, the gradation of the mix, and the ability of the surface to accept water. A segment is deemed defective when the percentage of coated‑aggregate particles falls below 95 %, when more than 4 % of the mixture passes the No. 200 sieve, or when the measured infiltration rate does not reach the minimum average of 200 in/hr. In addition, surface irregularities that exceed 3/8 inch and depth deficiencies of a half‑inch or greater require removal and replacement to restore an acceptable condition. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, corrective maintenance performance depends on the slab material properties and on strict compatibility measures. Each mix must achieve at least a 4,925 psi compressive strength and a 650 psi flexural strength, be fabricated 1/4 inch thicker than the nominal design thickness, and receive the specified surface texture. Compatibility requirements include incorporating an appropriate thermal‑expansion coefficient in the concrete, coating the portion of the dowels cast into the precast slab with bond breaker, locating load‑transfer dowels and tie bars within ±1/8 inch of the drawing locations, providing foam gaskets where required, and maintaining joint gaps between 1/4 inch and 1/2 inch (with longitudinal joints not exceeding 3/4 inch) and vertical differentials no greater than 1/4 inch. Existing pavement conditions that affect treatment success are a firm, even subbase surface within ±½ inch of grade, bedding material graded to within 1/8 inch over each slab length, and dowel and bedding grouts that must reach at least a 2,500 psi compressive strength before traffic is permitted. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 302–303), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Treatment procedures

To perform corrective maintenance on a pervious asphalt pavement the crew first identifies defects by testing surface tolerance with a straightedge and drilling 6‑inch diameter depth test holes as required; any irregularity exceeding 3/8‑inch is immediately corrected by loosening the surface mix and removing or adding pervious paving course, while sections that are deficient in depth by 1/2‑inch or more are removed and replaced longitudinally and transversely until the depth is within ½‑inch of the indicated level; test holes are backfilled with material acceptable to the Representative and compacted flush with the completed course, and any area that fails the infiltration requirement of a minimum average 200 inches per hour is also removed and replaced. No curing or additional finishing steps are specified. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 302–303)

Implementation of corrective maintenance using precast concrete pavement slabs requires a defined set of equipment and sequencing steps, as well as adherence to environmental tolerances and traffic control measures. Required equipment includes lifting devices that meet Section 1105 specifications together with cranes or excavators sized for the slab weight, rigging, delivery‑truck routes, laser‑controlled screeding devices for fine grading, setting bolts, strongback beams, and grout mixing/placement gear; the installation plan must detail these items at least 21 calendar days before work begins. Sequencing proceeds through a precast pavement planning meeting, a pre‑fabrication meeting, a pre‑placement meeting, followed by surveying, full‑depth diamond sawcutting, lift‑out removal of existing pavement, subbase preparation to within ±½ inch of grade, slab leveling (using setting bolts, polyurethane injection, or strongback beams), placement of dowels and tie bars, grout placement, and finally traffic opening only after the dowel and bedding grouts achieve a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi. Environmental constraints require that the final slab surface not vary more than 1⁄8 inch over a slab length and that vertical joint differentials not exceed ¼ inch (or ½ inch before grinding), while traffic restrictions limit any temporary opening before grout to no more than three days and mandate shims in ungrouted joints to prevent slab movement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Quality and workmanship

Corrective Maintenance must verify for pervious asphalt pavement systems that longitudinal joints are placed so the new course overlaps the edge of the previously placed lane by one to two inches and that transverse joints are formed at the end of each workday or after any interruption longer than thirty minutes using a full‑depth vertical bulkhead or saw cut. The finished surface is inspected with a twelve‑foot straightedge; any irregularity greater than three‑eighths of an inch must be corrected before final compaction and any remaining defects after compaction require removal and replacement. Depth uniformity is checked by drilling one six‑inch diameter test hole to the full course thickness for each three thousand square yards, and any section that is deficient by one‑half inch or more is removed and replaced. Selected areas are subjected to infiltration testing and must show an average rate of at least two hundred inches per hour. The pavement material is also examined to confirm that asphalt content meets specification, that coated aggregate particles constitute at least ninety‑five percent of the mix, and that sieve passing for No. 200 does not exceed four percent. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, Corrective Maintenance should verify that the submitted concrete mix designs are accompanied by trial batch test data demonstrating compliance with the minimum required compressive and flexural strength. Each slab’s fabricated dimensions must fall within the tolerances listed in Table A, and the subbase surface must be shaped to a firm, even plane within plus or minus one‑half inch of the specified grade. Vertical differentials across joints must not exceed one‑quarter inch and transverse joint widths must be between one‑quarter inch and one‑half inch along their entire length. Dowel and bedding grouts are tested and must achieve a minimum compressive strength of two thousand five hundred pounds per square inch before the pavement is opened to traffic. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 302–303), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

For pervious asphalt pavement systems, surface tolerance requires that any irregularity exceeding 3/8‑inch be immediately corrected by loosening, removing or adding pervious paving; if such irregularities remain after final compaction the area is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced. Depth verification dictates that sections deficient by 1/2‑inch or more must be removed and replaced until the depth is within 1/2‑inch of the indicated depth. Infiltration performance specifies that pervious asphalt pavement that does not meet a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour is considered defective and must be removed and replaced. Defective work also renders pavement unacceptable if the percent of coated aggregate particles is less than 95% or if the percent passing the No. 200 sieve exceeds the 4% maximum, requiring removal and replacement. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, acceptance is achieved when all dimensional and joint tolerances are met: load transfer dowels and tie bars must be placed within ±1/8 inch of the shop drawing locations; slab vertical differentials across any joint must not exceed 1/4 inch; transverse joint widths must stay between a minimum of 1/4 inch and a maximum of 1/2 inch, and longitudinal joints may not exceed 3/4 inch; the prepared subbase surface must be within ±1/2 inch of grade and bedding variations no greater than 1/8 inch over the length of each slab; and all dowel and bedding grouts must attain at least 2,500 psi compressive strength before traffic is permitted. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 302–303), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Performance and service life

Corrective Maintenance is a reactive approach that addresses deficiencies once they have developed and are affecting the facility’s performance[1]. For flexible pavements, recurring distresses commonly treated under corrective maintenance include potholes, localized pavement deterioration such as edge failures and shoulder grade separations, while for rigid pavements isolated joint or full‑width slab failures are typical examples[1]. These deficiencies develop and negatively impact safe, efficient operations of the facility, prompting repair actions that restore an acceptable level of service[1]. In pervious asphalt pavement systems, typical corrective‑maintenance failures are surface irregularities that exceed three‑eighths of an inch, depth deficiencies of a half‑inch or more, infiltration rates that fall below two hundred inches per hour, and insufficient coating of aggregate particles when less than ninety‑five percent is achieved[2]. Such distresses develop when compaction is uneven, joints are not properly formed after daily breaks, contaminating fines enter the mix, or the installed thickness does not meet design limits, causing the pavement to be classified as defective and requiring removal and replacement under the construction specifications[2]. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, common recurring distresses include manufacturing imperfections or handling damage to the top surface and edges, vertical differentials across joints that exceed one‑quarter inch, joint widths outside the required transverse range of one‑quarter to one‑half inch or greater than three‑quarters inch longitudinally, and grout that has not reached the minimum compressive strength of two thousand five hundred pounds per square inch before traffic is allowed[2]. The specification requires that finished vertical differentials shall not exceed one‑quarter inch, transverse joint widths must be at least one‑quarter inch and shall not exceed one‑half inch, and traffic may not be permitted until grout attains the two thousand five hundred psi threshold; failures are typically triggered by inadequate bedding preparation, improper placement tolerances, premature opening to traffic before dowel or bedding grout has hardened, or insufficient sealing of dowel slots, which can cause slab settlement and edge differentials greater than the allowable limits[2]. Recurring damage or distress in these slabs is cause for discontinuing installation until the underlying cause is identified and mitigated[2].

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 302–303), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)

Monitoring and follow-up

After corrective maintenance the treated pavement must be inspected by testing surface tolerance with a 12‑foot straightedge at locations the Representative suspects are irregular and at transverse joints; any irregularity exceeding 3/8‑inch is to be corrected immediately, while irregularities that develop after compaction are addressed by a method that does not produce contaminating fines or damage the base. Depth verification requires digging one 6‑inch diameter test hole for each 3,000 square yards of completed course (or additional holes where deficiency is suspected) and confirming the depth is within ½‑inch of the specified value before backfilling. Infiltration performance is monitored by testing three selected areas per 10,000 square feet in accordance with ASTM C 1701 and documenting that the average infiltration rate does not fall below 200 inches per hour. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 302–303)

For pervious asphalt pavement systems, the area treated with corrective maintenance should be re‑evaluated whenever surface irregularities exceed three‑eighths of an inch, depth measurements fall short by a half‑inch or more, infiltration performance drops below the required 200 inches per hour, coated‑aggregate content is under 95 percent, sieve‑passing percentage exceeds the four‑percent maximum for the No. 200 sieve, or any irregularities remain after final compaction; when any of these conditions are observed the pavement is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced according to the specifications. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, any vertical differential that exceeds one‑quarter inch across a joint—or approaches the upper limit of one‑half inch—signals that the slab must be re‑graded, reset, or diamond‑grinded before further service, and joint gaps wider than one‑half inch (or narrower than one‑quarter inch) together with temporary drop‑off conditions greater than two inches also require additional corrective action such as shimming, wedge transition, or joint sealing; if ungrouted slabs develop edge differentials over one‑quarter inch under traffic, or if recurring damage or distress is noted, the work must be halted and remedial repair performed before reopening to traffic. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 302–303), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 428–435)
