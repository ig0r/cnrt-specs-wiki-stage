Corrective Maintenance

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Corrective Maintenance is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
corrective-maintenance
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Corrective Maintenance is a reactive pavement‑preservation strategy that is initiated when a deficiency emerges which threatens the safe, efficient operation of the facility or compromises the future integrity of the pavement section[1][2]. Its objective is to repair damage that has already occurred—such as potholes, localized edge failures, joint problems, spalls, or cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third of slab thickness—and to restore the surface to an acceptable functional condition so service can resume[1][2]. The treatment is limited to isolated defects; it restores functionality by removing and replacing the defective portion in accordance with defined tolerances rather than attempting to prevent further deterioration or extend overall service life[1][2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Corrective Maintenance is invoked when a pavement exhibits a deficiency that threatens safe and efficient operation of the facility or compromises future integrity of the pavement section. For flexible pavements, the typical distress prompting such reactive repair includes potholes and other localized deterioration such as edge failures or grade separations along shoulders. For rigid pavements, isolated locations may require joint replacement or full‑width, full‑depth slab replacement to restore acceptability. In the case of pervious asphalt pavement, corrective maintenance is required when defects exceed the tolerances defined in Section 420.3, specifically surface irregularities greater than 3/8‑inch after compaction, depth shortfalls of at least ½‑inch, infiltration rates below the minimum average of 200 inches per hour (or ASTM C1701 values less than 200 inches per hour), or material deficiencies such as insufficient coated aggregate or excessive fine content. Each identified defect in a pervious asphalt system must be removed and replaced to return the pavement to an acceptable condition. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete slabs, corrective maintenance is triggered by damage such as spalling or cracking; spalls are repaired according to Section 501.3(t). Cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third of the slab thickness (D/3) require full slab replacement, while cracks wider than 0.007 inches but shallower than D/3 are repaired following the latest ACI 224R recommendations; very fine cracks that are 0.007 inches wide or less are sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate before traffic is allowed. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 310–311), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Corrective maintenance is described as a reactive strategy that is initiated when a deficiency appears that threatens safe, efficient operation or the future integrity of the pavement [1]. Its objective is to repair damage that has already occurred—such as potholes, localized edge failures, joint problems, spalls, or cracks wider than 0.007 inch and deeper than one‑third of slab depth—and to return the surface to an acceptable functional condition so that service can be resumed [1][2]. The actions are intended solely to restore functionality; they are not aimed at preventing further deterioration, slowing its progression, or extending the overall service life of the pavement [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 310–311), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Applicability and treatment selection

Corrective maintenance is intended for reactive repairs of localized deficiencies such as potholes, edge failures, or isolated joint replacements and is not the preferred treatment when the pavement condition requires broader or structural interventions. When a pavement is still in good condition with ample remaining service life, preventive‑maintenance actions like crack sealing or thin overlays are more appropriate. Age‑related surface cracking that is not confined to a single spot calls for minor rehabilitation techniques rather than corrective maintenance. If the distress involves loss of structural capacity or demands an increase in load‑carrying ability, major rehabilitation or full reconstruction should be selected instead of corrective maintenance. Catastrophic events that reduce service to a minimum level trigger catastrophic maintenance until a permanent solution can be designed. For precast concrete slabs, corrective maintenance is considered unsuitable when a crack exceeds 0.007 inches in width and its depth is greater than one‑third of the slab thickness (D/3); in that case the specification requires replacement of the entire slab. Cracks wider than 0.007 inches but shallower than D/3 may be repaired according to ACI 224R, while cracks 0.007 inches wide or less are sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate, and spalls are addressed per Section 501.3(t). If recurring damage or distress is observed, installation must be stopped until the cause is identified and mitigated, indicating that a different rehabilitation or preservation strategy should be pursued. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Condition assessment and timing

To decide if corrective maintenance is needed on a pervious asphalt pavement, the Representative must perform surface‑tolerance inspections with a 12‑foot straightedge and immediately address any irregularities that exceed three‑eighths of an inch; depth verification is done by drilling a six‑inch diameter test hole to the full course thickness for each area up to three thousand square yards and measuring any deficiency of one‑half inch or more; infiltration capacity is evaluated on three locations per ten thousand square feet in accordance with ASTM C1701, and any section that does not meet a minimum average infiltration rate of two hundred inches per hour is considered defective and must be removed and replaced. These inspections together identify the exact spots where corrective repairs such as loosening, adding material, or full replacement are required. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 310–311)

Corrective Maintenance should be applied when a deficiency has developed that negatively impacts the safe, efficient operation of the facility and threatens the future integrity of the pavement section; it is a reactive response to unforeseen conditions rather than a proactive strategy, with typical triggers including observable failures such as potholes, localized pavement deterioration like edge failures or grade separations on shoulders for flexible pavements, and isolated joint replacement or full‑width slab replacement for rigid pavements, all aimed at restoring an acceptable level of service [1]. For pervious asphalt pavement systems, corrective maintenance is required whenever defects exceed the limits set out in Section 420.3, specifically when surface irregularities are greater than 3/8‑inch after compaction, depth deficiencies are ½‑inch or more, infiltration rates fall below the minimum average of 200 inches per hour, coated‑aggregate content drops below 95 %, or the percent passing the No. 200 sieve exceeds the 4 % maximum; in such cases the affected area must be removed and replaced according to an approved corrective action plan [2]. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, corrective maintenance is mandated when cracking meets the limits of Section 529.3: cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third of the slab thickness (D/3) call for full‑slab replacement, cracks wider than 0.007 inches but shallower than D/3 are to be repaired following the latest ACI 224R recommendations, and very fine cracks of 0.007 inches or less are sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate after cleaning and sand topping before traffic is permitted [2].

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 310–311), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Materials and repair details

Corrective maintenance on flexible pavements is performed reactively to restore a pavement when deficiencies such as potholes, edge failures, or grade separations develop, and the required repair actions include filling potholes, patching localized deterioration, and replacing joints or performing full‑width and full‑depth slab replacement at the affected locations for rigid pavements. For pervious asphalt pavement the longitudinal joint is formed by overlapping the newly placed course 1 inch to 2 inches over the previous lane edge, and if work stops for more than a half‑hour a temporary vertical bulkhead must create a full‑depth transverse joint. Any surface irregularity that exceeds 3/8‑inch must be corrected immediately by loosening the mixture and adding or removing pervious paving course without grinding. Depth deficiencies of 1/2‑inch or more are remedied by removing and replacing the deficient section, and any required 6‑inch test holes shall be backfilled with material acceptable to the Representative and compacted flush. Areas that fail to achieve a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour, or that do not meet coating or sieve specifications, must also be removed and replaced, and all repairs are performed using the same pervious paving materials approved for construction. Corrective maintenance of precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs requires the slab to be fabricated 1/4‑inch thicker than the nominal design thickness, with lengths not exceeding 15 feet (20 feet if prestressed) and no less than 6 feet, while widths are set by the longitudinal joint layout. The top surface texture shall be broom, turf‑drag, or longitudinal/transverse tine finished as specified. Load‑transfer dowels and tie bars are cast square to the edge and parallel to the slab surface within ±1/8 inch of the shop‑drawing locations, and slots for these elements are also placed within ±1/8 inch. Lifting devices must be recessed at least 1 inch below the slab surface and any exposed device shall be filled with an accepted cementitious grout; dowel portions cast into the slab are to be coated with a bond breaker, and foam gaskets or slab‑setting bolt hardware are installed only if required by the manufacturer. For repair of concrete slabs, spalls are treated according to Section 501.3(t), cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third the slab depth require slab replacement, while narrower cracks are sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate followed about 20 minutes later by a dry‑blast sand finish. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 310–311), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Corrective maintenance on pervious asphalt pavements will be effective only when the existing pavement meets the material and condition limits set out in Section 420.3; surface irregularities greater than 3/8‑inch must be corrected before final compaction, depth deficiencies of 1/2‑inch or more require removal and replacement, the mixture must contain at least 95 % coated aggregate particles and must not exceed a 4 % passing rate on the No. 200 sieve, and the infiltration rate must be at least 200 inches per hour. When any of these thresholds are exceeded the pavement is considered defective and corrective maintenance must involve removal and replacement of the deficient area. The performance of corrective maintenance on full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs depends on several material and condition criteria specified in Section 529.3, including a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a minimum flexural strength of 650 psi for the slab mix, a sacrificial thickness of 1/4 inch that is not counted in design calculations, and joint tolerances that limit vertical differentials to 1/4‑inch (or up to 1/2‑inch only if diamond‑grinded). Existing pavement must be prepared to a firm, even subbase within ±½ inch of grade and any soft or wet material must be removed before slab placement. Crack repair limits are also defined: cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third the slab depth require full slab replacement, while narrower or shallower cracks may be sealed with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate after cleaning. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 310–311), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Treatment procedures

Corrective maintenance for pervious asphalt pavement systems is initiated by a visual and dimensional inspection using a twelve‑foot straightedge; any surface irregularity that exceeds three‑eighths inch must be immediately corrected by loosening the surface mixture and removing or adding pervious paving course, with grinding or milling avoided to prevent contaminating fines. When depth deficiencies of one‑half inch or more are identified, the deficient sections shall be removed and replaced, and the correction is extended longitudinally and transversely until the depth is within one‑half inch of the specified level; test holes created during this process are backfilled with acceptable material and compacted flush with the finished course. After the repair, the area is re‑tested for surface tolerance and infiltration, and any pavement that does not meet a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour must be removed and replaced as defective work. Corrective maintenance for full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs begins with a survey to establish the theoretical elevations, followed by full‑depth diamond saw‑cutting along the limits of the existing slab and the proposed longitudinal joints. The cut sections are then removed by the lift‑out method, which employs lifting devices to extract the concrete without disturbing the subbase. The exposed subbase is cleaned, unsuitable material is removed, and No. 2A coarse aggregate is compacted to within plus or minus one‑half inch of the required grade; a laser‑controlled screed subsequently creates a bedding surface whose variation does not exceed one‑eighth inch over each slab length. Each precast slab is positioned to the line and grade shown on the working drawings using setting bolts, polyurethane leveling, or strongback beams so that the tolerances of Section 529.3(f)6‑7 are satisfied; dowel bars and tie bars are installed with a bond‑breaker coating and joint gaps are maintained between one‑quarter inch and one‑half inch transversely and not more than three‑quarters inch longitudinally. Grouts for dowels, tie bars, and bedding are placed through designated ports and must attain a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is permitted, and vertical differentials across joints that exceed one‑quarter inch shall be corrected. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 310–311), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Corrective maintenance on pervious asphalt pavement systems must be sequenced so that surface irregularities greater than three‑eighths inch are corrected immediately by loosening the surface mix and removing or adding pervious paving course; any irregularities that appear after compaction must be treated with a method that does not generate contaminating fines or damage the base, and grinding or milling is prohibited. Depth deficiencies of one‑half inch or more require removal and replacement beginning at the identified point and extending longitudinally and transversely until the depth is within one‑half inch of the specified level; this section provides no explicit equipment requirements, environmental condition limits, or traffic restriction guidance. Corrective maintenance for full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs requires that lifting devices meeting Section 1105 specifications and appropriate cranes, excavators, diamond saws, screeding machines, setting‑bolt or strongback systems, and grout pumps be available, with rigging and delivery routes planned in advance. The work must follow a defined sequence that begins with pre‑placement meetings ten to fourteen calendar days before installation, proceeds through full‑depth diamond‑saw cutting, lift‑out removal of the existing pavement, subbase preparation, precise slab leveling using an approved method, placement of dowel bars and tie bars, rapid‑setting grout placement, and only then opening to traffic after the grout attains at least 2 500 psi compressive strength. Ambient average daily temperatures must be considered in the mix design to accommodate thermal expansion for panel sizing and joint locations. Traffic may be restricted by lane closures during slab installation; vertical differentials must not exceed one‑quarter inch across any joint, transverse joint widths must stay between one‑quarter and one‑half inch, longitudinal joints must not exceed three‑quarters inch, and any temporary opening before grouting is limited to three days with shims used to prevent slab movement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 310–311), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Quality and workmanship

For pervious asphalt pavement systems, the corrective maintenance inspection must verify that the finished surface meets the surface‑tolerance requirement by checking for irregularities greater than 3/8 inch and correcting them before final acceptance. The depth of the paving course is checked with 6‑inch test holes, and any section that is deficient by 1/2 inch or more must be removed and replaced. Infiltration performance is evaluated on three selected areas per 10,000 ft², and pavement whose average infiltration rate does not reach at least 200 inches per hour is considered defective and must be repaired. Material quality is confirmed by ensuring that the coated‑aggregate content is at least 95 % and that no more than 4 % of the composite mixture passes the No. 200 sieve, with any violation requiring removal and replacement. Corrective maintenance should verify that the concrete mix used for precast slabs meets the minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and flexural strength of 650 psi as shown on the approved shop drawings. It should also confirm that slab dimensions and tolerances conform to the values in Table A and that the sacrificial thickness is 1/4 inch greater than the design thickness. The inspection should check that load‑transfer dowels, tie bars and their slots are placed within ±1/8 inch of the drawing locations and that joint gaps do not exceed ½ inch transversely (minimum ¼ inch) or ¾ inch longitudinally. It should ensure vertical differentials across joints are no more than ¼ inch (or ½ inch before grinding), and it should test that all dowel and bedding grouts achieve at least 2,500 psi compressive strength before the slab is opened to traffic. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 310–311), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

For pervious‑asphalt corrective maintenance, the treatment is satisfactory only when surface irregularities after compaction do not exceed 3/8 inch and any greater deviation is immediately corrected without grinding; the pavement depth must be within 1/2 inch of the indicated design and sections deficient by that amount are removed and replaced; the restored pavement must meet or exceed a minimum average infiltration rate of 200 inches per hour, with lower rates considered defective and requiring replacement; additionally, the mix must contain at least 95 % coated aggregate particles and may not exceed a 4 % maximum passing the No. 200 sieve, otherwise it is deemed deficient and subject to corrective action. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, a satisfactory corrective‑maintenance repair must satisfy all dimensional and performance tolerances specified in Section 529.3, with slab dimensions falling within the limits shown in Table A, dowel and tie‑bar slots placed within ±1/8 inch of the drawing locations, joint widths at least 1/4 inch but not exceeding 1/2 inch for transverse joints and 3/4 inch for longitudinal joints, vertical differentials across any joint not exceeding 1/4 inch (or up to 1/2 inch only if the slab will be diamond‑ground to bring it into tolerance), bedding surface grade within ±½ inch of design, and surface variation over a slab length not exceeding 1/8 inch; grout used for dowel slots and bedding must reach a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic is allowed, the concrete mix must achieve at least 4,925 psi compressive and 650 psi flexural strength, and setting‑bolt cementitious grout must attain 50 psi before bolt removal. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 310–311), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Performance and service life

For pervious asphalt pavement systems, corrective maintenance restores condition by removing and replacing any area that exceeds the specified limits—irregularities greater than 3/8‑inch, depth deficiencies of 1/2‑inch or more, or infiltration rates below the required minimum of 200 inches per hour—and by correcting surface tolerance failures; these actions bring surface smoothness, thickness, and drainage performance back within the tolerances set out in the specification, thereby returning the pavement to an acceptable functional state. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs, corrective maintenance consists of repairing spalls as required by Section 501.3(t), replacing any slab that exhibits a crack wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third the slab depth (D/3), sealing narrower cracks with high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate followed by sand before traffic is allowed, and ensuring grout used for dowel or bedding achieves at least 2 500 psi compressive strength; these actions must be completed prior to opening the pavement and traffic may not be permitted until that strength is reached. By eliminating spalls and addressing crack dimensions according to the specified limits, the pavement’s surface condition is restored, load‑transfer functionality across joints is maintained, further deterioration is mitigated, and the slab system can continue to perform for its intended service life. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 310–311), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Corrective Maintenance is a reactive approach used to restore pavement serviceability when specific deficiencies develop that impair safe and efficient operation [1]. For flexible pavements the most common recurring distresses addressed by corrective maintenance include potholes, localized deterioration such as edge failures and shoulder grade separations, while rigid pavements may require treatment of isolated joint failures or full‑width slab failures [1]. In pervious asphalt pavement systems corrective‑maintenance failures are identified when surface irregularities exceed three‑eighths of an inch, when the pavement depth is deficient by a half‑inch or more, when the average infiltration rate falls below two hundred inches per hour, when the coated‑aggregate content drops below ninety‑five percent, or when fine‑aggregate passing the No. 200 sieve exceeds four percent; these conditions typically develop from poor compaction, contamination with fines, or improper joint construction and must be remedied by removing and replacing the defective sections rather than grinding or milling [2]. Full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs exhibit a range of corrective‑maintenance distresses: surface spalling must be repaired as specified in Section 501.3(t), cracks wider than 0.007 inches and deeper than one‑third of the slab depth (D/3) require full‑slab replacement, cracks of the same width but shallower are repaired according to ACI 224R, and cracks ≤0.007 inches are sealed with methacrylate; transverse joint openings must be maintained between a quarter‑inch minimum and a half‑inch maximum along their entire length, longitudinal joints may not exceed three‑quarters of an inch, and vertical edge differentials greater than a quarter‑inch (or greater than a half‑inch for removal) trigger re‑grading, resetting, or grinding. These failures are driven by manufacturing imperfections, handling or construction damage, inadequate subbase preparation or bedding grading, insufficient grout strength, and premature traffic exposure [2].

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 211–213); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 310–311), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

Monitoring and follow-up

After corrective maintenance the treated section must be inspected for surface tolerance by running a 12‑foot straightedge over any locations suspected of irregularity, including transverse joints and paving notches, and any irregularities greater than 3/8‑inch must be corrected immediately. Depth verification is performed by drilling one 6‑inch diameter test hole to the full depth of the pervious course for each 3,000 square yards (or less) of completed base, and any area found deficient by 1/2‑inch or more is removed and replaced. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 310–311)

In pervious asphalt pavement systems, if after final compaction the surface still exhibits irregularities greater than three‑eighths of an inch or depth measurements show a deficiency of one‑half inch or more, the treated area is considered defective and must be removed and replaced; similarly, infiltration testing that yields an average rate below two hundred inches per hour, laboratory analysis indicating coated aggregate content under ninety‑five percent, or sieve passing exceeding four percent for the No. 200 sieve are performance trends that signal the need for additional corrective maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, cracks wider than 0.007 inches and extending deeper than one‑third of the slab depth require slab replacement, while narrower cracks must be repaired in accordance with recommended cracking control procedures; a finished vertical differential across any joint that exceeds one‑quarter inch triggers regrading or diamond grinding when it does not exceed one‑half inch, but a differential greater than one‑half inch requires removal of the slab. Joint gaps that fall outside the specified one‑quarter‑to‑one‑half inch range for transverse joints or exceed three‑quarters inch for longitudinal joints also indicate corrective action is required, and any recurring damage or distress observed during inspection should halt installation until the cause is determined. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 310–311), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)
