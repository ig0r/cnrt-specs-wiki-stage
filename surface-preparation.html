Surface Preparation

Part of: Overlay is-subtopic-of
Surface Preparation is a topic under Overlay.
surface-preparation
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Surface preparation is the series of prerequisite actions applied to an existing pavement before a new overlay or treatment so that the bond between old and new material will be adequate and durable[1]. It eliminates loose, deteriorated, or contaminant material by requiring actions such as FWD testing, full‑depth patching, slab stabilization, provision of subdrainage, scarifying to a required depth, shot‑blasting or water‑blasting, cleaning within 30 minutes of placement, and, when needed, the placement of a scratch or leveling course that creates a uniformly roughened, dry, and level platform[2]. The process is defined by strict profile and cleanliness criteria—including removal of debris, oil, grease, standing water, temperature control below 120 °F, and final cleaning with an oil‑free, moisture‑free compressed‑air stream no more than thirty minutes before overlay—to achieve the bond‑strength acceptance values specified in the specifications[2].

Purpose and applicability

Surface preparation eliminates the risk that an existing pavement’s condition will prevent a new overlay from bonding and performing properly by requiring prerequisite actions such as FWD testing on the existing PCCP, full‑depth patching with PCC or asphalt concrete base course when approved, slab stabilization or jacking where slab movement or faulting exists, and provision of adequate subdrainage where subsurface drainage problems are present [1]. It also solves surface deterioration that would otherwise hinder proper bonding by placing a scratch or leveling course ahead of resurfacing, filling wheel ruts and other local small depressions with the scratch course, and re‑establishing the proper cross‑section with a leveling course of minimum 1‑inch thickness to provide a uniform working platform for the binder and wearing courses [1]. When the pavement base is stable, alternatives such as heater planing or milling may be considered [1]. Surface preparation eliminates loose, deteriorated, or contaminant material that would otherwise prevent an adequate bond between the existing pavement and a new overlay or treatment, thereby preventing defective bond strength [2]. In SECTION 545—Bonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfacied Pavements, the process creates a uniformly roughened profile by scarifying to the required depth, removing exposed reinforcement, shot‑blasting or water‑blasting, cleaning within 30 minutes of placement, protecting from oil and grease, and keeping the surface temperature below 120°F so that the overlay can meet the bond‑strength acceptance criteria of an average of at least 175 psi (each test not less than 160 psi) [2]. In SECTION 659—High Friction Surface Treatment, contaminants and surface irregularities are eliminated by washing with a mild detergent solution, rinsing, drying with a hot compressed‑air lance, mechanically sweeping or vacuum‑sweeping at least 180 cfm and 80 psi to remove dirt, loose aggregate, oils, greases and other deleterious material, shot blasting concrete to ICRI CSP 5, and pre‑treating cracks one‑quarter inch or wider after clearing loose material [2]. In SECTION 466—Asphalt Paving Interlayer System, all dirt, gravel, vegetation, oil, and other debris are removed, cracks are sealed as specified, and potholes or other distresses are repaired so that the substrate is dry, clean, and drainable with no depressions before interlayer placement [2]. In SECTION 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfacied Pavements, non‑uniform support caused by unstable slabs, moving slab fragments, loose debris, and oversized surface defects is prevented by stabilizing or repairing movable slabs, sweeping to remove loose material, filling defects larger than 4 sq inches and 1 inch deep when a geotextile interlayer is used, and milling joints faulting more than ¼ inch [2]. In SECTION 540—Bonded Concrete Overlay of Asphalt‑Surfacied Pavement, a weak bond is avoided by profiling the pavement with a mill to the plan‑specified depth, cutting and chipping inaccessible areas, filling cracks larger than ¾ inch with approved non‑shrink grout, thoroughly cleaning so no loose debris, standing water, oil or grease remain, performing final cleaning within 30 minutes using a compressed‑air stream free of oil and moisture at least 100 psi, and ensuring the asphalt temperature is below 120°F [2]. In SECTION 412—Superpave Thin Asphalt Overlay, contamination or irregularities that would hinder bonding are prevented by removing loose and foreign material, excess joint sealer, crack filler and thermoplastic markings with a power broom, repairing potholes and gouges greater than 1 inch deep, and sealing cracks wider than ⅛ inch before applying the tack coat [2]. In SECTION 527—Dowel Retrofit, the risk that loose material, slurry or other foreign debris will impair bond is eliminated by requiring damaged pavement be removed with a jackhammer not exceeding thirty pounds, cleaning slots to remove all loose and foreign material within twenty‑four hours before concrete placement, completing final cleaning no later than thirty minutes prior, and when air blasting is used applying a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi free of oil, moisture and other contaminants [2]. In SECTION 467—Heavy Duty Membranes, inability of the membrane to bond properly is prevented by removing dirt and debris from joints and cracks, filling any joint or crack wider than ¼ inch flush with PG 64S‑22 or PG 58S‑28, and confirming that the existing pavement is sound, dry, and free of deteriorated or loose material [2]. In SECTION 413—Superpave Mixture Design, contaminants and surface defects that would otherwise prevent a reliable bond between existing pavement and newly placed asphalt are eliminated by removing loose and foreign material, excess joint sealer and crack filler, correcting irregularities in the binder course, applying a tack coat as specified in Section 460 unless otherwise indicated, using a scratch course to fill wheel ruts and other local small depressions even with the surrounding pavement, and milling at tie‑in locations to provide proper notch geometry [2]. In SECTION 472—Asphalt Fog Seal, contaminant‑laden or wet pavement that would prevent proper bonding of the fog seal is eliminated by requiring the road surface be clean and dry before placing the fog seal, sweeping the pavement with a motorized broom to remove excess seal‑coat aggregate, debris and dust, and adjusting the operation if the seal coat surface aggregate is damaged until a satisfactory clean surface is achieved [2]. In SECTION 420—Pervious Asphalt Pavement System, adverse conditions on an existing pavement that would impair bonding and performance of new pervious asphalt layers are eliminated by correcting irregularities in the binder course, removing contaminated or uncleanable binder material, coating vertical surfaces with a thin uniform coating of emulsified asphalt, scarifying eroded subgrade to a minimum depth of 6 inches and removing accumulated fine materials so the subgrade remains permeable [2]. In SECTION 489—Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course, conditions that would prevent a reliable bond between the UTFCEM and the underlying pavement are eliminated by sealing all longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks 1/4‑inch or wider with the rubberized sealant specified in Section 469, removing thermoplastic or tape markings greater than 0.2 inches thick, thoroughly cleaning any grease, oil or fuel contamination, and sweeping away all loose particles immediately before placing the UTFCEM and UTWC [2]. In SECTION 469—Asphalt Joint and Crack Sealing, debris, moisture and loose material in cracks, joints and adjacent pavement that would inhibit proper bonding of sealant are solved by requiring cleaning, drying and removal of debris from the crack and surrounding surface, routing the crack to a reservoir ½ inch deep when required, extending the clean zone 4 inches to 6 inches on either side of the joint or crack, and using a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi measured at the source immediately before sealing [2]. In SECTION 450—Manual Asphalt Patching, compromised pavement is removed so that a new patch can bond reliably by cutting all edges vertically, excavating to sound stable material, thoroughly cleaning and drying the exposed surfaces, and applying a tack coat to the bottom and vertical faces as specified in Section 460.3(b), thereby eliminating loose or foreign material that would otherwise prevent proper adhesion and performance of the asphalt patch [2]. In SECTION 525—Concrete Pavement Partial‑Depth Repair, risk of poor adhesion between the new partial‑depth repair material and the existing concrete is eliminated by removing all unsound concrete to a minimum depth of 2 inches and a maximum depth of half of the slab thickness, creating a rough surface using a chipping hammer not exceeding 35 pounds operated at a 30–60 degree angle (or an appropriate milling machine), deploying the final cleaning method within 30 minutes before placing the repair material, and if a compressed‑air stream is used ensuring it is free of oil, moisture and other contaminants and has a minimum pressure of 100 psi measured at the source [2].

Surface preparation is required whenever an overlay—whether bonded or unbonded—is placed on existing pavement, because the rehabilitation guidelines list items such as falling‑weight deflectometer testing, full‑depth patching, slab stabilization or jacking, and adequate subdrainage as necessary to provide a stable platform before the new concrete or asphalt layer is installed [1]. The cracking, breaking and seating operations that are part of the preparation sequence are omitted for unbonded concrete overlays and are only applied when a flexible (asphalt) overlay is placed over a concrete pavement; in those cases another method rather than surface preparation should be used [1]. When the existing concrete is severely distressed, alternatives such as cracking/breaking and seating or rubblizing may be considered instead of the standard surface‑preparation sequence [1].

For an unbonded concrete overlay over an existing concrete‑surfaced pavement, surface preparation must be performed before any interlayer is placed; this includes stabilizing or repairing unstable slabs or slab fragments, patching shallow defects larger than 4 sq in and deeper than 1 in, sweeping to remove loose debris with a power broom, vacuum truck or other approved means, and then installing the chosen separation layer [2]. Joints that exceed ¼ inch must be milled before placing a geotextile interlayer, and joints that exceed ½ inch require a scratch course or milling/grinding of the joints prior to using an asphalt interlayer [2].

When a bonded concrete overlay is placed over an existing asphalt‑surfaced pavement, surface preparation is appropriate by profile‑milling the asphalt to the depth shown on the plans (or saw‑cutting and chipping where milling cannot reach), filling cracks larger than 3/4 inch with approved non‑shrink grout, eliminating all standing water after milling, thoroughly cleaning the milled surface, protecting it from oil or grease, performing a final cleaning no more than 30 minutes before concrete placement, and keeping the asphalt temperature below 120 °F; if the existing pavement is unsound and would otherwise be replaced with asphalt material, the specification calls for replacing that portion with Class AA cement concrete in conjunction with the overlay rather than using standard surface‑preparation milling [2].

Surface preparation is also appropriate before applying a High Friction Surface Treatment; the pavement must be cleaned immediately prior to HFST installation by washing with a mild detergent, rinsing with potable water and drying with hot compressed air, then removing loose material from cracks and vacuum‑sweeping or air‑washing at a minimum of 180 cfm and 80 psi; this step shall only be performed on pavements that have cured sufficiently—new asphalt pavements must have completed a mandatory 30‑day cure period and concrete surfaces or repairs must be at least 28 days old [2].

When the existing subgrade has become eroded, accumulated fine material, or exhibits surface ponding, surface preparation is appropriate by removing the material with light equipment and scarifying the soil to a minimum depth of 6 inches using a spring‑tooth rake or equivalent while leaving the subgrade undisturbed and uncompacted; it is also required before placing a wearing course to correct irregularities in the binder course, avoid traffic contamination, and remove and replace any binder course that cannot be cleaned to the Representative’s satisfaction. If these conditions are not met—that is, if the existing surface cannot be adequately cleaned or corrected—a replacement of the binder course rather than cleaning is mandated, tack coat should not be applied between lifts of pervious asphalt, and other methods such as full removal and reconstruction must be used [2].

For concrete‑pavement partial‑depth repairs, surface preparation is appropriate when unsound concrete can be removed to a minimum depth of 2 inches but not more than one‑half of the slab thickness, producing a rough surface by operating a chipping hammer at a 30–60 degree angle; if removal exceeds those limits or dowel bars are damaged so that the repair area effectively becomes full‑depth, a full‑depth concrete pavement patch should be constructed instead [2].

In overlay designs that require filling wheel ruts, smoothing small depressions, or correcting an erratic longitudinal profile, surface preparation using a separate scratch or leveling course is appropriate; a leveling course of at least 1 inch thickness provides a uniform working platform and re‑establishes the proper cross‑section, while a scratch course evens local depressions and should not be combined with a binder layer because quality control would be difficult [1]. When the existing pavement base is stable and removal of material will not affect performance—or when major adjustments to curb reveal, shoulders, guide rail, or drainage structures are needed—alternative methods such as heater planing or milling may be used instead of surface preparation [1].

Required inputs

Prior to any surface‑preparation activity the existing substrate must be clean, dry and free of foreign material, debris, oil, grease, standing water, ponded water or frost where applicable. Pavement surfaces are typically washed with a mild detergent solution, rinsed with clean potable water and dried using a hot compressed‑air lance, while asphalt decks are mechanically swept then vacuum‑swept or air‑washed with at least 180 cfm of airflow and 80 psi of clean dry compressed air to remove dust, debris and deleterious material. Where concrete is involved, shot blasting or water blasting is employed after mechanical scarifying to produce a roughened profile and to eliminate loose debris, rust, oil, grease and standing water. Full‑depth defects in existing Portland cement concrete pavement shall be patched with either Portland cement concrete or an asphalt concrete base course when approved. Shallow defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch are required to be repaired with an approved patching material such as asphalt or a cementitious repair before overlay placement. For unbonded concrete overlays the old pavement is separated from the new overlay by a fine‑graded asphalt separation layer having a minimum thickness of one inch, or by an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer. When a geotextile interlayer is used it shall be placed flat on the cleaned surface, overlapped by eight inches ± two inches, limited to no more than three layers, with edges extending at least four inches beyond the pavement and secured in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions; the fabric must remain clean, dampened but not saturated and its surface temperature shall not exceed 120 °F prior to concrete placement. A leveling course of at least one inch thickness may be placed to provide a uniform working platform before the binder or wearing course is applied. When an existing asphalt layer over a cement concrete or brick base is milled, a minimum of one‑and‑half inches of the original asphalt shall be retained to preserve structural integrity and bonding. Thin overlays thinner than one‑and‑half inches are permitted only when the existing pavement surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than two percent requiring patching, and both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded to good for the project. Superpave wearing courses of 6.3 mm nominal thickness require a PG 64E‑22 binder and may be accompanied by a non‑tracking asphalt tack coat where stopping, turning, or slow moving traffic conditions exist. Asphalt mixtures containing PG 64S‑22 may not be placed from November 1 to March 31 unless written permission is obtained, while mixtures containing PG 64E‑22 are similarly restricted from October 15 to March 31 without authorization. Prepared concrete surfaces shall be kept below 120 °F, and a clean oil‑free compressed‑air stream at a minimum pressure of 100 psi must be applied no more than thirty minutes before concrete placement. Concrete for overlays is placed when its temperature is between 65 °F and 90 °F, while roller‑compacted concrete mixtures are required to remain within a plastic temperature range of 50 °F to 90 °F during placement. Placement of roller‑compacted concrete is prohibited when the ambient air temperature away from artificial heat falls below 40 °F or when the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·hr unless mitigation measures are employed. Adequate subdrainage shall be provided wherever subsurface drainage problems exist, and surface and subsurface drainage must be good or upgraded to good for thin overlay applications. For pervious asphalt pavement systems the subgrade is left undisturbed and un‑compacted to preserve permeability; where fine material or ponding exists the soil is removed and the underlying layer scarified to a minimum depth of six inches using a spring‑tooth rake or equivalent equipment. Prior to placement of asphalt paving interlayer systems the existing pavement must be dry, clean and free of dirt, gravel, vegetation, oil and any loose debris; cracks shall be sealed as specified and allowed to cure, potholes repaired, and the prepared surface must be drainable with no depressions. Vertical surfaces that will contact new asphalt or concrete are painted with a uniform coating of emulsified asphalt classified as PennDOT Material Class TACK, or with NTT/CNTT, applied in two or more coats before a tack coat is placed according to Section 460. Equipment and personnel

Surface preparation for roller‑compacted concrete pavements requires that the subgrade or subbase be cleaned of foreign material, ponded water and frost before RCC placement, a condition achieved with at least one water truck equipped with a fog‑mist system. The RCC mix is produced by an approved mixing plant (batch type or continuous) which supplies a uniform mixture to covered haul trucks that continuously feed the material into a suitable paver capable of spreading and finishing the lift to the required thickness. Primary compaction for RCC is achieved with rollers conforming to Section 108.05(c)3, and if a minimum 95 % field density is not achieved with the paver, a self‑propelled smooth dual steel drum roller operating in static or vibratory mode having a minimum weight of 8 tons is used to achieve primary compaction before final compaction, followed by steel‑drum or rubber‑tired rollers for final consolidation and surface finishing. Curing is maintained by the same water truck applying an even fine mist without damaging the surface for a minimum of 72 hours, while in‑field density testing is performed with equipment conforming to ASTM C 1040 to verify that post‑rolling compaction exceeds 96 % of maximum wet density.

For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slab work preparation calls for a diamond saw to make full‑depth cuts and lifting devices such as cranes, excavators and rigging to lift out the cut concrete; fine grading of the prepared subbase is achieved with a laser or mechanically controlled screeding device while a straight‑edge not less than 10 feet long and a depth gauge not less than 6 feet long are used to verify tolerances before slab placement. Placement of the precast slabs uses the same lifting equipment together with setting bolts, strongback beams or polyurethane injection as described, and grout pumps and mixing units for installing dowel, tie‑bar and bedding grouts; the contract requires placing and compacting No. 2A coarse aggregate, so equipment capable of placing and compacting that material must be employed.

Bonded concrete overlay preparation requires a self‑propelled mechanical scarifier capable of uniformly removing the old surface across the entire cutting path, and shot blasting, sand blasting or water blasting equipment to remove any loose concrete and rust; a compressed air stream with a minimum pressure of 100 psi is used for final cleaning no more than 30 minutes prior to placement. When forecasted air temperature is 45 °F or less, adequate insulating blankets shall be provided to prevent rapid heat loss, and after finishing a curing compound shall be applied homogenously at a minimum rate of 1 gallon (± 0.1 gallon) per 125 square feet to provide uniform solid white opaque coverage on all exposed concrete surfaces; bond strength testing equipment in accordance with ASTM C 1583 shall be provided for the seven‑day test of the prepared surface.

High‑friction surface treatment preparation mandates mechanical sweepers and a high‑pressure air wash equipped with oil traps, followed by vacuum sweeping or air washing that delivers at least 180 cfm and 80 psi of clean dry compressed air; for concrete surfaces a shot‑blasting unit plus a hot compressed‑air lance is used to dry the pavement. Binder mixing for HFST is performed in calibrated containers using a low‑speed high‑torque drill fitted with a helical stirrer (or continuous‑mix equipment with a heated metering pump) to achieve volume proportions within ± 2 % and a uniform film thickness of 50‑65 mil, after which a serrated squeegee spreads the binder and mechanized or automated spreaders place aggregate at 12‑15 lb/yd² while monitoring rates and maintaining a minimum placement speed of 2 000 sq yd/hr; during curing no traffic or equipment may be on the HFST and final acceptance is verified by a calibrated skid test performed between 45 and 120 days in accordance with ASTM E274 using a smooth‑tire lock‑wheel apparatus.

Unbonded concrete overlay preparation calls for cleaning equipment such as a power broom or vacuum truck to sweep loose debris before any interlayer is placed, and joints that exceed 1/4 inch faulting are milled; when an asphalt interlayer is used, a rubber‑tire roller provides the required compaction, and slip‑form paving equipment is employed for concrete overlay placement. After finishing and texturing, curing is achieved with white PAMS liquid membrane forming compound applied uniformly and, when ambient temperatures are 40 °F or below, insulating blankets are laid to prevent rapid heat loss; no testing equipment is specified for the surface preparation phase.

Superpave thin‑asphalt overlay preparation involves removal of loose material, excess sealers and markings using a power broom and, where required, hand tools to apply an emulsified asphalt or hot binder tack coat; placement of the 6.3 mm overlay is performed with the specified paving equipment and a Material Transfer Vehicle as defined in Section 108.05(c)5. Immediate compaction uses an adequate number of steel‑wheeled rollers each weighing at least 10 tons equipped with a watering or soapy system, while areas inaccessible to rollers are compacted with mechanical vibrating hand tampers; finishing is achieved by keeping the roller wheels moist and clean to prevent material adhesion and by following the optimum rolling pattern established with control strips. Density testing after compaction employs an approved nuclear gauge such as Troxler Model 3411B, Campbell Pacific MC‑2, Seaman MC‑2, TransTech PQITM 300/301 or PaveTracker™ together with a licensed operator and current calibration certificate.

Fog seal surface preparation begins by cleaning the pavement with a motorized broom to remove excess seal‑coat aggregate, debris and dust; emulsified asphalt is placed using a distributor that meets the requirements of Section 460.3(b), and if directed fine aggregate is spread uniformly within five minutes of the emulsified application. The seal coat must cure at least two days before fog sealing, and traffic may not be permitted until the fog seal has fully cured; a 100‑foot test strip is constructed to review the emulsified‑asphalt application rate and serve as the field test for acceptance.

Ultra‑thin bonded wearing course preparation calls for cleaning the pavement by sweeping or other means necessary to eliminate loose particles before the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course is applied; placement requires asphalt pavers equipped with a built‑in spray bar positioned in front of the variable‑width heated screed unit so that the UTFCEM and UTWC can be spread in rapid succession, while the section does not specify equipment for compaction or consolidation, finishing, curing, or testing.

Manual asphalt patching surface preparation may use a milling or planing machine and requires that all debris be removed, the area cleaned and dried before tack coat application; placement of the asphalt wearing course is performed manually without specified equipment; compaction of the patch must be achieved with a steel‑wheeled roller or other approved compaction equipment, and for binder lifts an approved mechanical tamper may be used; finishing consists of compacting to bring the surface into grade with the surrounding pavement using the approved rollers or tampers; curing simply means allowing the tack coat to cure before further work, and no testing equipment is described. Work sequence

Before surface preparation can commence, the Pavement Policy Manual requires that a Falling Weight Deflectometer test be performed on the existing PCCP prior to overlay design [1]. Any necessary full‑depth patching of the original pavement with PCC or an asphalt concrete base course must be completed when approved before surface preparation begins [1]. If slab movement or faulting is observed, slab stabilization or jacking must be carried out prior to surface preparation [1]. Adequate subdrainage must be provided wherever subsurface drainage problems exist before the surface can be prepared [1]. The manual also mandates that coring be performed during the design phase to verify an adequate bond between the concrete or brick base and the existing asphalt material before surface preparation is undertaken [1]. Furthermore, the pavement base shall be in stable condition if alternative profile‑improving methods such as heater planing or milling are to be considered, meaning stability must be established prior to those methods being applied [1]. Under the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, a contractor must prepare a Field Operation quality‑control (QC) plan as required in Section 502.3(a)(1) and may not begin surface preparation until the Representative has reviewed and accepted that plan [2]. For bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements, a comprehensive QC plan detailing all operations, including surface preparation, must be prepared and submitted to the Representative for review prior to the start of work, and construction may not commence until that plan is accepted [2]. When performing a bonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements, the weather‑restriction requirements of Section 501.3(b) must be satisfied, and if cold‑weather work is performed the concrete temperature must be maintained between 65 °F and 90 °F before surface preparation proceeds [2]. All existing pavement markings, rumble strips, and adjacent utilities such as utility holes or inlets must be covered and protected as directed, and any markings that would conflict with a high‑friction surface treatment installation must be removed prior to surface preparation [2]. For asphalt paving interlayer systems, a copy of the manufacturer’s installation instructions must be provided to the Representative, a trained manufacturer’s technical representative must be present for every phase of installation unless a factory‑trained licensed installer is used with written approval from the material supplier, and a pre‑placement meeting that includes this technical representative must be held to discuss cleaning the pavement surface; placement may not proceed if weather conditions are unsuitable according to Section 413.3(b) and the manufacturer’s recommendations [2]. In unbonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements, all pre‑overlay repairs—including stabilization or repair of unstable slabs and slab fragments, concrete pavement patching, slab‑jacking, flexible base replacement, and shallow defect repairs—must be completed, and the existing pavement must be swept clean of any loose debris using a power broom, vacuum truck, or other approved means before placing the interlayer material [2]. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, surveying and engineering work must be finished before any surface preparation, including determining theoretical surface elevations and slab dimensions from the proposed longitudinal joint locations and analyzing the elevations of the existing abutting pavement to establish correct elevations for the new slabs, which are then used for slab fabrication [2]. Superpave thin asphalt overlay courses require that a paving‑operation QC plan on Form CS‑413 describing construction equipment and methods be submitted to the Representative before or at the pre‑construction conference, and paving may not start until after the Representative reviews that plan; additionally, an asphalt preplacement meeting with the Representative must be scheduled at least two weeks before placing any asphalt mixtures to review the specification, the QC plan, the sequence of paving operations, mixture and density acceptance criteria, and care and custody of acceptance samples [2]. Dowel retrofit work cannot proceed until dowel slots are first cut and cleared with a power‑driven self‑propelled saw capable of making two parallel cuts per slot, after which concrete between the cuts is removed with a jackhammer not exceeding 30 pounds; if damage occurs a lighter 15‑pound jackhammer must be employed before proceeding [2]. Heavy‑duty membrane installations require that the contractor supply a copy of the manufacturer’s written installation instructions, remove dirt and debris from all joints and cracks that will be under the membrane, and fill joints or cracks wider than 1/4 inch with PG 64S‑22 or PG 58S‑28; only after these preliminary steps is the pavement considered ready for detailed surface preparation [2]. Prior to beginning surface preparation, the existing pavement must be conditioned by removing and disposing of all loose or foreign material, excess joint sealer and crack filler—using a broom if necessary—and any vertical surfaces that will contact new asphalt must be painted with an approved emulsified‑asphalt or hot binder coating, followed by application of a tack coat as specified before overlaying [2]. For fog‑seal work, an asphalt seal coat must first be placed in accordance with Section 470 (or as directed) and allowed to cure for at least two days; only after that curing period may the road surface be swept clean and dried in preparation for the fog seal [2]. The subgrade must be fully prepared and formally accepted before any surface‑preparation steps, which includes excavating to undisturbed soil without compaction, removing fine material where ponding has occurred, scarifying the underlying soil to a minimum depth of 6 inches with a spring‑tooth rake or equivalent light tractor while avoiding heavy equipment traffic, repairing damaged areas to the Representative’s satisfaction and not compacting before geotextile and stone‑bed placement, and notifying the Representative for a final inspection and acceptance prior to proceeding [2]. All longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks that are 1/4 inch or wider must be sealed at least 24 hours before paving using the rubberized sealant specified in Section 469, thermoplastic and tape traffic markings greater than 0.2 inch thick must be removed, pavements contaminated with grease, oil, or fuel must be thoroughly cleaned, and immediately prior to applying an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course the surface must be swept or otherwise cleaned to remove all loose particles and unsuitable material [2]. For concrete pavement partial‑depth repair, a QC plan specific to that repair must be prepared and submitted for approval as required by Section 106.03(a)2, and the approved plan must outline all actions such as removal of unsound concrete, mixing, curing, joint sawing and sealing, sampling and testing before preparation begins [2].

Staging and logistics

Under Section 529, the installation plan for full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs requires the contractor to define the work zone by specifying the lifting capacity and footprint of all cranes, excavators and special lifting equipment, to detail the rigging and delivery‑truck routes, and to provide a traffic‑maintenance and protection strategy; material delivery and stockpiles are governed by shop‑drawing requirements that include a production note sheet describing source materials, testing methods, slab weights, tolerances and the size and position of required dunnage for yard storage. Equipment movement is coordinated by ensuring that adequate trained personnel and sufficient hauling units are scheduled before concrete placement to maintain continuity of placement. For bonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements the concrete temperature must be between 65 °F and 90 °F and the prepared pavement surface must be below 120 °F immediately before placement; if the forecasted air temperature is 45 °F or less, insulating blankets shall be provided until the air temperature rises above 45 °F or concrete strength exceeds 3,600 psi, and the rate of surface‑temperature change shall not exceed 40 °F per hour. For unbonded concrete overlays the fabric or pavement surface may not exceed 120 °F before placement; when ambient air is 40 °F or lower insulating blankets shall be provided and removed only once the air temperature rises above 40 °F while ensuring that the surface‑temperature change does not exceed 40 °F in any one‑hour period. For bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements the milled asphalt surface must be free of standing water and its temperature must be less than 120 °F before concrete placement; fog‑misting or protective covers may be used only if they leave no moisture on the surface, final cleaning shall occur within a 30‑minute window prior to overlay placement, and any compressed‑air stream used shall deliver at least 100 psi measured at the source. High‑friction surface treatment shall not be applied when wind speed exceeds 15 mph or wind gusts of 20 mph or higher are anticipated during placement, shall not be placed on new asphalt pavements until after a mandatory 30‑day cure period, and shall not be placed on concrete surfaces or repairs that are less than 28 days old. Asphalt paving interlayer systems require the pavement to be dry and clean, free of dirt, gravel, vegetation and oil; all loose debris must be removed, cracks sealed and allowed to cure, potholes repaired, and the surface must be drainable with no depressions, and placement is prohibited when weather conditions are unsuitable according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. For thin asphalt overlays surface preparation shall be completed before asphalt delivery and may not proceed when surfaces are wet or when air or surface temperatures are 50 °F or lower; placement is also prohibited during district‑specific prohibited dates ranging from October 1 to March 31 in several districts, and after cleaning the pavement with a power broom traffic should not be allowed on the surface if practical to prevent contamination. Fog seal applications may only be performed when air, surface and aggregate temperatures are above 60 °F, provided rain is not imminent and freezing temperatures will not occur within eight hours after application; the road surface must be clean and dry before applying the fog seal. Pervious asphalt mixtures may not be placed when the air or surface temperature is lower than 50 °F to ensure compaction can be achieved without exceeding four roller passes, and sediment runoff shall be controlled by covering detention bases with a minimum two‑inch layer of fine aggregate and wrapping basin sides in non‑woven geotextile. Ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses containing PG 64S‑22 may not be placed from November 1 to March 31, and those containing PG 64E‑22 may not be placed from October 15 to March 31 unless written approval is obtained; in all cases mixtures must not be placed on wet surfaces or when air or surface temperature is below 50 °F. Concrete placement shall not occur when the base, subbase or subgrade is frozen at the time of preparation, and the evaporation rate limitation of 0.20 pounds per square foot per hour applies as described above. Heavy‑duty membranes may only be placed on existing pavement that is clean, sound and dry, and placement must meet the temperature requirements specified by the manufacturer. [1][2]

Quality control and acceptance

Before any overlay is placed, the existing Portland cement concrete pavement must be evaluated through a series of surface‑preparation inspections. The Pavement Policy Manual requires that FWD testing shall be conducted on the current PCCP prior to designing an overlay; slab movement or faulting shall be examined so that slab stabilization or jacking can be specified when such movement is present; drainage conditions shall be checked and, if subsurface drainage problems exist, adequate subdrainage must be provided; and, depending on whether an unbonded concrete overlay is used, full‑depth patching of the original pavement with PCC or asphalt concrete base course may be required while cracking and seating operations are omitted for unbonded overlays [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require that the subgrade or sub‑base surface be inspected to confirm it is clean and free of foreign material, ponded water, frost or frozen material; it must be uniformly moistened, and if the sub‑base becomes dry it shall be wetted uniformly without forming mud or standing pools [2]. For bonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545) the inspector shall verify that scarification depth follows Section 591.3, that all loose material, reinforcement, steel plates and deleterious substances have been removed, that the surface is cleaned of debris, oil or grease and standing water, that joint backing is placed within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface, and that the temperature is less than 120°F; a seven‑day bond‑strength test shall be performed in accordance with ASTM C1583 and the average result shall not be below 175 psi and each individual result shall not be below 160 psi [2]. For high‑friction surface treatment (Section 659) the inspector shall verify that all existing pavement markings, rumble strips and utility openings adjacent to the HFST site are covered or protected as directed before any surface preparation begins; after washing with a mild detergent solution, rinsing with clean potable water and drying with a hot compressed‑air lance the prepared pavement shall be free of oils, greases and other deleterious material; a vacuum sweep or air wash shall provide at least 180 cubic feet per minute at 80 psi to remove all dust, debris and deleterious material, and for concrete surfaces compliance with the International Concrete Repair Institute (ICRI) standard for surface roughness (CSP) 5 shall be verified together with visual confirmation of the required concrete‑surface‑profile chips [2]. For asphalt paving interlayer systems (Section 466) the surface preparation shall result in a dry and clean pavement free of dirt, gravel, vegetation and oil; all loose debris shall be removed before the interlayer system is placed, cracks shall be sealed as specified in Section 469.3(d), potholes and other distresses shall be repaired as specified in Section 450, and the surface shall be placed on a drainable surface with no depressions [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays (Section 548) the inspector shall verify that any unstable slabs or slab fragments have been stabilized or repaired, that all surface defects larger than 4 sq inches and deeper than 1 inch are filled with an approved patching material and compacted to a flat, tight surface, that the pavement has been swept clean of loose debris using a power broom, vacuum truck or other approved means, that joints with faulting greater than¼ inch are milled before geotextile placement (faulting over½ inch shall be treated with a scratch course or milling for the asphalt interlayer), that geotextile overlap is 8 inches ± 2 inches with a free edge extending at least 4 inches beyond the pavement and no more than three layers are used, and that the surface temperature of the fabric or pavement shall not exceed 120°F before overlay placement [2]. For bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 540) the inspector shall verify that the existing asphalt has been profile‑milled to the depth shown on the plans, that any cracks larger than¾ inch have been filled with approved non‑shrink grout, that no standing water remains after milling, and that the milled face is completely free of loose debris, oil, grease or other deleterious material; a final cleaning shall be performed within 30 minutes of concrete placement using a compressed‑air stream that contains no oil or moisture and provides at least 100 psi measured at the source, and the asphalt temperature immediately before overlay shall be less than 120°F [2]. For dowel retrofits (Section 527) surface preparation shall be inspected for proper equipment and cleaning tolerances; the saw shall be a power‑driven, self‑propelled unit capable of making two parallel cuts per slot and may not cause spalling or aggregate fracture, the jackhammer shall not exceed 30 pounds in weight (or a 15‑pound jackhammer must be used if damage occurs), all loose material and slurry shall be removed from the slots within 24 hours of concrete placement with final cleaning completed no later than 30 minutes before placement, and when air blasting is used the compressed‑air stream shall be at least 100 psi free of oil, moisture and other contaminants [2]. For heavy‑duty membranes (Section 467) the inspector shall verify that all joints and cracks beneath the membrane have been cleared of dirt and debris, that any joint or crack wider than¼ inch has been filled flush with PG 64S‑22 or PG 58S‑28, and that the existing pavement is sound, dry and free of any debris, deteriorated material or loose particles; the surface shall also be prepared in accordance with the manufacturer’s written installation instructions [2]. For fog seal applications (Section 472) the pavement shall be inspected to confirm it is clean and dry; a motor‑powered broom shall be used to sweep away excess seal‑coat aggregate, debris and dust, and the sweeping operation shall be adjusted until a satisfactory clean surface is achieved without damaging the surface aggregate; all openings such as manholes, valve covers, sensors and similar items shall be covered to prevent emulsified asphalt from adhering, and protective coverings shall be removed before traffic is allowed [2]. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses (Section 489) all longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks¼ inch and wider shall be sealed according to Section 469 with sealant over‑band thickness and width kept within the tolerances specified in that section; thermoplastic or tape traffic markings greater than0.2 inches thick shall be removed, pavements contaminated with grease, oil or fuel shall be thoroughly cleaned, and immediately before applying the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course and its cementitious emulsion the surface shall be swept or otherwise cleaned to eliminate all loose particles and unsuitable material [2]. For joint and crack sealing (Section 469) the inspector shall verify that the pavement area around cracks or joints is clean, dry and free of debris and loose material; if a routed crack is required it shall provide a reservoir at least½ inch deep, the cleaned zone shall extend from four inches to six inches on each side of the joint or crack, and the compressed‑air cleaning device shall deliver a stream of at least100 psi measured at the source; when a hot compressed‑air lance is used it shall not cause overheating damage to the surrounding pavement [2]. For concrete partial‑depth repair (Section 525) surface‑preparation inspection shall begin with sounding the concrete in the presence of the Representative to verify the extent of delamination using a steel bar, drag chains or a hammer, and after removal the area shall be resounded to confirm that all delaminated concrete has been eliminated; exposed dowel bars shall be examined for corrosion, misalignment or extensive deterioration and, if only minor signs of corrosion are present, a bond‑breaking material shall be applied, otherwise a full‑depth patch may be required; the final cleaning step shall be inspected no later than30 minutes before placing repair material, and when a compressed‑air stream is used it must be free of oil, moisture and other contaminants and have a minimum pressure of100 psi measured at the source [2].

Surface‑preparation work is accepted only when it meets the dimensional and performance tolerances that are specified for each pavement type. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements, Section 502.3(k) requires that a straightedge test show no variation greater than 1/4 inch; any surface that varies by more than 1/4 inch must have high points removed with a self‑propelled diamond grinder or cutting tools at no additional cost and the test repeated until the slab conforms, while depressions exceeding 1/4 inch are deemed defective. In full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529.3) the subbase must be shaped within plus or minus 1/2 inch of grade and contour, the bedding surface may not vary more than 1/8 inch over the length of each slab, fabricated slab dimensions must fall inside the tolerances listed in Table A, vertical differential across any joint must not exceed 1/4 inch, transverse joint widths must be between 1/4 inch and 1/2 inch and longitudinal joints may not exceed 3/4 inch. Joints narrower than 1/4 inch must be saw‑cut to achieve the minimum width and slabs whose dimensions are outside Table A tolerances are rejected. Any nonconforming work that is deficient in depth, air content, bond strength, or that exhibits surface defects such as rain damage, honeycombing, or improper finish must be removed and replaced in accordance with Section 501.3(t) with a minimum removal length of 6 feet between transverse joints. Cracks that are one‑quarter inch wide or greater shall be pretreated with mixed binder but shall not be overfilled; any overfill shall be scarified back flush after the binder cures and any contaminated binder must be removed and replaced at no additional cost. All surface defects larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch shall be filled with an approved patching material and compacted to a flat, tight surface before overlay. Geotextile interlayer edges shall overlap by 8 inches ±2 inches, extend at least 4 inches beyond the pavement edge, and no more than three layers may be stacked in any location; any violation of these limits requires removal and proper re‑installation. Safety and environmental controls

During surface preparation for high‑friction surface treatment (HFST), the use of mechanical sweepers, high‑pressure air wash, shot blasting and vacuum sweeping generates dust and loose aggregate that must be promptly removed because any falling aggregate can strike an active adjacent travel lane, endangering motorists, while workers are exposed to airborne particles when operating compressed‑air equipment; the specification also requires covering and protecting all existing pavement markings, rumble strips and utilities before work begins, prohibits contractor equipment or traffic on the HFST surface during the curing period to avoid slip or impact hazards, and bars binder application when wind speeds exceed 15 mph (or gusts of 20 mph) to prevent binder spray that could affect workers or nearby traffic. During surface preparation for a bonded concrete overlay on asphalt‑surfaced pavement, milled asphalt must be kept free of standing water because residual moisture creates a slip hazard for both crew members and pedestrians, and any water that appears must be drained immediately; the cleaned surface must be protected from oil or grease drippings from compressors, trucks, spreaders and pavers because such contaminants can cause slipping and skin irritation, and they must be removed before the overlay is placed; final cleaning with a compressed‑air stream may only be done with air that is free of oil, moisture and other potential contaminants and at a minimum pressure of 100 psi, otherwise workers risk inhalation exposure to hazardous aerosols; in addition, the asphalt temperature must be less than 120°F before concrete placement, so hot pavement presents a burn hazard if not cooled by fog‑misting or protective covers. During surface preparation for fog sealing, workers must be aware that wet emulsified asphalt can adhere to exposed utilities, so manholes, valve covers, sensors and similar items must be covered to prevent adherence, otherwise workers could be exposed to sticky material and equipment may become fouled; the uncured emulsion also creates a slip‑and‑fall or traffic hazard, therefore traffic must not be allowed on the emulsified asphalt until it has cured. Curing, protection, and handoff

For roller‑compacted concrete pavements, Section 502.3 requires that immediately after final rolling and in‑field density testing the surface be kept moist until one or more curing methods are applied; the selected method—water cure using a misting‑equipped water truck, white polyethylene sheeting, a curing compound applied right after rolling, or cold‑weather curing—must be maintained for a minimum of 72 hours and vehicular traffic is prohibited during this period, and the pavement must develop a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi before opening. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete slabs, Section 529.3 states that any dowel or tie bars exposed at the end of the setting period are kept protected, and when slabs must be opened before grout placement a minimum of two incompressible shims are installed in each transverse joint to prevent spalling; traffic is not permitted until the rapid‑setting dowel slot and bedding grouts attain a compressive strength of at least 2,500 psi. For bonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements, Section 545.3 mandates that curing materials be applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and remain in place until the pavement reaches the required strength for traffic opening; when forecast air temperature is 45 °F or less insulating blankets shall be provided to prevent rapid heat loss and may be removed once the air temperature exceeds 45 °F or when average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, provided removal does not cause a surface‑temperature change greater than 40 °F in any one‑hour period; a curing compound is applied homogeneously at a minimum rate of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 ft² or as the manufacturer recommends. For unbonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements, Section 548.3 requires curing materials to be placed within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and kept until the required strength is achieved; when air temperature is 40 °F or less insulating blankets must be provided and may be removed only after the temperature rises above 40 °F or when average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, with removal performed so that surface temperature change does not exceed 40 °F in any one‑hour period; if a white‑pigmented liquid membrane‑forming curing compound is used it must be spread evenly to give a solid white opaque cover at a minimum rate of 1 gallon per 150 ft² for pavements thicker than 6 inches or 1 gallon per 125 ft² for pavements 6 inches or less, or the greater manufacturer rate, and may be applied in two equal coats with the second coat placed immediately after sawing, while joint faces must be kept free of compound or cleaned before sealant installation. For bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavement, Section 540.3 specifies that after milling the asphalt surface be thoroughly cleaned of dirt, oil, grease and other deleterious material, with final cleaning completed no more than 30 minutes before concrete placement; if a compressed‑air stream is used it must be free of oil and moisture and have at least 100 psi measured at the source; immediately prior to placing the overlay the surface temperature must be kept below 120 °F, which may be achieved with fog‑misting, protective covers or blankets provided that no standing water remains; once the concrete is finished and textured, curing material is applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and left in place until the pavement reaches the strength required for traffic opening, and when ambient air temperature is 45 °F or less insulating blankets are placed and may be removed when the air temperature exceeds 45 °F or when average compressive strength exceeds 3,000 psi, provided removal does not cause a temperature change greater than 40 °F in any one‑hour period. For high‑friction surface treatment, Section 659.3(e) requires that before any surface preparation the contractor cover and protect all existing pavement markings, rumble strips and adjacent utility openings as directed; after the binder is placed no equipment or traffic may be on the HFST during its curing period, new asphalt pavements must wait a mandatory 30‑day cure period before HFST can be applied while concrete surfaces or repairs must be at least 28 days old, and once the binder has cured the surplus aggregate is reclaimed prior to opening the lane to traffic. For asphalt paving interlayer systems, Section 466.3 states that after surface preparation the pavement must be dry and clean of all dirt, gravel, vegetation and oil, and any crack‑filling material applied during this step must be allowed to cure before the interlayer system is placed; the interlayer product itself is protected by keeping it dry, wrapped and stored elevated off the ground and covered so that ultraviolet light, precipitation, chemicals, sparks or other outdoor weather elements cannot damage it prior to installation. For dowel retrofit work, Section 527.3 protects the work by removing all loose and foreign material from the slots, with final cleaning completed within thirty minutes before concrete placement to avoid contamination; after the slot is filled with concrete patching material, vibrated and finished to match the existing pavement surface profile, the concrete is cured in accordance with Section 516.3(j). For heavy‑duty membranes, Section 467.3 permits traffic on the membrane only during the 7‑calendar‑day period immediately after placement; prior to placement the pavement must be sound, dry and free of debris or loose material, any joint wider than ¼ inch is filled flush with the specified sealant, and a primer or binder is applied to milled surfaces in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. For asphalt fog seal, Section 472.3 allows placement only after the underlying seal coat has cured at least two days and within two to thirty days of its final placement; before application the pavement must be swept clean and any manholes, valve covers or sensors must be covered to prevent adherence of the emulsified asphalt, with those coverings removed prior to traffic; the seal is applied when air, surface and aggregate temperatures exceed 60 °F and rain or freezing conditions are not expected within eight hours; after placement the fog seal must cure fully—shaded areas are checked—and traffic is prohibited until curing is complete, while permanent pavement markings may be installed only three days after the last fog‑seal day. For pervious asphalt pavement systems, Section 420.3 protects the finished installation by preventing sediment ingress and soil migration; the detention‑base bottom is covered with at least 2 inches of fine aggregate, the basin sides are wrapped in a non‑woven geotextile fabric, and temporary sediment control materials are removed only after full site stabilization; heavy equipment traffic on the newly prepared subgrade is avoided until the geotextile and stone bed are placed and no storage of equipment, material or soil is allowed on pervious beds during construction; placement of the wearing course must occur when air and surface temperatures exceed 50 °F, and after each lift the mixture is allowed to cool to 100 °F before subsequent layers are placed. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, Section 489.3 requires that all longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks that are ¼‑inch or wider be sealed at least 24 hours in advance using the rubberized sealant called out in Section 469 while keeping the sealant over‑band thickness and width within the tolerances of that section; any thermoplastic or tape traffic markings thicker than 0.2 inches must be removed, pavements contaminated with grease, oil or fuel are to be thoroughly cleaned, and immediately prior to applying the UTFCEM and the UTWC the surface is swept or otherwise cleared of all loose particles; placement of the asphalt mixtures is also protected by weather limits that prohibit paving when the surface is wet or when air or surface temperature is below 50 °F. For manual asphalt patching, Section 450.3 specifies that after the existing pavement is cleaned, dried and any loose material removed, a tack coat is applied to the bottom and vertical faces of the prepared area and must be allowed to cure before any new asphalt layers are placed; once the cured tack coat provides a bond, the asphalt wearing course (or binder and wearing courses for deeper patches) is laid and compacted with approved equipment such as a steel‑wheeled roller, each patch having a minimum area of at least one square foot, and lifts of binder course not exceed three inches uncompacted depth before curing of the tack coat. Expected performance and maintenance

During rehabilitation, surface preparation must address slab movement or faulting—including rocking and pumping slabs—as well as insufficient subsurface drainage and extensive surface spalling; failure to do so can lead to the need for slab stabilization or jacking, provision of adequate subdrainage, or selection of a bonded concrete overlay to correct the spalling [1]. In overlay projects, wheel ruts, local small depressions, and an erratic longitudinal profile are identified as distresses that can impair the bond of a new overlay if not treated during surface preparation [1]. When the existing asphalt layer has become debonded from a concrete or brick base, the specification requires full removal of that overlay to restore integrity [1]. For bonded concrete overlays, failures may appear as depth deficiencies, excessive air content, insufficient bond strength, or visible surface defects such as rain‑induced damage, improper final finish, and honeycombing; these conditions are classified as defective work and must be removed and replaced [2]. Bond strength is verified by ASTM C 1583 at seven days, and if the average of three test results is below 175 psi or any individual result is less than 160 psi, the area is deemed defective and additional testing is required to locate the problem [2]. In high‑friction surface treatment, cracks wider than ¼ inch that are overfilled must be scarified after the binder cures to restore a flush surface; contamination of the mixed binder or aggregate can impair retention and bonding, producing poor aggregate retention, delamination, or color discoloration that necessitates re‑preparation and retreat, and the binder must not be allowed to dry, separate, cure, or harden in a way that impairs aggregate bonding [2]. For asphalt paving interlayer systems, the pavement surface must be dry and clean of dirt, gravel, vegetation and oil, with all loose debris removed before placement; any remaining contaminants indicate inadequate preparation [2]. Cracks must be sealed as specified, and potholes and other distresses identified in Section 450 must be repaired prior to placement [2]. In unbonded concrete overlays, slabs or slab fragments that move or rock under normal traffic loads must be stabilized or repaired; surface defects larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch must be filled with approved patching material, joint faulting greater than ¼ inch must be milled, and if faulting exceeds ½ inch a scratch course or additional grinding is required; cracking that occurs outside of the sawed joints after placement is considered defective [2]. Equipment capable of causing pavement spall or surface‑aggregate fracture is prohibited; if damage results from a 30‑pound jackhammer the damaged area must be removed and a 15‑pound jackhammer used instead, and all loose and foreign material must be removed within 24 hours before concrete placement with final cleaning completed within 30 minutes of concrete placement to avoid contamination that leads to defective work [2]. Dowel retrofits deemed defective are removed and replaced at no additional cost; if dowel bars in the transverse joint are exposed and show little sign of corrosion, bond‑breaking material is applied, and any reinforcement exposed after removal of delaminated concrete must be cut and removed per the quality‑control plan [2]. During fog‑seal preparation, damage to the seal‑coat surface aggregate caused by sweeping must be corrected by adjusting the operation until a satisfactory clean surface is achieved, and the road surface must be clean and dry before placing the fog seal [2]. Improper subgrade preparation for pervious asphalt systems—such as accumulation of fine material that is not removed or scarifying the soil to less than the required minimum depth of 6 inches—creates a compacted or damaged base that must be repaired; allowing traffic on the binder course may contaminate it, and failure to coat vertical surfaces with an appropriate emulsified asphalt can cause bonding problems [2]. Over‑compacting the pervious mix more than four passes of a 10‑ton steel‑wheeled static roller eliminates free drainage or crushes aggregate, and placement when air or surface temperature is below 50 °F violates the weather limitation and may produce premature failure [2]. For partial‑depth concrete repairs, sounding the concrete to locate delamination identifies a primary distress; all unsound concrete must be removed to at least 2 inches but not deeper than one‑half of slab thickness using a chipping hammer no heavier than 35 pounds operated at a 30‑60° angle to avoid further damage while creating a rough bond surface [2]. Additional failures that may be encountered include corrosion or extensive deterioration of dowel bars, misalignment of dowels, and exposed reinforcement in jointed reinforced concrete, each requiring actions such as applying bond‑breaking material or cutting and removing the reinforcement per the quality‑control plan [2].

For rehabilitation projects covered in section 4.9, a falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD) test is required and should be conducted on the existing Portland cement concrete pavement (PCCP) before an overlay design is prepared [1]. When approved, full‑depth patching of the original pavement with a PCC or asphalt concrete base course may be applied as a repair measure [1]; slab stabilization or jacking shall be performed when slab movement or faulting is observed [1]; and adequate subdrainage must be provided where subsurface drainage problems exist [1]. In overlay projects described in section 5.12, coring must be performed during the design phase to determine whether an adequate bond exists between the existing asphalt and the underlying concrete or brick base [1]; if debonding is found, the entire depth of the existing asphalt overlay must be removed and a surface‑replacement item provided, and any missed debonded areas are addressed with the same surface‑replacement provision [1]. When milling or heater planing is considered as an alternative profile‑improvement method, it shall only be used when the pavement base is stable and removal will not affect performance, and it is preferred that at least 1 ½ inches of existing asphalt material remain in place so that structural integrity and bonding are retained [1].

During surface preparation for full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs (section 529), the subbase must be compacted and shaped so that its final elevation is within plus or minus ½ inch of grade and contour, and before slab placement the bedding surface is examined with a straight‑edge and depth gauge to verify it does not vary from the theoretical plane more than ⅛ inch over the length of each slab [2]. Vertical joint differentials are continuously monitored and must not exceed ¼ inch; if they exceed this limit but are not more than ½ inch, regrading or diamond grinding is required, whereas slabs with a differential greater than ½ inch must be removed [2]; any manufacturing imperfections, handling damage or construction‑induced cracks in the slab are repaired as directed under the repair procedure at no additional cost to the Department [2].

For bonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements (section 545), the existing concrete shall be scarified to the required depth and all loose material, exposed reinforcement, and any deteriorated concrete or asphalt must be removed before cleaning; minor surface defects may be repaired by overlay, and the prepared face is protected from oil, grease and standing water [2]. A final cleaning is performed in front of the paver no more than 30 minutes prior to placement using a compressed‑air stream that must be free of contaminants and at least 100 psi [2]; bond strength is monitored by ASTM C1583 testing at seven days with an average result not below 175 psi and each individual test not below 160 psi, otherwise the area is deemed defective and further repair actions are required [2].

High‑friction surface treatment (section 659) requires that existing markings, rumble strips and nearby utilities be covered, the pavement be washed with a mild detergent, rinsed with potable water and dried with a hot compressed‑air lance; asphalt surfaces are mechanically swept then vacuum‑swept or air‑washed at no less than 180 cfm and 80 psi, while concrete is shot‑blasted to meet ICRI CSP 5 and similarly vacuum‑swept [2]. Cracks one‑quarter inch wide or larger are cleared with compressed air and pre‑treated with the mixed binder, taking care not to overfill, and any excess must be scarified after cure; binder mixing and application are monitored to stay within ±2 % of the manufacturer’s volume proportions and to achieve a uniform binder film of 50‑65 mil thickness before spreading aggregate at 12‑15 lb/yd² [2]; after curing performance is checked by a calibrated skid test between 45 and 120 days, and any areas showing poor aggregate retention, delamination or discoloration are re‑prepared and retreated at no extra cost [2].

For asphalt paving interlayer systems (section 466), the surface must be kept dry and cleaned of all dirt, gravel, vegetation and oil; all cracks shall be sealed according to section 469.3(d) and allowed to cure, potholes and other pavement distresses shall be repaired as required by section 450 or as identified by the Representative, and the prepared surface must also be drainable with no depressions so that moisture does not become trapped under the interlayer [2]; ongoing monitoring should verify that these cleaning, sealing and repair steps have been completed and that the surface remains dry prior to placement [2].

Unbonded concrete overlays (section 548) may require stabilizing any slabs that move or rock under traffic, patching unstable concrete per section 516 (excluding 516.3(p)), performing slab stabilization per section 679, executing slab‑jacking per section 681, replacing flexible base per section 316, and filling shallow surface defects larger than four square inches and one inch deep with an approved patching material before overlay placement; these repairs are verified by visual inspection for uniform support, after which the pavement is swept clean of loose debris using a power broom, vacuum truck or other approved means [2]. Joints with faulting greater than ¼ inch shall be milled when a geotextile separation layer is used, the fabric must be overlapped 8 inches ± 2 inches, limited to three layers, kept clean and free of debris, dampened but not saturated, and its surface temperature must not exceed 120°F before overlay placement; if an asphalt interlayer is specified, joint faulting exceeding ½ inch requires a scratch course or milling, the asphalt layer shall be placed at a nominal depth of one inch and the pavement surface temperature also must not exceed 120°F [2].

Bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements (section 540) require that cracks greater than ¾ inch are cleaned and filled with non‑shrink grout or other Department‑approved material before surface cleaning, no standing water should remain on the milled surface and, if water occurs, immediate Department‑acceptable drainage measures are required; all loose debris is completely removed, a final cleaning is performed no more than 30 minutes before concrete placement using a compressed‑air stream that must be free of oil and moisture and have a minimum pressure of 100 psi, and the asphalt surface temperature is verified to be less than 120°F immediately prior to overlay installation [2].

Thin asphalt overlays (section 412) begin with removal of loose and foreign material, excess joint sealer and crack filler, stripping of thermoplastic markings after cleaning while traffic is avoided, correction of irregularities in the binder course, repair of potholes and gouges greater than 1 inch deep, and filling of all cracks or joints wider than ⅛ inch with PennDOT‑specified fillers or sealants [2]; vertical surfaces that will contact asphalt are painted with a uniform coating of emulsified asphalt (PennDOT Material Class TACK) or hot binder applied in two or more coats, a tack coat is then applied to the clean surface and allowed adequate time for the tack to break completely before placement of the new wearing course [2].

Dowel retrofit work (section 527) requires that all loose and foreign material be removed from the slots within 24 hours before concrete placement, a final cleaning must be completed within 30 minutes prior to placing concrete with re‑cleaning required if contamination occurs; slot opening equipment is limited to a jackhammer not exceeding 30 lb, and if damage is observed the damaged pavement must be removed and a lighter 15 lb jackhammer employed, any dowel retrofit judged defective shall be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department [2].

General preventive conditioning for surface preparation calls for removing and disposing of loose and foreign material, excess joint sealer and crack filler from the existing pavement surface using a broom when necessary, correcting any irregularities in the binder course, painting all vertical surfaces that will contact new asphalt with a thin, uniform coating of emulsified asphalt before applying the tack coat specified in section 460, and may include placing a scratch or leveling course where indicated to fill wheel ruts or provide a uniform platform; erosion‑induced fine material must be removed and the subgrade scarified to at least 6 inches using a spring‑tooth rake or equivalent, all damaged or compacted zones must be satisfactorily corrected and repaired to the Representative’s satisfaction, and the contractor must notify the Representative so that a final inspection and acceptance can be performed before any further pavement work proceeds [2].
