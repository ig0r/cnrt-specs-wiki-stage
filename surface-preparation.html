Surface Preparation

Part of: Overlay is-subtopic-of
Surface Preparation is a topic under Overlay.
surface-preparation
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

Surface preparation is the set of prerequisite actions performed on an existing pavement before any overlay or treatment is placed, intended to eliminate conditions that would prevent proper bonding, adhesion, and performance of the new layer. It involves evaluating structural integrity (e.g., FWD testing), repairing defects such as slab movement, faulting, wheel ruts, cracks and depressions, providing adequate subdrainage, and creating a clean, dry, uniformly profiled substrate through scarifying, milling, shot‑ or water‑blasting, washing, vacuum‑sweeping and final oil‑free compressed‑air cleaning within strict time limits. By removing loose debris, oils, moisture, rust, carbonation and other deleterious materials and by establishing a minimum one‑inch leveling or scratch course where required, surface preparation ensures a uniform working platform that meets bond‑strength requirements such as ASTM C1583 and ICRI CSP 5 roughness standards. [1][2]

Purpose and applicability

Surface preparation eliminates the risk that an existing pavement’s condition will prevent a new overlay from bonding and performing properly by requiring prerequisite actions such as FWD testing, full‑depth patching, slab stabilization or jacking, and provision of adequate subdrainage where needed. It also solves surface deterioration that would otherwise hinder proper bonding and performance of new pavement layers by placing a scratch or leveling course to fill wheel ruts and local depressions and re‑establish the proper cross‑section with a minimum one‑inch thick leveling course, thereby providing a uniform working platform for binder and wearing courses. For bonded concrete overlays over concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545), surface preparation eliminates the risk of poor adhesion by removing all loose debris, rust, oil, moisture and other deleterious materials through scarifying to the required depth, cutting off exposed reinforcement, shot‑ or water‑blasting to create a roughened profile, draining standing water, and performing a final cleaning within 30 minutes of placement with oil‑free compressed air at a minimum pressure of 100 psi, thus satisfying the ASTM C1583 bond‑strength requirement. In high‑friction surface treatment (Section 659), surface preparation eliminates oils, greases, dust, debris, loose aggregate, curing compounds, loosely bonded mortar, surface carbonation and other deleterious materials that would otherwise impair retention and bonding of the binder and aggregate by covering adjacent markings, removing conflicting thermoplastic markings, washing with a mild detergent solution followed by potable‑water rinse and hot compressed‑air drying, mechanically sweeping asphalt, using high‑pressure air wash with oil traps, vacuum‑sweeping at a minimum of 180 cfm and 80 psi, and shot blasting to meet ICRI CSP 5, all performed immediately before HFST installation. For an asphalt paving interlayer system (Section 466), surface preparation solves the problem of poor adhesion by providing a dry, clean and drainable substrate free of dirt, gravel, vegetation, oil and loose debris, with cracks sealed and potholes repaired before any material is applied. In thin asphalt overlay courses (Section 412), surface preparation eliminates contamination and irregularities that would prevent proper bonding by removing loose and foreign material, excess joint sealer and crack filler, cleaning off thermoplastic markings, repairing potholes and gouges greater than one inch in depth, and filling or sealing cracks wider than 1/8 inch before any new mix is placed; a uniform tack coat must then be applied and allowed to break completely so that the fresh wearing course can adhere reliably. For unbonded concrete overlays over concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 548), surface preparation eliminates non‑uniform support by requiring unstable slabs or slab fragments to be stabilized or repaired, cleaning the pavement of loose debris with a power broom or vacuum truck, and treating joint faulting—milling joints that exceed 1/4‑inch fault and applying a scratch course when fault exceeds 1/2 inch—so that the overlay can bond to a uniform, clean surface. During dowel retrofit work (Section 527), surface preparation solves the problem of contaminated or uneven pavement that would prevent proper bonding and performance of new concrete patches by requiring saw cuts to define slots, removal of damaged concrete with a jackhammer not exceeding 30 pounds (or 15 pounds if damage occurs), and thorough cleaning of the slot surfaces to remove all loose material and slurry within 24 hours before concrete placement, with final cleaning completed within 30 minutes prior to placement and air‑blasting at a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi that is free of oil, moisture and other contaminants. For heavy‑duty membrane applications (Section 467), surface preparation eliminates the problem of contaminated or unstable joints and cracks that would prevent a reliable bond by removing all dirt and debris from joints and cracks, filling any joint or crack wider than ¼ inch flush with PG 64S‑22 or PG 58S‑28, and confirming that the pavement is sound, dry and free of deteriorated or loose material before membrane placement. In Superpave mixture design and construction of plant‑mixed asphalt courses (Section 413), surface preparation eliminates contamination and surface irregularities that would otherwise prevent a reliable bond between existing pavement and newly placed asphalt layers by removing and disposing of loose and foreign material, excess joint sealer and crack filler—using a broom when necessary—and by painting vertical surfaces such as curbs and gutters with a uniform coating of emulsified asphalt or hot binder applied in two or more coats. For fog‑seal applications (Section 472), surface preparation solves the problem of a contaminated pavement that would inhibit proper bonding; the road surface must be clean and dry before placing the fog seal, and it is swept with a motorized broom to remove excess seal‑coat aggregate, debris and dust, adjusting the operation if the seal‑coat aggregate is damaged until a satisfactory clean surface is achieved. In pervious asphalt pavement systems (Section 420), surface preparation addresses the problem of a non‑permeable, contaminated subgrade and irregular existing binder surfaces that would prevent proper bonding of new pavement layers by removing accumulated fine material, scarifying the underlying soil to a minimum depth of six inches with a spring‑tooth rake or equivalent, avoiding compaction before geotextile and stone‑bed placement, replacing any binder course that cannot be cleaned to the Representative’s satisfaction, and coating vertical surfaces with tack coat so the subgrade remains permeable and the surface ready for the wearing course. For concrete pavement partial‑depth repair (Section 525), surface preparation eliminates delaminated or unsound concrete that would otherwise prevent a reliable bond between existing pavement and the new repair layer; all unsound concrete is removed to a minimum depth of two inches and a maximum depth of half of the slab thickness, edges are milled or sawed to produce a rough surface for material bond, and the substrate must be final‑cleaned within 30 minutes before placing the repair material. Surface preparation is required whenever an overlay—whether bonded or unbonded—is placed on existing pavement, because the guidelines list FWD testing, full‑depth patching, slab stabilization or jacking, and adequate subdrainage as required items for a successful overlay. The cracking, breaking and seating operations are not needed for unbonded concrete overlays and are only applicable when a flexible (asphalt) overlay is placed over a concrete pavement, so in those cases another method rather than surface preparation should be used. When the existing concrete is badly distressed, alternatives such as cracking/breaking and seating or rubblizing may be considered instead of a standard surface‑preparation sequence. A separate scratch or leveling course is appropriate when the overlay design requires filling wheel ruts, smoothing small depressions, or correcting an erratic longitudinal profile; a leveling course of at least 1‑inch thickness provides a uniform working platform and re‑establishes the proper cross‑section, while a scratch course evens local depressions and should not be combined with a binder layer. When the existing pavement base is stable and removal of material will not affect performance—or when major adjustments to curb reveal, shoulders, guide rail, or drainage are needed—alternative methods such as heater planing or milling may be used instead. Surface preparation is performed immediately before installing a High Friction Surface Treatment and is appropriate when asphalt pavements have been in service at least the mandatory 30‑day cure period and concrete surfaces are at least 28 days old; the procedure requires mechanical sweeping or shot blasting, washing with a mild detergent solution, rinsing with potable water, drying with hot compressed‑air, removal of loose material in cracks ¼ inch or wider and pre‑treatment with mixed binder. When the pavement is newer than those ages, HFST surface preparation shall not be applied and another repair or maintenance method must be used until the required age is reached. For a bonded concrete overlay over an existing asphalt surface, surface preparation is appropriate when the existing asphalt is profile‑milled to the plan‑specified depth, cracks larger than ¾ inch are filled with approved non‑shrink grout, the milled surface is thoroughly cleaned with final cleaning no more than 30 minutes before concrete placement, any compressed‑air blow is oil‑free, moisture‑free and at least 100 psi, and pavement temperature is below 120 °F. If the existing pavement is classified as unsound according to Section 492.3(c), surface preparation should not be used; instead the unsound portion is replaced with Class AA cement concrete and where milling cannot reach saw cutting or pneumatic‑hammer chipping may be employed. In pervious asphalt pavement systems, surface preparation is performed before placing the wearing course by correcting any irregularities in the existing binder course and, when practical, keeping traffic off the binder to avoid contamination; if the binder cannot be cleaned to the Representative’s satisfaction it must be removed and replaced rather than further prepared, and existing vertical surfaces that will contact asphalt mixtures are then coated with a thin, uniform layer of emulsified asphalt applied in two or more applications using the specified classes, while no tack coat is used between lifts of pervious asphalt. For manual asphalt patching, surface preparation—cutting to vertical edges, excavating to sound material, optionally using a milling or planing machine, then removing debris, thoroughly cleaning and drying before applying tack coat—is called for when the defect is a pothole or abrupt depression three inches and less in depth (Type A) or over three inches in depth (Type B); if the pavement defect does not meet the description of a pothole or abrupt depression within these depth limits, another repair method should be selected instead of the manual patching preparation outlined here. For concrete pavement partial‑depth repairs, surface preparation is appropriate when the delaminated area must be stripped to at least two inches but not deeper than half the slab thickness; removal may be performed with a chipping hammer weighing not more than 35 lb operated at a 30–60° angle or with a milling machine equipped with a V, flat, or rounded head, and a final cleaning must be applied within 30 minutes before placing the repair material. If removal exceeds half the slab thickness or unintentionally creates a full‑depth patch because of excessive concrete loss or dowel‑bar damage, the procedure requires constructing a full‑depth concrete pavement patch as specified in Section 516 instead of continuing with partial‑depth surface preparation. Required inputs

Before an overlay is designed on existing Portland cement concrete pavement, a falling‑weight deflectometer test shall be performed on the existing PCCP; any full‑depth defects must be patched with either Portland cement concrete or an asphalt concrete base course when approved, and slabs that exhibit movement or faulting shall be stabilized or jack‑ed. Adequate subdrainage shall be provided wherever subsurface drainage problems exist. For unbonded concrete overlays the old pavement is separated from the new overlay by a fine‑graded 1‑inch minimum asphalt separation layer or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer, and cracking and seating of JPCP or JRCP are not needed. Surface preparation may include placing a leveling course that shall be at least a 1‑inch thickness to provide a uniform working platform before the binder or wearing course is placed. When milling an asphalt layer over a cement concrete or brick base, a minimum of 1½ inches of existing asphalt shall be left in place so that structural integrity and bonding are retained; thin overlays thinner than 1½ inches are permitted only when the existing pavement surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than 2 % of the pavement requiring patching, and both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded to good for the project. Superpave 6.3 mm wearing courses used in this preparation require a PG 64E‑22 binder and may be accompanied by a Non‑Tracking Asphalt Tack Coat where stopping, turning, or slow moving traffic conditions exist. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs the contractor must remove the existing pavement by full‑depth diamond saw cuts and lift‑out removal, then eliminate any soft, wet or unsuitable subbase material and place and compact No. 2A Coarse Aggregate to create a firm, even surface whose grade and contour are within plus or minus ½ inch of the design elevation; the bedding surface must be fine‑graded so that it does not vary from the theoretical plane more than 1⁄8 inch over the length of each slab. The concrete mix for those precast slabs must be submitted at least twenty‑eight days before production and, through trial‑batch testing, demonstrate a minimum compressive strength of 4,925 psi and a minimum flexural strength of 650 psi. For a bonded concrete overlay on concrete‑surfaced pavements surface preparation shall employ a self‑propelled mechanical scarifier to uniformly remove the existing surface to the specified depth, followed by shot blasting, sand blasting or water blasting capable of eliminating loose concrete and rust from exposed reinforcement; after scarifying all deteriorated material must be removed, the surface fully cleaned of debris, oil, grease and standing water, and a final blast (shot or water) must produce a roughened profile. Any standing water must be drained and the cleaned face protected from contaminants, with the last cleaning performed no more than 30 minutes before placement using compressed air that is free of oil and moisture and delivers at least 100 psi at the source. The pavement surface temperature must be kept below 120°F immediately prior to overlay placement, while the concrete temperature for placement must be maintained between 65°F and 90°F, and joint backing or approved filling material must be installed within ±¼ inch of the existing transverse joints. For a bonded concrete overlay on an asphalt‑surfaced pavement surface preparation requires milling the existing asphalt to the specified depth and removing all loose debris; cracks wider than 3⁄4 inch must be filled using non‑shrink grout or another Department‑approved material that will enable bond, and after milling there must be no standing water on the surface. The cleaned pavement must be protected from oil, grease, or other deleterious substances until concrete placement, and a final cleaning with a compressed‑air stream that is free of oil and moisture and provides at least 100 psi at the source must occur within 30 minutes before the overlay is placed. The asphalt temperature at that time must be less than 120°F, and any fog‑mist or protective covering used to achieve this condition may not leave standing water or moisture on the surface. High‑friction surface treatment preparation calls for existing markings to be covered and protected, then the pavement washed with a mild detergent solution, rinsed with clean potable water and dried with a hot compressed‑air lance; all dust, debris and deleterious material must be removed by vacuum sweeping or air washing using a minimum of 180 cubic feet per minute and 80 psi of clean dry air. Concrete decks must be shot blasted to meet ICRI CSP 5 roughness before the final vacuum sweep, while asphalt decks are mechanically swept prior to the same air‑wash step; HFST may not be applied on new asphalt until after a mandatory 30‑day cure period and may not be applied on concrete surfaces or repairs that are less than 28 days of age. For an asphalt paving interlayer system surface preparation requires a dry and clean pavement free of dirt, gravel, vegetation, and oil, with all loose debris removed before the interlayer is placed; cracks must be sealed using the specified crack‑filling material and allowed to cure, any potholes or other distresses must be repaired as directed in Section 450, and the prepared surface must also be drainable and have no depressions so that the interlayer can bond properly. For a Superpave thin‑asphalt overlay all loose and foreign material, excess joint sealer, and crack filler must be removed and disposed of from the existing pavement, using a power broom if necessary, and thermoplastic markings must be stripped; potholes deeper than one inch and cracks wider than one‑eighth inch must be repaired with fillers and sealants that conform to PennDOT specifications, all vertical surfaces such as curbs, structures, gutters and pavements that will be in contact with the asphalt mixture must be painted with a uniform coating of either emulsified asphalt (PennDOT Material Class TACK) or NTT/CNTT applied in two or more coats or hot asphalt binder of the class and type designated for the course; a tack coat then must be applied to the clean surface per Section 460 and allowed sufficient time for the tack to break completely before any overlay material is placed, with traffic kept off after cleaning to prevent contamination. For an unbonded concrete overlay on concrete‑surfaced pavements unstable slabs must be repaired with an approved patching material that may be asphalt, cementitious or a similar pavement repair material, followed by thorough sweeping to remove loose debris using a power broom, vacuum truck, or other approved means; when a geotextile interlayer is used, joints must be milled if faulting exceeds ¼‑inch, the fabric must be placed flat with edges overlapped 8 inches ±2 inches (no more than three layers), extended at least 4 inches beyond the pavement edge, kept clean and free of debris, dampened but not saturated, and its surface temperature may not exceed 120°F before overlay placement. If an asphalt interlayer is specified, a nominal 1‑inch depth of Superpave mix design per Section 410.3 is placed, joint faulting greater than ½‑inch requires a scratch course or milling to reduce faulting, the pavement surface temperature may not exceed 120°F before placement, and temperature control methods such as misting, white‑pigmented curing compound, lime water, or other approved means must be used. For crushed‑aggregate base course clean coarse and fine aggregates must be measured separately, water added by weight or volume, and when directed calcium chloride blended at the rate of 7 lb per ton for Type 1 or 5.7 lb per ton for Type 2 before water is introduced; all materials must be proportioned and uniformly blended into a homogeneous mix with no frost or frozen pieces and kept at the proper moisture content, each compacted layer (3 in to 6 in thick) must achieve not less than 100 % of maximum density, the subbase or subgrade must be completed and maintained free of ruts, irregularities, and loose material for at least 1,500 ft in advance of the leading spreader, placement may not occur on a frozen surface, when air temperature is below 40°F, or when freezing temperatures are forecast within the next 24 hours, and surface irregularities exceeding ½‑inch must be corrected before proceeding to ensure positive bonding between layers. For dowel retrofit surface preparation requires cutting the existing pavement with a power‑driven, self‑propelled saw to produce parallel cuts and removing the intervening concrete with a jackhammer that does not exceed 30 lb (or a 15 lb jackhammer if damage occurs); all exposed slot surfaces must be cleaned of loose material and slurry within 24 hours before concrete placement, with final cleaning completed no later than 30 minutes prior to placing concrete; when air‑blasting the compressed‑air stream shall be at least 100 psi and free of oil or moisture. Heavy‑duty membrane installations call for removal of all dirt and debris from joints and cracks that will be under the membrane, and any joint or crack wider than ¼ inch must be filled flush with the surface using PG 64S‑22 or PG 58S‑28; the existing pavement must be sound and dry, free of deteriorated or loose material, and prepared in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations; where milled surfaces are present a primer or binder is applied as directed by the manufacturer. Pervious asphalt systems require an undisturbed, permeable subgrade that is left uncompacted before geotextile and stone‑bed placement; any eroded or ponded fine material must be removed and the soil scarified to at least 6 inches using a spring‑tooth rake or equivalent light tractor; the detention basin bottom receives a minimum of 2 inches of fine aggregate and is wrapped in non‑woven geotextile, while vertical surfaces that will contact asphalt are coated with a thin, uniform layer of an approved emulsified asphalt (AASHTO SS‑1, CSS‑1, SS‑1h, CSS‑1h, or TACK) applied in two or more coats; pervious pavement mixtures may not be placed when the air or surface temperature is lower than 50°F. Joint and crack sealing requires that all materials be sourced from a Bulletin 15 or Bulletin 41 listing, and the existing pavement must be cleaned, dried, and free of debris; cracks may need to be routed to a reservoir half‑inch deep with the surrounding area cleared for four to six inches on each side; immediately before sealing, a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi measured at the source—or a hot compressed‑air lance—must be used to remove moisture, taking care that the lance does not overheat the surrounding pavement. Manual asphalt patching calls for cutting the defect to sound, stable material—using a milling or planing machine if allowed—and trimming all edges vertically, with each patch having at least one square foot area; after debris removal, the exposed surfaces must be thoroughly cleaned of loose and foreign material and completely dried before a tack coat (emulsified asphalt, Class TACK or NTT, the PG‑Binder used in the pavement course or PG 64S‑22) is applied to both the bottom and vertical faces. For an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course all longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks one quarter inch or wider must be sealed at least twenty‑four hours before paving with the rubberized sealant specified in Section 469, keeping the sealant over‑band thickness and width within that section’s tolerances; any thermoplastic or tape traffic markings thicker than 0.2 inches must be removed, and pavements contaminated with grease, oil, or fuel are to be thoroughly cleaned; immediately prior to placing the UTFCEM or UTWC the surface must be swept—or otherwise treated—to eliminate all loose particles and any other unsuitable material. Concrete pavement partial‑depth repair requires that all unsound concrete be removed to at least a 2‑inch depth and the exposed surfaces roughened either with a chipping hammer not heavier than 35 lb operated at a 30–60° angle or with a milling machine equipped with a V, flat, or rounded head; any dowel bars left exposed must receive a bond‑breaking material; the repair area must be cleaned again within 30 minutes of placement, and when compressed air is used the stream shall be oil‑ and moisture‑free and provide at least 100 psi measured at the source. According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10), surface preparation for a concrete overlay in section 4.9 Rehabilitation must begin with mandatory Falling Weight Deflectometer testing of the existing Portland cement concrete pavement before any overlay design is performed [1]. The manual further requires that, where approved, the original pavement be full‑depth patched with either Portland cement concrete or an asphalt concrete base course, and that any slabs exhibiting movement or faulting be stabilized or jacked prior to overlay placement [1]. In locations where subsurface drainage problems are identified, adequate subdrainage must be provided before the overlay is placed, while cracking and seating operations are excluded for unbonded concrete overlays but required when a flexible overlay is applied over the existing pavement [1]. Section 5.12 Overlays of the same manual permits the placement of a separate scratch or leveling course ahead of resurfacing, specifying that the leveling course have a minimum thickness of 1 inch because it may be used alone to correct profile or re‑establish the proper cross‑section [1]. When milling an asphalt layer over a cement concrete or brick base pavement, the manual prefers that at least 1½ inches of existing asphalt material remain in place to retain structural integrity and achieve adequate bond; coring must be performed during the design phase to determine if an adequate bond exists, and any debonded areas not identified by coring are allowed for with a surface‑replacement item in the contract [1]. The Safety Edge is mandated as a standard pavement edge treatment on the outside edge of asphalt pavements and shoulders; it should be built with a 30° taper (construction tolerance 26° to 40°) applied to wearing and binder courses with a depth of 1½ inches or greater, while the total depth shall not exceed 5 inches [1].

The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 Specifications states in section 529 that the subbase must be shaped to a firm and even surface within a tolerance of plus or minus ½ inch of grade and contour, and that the bedding material be screeded so its surface does not vary more than ⅛ inch over the length of each precast slab [2]. Full‑depth sawcutting of the existing pavement shall be performed with a diamond saw; wheel saws with carbide steel tips are prohibited, and an accurate survey of longitudinal joint locations and theoretical elevations must be completed before any cutting or slab fabrication [2]. Section 545 requires the use of a self‑propelled mechanical scarifier to uniformly remove the old surface to the depth specified in section 591.3, followed by removal of all deteriorated material and cleaning of debris, oil, grease and standing water; final cleaning must occur no more than 30 minutes before placement of the concrete overlay, and any compressed‑air stream employed must be free of oil and moisture and provide a minimum pressure of 100 psi measured at the source [2]. Joint backing or other approved joint filling material shall be installed to within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface in all existing transverse joints, and bond strength shall be verified in the presence of the Inspector according to ASTM C1583 after seven days using five random locations per lot not exceeding 3,000 square yards; the area is deemed defective if the average of three tests is below 175 psi and each individual test is less than 160 psi [2].

In section 659, existing markings and utilities must be covered before any work; thereafter the pavement shall be washed with a mild detergent, rinsed with potable water and dried with a hot compressed‑air lance. Cracks one‑quarter inch or wider must be cleaned with compressed air and pre‑treated with mixed binder, all asphalt surfaces must be mechanically swept then vacuum‑swept or air‑washed at a minimum of 180 cfm and 80 psi to remove dust and debris, and concrete surfaces must be shot‑blasted to meet ICRI CSP 5 roughness; new asphalt pavements may not receive high‑friction surface treatment until after a mandatory 30‑day cure period, and concrete slabs or repairs less than 28 days old are excluded from treatment [2].

Section 466 requires the existing pavement to be dry and clean of dirt, gravel, vegetation and oil with all loose debris removed before placement of an interlayer system; cracks must be sealed in accordance with section 469.3(d) and allowed to cure, potholes and other distresses are to be repaired as directed in section 450, and the prepared surface must be drainable and free of depressions, with a pre‑placement meeting that includes the manufacturer’s technical representative to review cleaning procedures and provide training on surface preparation methods [2].

Section 540 calls for profile milling of the existing asphalt pavement to the depth indicated in the plans (section 492), cracks greater than three‑quarters inch to be filled with non‑shrink grout or another Department‑approved material before cleaning, removal of all standing water after milling, and a final cleaning no more than 30 minutes prior to concrete placement; if compressed air is used it must be oil‑free, moisture‑free and provide at least 100 psi measured at the source, and the surface temperature at overlay placement shall be less than 120 °F, with fog‑misting or protective covers permitted only to achieve this condition while ensuring no water remains on the pavement [2].

Section 412 specifies removal and disposal of all loose and foreign material, excess joint sealer, crack filler and thermoplastic markings from the existing pavement, preferably using a power broom; potholes and gouges deeper than 1 inch must be repaired, any cracks or joints wider than 1⁄8 inch must be filled and sealed with PennDOT‑specified fillers, and all vertical surfaces that will contact the new mix shall be painted with a uniform coating of emulsified asphalt (PennDOT Material Class TACK) or NTT/CNTT applied in two or more coats or hot binder, followed by a tack coat per section 460 allowed to break completely before placement [2].

In section 548, any slab or joint exhibiting faulting greater than ¼ inch shall be milled before a geotextile interlayer is placed, and if the fault exceeds ½ inch an asphalt interlayer must receive a scratch course or be milled to reduce the fault; surface defects larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch must be patched with an approved material and compacted flat before the interlayer, geotextile edges are to be overlapped by 8 inches ± 2 inches and extended at least 4 inches beyond the pavement edge with no more than three layers stacked in any spot, the asphalt interlayer is placed to a nominal depth of 1 inch using a rubber‑tire roller, and both geotextile and pavement surfaces must be kept below 120 °F prior to concrete placement [2].

Section 312 requires removal of existing pavement slated for replacement to neat lines as defined by section 203 before base course placement; surface irregularities of the newly placed base are to be checked with a template and a 10‑foot straightedge at intervals not more than 25 feet, and any deviation greater than ½ inch must be corrected by blading, reshaping or reconstruction; depth control is verified by cutting one test hole per 3,000 square yards (or less) to the full compacted depth, and if the measured depth is deficient by ½ inch or more the area must be removed and replaced [2].

Section 527.3 governs surface preparation for dowel retrofit, requiring a power‑driven self‑propelled saw to make two parallel cuts so that the dowel centre lies at the mid‑depth of the slab (or the thinner thickness when transitions occur), with cuts aligned parallel to the roadway centerline; concrete between cuts is removed with a jackhammer not exceeding 30 lb, stepping down to a 15‑lb tool if damage occurs, and the resulting slot bottom must be level; all loose material must be cleared from the exposed slot surfaces within 24 hours of concrete placement, and final cleaning must be completed no later than 30 minutes before placing concrete using air blasting at a pressure of at least 100 psi free of oil or moisture [2].

Section 501.3 mandates that prior to placing concrete the contractor submit a comprehensive field‑operation QC Plan at least two weeks before paving, which must include sampling frequencies, joint construction procedures, grade and depth control, straightedge testing, curing methods, and the method for non‑destructive depth testing; steel forms used for surface preparation must be at least ten feet long, have depth and base width equal to pavement depth, may be built up no greater than ½ inch, must not exceed 1⁄8 inch in ten feet from the true plane, and may not deflect more than 0.01 inch under a 1,700‑pound center load applied within a four‑foot span; the base course must be thoroughly sprinkled with water to achieve a moist condition without puddles [2].

Section 467.3 requires removal of all dirt and debris from joints and cracks that will receive a membrane, filling any joint or crack wider than ¼ inch with PG 64S‑22 or PG 58S‑28 so that it is flush with the existing surface, ensuring the pavement is sound, dry, and free of deteriorated or loose material before membrane placement, and following all manufacturer written installation instructions including any required primers or binders on milled surfaces [2].

Section 472.3 states that prior to fog sealing the pavement shall be clean and dry; it must be swept with a motorized broom to remove excess seal‑coat aggregate, debris and dust while covering manholes, valve covers and sensors to prevent adhesion, and a 100‑foot test strip is constructed to review emulsified‑asphalt application, with the residual asphalt rate required to fall between 0.02 and 0.05 gallons per square yard as determined in the field according to PTM No. 747 [2].

Section 420.3 governs pre‑construction tests for pervious asphalt pavement systems by requiring construction of two test sections of at least 225 square feet each on prepared subgrade, infiltrating them according to ASTM C1701 with an average exceeding 200 in/hr, and ensuring the compacted thickness is not more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness; subgrade scarification shall extend a minimum of 6 inches into the soil after removal of fine material, damaged areas must be repaired to the Representative’s satisfaction before geotextile placement, and final inspection is required prior to basin installation, with the detention‑basin bottom receiving a minimum 2‑inch layer of fine aggregate, coarse‑aggregate lifts may not exceed 8 inches, and compaction of the pervious mix limited to no more than four passes of a smooth steel‑wheeled static roller when the surface is cool enough to resist a 10‑ton roller [2].

Section 469.3 requires that surface preparation involve cleaning, drying and removing all debris from cracks, joints and adjacent pavement, routing cracks when required to create a reservoir ½ inch deep, and cleaning the pavement at least 4 inches and up to 6 inches on each side of the joint or crack; immediately before sealing, a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi measured at the source—or a hot compressed‑air lance—must be used to clean and dry damp cracks, and the hot lance must not damage the surrounding pavement area from overheating [2].

Section 450.3 governs manual asphalt patching preparation by classifying potholes or abrupt depressions 3 inches and less as Type A and those over 3 inches as Type B; each patch must have a minimum area of 1 square foot, edges are cut vertically, and after debris removal the area and surrounding pavement are thoroughly cleaned of loose material and dried before applying tack coat to the bottom and vertical surfaces [2].

Section 489.3(g) requires that at least 24 hours before paving all longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks measuring ¼‑inch and wider be sealed with the rubberized sealant called out in section 469 while keeping the sealant over‑band thickness and width within the Section 469 tolerances; any thermoplastic or tape traffic markings greater than 0.2 inches thick must be removed, pavements contaminated with grease, oil or fuel must be thoroughly cleaned, and immediately before applying the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course and membrane the surface shall be swept or otherwise cleaned to eliminate all loose particles and unsuitable material [2].

Section 525.3 mandates that a QC Plan be prepared and approved as required in section 106.03(a)2, and that all surface preparation be performed in the presence of the Representative who first sounds the slab to locate delamination; rectangular repair limits are laid out to extend approximately 3 inches beyond each edge of the delaminated area with a minimum size of 15 inches long by 10 inches wide by 2 inches deep, any two areas spaced less than 24 inches apart shall be combined into one repair, unsound concrete is removed to at least 2 inches depth but not more than half the slab thickness using a chipping hammer weighing no more than 35 pounds operated at a 30‑60‑degree angle and saw cuts of minimum 2 inches depth on all edges, and final cleaning must occur within 30 minutes before placing repair material with a compressed‑air stream that is free of oil, moisture and other contaminants and has a pressure of at least 100 psi measured at the source [2].

Equipment and personnel

Surface preparation may require a diamond saw to cut full‑depth joints for precast slabs (Section 529). For dowel retrofit, the preparation phase calls for a power‑driven self‑propelled saw capable of making two parallel cuts per dowel bar slot and a jackhammer not exceeding 30 lb (or a 15 lb unit if the larger hammer would damage pavement) to remove concrete between the cuts; air‑blasting must be performed with a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi measured at the source and free of oil or moisture (Section 527). Removal of loose concrete and rust from exposed reinforcement may be accomplished with a self‑propelled mechanical scarifier together with shot, sand or water blasting equipment; the air stream used for cleaning must have a minimum pressure of 100 psi measured at the source (Section 545). Where an existing asphalt surface is to be milled, a profile milling machine supplemented by pneumatic hammers may be used for areas the mill cannot reach and a compressed‑air blower delivering at least 100 psi free of oil and moisture is required (Section 540). High‑friction surface treatment calls for mechanical sweepers, high‑pressure air washers and a hot compressed‑air lance that provides at least 180 cubic feet per minute and 80 pounds per square inch of clean dry air; shot‑blasting equipment followed by vacuum sweeping is required for concrete surfaces (Section 659). Power brooms or vacuum trucks may be employed to clear debris before placement (Sections 412, 548). Light equipment may be used to remove accumulated material and a spring‑tooth rake operated by a light tractor must scarify the underlying soil to at least six inches (Section 420). Placement of new layers may involve setting bolts, strongback beams or polyurethane injection with at least four grout ports per slab (Section 529); plastic or non‑metallic chairs that provide a minimum 1/2‑inch clearance for dowel bar caps are required for dowel retrofit (Section 527). Mechanical or manual laydown equipment capable of providing a smooth installation with a minimum amount of wrinkling or folding is used for interlayer systems, and brooming or rubber‑tire rolling, or both, is required to maximize contact between the interlayer material and the pavement surface (Section 466). A Material Transfer Vehicle delivers mix to the paver, and a calibrated distributor‑truck spray bar must keep asphalt temperature between 290 °F and 320 °F; hand spraying, squeegee or brush application may be used where the truck cannot reach (Section 412). Low‑speed high‑torque drills fitted with helical stirrers are employed to uniformly mix binder components within ± 2 % of the manufacturer’s volume specifications (Section 659). Adjustable self‑propelled mechanical spreaders capable of placing and screeding base material without segregation may be used for base courses (Section 312). Compaction or consolidation may be achieved with steel‑wheeled rollers each weighing a minimum of 10 tons equipped with watering or soapy systems as specified in the paving operation requirements (Section 412). Rubber‑tire rollers are used to press interlayer material into full contact and to compact one‑inch asphalt interlayers (Sections 466, 548). Vibrators capable of transmitting vibration to concrete with a frequency not less than 100 impulses per second are required for concrete placement, and smooth steel‑wheeled rollers operated in static mode may be used for one to four passes; pneumatic‑tire rollers must not be used (Section 501). Compaction equipment referenced in Sections 108.05(c)3.a, 3.b, 3.f and 3.h is required to obtain not less than 100 % of maximum density as defined by PTM No. 106 Method B (Section 312). Mechanical vibrating hand tampers may be used where rollers cannot reach (Section 412). Finishing operations may include a straight‑edge at least 10 ft long together with a depth gauge at least 6 ft long to meet grade tolerances during screeding (Section 529); early‑entry green cut saws are recommended for joint creation once concrete has hardened sufficiently (Section 540). A serrated squeegee is used to spread binder to the specified thickness before aggregate placement (Section 659). Straightedge testing and temperature monitoring are part of the quality‑control plan for finishing (Section 501). Curing may require insulating blankets when forecast air temperature is 45 °F or less, appropriate curing materials and homogeneous application of a white‑pigmented curing compound at not less than one gallon per 125 ft² (Sections 545, 540). Misting, white‑pigmented curing compound or lime water may be employed to keep surface temperature below 120 °F (Section 548). Contractor equipment or traffic must be prohibited on high‑friction surface treatment until at least 31 days have elapsed (Section 659). Cementitious bedding material must reach a compressive strength of 50 psi before bolt removal and dowel and bedding grouts must attain a minimum compressive strength of 2,500 psi before traffic (Section 529). Windbreaks or fog‑mist equipment that atomizes droplets without leaving water deposits shall be ready whenever the evaporation rate would exceed 0.20 pounds per square foot per hour (Section 420). Testing may involve standard compressive‑strength testing of grout (Section 529) and bond‑strength testing with equipment that complies with ASTM C1583 (Section 545). A calibrated skid test performed no sooner than 31 days after high‑friction surface treatment application shall use a smooth tire lock‑wheel apparatus in accordance with ASTM E274/E274M (AASHTO T 242) (Section 659). Coring drills conforming to PTM 606, measuring devices per PTM 614 and compressive‑strength cylinders as required by PTM 604 are used for concrete overlay verification (Section 540). Density testing shall be conducted with approved nuclear gauges such as Troxler Electronics Model 3411B or Model 4640B, Campbell Pacific MC‑2, Seaman MC‑2, TransTech PQI™ Model 300/301 or Troxler PaveTracker™ in accordance with PTM 402 (Section 412). Test holes dug at prescribed intervals verify depth and density of base courses (Section 312). Infiltration testing shall be performed in accordance with ASTM C1701 and the average infiltration value must exceed 200 inches per hour (Section 420). [2]

Work sequence

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, before any surface‑preparation activities can be undertaken under the Rehabilitation provisions of Section 4.9, the items listed in Section 4.9 B must first be satisfied; this includes performing a Falling Weight Deflectometer test on the existing Portland cement concrete pavement (PCCP) prior to developing an overlay design, completing any required full‑depth patching of the original pavement with PCC or an asphalt concrete base course when approved, carrying out slab stabilization or jacking whenever slab movement or faulting is observed, and providing adequate subdrainage wherever subsurface drainage problems exist [1]. The same manual further requires that, for overlay projects covered by Section 5.12, coring be performed during the design phase to verify an adequate bond between the concrete or brick base and the existing asphalt material, and that the pavement base be in a stable condition before alternative profile‑improving methods such as heater planing or milling are considered [1]. For a bonded concrete overlay of a concrete‑surfaced pavement (Section 545), the weather‑restriction provisions of Section 501.3 must be met, meaning that concrete temperature must be maintained between 65 °F and 90 °F and all required approvals—including the transverse‑joint location method—must be submitted and accepted at least twenty days before construction can begin [2]. For a high‑friction surface treatment (Section 659), existing pavement markings, rumble strips and adjacent utilities must be covered and protected as directed, and any thermoplastic markings that would interfere with the HFST installation together with any other markings that are one year old or newer must be removed in accordance with Section 963.3 before surface preparation can start [2]. Prior to surface preparation for an interlayer system under Section 466, a copy of the manufacturer’s installation instructions must be provided to the Representative and a pre‑placement meeting that includes a trained manufacturer technical representative must be held to review material type, application rates, mixture acceptance and pavement‑surface cleaning; additionally, the interlayer must be kept dry, wrapped, stored elevated and covered as required by Section 466.3(c), and weather conditions must satisfy the limits set in Section 413.3(b) together with the manufacturer’s recommendations [2]. Before surface preparation for a bonded concrete overlay of an asphalt‑surfaced pavement (Section 540) can commence, a comprehensive quality‑control plan that details all operations—including timing and sequencing of milling, cleaning and other work—must be prepared and submitted to the Representative for review, and no construction‑related work may begin until that QC Plan has been accepted [2]. For a Superpave thin asphalt overlay (Section 412), the existing pavement must first be conditioned by removing all loose or foreign material, excess joint sealer and crack filler—using a power broom if necessary—and by taking off any thermoplastic markings; traffic should be kept off the cleaned surface when practical, potholes or gouges deeper than 1 inch must be repaired, cracks wider than 1/8 inch must be filled and sealed, vertical surfaces that will contact asphalt must be painted with a uniform coat of emulsified or hot binder, and a tack coat must be applied to the clean surface and allowed to break completely before any new mixture is placed [2]. For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 501), the contractor must submit a comprehensive paving‑operation quality‑control plan at least two weeks prior to the scheduled start of paving operations, and paving may not commence until that QC plan has been reviewed and accepted by the Representative [2]. Under Section 467, the contractor must first satisfy the general requirement in 467.3(a) by supplying a copy of the manufacturer’s written installation instructions; only after this documentation is provided may the crew begin cleaning joints, filling cracks wider than 1/4‑inch and ensuring that the pavement is sound, dry and free of debris as required for membrane placement [2]. For surface preparation under Section 413, the existing pavement must be conditioned by removing all loose or foreign material, excess joint sealer and crack filler and disposing of them, using a broom when needed, painting any vertical surfaces that will contact new asphalt with a uniform coating of the specified emulsified asphalt applied in two or more coats or with the appropriate hot binder, and, unless another instruction applies, placing a tack coat per Section 460 before overlay [2]. Fog‑seal work covered by Section 472 may not begin until the underlying asphalt seal coat has been placed in accordance with Section 470 and allowed to cure for at least two days; thereafter the pavement surface must be swept clean and dried while covering manholes and other fixtures before the fog seal is applied [2]. Prior to surface preparation for a pervious‑asphalt pavement system (Section 420), the subgrade must be prepared as described in Section 420.3(b): it must be excavated to undisturbed soil without compaction, any accumulated fine material removed with light equipment, the underlying soil scarified to a minimum depth of 6 inches using a spring‑tooth rake or equivalent, damaged areas repaired to the Representative’s satisfaction, and the Representative notified upon completion for final inspection and acceptance before any basin, choker course or other pavement work proceeds [2]. Before surface preparation for joint and crack sealing (Section 469) can start, the required material must first be obtained from a source listed in Bulletin 15 or Bulletin 41 as applicable, and the material must be heated within the safe heating temperature range and the recommended pouring temperatures shown on the manufacturer’s shipping container label; batches may not be kept at the pouring temperature for more than six hours, and if longer storage is needed reheating must follow the manufacturer’s guidance [2]. Manual‑asphalt‑patching work under Section 450 requires that the job be defined in accordance with Section 413.3 and that the patch location already be outlined and marked by the Representative before surface preparation begins [2]. For an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course (Section 489), all longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks that are 1/4‑inch or wider must be sealed at least twenty‑four hours prior to paving using the rubberized sealant called for in Section 469, with the sealant over‑band thickness and width kept to a minimum and never exceeding the tolerances of Section 469; any thermoplastic or tape traffic markings greater than 0.2 inches thick must be removed, pavements contaminated with grease, oil or fuel must be thoroughly cleaned, and the surface should be swept or otherwise cleared of all loose particles and unsuitable material immediately before applying the UTFCEM and UTWC [2]. Concrete‑pavement partial‑depth repair work under Section 525 cannot proceed until the contractor prepares and submits for approval a quality‑control plan specific to the repair as required by Section 106.03(a)2 [2].

In the rehabilitation context (section 4.9) surface preparation begins with required FWD testing on the existing PCCP before any overlay design is made; if approved, full‑depth patching of the original pavement with PCC or an asphalt concrete base course follows; slab stabilization or jacking is then carried out whenever slab movement or faulting is present; adequate subdrainage is provided wherever subsurface drainage problems exist; and for unbonded concrete overlays the operations of cracking and seating a JPCP and breaking and seating a JRCP are omitted because those apply only to flexible‑overlay situations. When pre‑overlay surface preparation is required (section 5.12) the first operation is to place a separate scratch or leveling course ahead of resurfacing; a scratch course is used to fill wheel ruts and other local small depressions; where correction of the transverse or longitudinal profile or re‑establishment of the proper cross‑section is needed, a leveling course with a minimum 1‑inch thickness is placed to provide a uniform working platform, may be counted as a 1‑inch thick paving course in structural calculations but should not be combined with a binder course because quality control would be difficult; if profile correction calls for material removal, heater planing or milling may be employed provided the base is stable; and when milling over a cement concrete or brick base, at least 1½ inches of existing asphalt material should remain to retain structural integrity. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs preparation begins with a survey of existing elevations and layout of the proposed longitudinal joint locations before any cutting, followed by the use of a diamond saw to sawcut the full depth of the slab limits and the longitudinal joints; the existing concrete or composite pavement is then removed using the lift‑out method without disturbing the subbase, after which unsuitable material is removed from the subbase, No. 2A coarse aggregate is placed and compacted, and the final surface is shaped to a firm, even plane within a tolerance of plus or minus ½ inch of grade and contour; fine grading of the bedding material is performed with a laser‑controlled screeding device so that the surface does not vary from the theoretical plane more than 1/8 inch over the length of each slab, and any variation greater than 1/8 inch is corrected before slab placement. For bonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements a self‑propelled mechanical scarifier together with shot, sand, or water blasting equipment is provided to uniformly remove the existing concrete surface to the depth indicated and to eliminate loose reinforcement, deteriorated material, full‑depth asphalt patches and any exposed steel plates; after scarifying the pavement is shot‑blasted or water‑blasted to create a roughened profile, standing water is drained, all debris is removed, and the surface is protected from oil or grease drippings; joint backing material is placed within ±¼ inch of the surface and must not be dislodged by paving; a final cleaning is performed in front of the paver no more than 30 minutes before overlay using compressed air that is free of oil and moisture and has a minimum pressure of 100 pounds per square inch measured at the source, and immediately prior to placing the concrete overlay or inlay the surface temperature must be less than 120 °F, which may be achieved with fog‑misting, protective covers, or blankets, and any standing water is completely blown off with compressed air. For high friction surface treatment all existing pavement markings, rumble strips and nearby utilities are covered or protected and any thermoplastic markings newer than one year are removed; the pavement is then washed with a mild detergent solution to dissolve oils and greases, rinsed with clean potable water and dried with a hot compressed‑air lance; cracks ¼ inch wide or greater have loose material blown out with compressed air, are pre‑treated with the mixed binder without overfilling (any overfill is scarified after cure) and HFST is placed only after the binder has gelled; for asphalt surfaces the preparation consists of mechanical sweeping followed by a high‑pressure air wash equipped with oil traps and a vacuum or air sweep delivering at least 180 cfm at 80 psi to remove all dust and debris, and new asphalt must have cured for at least 30 days before HFST; for concrete surfaces the pavement is shot‑blasted to meet ICRI CSP 5, visually verified with profile chips, then vacuum/air swept under the same minimum airflow and pressure, and concrete less than 28 days old may not receive HFST. For asphalt paving interlayer systems surface preparation begins by drying and cleaning the existing pavement so it is free of dirt, gravel, vegetation and oil; all loose debris is removed before any interlayer material is placed, cracks are sealed as specified in Section 469.3(d) and the crack‑filling material is allowed to cure prior to further work, potholes and other pavement distresses are repaired as specified in Section 450 and any additional repairs identified by the Representative are performed, and finally the prepared surface must be drainable with no depressions before the asphalt paving interlayer system is placed. For bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavements the asphalt surface is first profile‑milled to the depth indicated in the plans (Section 492), and where milling cannot reach saw cutting and chipping with pneumatic hammers may be used; all cracks greater than ¾ inch are then cleaned and filled with non‑shrink grout or other Department‑approved material to enable bond, no standing water should remain after milling, the milled surface is completely cleared of loose debris, protected from oil or grease drippings and kept free of deleterious material, a final cleaning is conducted no more than 30 minutes before concrete placement using a compressed‑air stream that is free of oil, moisture and other contaminants and delivered at a minimum pressure of 100 pounds per square inch measured at the source, and immediately before placing the overlay ensure the asphalt surface is less than 120 °F and that any standing water or moisture has been eliminated. For thin asphalt overlay courses surface preparation begins by removing and disposing of all loose or foreign material, excess joint sealer and crack filler from the existing pavement, using a power broom when necessary and stripping any thermoplastic markings; traffic is kept off the cleaned surface to avoid contamination, the binder course is inspected and any irregularities are corrected, potholes and gouges greater than 1 inch in depth are repaired, all cracks or joints that exceed 1/8 inch in width are filled and sealed with PennDOT‑specified fillers and sealants, existing vertical elements such as curbs, structures and gutters that will contact the new asphalt are then painted with a uniform coating of emulsified asphalt (PennDOT Material Class TACK) or NTT/CNTT applied in two or more coats, or with the hot asphalt binder specified for the course, and finally a tack coat is applied to the clean surface per Section 460 and allowed sufficient time for the tack to break completely before any new paving material is placed. For heavy‑duty membranes surface preparation starts by removing all dirt and debris from every joint and crack that will be covered by the membrane, conditioning the surface by removing and disposing of loose and foreign material, excess joint sealer and crack filler and, if needed, sweeping with a broom; any joint or crack wider than ¼ inch is filled flush with the surface using PG 64S‑22 or PG 58S‑28, and at least twenty‑four hours before paving operations longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks one‑quarter inch and wider are sealed with the rubberized sealant called for in Section 469 while minimizing over‑band thickness and width and not exceeding Section 469 over‑band tolerances; after filling the pavement is verified as sound, dry and free of any debris, deteriorated material or loose particles, vertical surfaces such as curbs and gutters are painted with a uniform coating of emulsified asphalt—PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT applied in two or more applications—or the specified hot binder, followed by the required tack coat per Section 460; where indicated a scratch course is placed to fill wheel ruts and small depressions or a leveling course is installed before any new binder or wearing layer, and finally paving notches are created by milling the existing surface at tie‑in locations according to the Standard Drawings using the equipment prescribed in Section 491.3(a). the subgrade is then excavated to undisturbed soil, any fine material or ponding is removed, and the underlying soil is scarified at least six inches deep with a spring‑tooth rake while avoiding heavy equipment traffic, after which damaged areas are repaired; a detention basin is built by placing a minimum two‑inch layer of fine aggregate, wrapping the sides with non‑woven geotextile, and installing coarse aggregate in lifts not exceeding eight inches, compacted as specified; if required cracks are routed to create a reservoir one‑half inch deep and the surrounding pavement is cleaned for four to six inches on either side of the joint or crack, and immediately before sealing a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi measured at the source—or a hot compressed‑air lance—is applied to clean and dry damp cracks, taking care that the hot lance does not overheat or damage the surrounding pavement area. For manual asphalt patching the outlined area is first cut out and patched as marked by the Representative, each patch having a minimum area of one square foot; the area is excavated to sound, stable material with milling or planing allowed and all edges cut vertically, after removal of debris the area to be patched and the surrounding pavement are thoroughly cleaned of all loose and foreign material and all surfaces are thoroughly dried, then a tack coat is applied to the bottom and vertical surfaces of the prepared area as specified in Section 460.3(b) before any paving work proceeds. For concrete pavement partial‑depth repair a quality‑control plan is first prepared and approved per Section 106.03(a)2; in the presence of the Representative the concrete is sounded with a steel bar, drag chains or hammer to locate delamination and rectangular repair limits are laid out extending approximately three inches beyond the edges and meeting minimum dimensions of fifteen inches by ten inches by two inches; the perimeter of the repair area is then saw‑cut to at least a two‑inch depth for Type 1 repairs and all unsound concrete is removed to a minimum depth of two inches using a chipping hammer weighing not more than thirty‑five pounds, operated at a 30–60° angle to produce a rough bonding surface, when a milling machine is used the head must be a “V” head, flat head or rounded head and corners are squared with an additional two‑inch saw cut followed by chipping; after removal any exposed reinforcement in jointed reinforced concrete is cut and removed, dowel bars that show little corrosion receive a bond‑breaking material on all exposed surfaces, the area is re‑sounded to confirm complete delamination removal, and a final cleaning using an oil‑, moisture‑free compressed‑air stream at a minimum pressure of one hundred pounds per square inch is deployed within thirty minutes before placing the partial‑depth repair material. Staging and logistics

During surface preparation, the contractor follows an Installation Plan that defines the delivery‑truck routes, the required equipment footprint within lane closures, and traffic‑protection measures; the plan must show size, weight, load distribution and lifting capacity of all installation cranes, excavators and special lifting gear and demonstrate that the equipment will not impose detrimental loads on any pavement. A pre‑placement meeting confirms lifting capacity and footprint, reviews access routes and stockpile handling, while a shop‑drawing production note sheet records yard storage size, dunnage placement and shipping requirements for the precast slabs. The work zone limits are established by sawcutting with a diamond saw and lift‑out removal of existing pavement; the subbase is then prepared to within plus or minus 1/2 inch of grade before slab placement, and traffic may be opened only after grout strength criteria are met and joint differentials do not exceed 1/4 inch. Any temporary vertical drop‑off greater than 2 inches must be mitigated with a temporary asphalt wedge transition, and equipment movement is controlled by detailed rigging instructions, four symmetrically placed setting bolts or strongback beams for slab elevation.

In parallel, surface preparation of the asphalt interlayer system requires that the existing pavement be dried and cleaned of dirt, gravel, vegetation, oil and loose debris, that cracks be sealed and potholes repaired as specified, and that the surface be drainable with no depressions. Material delivery is controlled by keeping the interlayer system dry, wrapped, elevated off the ground and covered to protect it from weather, ultraviolet light, precipitation, chemicals and sparks; any damaged outer wrap must be discarded before installation. Traffic is limited to construction vehicles only—no traffic may travel on freshly applied asphalt material, and after placement only construction traffic required for placement is allowed. Turning of the paver and other vehicles must be done gradually and kept to a minimum, avoiding abrupt starts and stops to prevent damage to the interlayer system. [2]

Quality control and acceptance

Before any overlay is placed, the existing Portland cement concrete pavement must be evaluated through a series of surface‑preparation inspections. The Pavement Policy Manual requires that falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD) testing shall be conducted on the current PCCP prior to designing an overlay; the pavement is examined for slab movement or faulting so that slab stabilization or jacking can be specified when such movement is present; drainage conditions are checked and, if subsurface drainage problems exist, adequate subdrainage must be provided; and, depending on whether an unbonded concrete overlay is used, full‑depth patching of the original pavement with PCC or asphalt concrete base course may be required while cracking and seating operations are omitted for unbonded overlays [1].

For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slab installations, Section 529 requires that the subbase be shaped to a firm and even surface within plus or minus ½ inch of the required grade and contour, and any variation greater than ⅛ inch in the bedding material shall be corrected before slab placement; after slabs are set, the finished vertical differential across any joint may not exceed ¼ inch, transverse joint widths must lie between ¼ and ½ inch, and longitudinal joints may not exceed ¾ inch [2].

When a bonded concrete overlay is placed on a concrete‑surfaced pavement (Section 545), the scarified and cleaned surface shall be inspected to confirm removal of all loose debris, oil, grease and standing water; a compressed‑air stream used for final cleaning must be free of oil and moisture and deliver at least 100 psi measured at the source; the surface temperature immediately before overlay placement shall be less than 120 °F; joint backing material shall be placed to within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface in all existing transverse joints; and bond strength shall be tested in the presence of the inspector at seven days per ASTM C1583, with the area deemed defective if the average of three results is below 175 psi or any individual result is less than 160 psi [2].

For high‑friction surface treatment (Section 659), the inspector must verify that all pavement markings are protected and that oils, greases and other deleterious material have been removed by washing with a mild detergent and rinsing; subsequent air‑wash or vacuum sweep shall achieve at least 180 cubic feet per minute flow at 80 psi of clean dry compressed air to eliminate dust and debris; the concrete surface must meet the ICRI CSP 5 roughness requirement, visual verification chips shall be provided, and any cracks one‑quarter inch or wider shall have loose material removed and be pre‑treated with binder without overfilling [2].

The asphalt paving interlayer system (Section 466) requires that the pavement be dry and clean, free of dirt, gravel, vegetation and oil; all loose debris must be removed before placement; cracks shall be sealed according to Section 469.3(d) and the sealant allowed to cure prior to placement; potholes and other distresses shall be repaired as specified in Section 450 or as identified by the Representative; and the surface on which the interlayer is placed must be drainable with no depressions [2].

For a bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavement (Section 540), the milled asphalt surface shall be inspected to verify that no standing water remains, that all cracks greater than ¾ inch have been filled with an approved non‑shrink grout, and that loose debris, oil or grease drippings have been completely removed; a final cleaning performed no more than 30 minutes before placement must use a compressed‑air stream free of oil and moisture delivering at least 100 psi measured at the source while confirming the surface temperature is less than 120 °F; core depth testing in accordance with PTM No. 614 shall be conducted, and any core measurement may not be deficient by more than ¼ inch from the required depth [2].

Thin‑asphalt overlay courses (Section 412) require inspection of the existing pavement for loose or foreign material, excess joint sealer and crack filler, and residual thermoplastic markings, all of which shall be removed using a power broom if necessary; surface irregularities in the binder course must be corrected, potholes and gouges greater than 1 inch in depth shall be repaired, and any cracks or joints wider than ⅛ inch shall be filled and sealed with PennDOT‑specified fillers and sealants; all vertical surfaces that will contact the new asphalt shall receive a uniform coating of emulsified asphalt (PennDOT Material Class TACK) applied in two or more coats, and a tack coat must be applied to the cleaned surface with sufficient break time before overlay placement [2].

Unbonded concrete overlays (Section 548) require that unstable slabs or slab fragments that move or rock visibly under normal traffic loads shall be stabilized or repaired; joints shall be measured and milled when faulting exceeds ¼ inch, and faulting greater than ½ inch requires a scratch course or additional milling to reduce the faulting for an asphalt interlayer; the surface shall be swept with a power broom, vacuum truck, or other approved means to remove loose debris [2].

Crushed aggregate base courses (Section 312) require each layer to be compacted to not less than 100 % of the maximum density as defined by PTM No. 106 Method B and confirmed by field density testing in accordance with AASHTO T 191; cross‑sectional tolerances shall be checked using a template at intervals of no more than 25 feet, longitudinal smoothness assessed with a 10‑foot straightedge, correcting any irregularities that exceed ½ inch; a test hole representing the full depth of the completed base course shall be excavated for each 3,000 square yards or less to measure depth, and any area deficient by ½ inch or more shall be removed and replaced [2].

Dowel retrofits (Section 527) require verification that a power‑driven self‑propelled saw capable of two parallel cuts is used, that no equipment likely to cause spalling or aggregate fracture is employed, and that the saw cuts place the slot centre at the mid‑depth of the slab (or at the mid‑depth of the thinner slab when thickness varies) with three slots per wheel path aligned parallel to the roadway centreline; a jackhammer used to break out concrete may not exceed 30 pounds, and if damage occurs a 15‑pound jackhammer must replace it; the bottom of each slot must be level and parallel to the road surface, all loose or foreign material must be removed within 24 hours before concrete placement, slurry eliminated, and final cleaning completed no later than 30 minutes prior to placement, with any re‑cleaning performed if contamination occurs; when air blasting, the compressed‑air stream shall be at least 100 psi and free of oil, moisture or other contaminants [2].

Heavy‑duty membrane installations (Section 467) require removal of dirt and debris from all joints and cracks that will be under the membrane, proper filling of any joint or crack wider than ¼ inch flush with the surface using PG 64S‑22 or PG 58S‑28, and verification that the existing pavement is sound, dry, and free of any debris or deteriorated and loose materials while being prepared in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations [2].

Asphalt fog seal applications (Section 472) require confirmation that the road surface is clean and dry before fog seal placement; a motorized broom shall be used to sweep away excess seal‑coat aggregate, debris and dust, and the sweeping operation must be adjusted until a satisfactory clean surface is achieved without damaging the surface aggregate; inspectors also verify that all manholes, valve covers, sensors and similar items are covered to prevent adhesion of the emulsified asphalt, and that these protective coverings are removed before traffic is allowed [2].

Pervious asphalt pavement systems (Section 420) require the subgrade to be left undisturbed and permeable; any fine material or ponding must be removed and the underlying soil scarified to at least 6 inches using a spring‑tooth rake or equivalent light tractor, and damaged areas must be repaired to the Representative’s satisfaction before final inspection; two test sections of at least 225 ft² each are required, and their infiltration values measured in accordance with ASTM C1701 must exceed 200 inches per hour; the compacted thickness cannot be more than ¼‑inch less than the design thickness, and acceptance of the subgrade work is obtained only after the Representative inspects and approves the scarification depth, removal of fines, and overall condition [2].

Asphalt joint and crack sealing (Section 469) requires inspection to confirm that the pavement is clean, dry and free of debris or loose material on cracks, joints and the adjacent area; any required routing must produce a reservoir at least ½ inch deep, and the cleaned zone should extend 4 inches to 6 inches on each side of the joint or crack; a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi measured at the source (or an approved hot air lance) must be used to dry damp cracks without causing overheating damage to surrounding pavement [2].

Ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses (Section 489) require that longitudinal and transverse joints and cracks one‑quarter inch and wider shall be sealed, the sealant over‑band thickness and width shall not exceed Section 469 tolerances, thermoplastic or tape traffic markings thicker than 0.2 inches must be removed, any pavement impregnated with grease, oil or fuel shall be thoroughly cleaned, and all loose particles and unsuitable material shall be swept away before applying the UTFCEM and UTWC [2].

Concrete pavement partial‑depth repairs (Section 525) require the inspector to confirm the delamination extent by sounding the concrete in the presence of the Representative, verify that all unsound concrete has been removed to at least a 2‑inch minimum depth but not beyond half of the slab thickness, and check that the resulting face is sufficiently rough for bonding; the condition of any exposed dowel bars must be examined for corrosion, damage, or misalignment before deciding whether to apply bond‑breaking material or proceed with a full‑depth patch; finally, the final cleaning operation must be performed within 30 minutes of placement and, if compressed air is used, it must be free of oil and moisture and measured at a minimum pressure of 100 psi at the source [2].

For bonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545.3) acceptance is based on bond‑strength testing; the average of three tests must not be below 175 psi and each individual test must have a minimum strength of 160 psi, and edge‑slump tolerances are limited to a maximum of 1/4 inch in the outside six inches next to the shoulder and 1/8 inch in the outside six inches next to an adjacent lane. When bond‑strength criteria are not met the area is considered defective; additional tests at 100‑foot intervals must be performed to determine the extent of nonconforming work, and any overlay that is defective in depth, air content, bond strength or surface condition must be removed and replaced with a minimum removal length of six feet between transverse joints as specified in Section 501.3(t). For high‑friction surface treatment (Section 659(e)) surface preparation must result in a clean, dry substrate free of oils, greases and loose material; asphalt surfaces are required to be mechanically swept then vacuum‑swept with at least 180 cfm at 80 psi, while concrete must be shot‑blasted to ICRI CSP 5 and similarly air‑washed. Binder mixing tolerances are limited to ±2 % by volume and the applied binder film must be a uniform 50–65 mil thickness, with aggregate placed at 12–15 lb/yd². Any overfilled cracks must be scarified after cure and any contaminated binder must be removed and replaced at no extra cost; areas that show poor aggregate retention, delamination or discoloration are to be re‑prepared and retreated without charge. For bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 540.3) the milled pavement must be free of standing water; if standing water occurs immediate drainage measures acceptable to the Department must be provided. The surface must be cleaned of all loose debris, protected from oil or grease, and a final cleaning performed no more than 30 minutes before concrete placement; compressed‑air cleaning must deliver at least 100 psi measured at the source. Asphalt temperature immediately prior to overlay must be less than 120°F, and cracks larger than ¾ inch must be filled with approved non‑shrink grout before cleaning. Failure to meet these criteria constitutes nonconforming work and triggers corrective actions such as water removal, re‑cleaning, or repair of cracks before the concrete overlay can proceed. For 6.3 mm thin asphalt overlay courses (Section 412.3) acceptance requires that each mixture lot’s asphalt content and percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve fall within the single‑sample tolerances specified in Table B; any lot that fails these n=1 limits is deemed defective work. If the asphalt content or the sieve result is out of tolerance for two consecutive lots or three lots overall, production must be stopped until the cause is identified and an approved corrective solution is provided. The prescribed corrective action for nonconforming material is to remove and replace the defective portion with fresh, hot 6.3 mm paving mixture unless written direction from the District Executive states otherwise. For unbonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 548.3) acceptance is limited to joint faulting reduced to 1/4 inch or less before placing a geotextile interlayer; any joint showing greater than 1/4‑inch faulting must be milled. The geotextile must be laid with an 8‑inch overlap toleranced at ±2 inches, no more than three layers in one location, and its free edge extending at least 4 inches beyond the pavement edge while keeping fabric temperature below 120°F. For an asphalt interlayer a nominal depth of about 1 inch is required, joint faulting may not exceed 1/2 inch—if it does a scratch course or milling must be applied to reduce the faulting—and surface temperature also must not exceed 120°F before placement. Any surface defect larger than 4 square inches and deeper than 1 inch must be filled with an approved patching material prior to interlayer installation; otherwise the work is non‑conforming and must be repaired. For crushed‑aggregate base courses (Section 312.3) surface irregularities may not exceed ½ inch, depth measurements may not be deficient by ½ inch or more, and each compacted layer must achieve at least 100 % of the maximum density as defined in PTM No. 106 Method B. High spots beyond the half‑inch tolerance must be bladed off; if that fails the top layer over the area is to be loosened, excess material removed, moisture adjusted, reshaped and re‑compacted, with removal and reconstruction of the entire top layer required if those steps are unsatisfactory. Low spots are corrected by loosening the top layer, incorporating correctly graded fill, reshaping and re‑compacting, and must also be removed and rebuilt if the surface remains unacceptable. Depth deficiencies of ½ inch or more trigger removal and replacement of the deficient area, while density shortfalls first require additional compaction, then loosening, remoistening, reshaping and re‑compaction, and ultimately replacement of the affected layer if the required density cannot be obtained. For dowel retrofit work (Section 527.3) placement is accepted only when vertical tilt or horizontal skew does not exceed a 0.25‑inch maximum displacement of each end of the dowel relative to its midpoint, and both longitudinal translation along the joint direction and horizontal translation across the joint are limited to a 1‑inch maximum; any dowel retrofit that fails these dimensional limits is considered defective and must be removed and replaced at no additional cost to the Department. For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 501.3) surface preparation is accepted only when form faces remain within an allowable deviation of not more than 1/8 inch in 10 feet from the true plane and the forms do not deflect or deform more than 0.01 inch under a 1,700‑lb center load applied over a 4‑foot span; concrete that extrudes beyond the forms must be removed before adjacent placement, and any damage to the prepared base course, misplaced depth‑testing targets, or segregated concrete must be repaired, replaced, or discarded at no cost to the Department as corrective action. For pervious asphalt pavement systems (Section 420.3) the subgrade must be left undisturbed; erosion‑induced fines or ponding are removed with light equipment and then scarified at least six inches. Damaged or compacted zones must be satisfactorily repaired to the Representative’s satisfaction before work may proceed, and acceptance is withheld until the Representative inspects and accepts the repair. For test sections the average infiltration measured in accordance with ASTM C1701 must exceed 200 inches per hour and the compacted thickness may not be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness; if either value falls short the section must be reworked to meet those limits before acceptance. For concrete pavement partial‑depth repairs (Section 525.3) the repair area must be laid out rectangular with minimum dimensions of 15 inches in length by 10 inches in width by 2 inches in depth, and unsound concrete must be removed to at least 2 inches but not deeper than half the slab thickness. A chipping hammer weighing no more than 35 pounds operated at a 30‑60‑degree angle is required to achieve the rough surface, and final cleaning must occur within 30 minutes before placing the repair material. If removal exceeds the allowable depth or damages dowel bars so that the patch effectively becomes full‑depth, the work is considered nonconforming and the corrective action is to construct a full‑depth concrete pavement patch as specified in Section 516 at no additional cost. Safety and environmental controls

During surface preparation for high‑friction surface treatment, workers are exposed to hazards generated by high‑pressure air washing, shot blasting and vacuum sweeping that can create dust and debris, so the Representative may suspend all operations if there is excessive dust or poor visibility. Wind speeds above 15 mph or wind gusts of 20 mph or higher are prohibited because they can disperse loose aggregate onto traffic, and any loose aggregate that falls onto an active travel lane must be removed immediately after the traffic is temporarily held to protect the public. All equipment and traffic are barred from the area while the binder cures to prevent exposure, and the required cleaning steps—detergent wash, rinsing with potable water, hot‑air drying, and thorough vacuum sweeping at a minimum of 180 cfm and 80 psi—must be followed precisely. During surface preparation for fog seal, workers are at risk that emulsified asphalt can bond to uncovered utilities such as manholes, valve covers or sensors, so these items must be covered to prevent adherence. The public is at risk if traffic is allowed on the freshly applied emulsified asphalt before it has cured; therefore vehicles must be kept off until curing is complete. [2]

Curing, protection, and handoff

After surface preparation, the completed work is protected and cured in accordance with the requirements of each applicable specification. For SECTION 529—FULL DEPTH PRECAST/PRESTRESSED CONCRETE PAVEMENT SLABS any dowel or tie bars that become exposed at the end of the setting period must be protected; a minimum of two incompressible shims must be placed in each transverse joint when slabs are opened to traffic before the dowel and bedding grouts have cured, the grout used for dowels, ties, and undersealing must reach a compressive strength of 2,500 pounds per square inch before traffic is allowed, and finished vertical differentials across joints must not exceed 1/4 inch. In SECTION 545—BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF CONCRETE‑SURFACED PAVEMENTS the cleaned surface must be protected from oil or grease drippings; a final clean with a compressed‑air stream that is free of oil, moisture and other contaminants must be performed no more than 30 minutes before concrete placement, curing materials are applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and must remain in place until the pavement reaches the strength required for traffic opening, insulating blankets are used when forecast air temperature is 45°F or less and may be removed only after the air temperature exceeds 45°F or the average compressive strength reaches 3,600 psi, with surface temperature change not exceeding 40°F in any one‑hour period, and a curing compound is applied uniformly to form a solid white opaque film at a minimum rate of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 ft² or as the manufacturer recommends, whichever is greater, while joint faces must be kept free of the compound before sealant installation. SECTION 540—BONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF ASPHALT‑SURFACED PAVEMENT requires that the prepared asphalt surface be shielded from oil, grease and any other deleterious material before concrete placement; a final cleaning is required no more than thirty minutes prior to placement using a compressed‑air stream free of contaminants, curing materials are applied within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and maintained until the slab reaches a compressive strength of three‑thousand psi for traffic opening, insulating blankets are placed when ambient air temperature is forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or less and may be removed only when the temperature rises above forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or the measured strength exceeds three thousand psi, ensuring that the rate of surface temperature change does not exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit per hour, and a white‑pigmented liquid membrane‑forming curing compound is spread uniformly at a minimum of one gallon per 125 square feet, with joint faces protected from the compound until sealant installation. For SECTION 659—HIGH FRICTION SURFACE TREATMENT the contractor’s equipment and traffic are not allowed on the HFST during the curing period, the binder may not be exposed, separated, dried or otherwise hardened in a way that would impair aggregate retention, new asphalt pavements must wait for a mandatory 30‑day cure period before HFST can be applied, concrete surfaces or repairs younger than 28 days may not receive HFST, and after the binder treatment has cured a first aggregate cleanup is performed before traffic is reopened followed by a second cleanup between 15 and 30 days after placement. SECTION 466—ASPHALT PAVING INTERLAYER SYSTEM requires that the pavement be kept dry and free of contaminants after surface preparation, any crack‑filling material applied during this step must be allowed to cure before the interlayer system is placed, and the prepared surface must remain protected from moisture until the overlay is installed; rain‑wet surfaces are required to dry before paving can continue. In SECTION 412—SUPERPAVE MIXTURE DESIGN, CONSTRUCTION OF PLANT‑MIXED 6.3 MM THIN ASPHALT OVERLAY COURSES all existing vertical surfaces that will contact the new asphalt are painted with a uniform coating of emulsified asphalt (PennDOT Material Class TACK) or hot binder in two or more applications, and the tack must be allowed to break completely before any overlay material is placed, thereby protecting the newly laid pavement during its cure period. SECTION 548—UNBONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF CONCRETE‑SURFACED PAVEMENTS protects the separation layer before concrete placement by laying a geotextile interlayer flat, overlapping edges 8 inches ±2 inches on each side, limiting to no more than three layers in any location, extending at least 4 inches beyond the pavement edge, keeping it clean and free of loose debris, dampening but not saturating it, and ensuring its surface temperature does not exceed 120°F before overlay placement; when an asphalt interlayer is used a nominal 1‑inch Superpave mix is placed, joints with faulting over ½ inch are milled or given a scratch course, the pavement surface temperature also must not exceed 120°F before concrete placement and may be controlled by misting, white‑pigmented curing compound, lime‑water whitewash, or any other method approved by the Representative. SECTION 312—CRUSHED AGGREGATE BASE COURSE, TYPE DG requires that the placed base course be kept moist until the next layer is applied to ensure proper bonding, that the finished surface then be maintained according to Section 901.3(b), that only necessary local traffic and essential construction equipment be permitted on the base course, and that any marred or distorted areas be repaired or replaced; these actions protect the work until the surface course is placed. For SECTION 527—DOWEL RETROFIT and SECTION 501—PLAIN OR REINFORCED CEMENT CONCRETE PAVEMENTS the slot must be thoroughly cleaned with all loose material and slurry removed within 24 hours before concrete placement, a final clean completed within 30 minutes of placing the concrete, and any re‑cleaning performed if contamination occurs; air‑blasting shall use a compressed‑air stream of at least 100 psi free of oil, moisture and other contaminants, and after the slot is filled curing follows Section 516.3(j) with a QC plan listing procedures to ensure proper curing application and temperature monitoring; concrete shall not be placed when the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·h unless mitigation measures are employed, fog‑misting equipment shall atomize droplets without causing water deposits, concreting operations shall be discontinued when the descending air temperature away from artificial heat falls to 40°F (45°F for accelerated strength concrete) and resumed only above those limits, and in hot weather operations shall be stopped if the plastic concrete temperature exceeds 90°F after mixing. SECTION 467—HEAVY DUTY MEMBRANES protects the newly installed membrane by restricting traffic; vehicles may be allowed on the membrane only after a seven‑calendar‑day period, the surface must be sound, dry and free of debris before placement, and joints or cracks wider than 1/4 inch are filled flush to the surface with PG 64S‑22 or PG 58S‑28 to ensure proper bonding before curing begins. In SECTION 472—ASPHALT FOG SEAL FOR ASPHALT SEAL COATS the seal coat must be allowed to cure for at least two days before any fog‑seal emulsion is applied, the fog seal itself must be placed between two and thirty days after the last day of seal‑coat placement on a clean and dry surface that has been swept with a motorized broom, manholes, valve covers, sensors and similar items are covered to keep the emulsified asphalt from adhering and those protective coverings are removed before traffic is permitted, traffic may not reenter until the fog seal has fully cured, interim pavement markings are allowed after cure and permanent markings are delayed until three days after the last day of fog‑seal placement, application temperatures must exceed 60°F and rain or freezing within eight hours is prohibited. SECTION 420—PERVIOUS ASPHALT PAVEMENT SYSTEM protects the work after surface preparation by applying a thin, uniform coating of an approved emulsified asphalt such as AASHTO SS‑1, CSS‑1, SS‑1h, CSS‑1h, or TACK in two or more applications to any existing vertical surfaces that will contact the new mix; a tack coat between pervious lifts is prohibited, the wearing course is placed as soon as practical after the binder course, the mixture is allowed to cool to 100°F before any subsequent layer is added, and compaction of the pervious asphalt is limited to no more than four static‑roller passes. Finally, SECTION 450—MANUAL ASPHALT PATCHING requires that after cleaning and drying a tack coat be applied to the bottom and vertical faces of the prepared surface as required by Section 460.3(b), that the tack coat be allowed to cure before any asphalt wearing‑course or binder‑course material is placed, and that all excavated voids be filled before the close of each workday, providing additional protection for the unfinished portion until final compaction.
