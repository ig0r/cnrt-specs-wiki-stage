Functional Classification

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Functional Classification is a topic under Pavement Design.
functional-classificationfunctional-classifications
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

Functional Classification, expressed as a Maintenance Functional Classification (MFC), is a systematic roadway categorization that sets the minimum Terminal Serviceability Index (TSI) to be applied in any new‑pavement, reconstruction or overlay design [1]. It couples each functional class with prescribed reliability percentages and quantitative condition ratings such as the Present Serviceability Index/Rating, thereby linking performance thresholds directly to a road’s intended speed, volume, travel length, and the uncertainty inherent in traffic forecasts [1]. The classification further governs pavement type, thickness and minimum depth for ramp structures by requiring designers to use the class of the higher‑traffic‑volume roadway within an interchange, ensuring that structural provisions match the most demanding traffic loads [1].

Purpose and decision context

Functional Classification, expressed as a Maintenance Functional Classification, is used to decide which terminal serviceability index (TSI) – the minimum acceptable riding condition – shall be applied in pavement design; designers must select at least that TSI value for new, reconstructed or overlay projects and may choose a higher TSI when traffic volumes are significantly high for the roadway’s functional class. The same classification also guides the selection of the reliability percentage used in an AASHTO pavement design, with each functional class having a prescribed range that must be used for Department pavements, and designers should utilize the upper end of the range when the traffic prediction is less certain. In addition, Functional Classification (roadway classification) determines which pavement type and thickness shall be applied to ramp structures, requiring designers to adopt the classification of the higher‑traffic‑volume road in the interchange so that the ramp’s concrete or asphalt pavement and its thickness match those of that roadway. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 150–152)

Functional Classification addresses several interrelated needs in pavement engineering. In section 6.3, it resolves the need for setting performance thresholds that match a roadway’s intended role by linking a Maintenance Functional Classification (MFC) to a Terminal Serviceability Index (TSI); this linkage allows designers to specify the minimum ride‑quality level at which a pavement ceases to be adequate for its users, with higher‑function roads such as Interstates receiving higher TSI values that reflect their higher speeds, volumes and longer travel durations, while lower‑function roads are assigned correspondingly lower thresholds, thereby guiding design, rehabilitation and maintenance decisions. In section 6.4, it addresses the need to manage the variability and uncertainty inherent in traffic and performance predictions so that a pavement will survive its intended analysis period; by assigning each road type a specific reliability percentage, the department can apply the reliability ranges listed for each functional class—ranges that are more restrictive than earlier AASHTO guidance—and select the upper end of the range when traffic forecasts are especially uncertain. In section 6.6, it meets the need to ensure that ramp pavement structures match the demands of the most heavily used road within an interchange; consequently, a ramp shall be designed using the same roadway classification as the higher‑traffic‑volume roadway so that its pavement type, thickness and minimum depth are appropriate for the highest traffic loadings and any potential heavy‑truck development. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 150–152)

Scope and applicability

Functional Classification, expressed as a Maintenance Functional Classification (MFC), is applied when determining the Terminal Serviceability Index that governs pavement design and therefore must be known for any new‑pavement, reconstructed‑pavement or overlay design. In those project types the design shall use at least the terminal serviceability value associated with the roadway’s MFC, and a higher TSI may be selected when traffic volumes are significantly high for that classification; the MFC also defines the minimum riding quality considered acceptable for each functional class, so it is used during the design stage to set both initial and terminal serviceability targets based on the existing asset condition. Functional Classification is likewise applied when designing pavement structures for ramps within limited‑access highway interchanges, covering both proposed and existing ramp projects; at the design stage a ramp shall be designed using the same roadway classification as the higher‑traffic‑volume road to which it connects, which determines the required pavement type, thickness and minimum depths, and designers should give careful consideration to any anticipated development that could generate heavy truck traffic, adjusting the classification‑based design accordingly. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 150–152)

Decision criteria

The decision about a road’s Functional Classification is governed by the performance level expressed through the Terminal Serviceability Index; each Maintenance Functional Classification has an associated minimum serviceability that must be met, and higher‑order classes such as Interstates require a higher TSI because they serve higher speeds, volumes and longer trips. Consequently, design and rehabilitation choices are driven primarily by meeting or exceeding these serviceability thresholds rather than cost, schedule, or other factors. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 150–151)

Analysis methods and tools

Functional Classification relies on quantitative condition ratings such as the Present Serviceability Index and the Present Serviceability Rating, both expressed on a 0‑to‑5 scale, to gauge current pavement performance. The Terminal Serviceability Index provides the minimum ride quality required for each Maintenance Functional Classification, linking serviceability thresholds directly to road function. Non‑destructive testing tools—including a single locked wheel skid tester for friction, high‑speed laser profilers for riding quality, and falling weight deflectometers for structural capacity—supply the objective measurements that feed these indices and support rehabilitation selection. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 150–151)

The results for a given functional classification depend on the assumed reliability percentage, which reflects the variability and uncertainty inherent in pavement design; the AASHTO procedure assumes that both traffic‑prediction variation and performance‑prediction variation affect the outcome, so the reliability range listed in Table 6.5 for each class is used to capture those uncertainties, and because these ranges are more restrictive than earlier guidance, the method advises using the upper end of the range when the traffic prediction is less certain. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (p. 152)

Stakeholders and constraints

The selection of a road’s functional class is constrained by several design standards and policy requirements. Under the AASHTO 93 Pavement Design procedure, any new pavement, reconstruction or overlay must be performed using at least the terminal serviceability index (TSI) prescribed for the roadway’s Maintenance Functional Classification (MFC); a higher TSI may be employed when traffic volume is significantly high for that functional classification. Consequently, the MFC identified in the RMS and its associated TSI values limit which functional class can be assigned. In addition, the Department’s pavement design process imposes a reliability requirement that varies with functional classification; Table 6.5 establishes specific reliability percentage ranges for each class that are more restrictive than those in the 1993 AASHTO Guide, and the upper end of the range should be used when the traffic forecast is less certain, thereby further constraining the feasible classification. For ramp segments within interchanges, ramps should be designed using the same roadway classification as the higher‑traffic‑volume road to which they connect; the pavement type for the ramp shall be the same type as that higher‑traffic road, and the acceleration and deceleration sections must have the identical pavement type and thickness as the merging roadway, with design depths based on the highest traffic loading among them. [1]

Table 6.5 specifies the reliability percentage ranges for each functional classification.

Timing and implementation

Action for a given functional classification is triggered when the pavement’s present serviceability falls to the Terminal Serviceability Index (TSI) defined for its Maintenance Functional Classification, because at that point the pavement is no longer considered adequate; designers must then plan resurfacing, reconstruction or other rehabilitation. If traffic volumes are significantly high for that classification, a higher TSI value may be adopted, prompting earlier intervention, and conditions such as inadequate friction, geometric upgrades or insufficient capacity can also initiate treatment even before reaching the TSI. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 150–151)

Outcomes and follow-up

Success for a roadway’s functional classification is realized when its pavement condition meets or exceeds the Terminal Serviceability Index (TSI) associated with its Maintenance Functional Classification (MFC), thereby confirming adequacy for the users it serves. The required TSI value is applied during design, and new pavement, reconstructed pavement and overlay designs shall be performed using at least that terminal serviceability value; the next higher TSI value may be used when traffic volume is significantly high for the functional classification of the highway. Pavement condition is measured through the Present Serviceability Index (PSI), derived from roughness, cracks, patches and rut depth, or the equivalent Present Serviceability Rating (PSR), which provides the numeric basis to confirm that the TSI threshold has been satisfied. In parallel, success for a functional classification is defined by achieving the reliability percentage prescribed for that class in the AASHTO design framework; the required reliability is taken from the range listed in Table 6.5 for each functional classification and must fall within those limits to ensure the pavement will last through the analysis period. When traffic forecasts are uncertain, Utilize the upper end of the applicable range as a conservative measure, and compliance with these more restrictive ranges serves as the metric by which success is measured. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 150–152)

Monitoring of functional classification results is achieved by measuring pavement roughness, cracks, patches and rut depth to compute a Present Serviceability Index (PSI) or Present Serviceability Rating (PSR), and recording those values together with the Maintenance Functional Classification (MFC) in the RMS. The documented PSI/PSR and known MFC are then used to determine the Terminal Serviceability Index (TSI), which defines the minimum ride quality acceptable for that functional class, and design of new or rehabilitated pavement shall use at least that TSI value, with a higher TSI permissible when traffic volume is significantly high for the functional classification. This documented feedback loop informs later design and maintenance decisions by linking observed serviceability to the required thresholds for each functional category. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 150–151)
