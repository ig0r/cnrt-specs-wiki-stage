Rubblization

Part of: Rehabilitation is-subtopic-of
Rubblization is a topic under Rehabilitation.
rubblizationrubblizedrubblizingrubblized-projectsrubblizing-and-seating
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Rubblization is a rehabilitation technique applied to concrete pavements that have become severely distressed, especially when the underlying subgrade or subbase is weak or excessively moist [1]. The method fractures the existing slab into uniformly small interlocking fragments—limited to 12 inches for Type 1 or 8 inches for Type 2—and immediately seats and compacts these pieces into the base or sub‑base, creating a low‑stiffness, uniform foundation that provides only minimal structural support [1]. Because this rubblized layer offers the lowest level of support, a relatively thick asphalt or concrete overlay must be placed to achieve adequate load‑carrying capacity, thereby addressing the original distress, improving drainage, and extending the service life of the rehabilitated roadway [1].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Rubblization is used when a concrete pavement is badly distressed, especially when the underlying subgrade is weak or excessively moist. By breaking the slab into small pieces that are rolled into the base or subbase, the method provides only minimal support for an overlay, which therefore must be relatively thick to achieve adequate performance. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Rubblization is employed on badly distressed concrete pavements with poor or moist subgrade conditions; the slab is broken into small pieces and rolled into the base to provide a stable platform for an overlay. This treatment addresses the underlying deficiencies and secures good performance of the new surface, thereby extending the service life of the rehabilitated roadway. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Distress characterization and causes

Rubblization is used when the existing concrete pavement is badly distressed—typically where poor subgrade conditions and high moisture levels make patching uneconomical. The distress is severe enough to require breaking the slab into small pieces (12‑inch maximum for Type 1 or 8‑inch maximum for Type 2) that are rolled into the base/subbase before an overlay, providing the lowest level of support and thus demanding a thicker overlay. While the source details the location, extent and severity of this distress and the rubblizing procedure, it does not specify a progression rate or describe explicit safety or operational consequences beyond implying that untreated distress would degrade pavement performance. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Rubblization is employed when a concrete pavement has become severely distressed because the underlying subgrade provides inadequate or weak support and excess moisture infiltrates the subbase, creating drainage problems that lead to pumping, faulting, and loss of structural capacity. The technique fractures the slab into small (8‑ to 12‑inch) pieces that are rolled into the base or subbase, producing a low‑stiffness, uniform foundation that can accommodate a thick overlay designed to restore load‑carrying ability and extend service life despite the poor support conditions. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Applicability and treatment selection

Rubblization is used when an existing concrete pavement has deteriorated to a badly distressed condition and the underlying subgrade or subbase is weak or contains excessive moisture; these site conditions provide only minimal support, so a relatively thick overlay must be placed to achieve adequate structural capacity. The technique therefore targets severe pavement distress combined with poor or wet subgrade environments. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Rubblization is reserved for concrete pavements that are badly distressed and that suffer from poor subgrade conditions or high moisture in the underlying layers; under those adverse conditions it provides the lowest level of support and therefore requires a thick overlay. When the existing pavement is not severely damaged, the subgrade is stable, and moisture levels are acceptable, rubblizing adds unnecessary cost without performance benefit, so an alternative such as an unbonded jointed plain concrete (JPCP) overlay should be selected. In fact, if the pavement is already concrete, rubblization does not improve the performance of an unbonded concrete overlay and only increases project expenses. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Condition assessment and timing

To decide if rubblization is warranted, the pavement must first be inspected for severe distress such as extensive cracking, slab movement or faulting and for subgrade problems like poor drainage or high moisture content. A Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) test on the existing concrete pavement is required before any overlay design, providing the structural capacity data needed to judge whether the low‑support rubblize option is appropriate. When these visual signs of bad condition and the FWD results indicate inadequate support, the engineer can specify Type 1 or Type 2 rubblization based on the observed subgrade and moisture conditions. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Rubblization should be used when an existing concrete pavement is badly distressed, especially if the underlying subgrade or subbase is poor or contains a high moisture content. In such cases the rubblized pieces become the base for a new overlay, providing the lowest level of support and therefore necessitating the thickest overlay to achieve satisfactory performance. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Materials and repair details

Rubblization of a deteriorated concrete pavement begins by breaking the existing slab into small interlocking fragments that become part of the structural base. Pennsylvania specifies two rubblize types: Type 1 limits the broken pieces to a maximum size of 12 inches, while Type 2 limits them to a maximum of 8 inches. After fragmentation, the rubble is rolled or compacted into the existing base or sub‑base layer. Any material found unsuitable after breaking is removed and replaced with AASHTO No. 1 coarse aggregate rather than flexible‑base or concrete patch material.

Once the broken slab has been reseated, a continuous pavement‑base drain is installed to provide drainage beneath the new surface. Where the underlying base requires augmentation, AASHTO #1 aggregate is placed as needed to achieve the design thickness and support. Before placing the overlay, a leveling course of at least one inch in thickness is spread over the rubblized layer; this course includes a bond‑breaker membrane and serves to correct cross‑slope and create a uniform surface.

The design‑specified asphalt (or concrete) overlay is then placed at the thickness dictated by the project plans, extending across full‑depth shoulders that meet required shoulder widths and bridge clearances. Guide rails and other drainage structures are reset or replaced as necessary after the new pavement has cured. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Rubblization performance is governed first by the condition of the in‑place concrete slab; the pavement must be sufficiently distressed to allow it to be broken into interlocked fragments that can be reseated. When the underlying subgrade or sub‑base is poor, especially when moisture levels are high, the method is appropriate because the resulting rubblized layer provides only minimal structural support and therefore dictates the need for a thicker overlay. The breaking process produces either Type 1 pieces up to twelve inches in size or Type 2 pieces limited to eight inches; these fragments are then rolled into the existing base or sub‑base before the new surface is placed. Compatibility of the underlying layer is essential: if the original base material cannot support the rubblized mass, it must be repaired with AASHTO No. 1 coarse aggregate rather than flexible base course or concrete patches. Continuous pavement‑base drainage must be provided to maintain stability of the rubblized mass. Prior to overlay placement a proper leveling course and bond‑breaker are installed, and any cross‑slope deficiencies are corrected, ensuring that the newly seated rubblized layer can adequately support the designed thickness of the overlying asphalt or concrete. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Treatment procedures

Before any breaking begins the contractor must verify that existing drainage problems have been corrected, because rubblization is typically selected for heavily distressed concrete slabs on weak or moisture‑prone subgrades. The chosen rubblize type determines the maximum fragment size: a Type 1 operation limits pieces to 12 inches, while a Type 2 operation limits them to 8 inches. Using the appropriate equipment the slab is fractured into small interlocked fragments of the specified size and the broken material is immediately seated in place. The fragments are then rolled and compacted to form a uniform base or sub‑base layer that will support the new overlay. If, after compaction, the rubblized mass is judged unsuitable, it is removed and replaced with AASHTO No. 1 coarse aggregate before proceeding. Once a satisfactory base exists, a continuous pavement‑base drainage system is installed to provide long‑term moisture control. Over the compacted base a leveling course is placed; the layer must be at least one inch thick, incorporates a bond breaker, and is shaped to correct any cross‑slope deficiencies. After the level has been achieved, the design‑specified asphalt (or concrete) overlay is laid together with full‑depth shoulders, any required widening fill, and the resetting or replacement of guide rails and other drainage structures. Because the rubblized mass functions as a structural base, no curing period is required after placement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Rubblization is reserved for heavily distressed concrete pavements that sit on poor or moist subgrades, conditions that make other rehabilitation methods less effective. The process can be performed as a Type 1, breaking the slab into pieces no larger than 12 inches, or as a Type 2, limiting fragments to 8 inches; the choice is left to the district pavement engineer based on project conditions. After the concrete is broken, the resulting rubblized material is rolled into the base or subbase layer before the new overlay is placed, and any unsuitable material may be replaced with AASHTO No. 1 coarse aggregate prior to overlaying. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Quality and workmanship

A satisfactory rubblization is confirmed when the concrete pavement has been fully fractured into pieces that meet the size limits prescribed for the chosen rubblize type—no fragment larger than twelve inches for a Type 1 treatment or eight inches for a Type 2 treatment—and those fragments have been properly seated and compacted into the underlying base or sub‑base. The broken material must be followed by placement of a leveling course that provides at least one inch of thickness, incorporating any necessary cross‑slope correction, before the asphalt overlay is placed. In addition, the base repair must include the specified aggregate, maintain continuous drainage, and provide full‑depth shoulders in accordance with the design specifications. Where unsuitable material has been removed during the process, it must be replaced with AASHTO No. 1 coarse aggregate prior to overlay placement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Performance and service life

Rubblization is used when a concrete pavement is badly distressed, especially if the subgrade is weak or excessively moist; the process crushes the slab into uniformly small pieces (12‑inch or 8‑inch maximum) that are rolled into the base or subbase before an overlay. Because the rubblized material provides only the lowest level of support, a thicker asphalt or concrete overlay is required, but this treatment directly addresses the underlying distress and moisture problems and therefore promotes better overall pavement condition, functionality, and durability, extending the service life of the rehabilitated section. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)
