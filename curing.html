Curing

Part of: Portland Cement Concrete is-subtopic-of
Curing is a topic under Portland Cement Concrete.
field-curingcuring-conditionswax-based-white-pigmented-curing-compound
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Curing is the controlled retention of moisture or a membrane‑forming compound on freshly placed concrete surfaces immediately after finishing and texturing—typically within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation—to prevent premature water loss and to regulate temperature while the material hydrates to the compressive strength required for traffic opening [1]. It functions by maintaining the slab at acceptable temperatures (not exceeding 160 °F and generally above 40 °F) and by using covers, blankets, or liquid membranes that remain in place until the specified strength is achieved, with hourly temperature monitoring to ensure changes do not exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit per hour [1].

Purpose and applicability

For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements constructed by the slip‑form method (Section 501), curing is appropriate after finishing and texturing when it can be applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and then maintained until the pavement reaches the strength required for opening to traffic as specified in Section 501.3(q). The same curing method shall be used throughout the job unless the Representative directs a change, and concrete temperature may not exceed 160°F during curing. If normal curing cannot be started as planned, an intermediate monomolecular‑film cure may be applied before resuming the regular method, and alternative approved methods such as white polyethylene sheets, burlap‑backed covers, or PAMS liquid membrane may be used instead. In cold weather when forecast air temperature is expected to fall below 50°F, thermometers must monitor concrete; a temperature between 40°F and 35°F requires extending the cure period by an additional day, while any reading below 35°F renders the concrete defective. For unbonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 548), curing is appropriate when the overlay is finished and textured, provided that curing materials are applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and remain in place until the pavement reaches the strength required for opening to traffic as set out in Section 548.3(q). Insulating blankets must be used when air temperature is 40°F or less and may be removed once temperature exceeds 40°F or average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, with removal performed so that surface temperature does not change more than 40°F in any one‑hour period. A white‑pigmented liquid membrane‑forming curing compound shall be applied as a uniform opaque coating at the minimum rate of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 150 ft² for pavements over 6 inches thick or per 125 ft² for pavements 6 inches or less; joint faces must be protected from the compound or cleaned before sealant. If curing is performed by any method other than this white‑pigmented compound, the covering must be removed at each joint location before sawing and replaced promptly, with removal not exceeding a half hour. For bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavement (Section 540), curing with a white‑pigmented liquid membrane‑forming compound is appropriate when it is applied after finishing and texturing within 30 minutes of bleed water dissipation and retained until the slab reaches the minimum required compressive strength of 3,000 psi for opening to traffic as defined in Section 540.3(q). Insulating blankets should be used instead of or in addition to curing compound when air temperature is 45°F or less, and may be removed once temperature exceeds 45°F or the concrete’s average compressive strength surpasses 3,000 psi provided the surface temperature does not change more than 40°F in any one‑hour period. If a curing method other than the specified white‑pigmented compound is employed, the curing material must be locally removed from joint locations before sawing and replaced afterward. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502), curing is required immediately after the final rolling and in‑field density testing; the RCC surface must be kept moist until a chosen curing method—water cure, white polyethylene sheeting, curing compound, or cold‑weather curing—is applied, and that method shall be maintained for a minimum of 72 hours and until the pavement reaches a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi. If those conditions have not been satisfied, an alternative moisture‑retention treatment such as water misting, sheet covering, or applying a curing compound should be used instead of allowing the surface to dry. For roller‑compacted concrete base courses (Section 323), curing with traditional curing compounds is not appropriate; instead a uniform, void‑free layer of emulsified asphalt shall be applied over the RCC surface using equipment approved by the Representative and that will not damage or mar the pavement, and only after this cure period may the RCC pavement be opened to local traffic. For concrete pavement partial‑depth repairs (Section 525), curing is appropriate for Class AA Cement Concrete Modified repairs by spraying a white membrane‑forming curing compound with an atomizing tank as specified; Class AA Cement Concrete Accelerated repairs receive the same compound but must be insulated or heated if ambient air temperature drops below 80°F and the repair area’s temperature must be checked at least hourly to ensure no change exceeds 40°F in any one‑hour period, a temperature excursion beyond that renders the work defective. For Rapid‑Set Concrete Patching Material, curing shall follow the manufacturer’s recommendations rather than using the standard membrane compound. For cement‑treated permeable base courses (Section 303), curing must begin immediately after compaction, using a white membrane‑forming curing compound applied at a rate of 1 gallon per 100 ft² ±10% of the surface area, or alternatively covering the CTPBC with white polyethylene sheeting. The curing material shall be maintained for 24 hours, and if the CTPBC temperature falls below 40°F during that period the cure time is extended by an additional 24 hours. For concrete pavement patching (Section 516), curing with a liquid‑membrane forming compound is required for normal concrete patches as specified in Section 501.3(l); either a Type 2 white pigmented curing compound or the traditional white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene (PAMS) compound may be used. For rapid‑set concrete patching material repairs, the contractor must follow the manufacturer’s curing recommendations, and the date of placement is scribed on the fresh surface before any curing material is applied. For plain cement concrete base courses (Section 301), a liquid membrane forming curing compound may be used only in the black formulation and only when the base course will not be exposed to traffic before the surface course is placed; the white formulation is prohibited. If the black compound ceases to perform satisfactorily as a curing agent, its use must be stopped and curing should be continued by other methods as specified elsewhere in the specifications. For high‑friction surface treatment (Section 659), curing is appropriate only after the pavement has reached its minimum age; new asphalt pavements may not receive HFST until after a mandatory 30‑day cure period, and concrete surfaces or repairs may not receive HFST when the surface is less than 28 days old. When those ages have not been met, postponing HFST is required. For fine aggregate that is steel slag (Section 703), curing is appropriate after gradation preparation when the material is placed in a controlled stockpile no larger than 30,000 tons, fully soaked with water and kept uniformly moist for at least six months before testing; if the average total volumetric expansion measured by PTM No. 130 exceeds 0.50 %, the material must remain in cure for a further two months or more until the criterion is met. Other fine aggregates such as natural sand or manufactured sand have no curing requirement. [1]

Required inputs

For slip‑form paving (Section 501) curing shall be started within 30 minutes after bleed water dissipates and continued until the pavement reaches the compressive‑strength level required for opening to traffic; the contractor shall have on hand appropriate curing materials such as white polyethylene sheeting, burlap‑backed or fiber‑backed sheets, a liquid membrane‑forming compound (PAMS or black compound) applied at the specified rate, and a monomolecular‑film curing agent sprayed in a light fog according to the manufacturer’s water‑to‑curing‑agent ratio, with burlap coverings kept saturated for the entire cure period. For accelerated‑strength concrete the pavement shall be insulated or heated and its temperature monitored at least hourly so that any change does not exceed 40 °F in one hour, and the concrete surface temperature may never rise above 160 °F during curing; cold‑weather curing requires air temperatures not below 40 °F (the cure period shall be extended if the high‑low thermometer falls between 40 °F and 35 °F, and the pavement shall be considered defective if it falls below 35 °F) and insulation blankets when ambient is ≤45 °F for slabs less than 6 inches thick. For unbonded concrete overlays (Section 548) curing materials shall be applied immediately after finishing and texturing, within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation, and retained until the pavement reaches the strength required for traffic opening; when air temperature is 40 °F or less adequate insulating blankets shall be used to prevent rapid heat loss, and the blankets may be removed only when the air temperature rises above 40 °F or when the average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, provided that removal does not cause a surface‑temperature change greater than 40 °F in any one‑hour period; if a white PAMS liquid membrane‑forming curing compound is specified it shall be spread uniformly to give solid white opaque coverage at a minimum rate of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 150 ft² for pavements thicker than 6 inches and per 125 ft² for pavements 6 inches or thinner, with the option to apply in two equal passes if conditions require. For bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavement (Section 540) a white‑pigmented liquid membrane‑forming curing compound shall be applied within 30 minutes after bleed water has dissipated at a minimum coverage of one gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 ft², and insulating blankets shall protect the fresh slab whenever ambient air temperature is 45 °F or less; the blankets may be removed once the air temperature exceeds 45 °F or when the average compressive strength surpasses 3,000 psi, provided that removal does not cause a surface‑temperature change greater than 40 °F in any one‑hour period, and the curing compound shall be applied uniformly to all exposed surfaces and may be reapplied after joint sawing if conditions warrant. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502) the surface shall be kept moist immediately after final rolling and in‑field density testing, and a curing regime shall be maintained for at least 72 hours until the concrete develops a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi; moisture may be supplied by water cure using a water truck equipped with misting spray nozzles, soaking hoses or sprinklers, by covering with white polyethylene sheeting, or by applying a curing compound that produces a uniform solid opaque coverage, and cold‑weather curing methods are also permitted. For cement concrete (Section 704) each batch shall be represented by sufficient 6‑in × 12‑in cylinders placed in a PTM No. 611 curing tank or equivalent boxes capable of keeping the temperature adjacent to the cylinders in the range of 60 °F to 80 °F for the first 24 ± 2 hours, and a Bluetooth‑enabled digital system shall record temperature at least hourly within 30 °F to 120 °F with an accuracy of 1 °F; the fresh mix shall meet a selected slump target and be kept within 1½ inches of that target, and air content and concrete temperature shall be tested on the first three trucks and thereafter as required, with any slump exceeding the upper limit triggering rejection. For Class AA cement‑concrete, Modified (Section 525) a white membrane‑forming curing compound shall be applied with an atomizing spray tank that has an adjustable nozzle, the solution shall be agitated before each application, and insulation or heating shall be provided if ambient air temperature drops below 80 °F during curing; temperature shall be monitored at least hourly so that the repair area does not experience a curing temperature change in excess of 40 °F within any one‑hour period, and test cylinders shall be cured under the same conditions. For pervious concrete pavement (Section 520) curing shall begin within 20 minutes of concrete discharge, the fresh surface shall be completely covered with polyethylene sheeting that is thoroughly secured at all exterior edges and interior laps, and the cover shall be maintained for a minimum of 7 days; the cured pavement must meet mixture properties of a void structure of 20 % ± 5 % and a hardened density not exceeding 130 lb/ft³ with a tolerance of ± 5 lb/ft³; curing is only permitted when air temperatures are not projected to fall below 40 °F within the 24‑hour period after placement, and the seven‑day cure must include ambient temperature exceeding 55 °F at any time before traffic is allowed. For precast concrete units (Section 714) a quality‑control plan based on ACI 308 shall be followed, primary cure shall be maintained in the forms until the unit reaches at least 70 % of its specified 28‑day compressive strength, and after stripping secondary curing may be applied by any approved method while the units are kept in an environment of no less than 50 °F until they attain 80 % of the 28‑day minimum strength shown on the shop drawing; placement in temperatures below 32 °F is prohibited unless the temperature difference between the unit and ambient air is under 30 °F, and the units remain stored until full 28‑day compressive strength is achieved, after which a protective coating—either boiled linseed oil or a non‑discoloring penetrating sealer—is applied before shipment. For cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303) curing shall begin immediately after compaction and may be performed either by applying a white membrane‑forming curing compound at a rate of 1 gallon per 100 ft² ± 10 % of the surface area or by covering with white polyethylene sheeting, the material shall remain in place for 24 hours, and if the temperature falls below 40 °F during that period the cure time shall be extended an additional 24 hours; if the temperature drops to 32 °F within 72 hours of placement the base must be removed and replaced at no cost. For plain cement concrete base course (Section 301) only a black liquid membrane‑forming curing compound may be used, the white version is prohibited, and the black compound may be applied only while the surface remains inaccessible to traffic until the overlying surface course is placed; if the compound becomes unsatisfactory it shall be discontinued and another curing method employed. For bonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545) curing material must be applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and maintained until the concrete reaches the strength specified for opening to traffic, insulating blankets shall be used when forecast air temperature is 45 °F or less and may be removed only after the air temperature exceeds 45 °F or the average compressive strength surpasses 3,600 psi provided that surface‑temperature change does not exceed 40 °F in any one‑hour period, and the curing compound shall be spread uniformly as a solid white opaque film at a minimum of 1 gallon (±0.1) per 125 ft² or the higher manufacturer rate; joint faces must be protected from the compound or cleaned before sealant, and a second equal application may follow sawing if conditions warrant. For chemically stabilized full‑depth reclamation material (Section 344) the reclaimed layer shall be kept damp by daily water application or by spraying an emulsified asphalt primer followed by a fog seal at a residual rate between 0.02 and 0.10 gallons per square yard, or by using a tack coat, the applications shall occur while the material is at its optimum moisture content but not exceeding more than 3 %, heavy traffic shall be prohibited during the required 7‑day cure period, and appropriate signs must be posted. For roller‑compacted concrete base course (Section 323) curing does not permit any curing compounds; instead a uniform, void‑free layer of emulsified asphalt shall be placed over the compacted surface as directed in Section 460.3(b), using equipment approved by the Representative that does not damage or mar the RCC surface. Acceptance of roller‑compacted concrete pavement (Section 502) requires each lot to achieve an average density of at least 98 % of the maximum wet density with no individual test below 96 %, a three‑day compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi (or seven days if lower) to discontinue field cure, and a final 28‑day compressive strength of at least 3,500 psi; pavement depth must be within ¼ inch of the planned thickness, and if any threshold is not met curing shall continue. For cold‑recycled asphalt base course stabilized with emulsified asphalt (Section 341) the material shall remain at least two weeks before placement of a wearing or binder course—or until its average moisture content drops below 2 %—and three random core samples shall be taken and their moisture measured according to PTM No. 749, with any core holes backfilled and compacted using the same material or an approved alternate; the same moisture‑content threshold applies to foamed‑asphalt‑stabilized CRABC, and the mix must retain adequate total liquids to ensure thorough mixing of reclaimed aggregate with the stabilizing agent. For pervious concrete pavement systems (Section 520) curing requires a concrete curing material as specified in Section 711.1(a) or (b) together with a quality‑control plan that addresses cold‑weather and hot‑weather site conditions; for cold weather the plan shall provide protection from freezing throughout the 7‑day curing period in accordance with ACI 306.1, and for hot weather it shall detail the amount of hydration‑stabilizing admixture to be added in 5 °F increments. The pervious concrete mix to be cured shall be designed to a plastic unit weight between 115 lb and 130 lb and a water‑cement ratio of 0.36 ± 0.04. For high‑friction surface treatment (Section 659) the binder components shall be mixed to within ±2 % of the manufacturer’s volume specifications, applied at a uniform thickness of 50–65 mils, and covered with aggregate spread at a rate of 12–15 pounds per square yard; new asphalt pavements shall not receive HFST until after a mandatory 30‑day cure period, concrete surfaces or repairs less than 28 days old are excluded, and contractor equipment and traffic shall be prohibited on the treatment during the curing interval. Water used for mixing or curing cement concrete, mortar, or grout (Section 720) shall conform to ASTM C1602 and shall be reasonably clean, free from vegetable matter, oil, acid, alkali, sugar, or any other substances injurious to the finished product. Bridge approach slabs (Section 505) shall be cured by water for a minimum of 7 days while being protected with covers of double‑thickness burlap, which provide continuous moisture to the concrete surface and retain that moisture throughout the specified period. Slurry seal mixtures (Section 482) shall be placed at an application temperature not less than 50 °F and not more than 125 °F so that the material can achieve a firm condition; after placement traffic shall be kept off the surface until directed, allowing the seal to completely cure. Steel‑slag fine aggregate stockpiles (Section 703) shall be limited to a maximum of 30,000 tons, shall be completely soaked with water before or during stockpiling, and shall be maintained uniformly moist for not less than 6 months; the representative shall accept the stockpile only if the average total volumetric expansion measured by PTM No. 130 is less than 0.50 %, and if that criterion is not met the stockpile shall continue curing for a minimum of 2 additional months before retesting. Long‑life concrete pavement (Section 530) shall be cured with a method that protects joints and prevents moisture loss from concrete in and adjacent to joints during and after joint sawing; the mix used for curing shall meet the plastic air content requirement of 7.0 % plus or minus 1.5 %, and any truck failing this test shall not be placed until three consecutive passing results are obtained; a concrete temperature control plan shall be included whenever concrete temperatures at placement exceed 80 °F to keep temperatures within acceptable limits during curing. Field curing requirements are defined separately for each pavement type and specification section, and the applicable design details, dimensional limits, tolerance criteria, and pre‑construction testing procedures are enumerated in the respective sections. For slip‑form cement concrete pavements (Section 501.3(g)) the contractor must apply curing materials after finishing and texturing within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation, must keep the concrete temperature at or below 160°F during any curing operation, and when ambient conditions are cold the surface temperature must be maintained at a minimum of 40°F; if a high‑low thermometer records a temperature between 40°F and 35°F the cure period is extended one day, while any reading below 35°F renders the work defective. Sheet covers used for slip‑form curing must be wide enough to cover the surface and sides, must overlap a minimum of 12 inches, and must be sealed tightly on all seams; liquid‑membrane compounds applied for this method shall be placed at a rate of 1 gallon per 150 sq ft or as directed by the manufacturer. For unbonded concrete overlays (Section 548.3(i)) curing materials also must be applied after finishing and texturing within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and retained until the pavement reaches the strength level required for opening to traffic; when air temperature is 40°F or less insulating blankets shall be provided and may be removed only when the air temperature rises above 40°F or when the average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, and removal shall not cause the concrete surface temperature to change more than 40°F in any one‑hour period. A white‑pigmented liquid‑membrane curing compound for unbonded overlays shall be applied uniformly at a minimum rate of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 150 sq ft for pavements thicker than 6 inches and at a minimum rate of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 sq ft for pavements 6 inches or less, or according to the manufacturer’s recommendation whichever is greater, may be placed in two equal passes with the second pass after sawing, and joint faces must be protected from the compound or cleaned before sealant installation. For bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 540.3) the comprehensive QC plan shall specify that curing materials be applied after finishing and texturing within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and left in place until a minimum compressive strength of 3,000 psi is verified by cylinder testing or the maturity method; when ambient temperature is 45°F or less insulating blankets are required and may be removed only after the slab’s average compressive strength exceeds 3,000 psi or the air temperature rises above 45°F, with removal not causing a surface‑temperature change greater than 40°F in any one‑hour period. The curing compound for bonded overlays shall be applied uniformly as an opaque white film at a minimum rate of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 sq ft—or the manufacturer’s recommendation, whichever is higher—and may be placed in two equal applications with the second after joint sawing. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502.3(a)) the contractor must keep the freshly rolled surface moist immediately after final rolling and in‑field density testing, must maintain the selected curing method for a minimum of 72 hours while confirming that the pavement has reached at least 3,000 psi compressive strength as determined per the specified test, and acceptable methods include water cure with a misting‑equipped truck, white polyethylene sheeting, or a curing compound applied immediately after rolling to give a uniform opaque film; cold‑weather curing follows the provisions of Section 501.3(l). Prior to full‑scale construction a minimum 50‑foot test section shall be built to verify mix consistency, placement equipment performance, density acceptance and the curing protocol. For concrete cylinders (Section 704.1(d)) cylinder molds shall be 6‑inch by 12‑inch with tight‑fitting domed caps, curing tanks or boxes must be capable of maintaining a temperature adjacent to the field‑cured cylinders between 60°F and 80°F for the first 24 ± 2 hours, and a Bluetooth‑enabled digital recorder shall log temperature at least hourly within a range of 30°F–120°F with an accuracy of 1°F; all curing cylinders must be field‑cured in accordance with PTM No. 611 Section 12.2 for the specified period, after which they may be moved to a secure area while maintaining the same protection. The contractor shall submit a QC plan and a field‑operation QC plan before construction begins—at the preconstruction conference or at least three weeks before the first concrete pour—and all testing equipment must be calibrated no more than two weeks before use and recalibrated every two weeks in the inspector’s presence. For Class AA cement‑concrete partial‑depth repairs (Section 525.3(f)) a white membrane‑forming curing compound shall be applied with an atomizing spray tank that has an adjustable nozzle, the solution being agitated before each application; test cylinders shall be cured under the same conditions as the repair, insulation or heating shall be provided if ambient air temperature drops below 80°F, and the curing temperature shall be monitored at least hourly so that any change does not exceed 40°F within a one‑hour period, otherwise the work is considered defective. For pervious concrete pavement systems (Section 520.3) two test sections of at least 225 sq ft each must be produced before curing begins using the approved mix design; thickness may not be more than ¼ inch less than the design thickness, void content shall be 20% ± 5%, hardened density shall stay within ±5 lb/ft³ and shall not exceed 130 lb/ft³, and infiltration testing per ASTM C1701 must show an average rate exceeding 200 in/hr. Curing then must start within 20 minutes of concrete discharge, the surface must be completely covered with polyethylene sheeting that is securely sealed at all edges and laps, and the cover shall be maintained for a minimum of 7 days; traffic may not be allowed until after those 7 days have passed and the ambient temperature has exceeded 55°F. For precast concrete products (Section 714.8) an approved QC plan developed per ACI 308 shall govern curing and protective coating; primary cure shall be maintained in the forms until the unit reaches 70% of its 28‑day strength, with any reduced stripping time or strength calculations submitted as a QC‑plan addendum sealed by a Professional Engineer, secondary curing shall be performed by approved methods while keeping the units in an environment of at least 50°F until they achieve 80% of the 28‑day minimum compressive strength shown on the shop drawing, and after secondary cure the units may not be placed in temperatures below 32°F unless the temperature differential between unit and air is less than 30°F. For cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303.3) curing must start immediately after compaction and may be achieved either with a white membrane‑forming curing compound applied at a rate of one gallon per 100 sq ft ±10% of the surface area or by covering the surface with white polyethylene sheeting; the curing material shall remain in place for 24 hours, and if the CTPBC temperature falls below 40°F during that period the cure time must be extended an additional 24 hours, while a drop to 32°F within 72 hours of placement may cause the department to require removal and replacement at no cost. For concrete pavement patching (Section 516.3) a Type 2 white pigmented liquid membrane‑forming curing compound is allowed in place of the standard white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene (PAMS) compound, rapid‑set concrete patch repairs shall be cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, and QC cylinders are also cured per the manufacturer’s guidance; the Representative will permit opening to traffic only when cylinder testing shows that the concrete has reached a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi as specified in PTM No. 604. For plain cement concrete base course (Section 301.3) field curing shall be performed with a liquid membrane‑forming curing compound that complies with the color restrictions; white compound is prohibited and black compound may be used only if the base course will not be accessible to traffic before the surface course is placed, and if the selected compound proves unsatisfactory its use must be stopped and curing continued by alternative methods as specified. For bonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545.3) curing must begin within thirty minutes after bleed water has dissipated and continue until the pavement reaches the compressive strength required for traffic opening, a minimum of 3,600 psi as defined in 545.3(l); curing material shall be applied uniformly to give a solid white opaque film at a minimum rate of one gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 ft² or the greater amount recommended by the manufacturer, may be placed in two equal applications if sawing is required, and joint faces must be protected from the compound or cleaned before sealant placement; when forecast air temperature is forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or less insulating blankets shall be provided and may be removed only when ambient temperature exceeds forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or when average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, and removal must not cause the concrete surface temperature to change more than forty degrees Fahrenheit in any one‑hour period. For full‑depth reclamation (Section 344.3(e)) the reclaimed layer must be cured until the required seven‑day strength is achieved and heavy traffic is prohibited for that full seven‑day period; during cure the surface shall be kept damp either by daily water application or by applying an emulsified asphalt primer followed by a fog seal at a residual rate between 0.02 and 0.10 gallons per square yard (or Tack Coat as required), and any such application must be made while the material remains at optimum moisture content but not exceeding more than three percent; before traffic is allowed, proof‑rolling with a vehicle comparable to the heaviest expected load or confirming strength by testing is required, and light vehicles under five tons may be permitted after about two hours once proof rolling shows no damage. For roller‑compacted concrete base course (Section 323.3) curing compounds are prohibited and a uniform void‑free layer of emulsified asphalt must be placed over the RCC surface in accordance with Section 460.3(b) using equipment that does not damage, mar, or alter the compacted surface and that has Representative approval; once the prescribed cure period has elapsed the RCC pavement may be opened to local traffic prior to applying the asphalt wearing course. Acceptance of roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502.3(m)) is governed by a sequence of compressive‑strength tests on cylinders molded per ASTM C1435 and PTM No. 604, whereby if the three‑day result reaches at least 3,000 psi the field cure may be discontinued unless otherwise directed, the same threshold applies to the seven‑day test, and a minimum of 3,500 psi must be achieved at twenty‑eight days for acceptance; density acceptance requires that the final average cannot be less than ninety‑eight percent of the maximum wet density and no individual test fall below ninety‑six percent of that maximum, with any deficiency prompting core sampling for strength verification, and pavement depth checked by coring in accordance with PTM No. 614, the measured thickness having to be within one‑quarter inch of plan depth otherwise additional cores are taken and the average compared to the same tolerance. For cold‑recycled asphalt base course (Section 341.3) emulsified‑asphalt stabilized material must be cured for at least two weeks before a wearing or binder course is placed, and both emulsified and foamed‑asphalt stabilized CRABC must be cured until the average moisture content is less than two percent; prior to placement of the next layer a minimum of three random core samples each weighing at least 1,200 grams shall be dry cut or extracted according to PTM No. 1 and their moisture determined per PTM No. 749, with any core holes back‑filled and compacted using CRABC or an approved alternate material. For pervious concrete pavements (Section 520) curing is governed by the requirement to use a concrete curing material as specified in Section 711.1(a) or (b) and by a QC Plan that must be prepared, submitted at the start of the project and approved before any production begins; cold‑weather provisions must protect the pavement from freezing throughout the seven‑day curing period according to ACI 306.1, while hot‑weather plans must detail the amount of hydration‑stabilizing admixture to be added in five‑degree Fahrenheit increments to allow placement and finishing before initial set. For high‑friction surface treatment (HFST) applied to new asphalt pavements (Section 659.3(e)) a mandatory thirty‑day cure period is required before the treatment may be applied, the treatment may not be placed on concrete surfaces or repairs that are less than twenty‑eight days of age, contractor equipment and traffic are not allowed on the HFST during the curing period, the binder must be mixed within ±2 % of the manufacturer’s volume specifications and spread to a uniform thickness of 50‑65 mils with aggregate placed at 12‑15 lb per square yard, and after the binder has cured a first aggregate cleanup is performed before reopening to traffic followed by a second cleanup between fifteen and thirty days later, after which the Department conducts a calibrated skid test a minimum of thirty‑one days after the HFST is applied. The curing and protecting covers required in Section 711 shall be supplied as white polyethylene sheeting that conforms to ASTM C171; when the sheet is synthetic burlap backed its weight shall be 8.0 ounces per square yard. For bridge approach slabs (Section 505.3) concrete curing must follow Section 1001.3(p) except the excluded sub‑clause, requiring a water cure that lasts at least seven days and the placement of covers having double the thickness of burlap over the slab surface. Before roller‑compacted concrete pavements are placed in service (Section 502.3(k)) the finished surface must be inspected with a twelve‑foot straightedge or crown surface template held against the slab in successive positions parallel to the centreline, advancing no more than five feet at a time; any variation exceeding one‑quarter inch is deemed defective and must be removed by grinding until the surface conforms to the required grade and cross‑section within that tolerance, the pavement must remain protected from vehicular traffic for the entire curing period and may only be opened to traffic when the cure period specified in Section 502.3(j) has been completed. For steel‑slag fine aggregate (Section 703.1) a stockpile limit of a maximum of 30,000 tons must be kept uniformly moist for a period not less than six months before use; after this minimum cure the Representative samples the pile and tests its expansive characteristics according to PTM No. 130, approving it only when the average total volumetric expansion is less than 0.50 %; if the expansion exceeds that tolerance the pile must be cured for a minimum of two additional months and retested, and the LTS may also require petrographic analysis per PTM No. 518 or other tests to demonstrate acceptable durability before construction. For long‑life concrete pavement (LLCP) (Section 530.3) the curing regime is governed by a QC Plan that must include a method to cure and protect joints during the curing period and a concrete temperature control plan that outlines actions if concrete temperatures exceed 80°F at placement; the QC Plan also mandates that the first truck in each lot be sampled for plastic air content, which must be 7.0 % ± 1.5 %; any truck failing this requirement must be retested until three consecutive passing results are obtained, and for placements greater than 700 square yards vertical and horizontal LTU alignment must be verified by an acceptable nondestructive method within eighteen hours after the day’s work, checking at least the first five transverse joint locations across the full width and one joint per 2,640 linear feet. Equipment and personnel

Preparation of slip‑form pavements requires a fully energized self‑propelled slip‑form paving machine equipped with side forms, automatic alignment controls and integrated vibrating/tamping units; bonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements also require a self‑propelled mechanical scarifier to strip the existing surface together with shot, sand or water blasting equipment and a compressed‑air stream free of oil and moisture at a minimum pressure of 100 psi to remove standing water. Placement of slip‑form slabs is performed with concrete trucks, spreaders and pavers that must be kept from contaminating the cleaned slab, while bonded concrete overlays use the same type of concrete trucks, spreaders and pavers; roller‑compacted concrete is placed by a batch‑type or continuous mix plant producing the mix within design tolerances, covered haul trucks to prevent moisture loss, and a suitably weighted paver equipped with compaction devices that places the material to the required thickness. Primary and final compaction of roller‑compacted concrete is achieved with self‑propelled smooth dual steel‑drum rollers of at least eight tons (or rubber‑tired rollers as needed) to obtain a minimum 95 % field density before finishing; pervious concrete consolidation uses smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode for one to four passes, full‑depth reclamation relies on roller equipment operated according to a control‑strip pattern with density verification after each pass by a properly calibrated nuclear gauge operating in direct transmission mode, and cold‑recycled asphalt base course layers 2 inches to 5 inches thick are compacted with rollers to at least 96 % of target density. Finishing of slip‑form slabs employs a finishing machine that includes a rubber‑tired spreader wheel and transverse screed, with manual rigid screeds at least two feet longer than the slab width together with mechanical or long‑handled floats; roller‑compacted concrete is finished by the weighted paver equipped with compaction devices to the required thickness, pervious concrete surface finish may be achieved with a maximum 2 ton roller and optional steel troweling provided voids remain open, while CTPBC surfaces are checked with a 10‑foot straightedge and irregularities greater than one‑half inch are corrected by loosening or adding material. Curing of slip‑form slabs may be performed by applying white polyethylene or burlap‑backed sheeting that overlaps at least 12 in, saturated burlap blankets, or a monomolecular film curing compound sprayed from a pressure‑spray tank calibrated to about one gallon per 150 sq ft; insulation blankets and high‑low thermometers are used in cold weather to keep slab temperature above 40°F and never below 35°F. For bonded concrete overlays the curing compound is applied homogeneously at a minimum rate of one gallon per 125 ft² within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation, with insulating blankets provided when air temperature is 45°F or less and removed once temperature exceeds 45°F or compressive strength passes 3,600 psi provided surface temperature change does not exceed 40°F in any one‑hour period. Roller‑compacted concrete curing requires at least one water truck on site equipped with misting spray nozzles, soaking hoses or a sprinkler system, and moisture together with optional white polyethylene sheeting or curing compound must be maintained for a minimum of 72 hours until compressive strength reaches at least 3,000 psi. Partial‑depth repair cures with a white membrane‑forming curing compound applied by an atomizing spray tank with an adjustable nozzle, while pervious concrete is cured by completely covering the pavement with polyethylene sheeting securely fastened at all edges and laps for a minimum of seven days; CTPBC curing begins immediately after compaction using either a white membrane‑forming curing compound applied at about one gallon per 100 ft² (±10 %) or white polyethylene sheeting, both left in place for twenty‑four hours and extended another twenty‑four hours if temperature falls below 40°F. Testing of slip‑form pavements includes surface correction checks with a 12‑foot straightedge, tie‑bar pull‑out testing using a center‑pull hydraulic jack equipped with a load‑measuring gauge and bearing ring capable of 12 000 lb or 1/32 in slippage, field‑density verification with equipment complying with ASTM C1040 (direct transmission) and compressive‑strength test apparatus; bonded concrete overlay acceptance requires bond‑strength testing at seven days per ASTM C1583 and cylinder compressive‑strength testing according to PTM No. 604. Roller‑compacted concrete quality control employs thermometers, air meters meeting AASHTO T196/T152, slump cones per AASHTO T119, 6‑inch by 12‑inch cylinder molds (PTM No. 611), a compression machine with capping device (PTM No. 604), calibrated scales and a curing tank conforming to PTM No. 611 that maintains temperature between 60°F and 80°F for the first 24 ± 2 hours, together with a Bluetooth‑enabled digital system recording temperature at least every hour over a range of 30°F to 120°F with 1°F accuracy; maturity monitoring may use one or more maturity meters and temperature sensors conforming to PTM No. 640 or AASHTO T413. Partial‑depth repair cylinders are cured under the same conditions using the same curing equipment, while pervious concrete testing includes unit‑weight testing on every truck per ASTM C1688, removal of three 4‑inch diameter cores per 5 000 ft² for oven‑dry density and void content evaluation (ASTM C1754), and infiltration testing on three areas per 10 000 ft² according to ASTM C1701. Full‑depth reclamation testing employs nuclear density readings after each compaction pass, preparation of three standard Proctor specimens per ASTM D1633 (or compaction with a Superpave Gyratory Compactor to a thickness between 2 and 3 inches using 50 gyrations) followed by seven‑day curing and strength evaluation with indirect tensile or unconfined compressive test apparatus. Cold‑recycled asphalt base course testing determines in‑place density for each 3 000‑square‑yard area using AASHTO T310 and checks surface tolerance with a 12‑foot straightedge, while high‑friction surface treatment acceptance is verified by a calibrated skid test performed at least 31 days after placement using a smooth‑tire lock‑wheel apparatus in accordance with ASTM E274/E274M (AASHTO T242). Work sequence

Before any field curing can be initiated, the contractor must first complete every construction activity that precedes the placement of curing materials for the specific pavement type. In Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, curing may only be started after all finishing and texturing operations are complete and the bleed water has dissipated; the curing material must then be applied within 30 minutes of that dissipation. In Section 540—Bonded Concrete Overlay of Asphalt‑Surfaced Pavement, curing may only begin after the concrete overlay has been placed, finished and textured and once bleed water has dissipated, with the curing compound to be applied within 30 minutes of that dissipation. In Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfacied Pavements, the concrete overlay must first be finished and textured and the bleed water must have dissipated before any curing material can be placed, and the curing compound is required to be applied within 30 minutes of that dissipation and then maintained until the pavement reaches the strength specified for traffic opening. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements covered by Section 502, the RCC slab must first undergo final rolling followed by in‑field density testing performed according to ASTM C1040 no later than 30 minutes after rolling; the test result must show a compaction greater than 96 percent of the maximum wet density for all thicknesses, and only after these steps may the surface be kept moist with water cure, polyethylene sheeting, curing compound or cold‑weather curing for at least 72 hours until a compressive strength of 3 000 psi is achieved. Section 704 requires that a quality‑control (QC) plan prepared in accordance with Section 106.03 be submitted for Department review and that the Department’s review be completed before work begins; a field‑operation QC plan on Form CS‑704 must also be submitted at the preconstruction conference or no later than three weeks before the first concrete pour, and only after these plans are approved may curing tanks, boxes and other required equipment be placed in service. In Section 525—Concrete Pavement Partial‑Depth Repair, the repair area must first be fully prepared: after final cleaning a grout or epoxy bonding compound must be applied to every exposed surface while still wet or tacky, any dried grout or hardened epoxy must be removed and the surface re‑cleaned and recoated, concrete may be placed only when ambient air and surface temperatures are 40 °F and rising, and the repaired section must be protected from freezing for at least 24 hours before curing can commence. Section 520—Pervious Concrete Pavement System requires that the pavement receive its final surface finish using a maximum two‑ton roller to eliminate seams while meeting density and permeability requirements; steel troweling may be used provided surface voids remain open, all exterior formed surfaces must be edged to a radius of not less than one‑quarter inch, and curing must start within 20 minutes of concrete discharge. Section 714—Precast Concrete Products mandates that a QC plan developed in accordance with ACI 308 be prepared and approved before any curing or protective coating is applied. For the cement‑treated permeable base course described in Section 303, the material must first be placed and fully compacted with steel‑wheel power rollers of 8 to 10 tons weight, using at least one pass without rendering the material non‑draining or crushing aggregate, and compaction must be completed within one hour after water is added; curing then begins immediately. In Section 516—Concrete Pavement Patching, all construction activities up to the final finish—including forming patch sides, designing, mixing and placing concrete as required by Section 501.3(a)‑(h), performing final strike‑off and consolidation, finishing the surface to match existing cross‑section for Type A and B patches, texturing to correspond with surrounding texture, and scribing the placement date on the fresh concrete—must be completed before a curing compound may be applied. Section 301—Plain Cement Concrete Base Course requires that the base receive its final strike‑off and be finished by raking or brooming, and it must remain inaccessible to traffic until the surface course is placed when a black liquid membrane forming curing compound is used. In Section 545—Bonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfacied Pavements, the existing concrete must be scarified, shot‑blasted or water‑blasted, all loose debris and oil/grease removed, joint backing or approved filling material installed to within ±¼ inch of the pavement surface in every transverse joint, the overlay placed, finished and textured with surface temperature kept below 120 °F, and only after bleed water has dissipated may curing compounds be applied within 30 minutes of that point. Section 344—Full‑Depth Reclamation requires pulverizing the roadway to the specified size and depth, mixing with required aggregates, RAP, stabilizing additives and water at the recommended moisture content, compacting to a minimum density of at least 95 % of the laboratory‑compacted maximum density (including a 300‑foot control strip and ten random nuclear gauge tests completed within four hours of mixing), verifying original moisture content does not exceed optimum by more than 3 %, and finishing the surface so irregularities do not exceed 1½ in; only after these actions are satisfied may the material be cured. Section 341—Cold‑Recycled Asphalt Base Course mandates that the material first be processed, then uniformly spread, shaped and graded to the lines, grades and depth indicated in the specifications; only after these placement steps are completed may the base course be cured prior to compaction. For high‑friction surface treatment covered by Section 659, every surface‑preparation task listed in Section 659.3(e) must be finished—including covering and protecting existing markings and utilities, removing conflicting thermoplastic markings, cleaning with detergent, rinsing with potable water and drying with hot compressed air, vacuum‑ or air‑washing to eliminate dust and debris, and pre‑treating cracks ¼ in wide or larger with mixed binder and allowing the binder to gel—before the binder may be applied and its subsequent curing begin. In Section 505—Bridge Approach Slabs, the sleeper‑slab excavation must be finished after the subbase is placed (unless a geosynthetic stabilized subbase is used), two layers of polyethylene sheeting at least 4.0 mil thick must be installed as a bond breaker with seams set longitudinally and overlapped a minimum of 12 inches, and only once these steps are complete does the water‑curing process start. For slurry seal applications in Section 482, the existing pavement surface must first be conditioned by sealing open joints and cracks, removing heavy paint buildup and protecting or replacing raised pavement markers; a fog coat of water not exceeding 0.05 gallons per square yard is then applied, the mixture temperature must be controlled to be no less than 50 °F and no more than 125 °F, and both transverse and longitudinal joints must be constructed before curing may be allowed to begin. Section 703—Fine Aggregate (steel‑slag) requires that after gradation preparation the aggregate be placed in a controlled stockpile not exceeding 30 000 tons, completely soaked with water before or during stockpiling, after which the stockpile may be kept uniformly moist for the required curing time. Finally, long‑life concrete pavement described in Section 530 demands that the contractor provide a written QC plan approved by the Department, detailing concrete temperature‑control actions, anchorage and alignment of LTU assemblies prior to placement, sampling the first truck in each lot for plastic air content with an acceptance limit of 7 % ± 1.5 %, and specifying the method to cure and protect joints during the curing period. For unbonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 548), the same requirement applies—curing materials must be applied after finishing and texturing and within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation, and when non‑white membrane curing is used the covers protecting the pavement may be removed for joint sawing no longer than a half hour before the cut is made; insulating blankets may only be taken off when the concrete surface temperature does not change more than 40 °F in any one‑hour period. For bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 540), curing materials must be applied after finishing and texturing and within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation, and when a joint is to be cut the pavement curing covers may be removed for no longer than a half hour before being replaced. For bonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545), after finishing and texturing, curing materials shall be applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and must remain until the pavement attains the strength required for traffic opening; when insulating blankets are used because forecast air temperature is 45 °F or less they may be removed only after the temperature exceeds 45 °F or compressive strength passes 3,600 psi, and removal shall be performed so that concrete surface temperature does not change more than 40 °F within any one‑hour period. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502), the surface must be kept moist immediately after final rolling and in‑field density testing until a water cure, polyethylene sheeting or curing compound is applied, and that method must then be maintained for a minimum of 72 hours while the pavement develops a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi; cylinders are tested at three days and if the 3‑day compressive strength is at least 3,000 psi the field cure may be discontinued unless otherwise directed, otherwise curing continues until a seven‑day test reaches at least 3,000 psi, and if that still falls short curing must proceed to twenty‑eight days when cylinders are tested for a minimum compressive strength of 3,500 psi. For field‑cured concrete cylinders (Section 704), the curing equipment must keep the concrete temperature between 60 °F and 80 °F for the first 24 ± 2 hours, and temperature must be logged at least once each hour throughout that period. For Class AA accelerated partial‑depth repairs (Section 525), temperature monitoring is required at least hourly and the repair area may not experience a curing temperature change in excess of 40 °F within any one‑hour period; repairs must be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours after placement before curing proceeds. For pervious concrete pavements (Section 520), the surface must be covered with polyethylene sheeting and curing begun within 20 minutes of concrete discharge; if the sheet is removed it must be reinstated within 60 minutes of the initial removal, the curing cover must then be maintained for a minimum of 7 days, and rollers are not permitted to bridge the cured concrete for 7 days or until the Representative confirms sufficient strength. For precast concrete products (Section 714), units must remain in their forms with primary cure continuing until the concrete reaches at least 70 % of its 28‑day strength; after stripping, units are kept in an environment of no less than 50 °F during secondary curing until they achieve at least 80 % of the 28‑day minimum compressive strength shown on the shop drawing, may not be placed in temperatures below 32 °F after secondary cure unless the temperature differential between unit and air is under 30 °F, and if a curing compound is employed for secondary curing a further wait of 14 days is required before any protective coating is applied. For cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303), curing must begin immediately after compaction; the curing compound or polyethylene sheeting shall remain on the surface for 24 hours, and if the temperature falls below 40 °F at any time during that period the cure period shall be extended by an additional 24 hours; the interval between adding water to the aggregate‑cement mix and completing compaction may not exceed 1 hour. For chemically stabilized full‑depth reclamation (Section 344), compaction must be finished within four hours after the water or additive is mixed into the material, and thereafter the layer shall remain undisturbed until it reaches the required seven‑day strength; heavy traffic is prohibited during that seven‑day cure period, which may last as little as two to three days or extend to at least two weeks depending on weather. For plain cement concrete curb gutter or mountable curb gutter (Section 641), forms may not be rubbed to correct irregularities until the full curing period has elapsed, so that correction step must wait until after the prescribed cure time is complete. For cold‑recycled asphalt base course (Section 341), when an emulsified asphalt stabilizing agent is used the material shall be cured for at least two weeks before placement of a wearing or binder course, although curing may end earlier if testing shows average moisture content has dropped below 2 %; when a foamed‑asphalt stabilizing agent is used no fixed time period is prescribed and curing continues until the average moisture content falls below 2 %. For high friction surface treatment (Section 659), new asphalt pavements must wait a mandatory thirty‑day cure period before HFST can be placed, while concrete surfaces or repairs may not receive HFST until they are at least twenty‑eight days old; after the binder has cured, a first aggregate cleanup is performed immediately, a second cleanup occurs between fifteen and thirty days after HFST placement, and a calibrated skid test is scheduled no earlier than thirty‑one days after application. For bridge approach slabs (Section 505), the concrete shall be water‑cured for a minimum of seven days, using burlap blankets applied at double thickness; this seven‑day period constitutes the only timing limit between slab placement and the end of curing. For long‑life concrete pavement (Section 530), after each day’s placement and finishing of more than 700 SY, the LTU vertical and horizontal alignment must be verified within 18 hours using a nondestructive method acceptable to the Representative, and this verification is tied to the curing control requirement that joints be cured and protected during the curing period. For fine‑aggregate stockpiles (Section 703), after steel‑slag fine aggregate has been placed in a controlled stockpile and fully soaked, the material must be kept uniformly moist for a period of not less than 6 months before the Representative samples and tests it; if the average total expansion exceeds 0.50 %, the stockpile must then continue curing for a minimum of 2 additional months before another sample is taken and retested. Staging and logistics

Curing is coordinated through a QC Plan that must describe appropriate action points for all phases of construction, including concrete mixing and curing, joint sawing and sealing, and testing before opening to traffic; the plan also requires adequate equipment, trained personnel and sufficient hauling units be scheduled to maintain continuity in placement, thereby controlling material delivery and equipment movement; work zones are managed by constructing concrete pavement patches one lane at a time when two‑lane width work would interfere with traffic, and traffic is protected from drop‑off conditions as required. Field curing operations across a variety of pavement treatments are governed by an extensive set of spatial, site‑access, weather and environmental constraints that differ according to the material and specification. For slip‑form paving (Section 501) the contractor must provide enough space to lay covers or blankets over the entire surface and sides with at least a 12‑inch overlap that is secured to form a tight seal; curing must begin within 30 minutes after bleed‑water dissipates, the concrete temperature may not exceed 160°F, and normal curing may proceed only if air temperature stays above 40°F. When forecasted temperatures fall below 40°F high‑low thermometers shall be placed on the concrete surface and insulating blankets provided; insulation may be removed when the air temperature exceeds 40°F or when average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, and removal must not cause a surface‑temperature change greater than 40°F in any one‑hour period. Rain protection is mandatory before initial set and paving must be stopped and covered if rain threatens. During curing of an unbonded concrete overlay (Section 548) the curing material is applied after finishing and texturing within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and retained until the strength required for traffic opening is achieved. Insulating blankets are required when air temperature is 40°F or less; they may be removed only after the air temperature exceeds 40°F or average compressive strength surpasses 3,600 psi, with removal limited to a surface‑temperature change not exceeding 40°F per hour. For a bonded concrete overlay of an asphalt‑surfaced pavement (Section 540) ambient temperature governs blanket use; adequate blankets are required when air temperature is 45°F or less and may remain until the temperature rises above 45°F or average compressive strength exceeds 3,000 psi, with removal limited to a surface‑temperature change not exceeding 40°F in any one‑hour period. The pavement surface must be cooled to less than 120°F before placement, curing compounds are applied within 30 minutes after bleed water has dissipated and kept on the slab until strength sufficient for traffic is achieved, standing water or moisture on the prepared surface must be eliminated, and fog‑misting blankets may be used only to achieve the required temperature condition without leaving residual water. Roller‑compacted concrete (Section 502) requires a water‑truck equipped with misting spray nozzles on site that can circulate over the fresh surface, which in turn demands sufficient space for the truck’s movement. Curing must be delayed when the evaporation rate exceeds 0.20 lb/ft²·hr unless windbreaks or fog‑mist mitigation are employed. Placement cannot occur when ambient temperature falls below 40°F nor when hot ambient conditions cause the RCC mixture to exceed 90°F, because both conditions would impede maintaining a moist cure for the required minimum of 72 hours until the pavement reaches at least 3,000 psi compressive strength. Field‑cured concrete cylinders (Section 704) require a plant office providing at least 120 sq ft of floor space; curing equipment must keep the temperature adjacent to the cylinders between 60°F and 80°F for the first 24 ± 2 hours, and a digital recorder must capture temperatures from 30°F up to 120°F with an accuracy of 1°F. All cured cylinders must remain protected from the elements in the same manner as the surrounding placed concrete until testing. Class AA cement‑concrete partial‑depth repair (Section 525) calls for insulation or heating when ambient air temperature drops below 80°F, and the repair‑area temperature must be monitored at least hourly to keep any change within 40°F in a one‑hour period; otherwise the work is considered defective. All repairs must be protected from freezing for a minimum of 24 hours after placement. Pervious concrete pavement (Section 520) requires curing to start within 20 minutes of concrete discharge and the entire surface to be completely covered with polyethylene sheeting that is securely fastened at all edges and laps to prevent wind removal, remaining in place for a minimum of 7 days. Placement is prohibited if air temperatures are projected to fall below 40°F or drop below that level any time during the first 24 hours after placement, or if rain is forecast before final finishing and curing. The pavement cannot be opened to traffic until after the 7‑day cure period during which ambient temperature must have exceeded 55°F at least once each day. Precast concrete units (Section 714) must undergo secondary curing in a space that can sustain at least a 50°F environment, and units may not be placed in any environment below 32°F after the secondary cure unless the temperature difference between unit and ambient air is less than 30°F. Primary cure continues while the product remains in its form until it reaches 70 % of the 28‑day strength; secondary curing proceeds until the unit attains at least 80 % of the specified 28‑day compressive strength. Cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303) must begin curing immediately after compaction and may be performed either by applying a white membrane forming curing compound at a rate of 1 gallon per 100 ft² ± 10 % or by covering the surface with white polyethylene sheeting; the material must remain in place for 24 hours, but if the CTPBC temperature falls below 40°F during that period the cure time shall be extended an additional 24 hours, and a drop to 32°F within 72 hours requires removal and replacement of the material at no cost. Concrete patching (Section 516) is constrained by site‑access limits because excavated areas may not remain open for more than 48 hours or over weekends or holidays; adequate equipment, trained personnel and hauling units must be available to keep placement continuous. If rain occurs while the patch area is open an outlet must be excavated through the shoulder at the lowest point of the patch, and only Type 2 white pigmented liquid membrane forming curing compound may replace the standard PAMS compound. Rapid‑set repairs must be cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Plain cement concrete base courses (Section 301) may use a black liquid membrane forming curing compound only if the base course remains inaccessible to traffic until the surface course is placed; should the black compound become unsatisfactory it must be discontinued and curing resumed by other methods as specified. Bonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545) permits placement only when concrete temperature is between 65°F and 90°F and the existing surface is kept below 120°F before placement. Insulating blankets are required whenever forecast air temperature is 45°F or less and must remain until the air temperature exceeds 45°F or average compressive strength passes 3,600 psi; removal is limited so that surface‑temperature change does not exceed 40°F in any one‑hour period. Curing compound must be applied within 30 minutes after bleed water dissipates at a minimum rate of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 ft² and kept on the pavement until the required strength is achieved; joint faces must be protected from the compound or cleaned before sealant installation. Chemically stabilized full‑depth reclaimed material (Section 344) must be protected from heavy traffic for the required seven‑day strength period, with appropriate traffic signs posted to restrict site access until curing is complete. The layer must be kept damp by daily water application or approved emulsified asphalt treatments while its moisture content does not exceed the mix‑design optimum by more than 3 %; favorable weather (no rain, sunshine, low humidity and high temperature) accelerates strength gain whereas adverse climatic conditions can extend cure time from a few days to several weeks. Roller‑compacted concrete acceptance criteria (Section 502) allow field cure to be discontinued when three‑day compressive strength is at least 3,000 psi; otherwise curing continues until seven‑day verification or until the 28‑day test reaches 3,500 psi. No other space, site‑access or environmental constraints are specified in the acceptance criteria. Cold‑recycled asphalt base course (Section 341) is governed by moisture content and time limits rather than spatial or access considerations: emulsified‑asphalt stabilized material must be cured for at least two weeks before a wearing or binder course is placed, or until its average moisture content falls below 2 %; foamed‑asphalt stabilized material must be cured until its average moisture content is less than 2 %. Pervious concrete pavement systems (Section 520) must include provisions to protect the concrete from freezing for the entire seven‑day curing period as required by ACI 306.1, and in hot conditions the plan shall detail the amount of hydration‑stabilizing admixture to be added in 5°F increments to allow sufficient time for placing and finishing before initial set. High‑friction surface treatment (Section 659) prohibits any contractor equipment or traffic from occupying the treated lane during the curing phase; placement and cure must not proceed when wind speed exceeds 15 mph or gusts of 20 mph or higher are anticipated because high winds can impair binder retention, and the Representative may suspend all operations if safety concerns such as excessive dust or poor visibility arise. Slurry seal (Section 482) requires that traffic not be allowed on the surface until it has fully cured to a firm condition as directed. Steel‑slag fine aggregate (Section 703) is limited to a maximum stockpile size of 30,000 tons; the material must be completely soaked with water before or during placement and then maintained in a uniform moist condition for not less than six months, with acceptance based on an average total volumetric expansion of less than 0.50 %. Long‑life concrete pavement (Section 530) mandates that if concrete temperatures exceed 80°F at placement a concrete temperature control plan must be implemented, and the quality‑control plan must specify a method for curing and protecting joints during the curing period so that moisture loss from concrete in and adjacent to joints is prevented during and after joint sawing. Quality control and acceptance

Field curing inspections require verification of material quality, timing, temperature control, strength development, protective measures, and post‑curing conditions as prescribed for each pavement specification. In Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements the contractor must apply curing material within 30 minutes after bleed water has dissipated, keep it in place until the slab reaches the strength required for opening to traffic, and use a single method unless directed otherwise; the concrete temperature may not exceed 160 °F at any time, hourly monitoring must show that temperature changes do not exceed 40 °F in any one‑hour period, and any excess renders the work defective; if the high‑low thermometer falls between 40 °F and 35 °F the cure period is extended one day, while a reading below 35 °F makes the concrete defective; when a monomolecular film curing compound is used it must be applied at a rate of one gallon per 150 sq ft in a continuous film, and emergency‑curing material must be on hand. In Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfac ed Pavements the inspector must verify that curing material is placed within 30 minutes after bleed water dissipates and remains until the concrete reaches the strength required for traffic opening as defined in Section 548.3(q); when air temperature is 40 °F or less insulating blankets must be provided and later removed only when the temperature rises above 40 °F or when measured compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, ensuring that surface‑temperature change does not exceed 40 °F per hour; temperature readings are taken during blanket removal to confirm compliance; if a white‑pigmented liquid membrane‑forming curing compound is used it must be applied evenly to give a solid white opaque film at a minimum rate of one gallon per 150 sq ft for pavements thicker than six inches (or per 125 sq ft for six inches or less) or according to the manufacturer’s recommendation, and joint faces must be kept free of compound or cleaned before sealant placement; for non‑white curing covers only enough material is removed at joint locations and the removal cannot exceed a half‑hour, after which the covering is replaced. In Section 540—Bonded Concrete Overlay of Asphalt‑Surfac ed Pavement curing material must be applied within 30 minutes after bleed water disappears and remain until the slab reaches the minimum compressive strength of 3,000 psi required for traffic opening, which must be verified either by cylinder testing per PTM No. 604 or by the maturity method (AASHTO T 413); if ambient air temperature is 45 °F or lower insulating blankets must be provided and later removed only when the air temperature exceeds 45 °F or when the average slab strength surpasses 3,000 psi, and removal shall not cause a surface‑temperature change greater than 40 °F in any one‑hour period; the curing compound must be applied uniformly as an opaque white film at a minimum rate of one gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 sq ft, and joint faces must be kept free of the compound or cleaned before sealant installation. In Section 502—Roller Compacted Concrete Pavements immediately after final rolling and density testing the RCC surface must be kept continuously moist until one or more curing methods—water misting, white polyethylene sheeting, curing compound, or cold‑weather curing—is applied; the selected method must be maintained for at least 72 hours and a compressive strength of not less than 3,000 psi must have been achieved as determined by the Section 502.3(m)3 procedure. In Section 704—Cement Concrete the contractor must supply curing tanks or boxes that can keep the temperature next to the cylinders within the range of 60 °F to 80 °F for the first 24 ± 2 hours, and a Bluetooth‑enabled digital system must record and store that temperature at least every hour over a span of 30 °F to 120 °F with an accuracy of 1 °F; the collected data for the initial 24‑hour period must be provided to the Representative and be available in real time on request. In Section 525—Concrete Pavement Partial‑Depth Repair a white membrane‑forming curing compound must be applied using an atomizing spray tank with an adjustable nozzle and agitation before each application as prescribed; for accelerated repairs the contractor must provide insulation or heating when ambient air temperature falls below 80 °F and must control and record the concrete temperature at least hourly, ensuring that no temperature change exceeds 40 °F in any one‑hour period, and a violation of this limit renders the work defective. In Section 714—Precast Concrete Products the precast unit must remain in its form until it reaches at least seventy percent of the twenty‑eight‑day compressive strength, with stripping time based on the product’s inherent characteristics (or a reduced stripping time/strength calculation approved by the CSME when not shown on drawings); secondary curing must be performed by an approved method while the unit is kept in an environment no lower than fifty °F until it attains at least eighty percent of the twenty‑eight‑day minimum compressive strength indicated on the shop drawing, after which the unit may not be placed in temperatures below thirty‑two °F unless the temperature differential between the unit and ambient air is less than thirty °F, and the unit must be stored until the full twenty‑eight‑day minimum compressive strength is achieved. In Section 303—Cement Treated Permeable Base Course the white membrane‑forming curing compound must be applied at a rate of one gallon per 100 square feet ± 10 % of the surface area and must remain in place for a continuous 24‑hour period; concrete temperature must be monitored, and if the temperature falls below 40 °F during those 24 hours the cure must be extended an additional 24 hours, while any drop to 32 °F within 72 hours of placement obligates removal and replacement of the CTPBC. In Section 516—Concrete Pavement Patching the curing step must employ an approved liquid‑membrane forming compound, either the Type 2 white pigmented product or the white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene (PAMS) type, applied in accordance with the patch material manufacturer’s recommendations; rapid‑set concrete repairs are cured per those same recommendations and then tested for compressive strength, and pavement may only be opened to traffic when the test shows a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi as measured by PTM No. 604. In Section 301—Plain Cement Concrete Base Course the inspector must verify that only a black liquid membrane‑forming curing compound is applied and that no white compound is present, must confirm that the base course is not accessible to traffic before the surface course is placed, and must monitor the condition of the curing compound so that use is discontinued if it becomes unsatisfactory as a curing agent and alternative methods are employed. In Section 545—Bonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfac ed Pavements the inspector must verify that curing material is placed within 30 minutes after bleed water has dissipated and remains until the pavement attains the required strength; insulating blankets shall be provided when forecast air temperature is 45 °F or less and may be removed only when the air temperature rises above 45 °F or the average concrete compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi; the surface‑temperature change must not exceed 40 °F in any one‑hour period, otherwise the work is deemed defective; and a curing compound shall be applied uniformly at no less than one gallon per 125 ft² as specified. In Section 344—Full Depth Reclamation the curing process must be verified by confirming that the reclaimed layer reaches the specified seven‑day strength, which for chemically stabilized material is an unconfined compressive strength of 300 to 500 psi (or 280 to 500 psi when an asphalt overlay of three inches or greater is used) and for asphalt‑stabilized mixes a minimum indirect tensile strength of 50 psi; heavy traffic is prohibited during the seven‑day cure period and appropriate signs must be posted while the layer is kept damp by daily water application or by applying an emulsified‑asphalt primer followed by a fog seal at a residual rate between 0.02 and 0.10 gallons per square yard (or tack coat as required); because curing time varies with weather, strength gain may require from two or three days to at least two weeks before proof rolling with the heaviest design vehicle or a strength measurement is performed to authorize traffic opening. In Section 323—Roller Compacted Concrete Base Course the sole characteristic to be inspected for curing is the absence of any curing compound; the specification explicitly requires that no curing compounds be used. In Section 502—Roller Compacted Concrete Pavements each lot must be inspected for density, compressive strength at specified ages, and pavement depth; the final average density cannot be less than 98 % of the maximum wet density and no individual test may be less than 96 %; compressive‑strength cylinders are tested at three, seven, and twenty‑eight days with field curing allowed to stop if the three‑day or seven‑day result is greater than or equal to 3,000 psi, while the twenty‑eight‑day result must reach a minimum of 3,500 psi, and core depth is acceptable when it is within one‑quarter inch of plan depth. In Section 711—Concrete Curing Material and Admixtures white polyethylene curing covers must comply with ASTM C171, confirming that the material meets the specified minimum tensile‑strength requirements and, for synthetic‑burlap‑backed sheets, that its weight is eight point zero ounces per square yard. In Section 482—Slurry Seal the cured surface must be inspected to confirm it has reached a firm condition that will prevent pick‑up of the mixture before any traffic is permitted; traffic may not be allowed until directed by the responsible party. Safety and environmental controls

During slip‑form paving, the concrete temperature must not exceed 160 °F during curing, which creates a heat‑related hazard for anyone on or near the slab. The curing compound forms a continuous membrane that workers, materials and equipment must stay off unless adequate precautions are taken to protect the film, indicating slip or chemical exposure risks; if rain falls on the newly coated pavement before the film has dried sufficiently to resist damage or if the film is damaged in any way before the pavement is cured, a new coat must be applied immediately, requiring further handling of the chemicals. During full‑depth reclamation, the reclaimed material must remain undisturbed until it reaches the required 7‑day strength; consequently workers and the public are at risk of injury from vehicles on a still‑weak base, so heavy traffic is prohibited for the full cure period and appropriate traffic signs must be posted to keep vehicles away. Only after the base has achieved the specified strength may limited light traffic be allowed, and any damage caused by premature traffic must be repaired before further use. During high‑friction surface treatment (HFST) installation, contractor equipment and traffic are not allowed on the HFST during the curing period because the wet binder can release chemicals and the uncured surface is prone to losing aggregate; any loose aggregate that drops onto an active lane creates a slip or debris hazard for the traveling public and must be removed immediately after traffic is temporarily held. Workers must minimize walking or standing on the binder, which reduces exposure to the adhesive material and prevents disturbance of the treatment. For new asphalt pavements HFST may not be applied until a mandatory 30‑day cure period has elapsed, and concrete surfaces must be at least 28 days old before HFST installation, further limiting premature exposure risks. Curing, protection, and handoff

In slip‑form paving of plain or reinforced cement concrete (Section 501) the contractor shall apply curing material within thirty minutes after bleed‑water has dissipated and shall keep it in place until the slab reaches the compressive strength required for traffic opening as defined in Section 501.3(q); the same method must be used throughout unless the Representative directs a change, and any interruption that leaves the pavement insufficiently cured will cause immediate suspension of work. Acceptable methods include covering the surface and sides with sealed white polyethylene (or burlap‑backed) sheets or applying a liquid membrane‑forming compound such as PAMS at a rate of one gallon per 150 ft² when air temperature is above 40 °F, provided the film is applied in a continuous mist without marring the surface; concrete temperature shall not exceed 160 °F during curing and emergency curing material shall be kept on site.

For unbonded concrete overlays (Section 548) the contractor shall place curing materials within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and retain them until the overlay attains the strength required for traffic opening as defined in Section 548.3(q). When ambient temperature is 40 °F or lower, insulating blankets shall be installed to prevent rapid heat loss and may be removed only after the air temperature exceeds 40 °F or the average compressive strength surpasses 3,600 psi, with removal performed so that surface temperature does not change more than 40 °F in any one‑hour period. If a white‑pigmented liquid membrane‑forming curing compound is used, it shall be spread uniformly to give solid white coverage at a minimum of one gallon per 150 ft² for overlays thicker than six inches or one gallon per 125 ft² for thicknesses six inches or less (or the greater manufacturer rate), and may be applied in two equal coats with the second coat placed immediately after sawing; joint faces must be shielded from the compound or cleaned before sealant. When other curing covers are employed, only enough cover is removed to allow saw cuts and the covering shall be replaced as soon as the joint is cut, with removal not exceeding a half‑hour.

In bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavement (Section 540) curing materials shall be applied within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and kept until the slab reaches an average compressive strength of 3,000 psi required for traffic opening. When ambient air temperature is 45 °F or less, insulating blankets shall be placed to prevent rapid heat loss; they may be removed only after the air temperature exceeds 45 °F or the concrete’s average compressive strength exceeds 3,000 psi, and removal must not cause a surface‑temperature change greater than 40 °F in any one‑hour period. Curing compound shall be spread uniformly as an opaque white film at a minimum rate of one gallon per 125 ft² (±0.1 gallon), and if needed may be applied in two equal coats with the second coat placed immediately after sawing, while joint faces are protected from the compound or cleaned before sealant.

For roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502) the pavement shall be kept moist immediately after the final rolling pass and completion of in‑field density testing by one or more prescribed curing methods such as a water cure using a misting spray truck, soaking hoses, sprinklers, covering with white polyethylene sheeting, applying a curing compound that forms a uniform solid opaque film, or following the cold‑weather procedure. The selected method shall be maintained for at least seventy‑two hours and continued until the pavement reaches a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi; moisture application shall not wash out or damage the finished surface.

Field‑cured concrete test cylinders (Section 704) are protected by using a PTM No. 611 curing tank or curing boxes that must maintain the temperature adjacent to the cylinders between 60 °F and 80 °F for the first twenty‑four ± 2 hours, with temperature data recorded at least hourly. The cylinders are then field‑cured according to PTM No. 611 Section 12.2 for the required period, and after curing is stopped they may be moved to a preapproved secure area where they must continue to receive the same protection from the elements on all surfaces until compressive‑strength testing is performed.

In concrete pavement partial‑depth repairs (Section 525) the completed repair shall be cured by applying a white membrane‑forming curing compound as required in Section 516.3(j); for Class AA Modified concrete an atomizing spray tank with an adjustable nozzle shall be used and the solution agitated before each application. When ambient air temperature falls below 80 °F, insulation or heating shall be provided, and the curing temperature shall be monitored at least hourly so that any change does not exceed 40 °F within a one‑hour period; Class AA Accelerated concrete follows the same compound application and test cylinders are cured under identical conditions. Rapid‑Set Concrete Patching Material is cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations.

Pervious concrete pavement systems (Section 520) shall begin curing within twenty minutes of concrete discharge, and the entire surface shall be completely covered with polyethylene sheeting that is thoroughly secured at all exterior edges and interior laps to prevent wind removal. The covering shall remain in place for a minimum of seven days; vehicular traffic may not be opened until the concrete has cured for at least seven days and the ambient temperature has exceeded 55 °F during any time of the day.

Precast concrete products (Section 714) are kept in their forms and given a primary cure until they reach seventy percent of the twenty‑eight‑day strength as required by the approved quality‑control plan. After stripping, secondary curing is performed by approved methods while the units are stored in an environment of at least 50 °F until they have reached eighty percent of the specified twenty‑eight‑day compressive strength; units shall not be placed in an environment below 32 °F after the secondary cure unless the differential between the unit and air temperature is less than thirty °F. Once curing is complete, a protective coating—either boiled linseed oil or a penetrating sealer that does not discolor the concrete—is applied to barrier and inlet tops before shipment, and if a curing compound was used for secondary curing, a fourteen‑day wait is observed before applying the coating.

In cement treated permeable base course (Section 303) curing must start immediately; the contractor shall apply a white membrane‑forming curing compound at a rate of one gallon per 100 ft² ± 10 % (or may cover the surface with white polyethylene sheeting) and shall keep the material in place for twenty‑four hours. If concrete temperature falls below forty degrees Fahrenheit during that period, the cure time is extended an additional twenty‑four hours, and if the temperature drops to thirty‑two degrees Fahrenheit within seventy‑two hours of placement the department requires removal and replacement at no cost. During curing and the subsequent protection phase construction traffic is prohibited on the CTPBC for at least three days; after the three‑day period only trucks and equipment needed for the next layer or adjacent lifts are permitted, and any damaged or contaminated areas must be removed and replaced at no cost.

Concrete pavement patching (Section 516) shall be cured with a liquid membrane‑forming curing compound, either standard white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene (PAMS) or the permitted Type 2 white pigmented compound; rapid‑set repairs are cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, and the Representative will allow traffic only when test cylinders cured in the same manner reach at least 1,000 psi as required by PTM No. 604.

Plain cement concrete base course (Section 301) shall be cured with a liquid membrane‑forming curing compound; the white‑colored compound is prohibited while the black‑colored compound may be applied only when the base course will not be opened to traffic before the surface course is installed, and if the black compound proves unsatisfactory its use must be stopped and another approved method employed.

Bonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545) shall have curing materials applied within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and retained until the overlay attains the strength required for traffic opening; when forecast air temperature is 45 °F or less insulating blankets are placed to prevent rapid heat loss and may be removed once temperature rises above 45 °F or average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi provided surface temperature does not change more than 40 °F in any one‑hour period. Curing compound shall be spread uniformly over all exposed concrete at a minimum of one gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 ft² (or per manufacturer guidance), may be applied in two equal coats with the second after sawing, while joint faces are protected from the compound or cleaned before sealant installation.

Full‑depth reclamation (Section 344) shall be cured until the required seven‑day strength is achieved; heavy traffic is prohibited during that period and appropriate signs must be posted. Chemically stabilized sections are kept damp each day either by applying water or by spraying an emulsified asphalt prime followed by a fog seal at a residual asphalt rate between 0.02 and 0.10 gallons per square yard (or tack coat as specified) while ensuring moisture content does not exceed three percent. Proof rolling with a vehicle comparable to the heaviest expected traffic may be used, light traffic (vehicles under five tons) may be allowed after about two hours once proof rolling shows no damage, but unlimited traffic is not permitted until the seven‑day strength is met.

Plain cement concrete curb gutter (Section 641) shall be protected by applying the covers specified in Section 711.1 and cured using the curing compound referenced in Section 711.2(a); forms must not be rubbed to correct irregularities until they have been removed and the full curing period has elapsed, and any minor defects are filled with mortar.

Roller‑compacted concrete base course (Section 323) shall be cured by applying emulsified asphalt over the entire RCC surface in accordance with Section 460.3(b); the coat must be uniform and void‑free, the application equipment must be approved by the Representative and must not damage, mar or alter the compacted surface, and curing compounds are expressly prohibited so protection relies solely on this emulsified‑asphalt layer. When the prescribed cure period has elapsed the RCC pavement may be opened to local traffic before the asphalt wearing course is placed.

In cold‑recycled asphalt base course (Section 341) the base must be protected from marring, distortion and other damage while it remains exposed to construction equipment, and any damage must be corrected immediately to the Representative’s satisfaction. For emulsified‑asphalt stabilized CRABC the material shall be cured for at least two weeks before a wearing or binder course is placed, or until the average moisture content drops below two percent; three random core samples of at least 1,200 g each are dry cut and tested per PTM No. 749 with any voids backfilled using CRABC material or an approved alternate. Foamed‑asphalt stabilized CRABC shall be cured until the average moisture content is less than two percent with the same sampling requirement.

High‑friction surface treatment (Section 659) must be left undisturbed for its prescribed cure time, and contractor equipment and traffic are prohibited on the treatment during this period; new asphalt pavements may not receive HFST until after a mandatory thirty‑day cure period, concrete surfaces or repairs must be at least twenty‑eight days old before HFST is placed, after placement a first aggregate cleanup is performed before reopening to traffic, a second cleanup occurs between fifteen and thirty days, and a calibrated skid test is required no earlier than thirty‑one days after placement.

Curing of cement, mortar or grout (Section 720) shall be accomplished by applying water that conforms to ASTM C1602 and is reasonably clean, being free from vegetable matter, oil, acid, alkali, sugar, or any other substances injurious to the finished product. The completed work shall also be protected and cured by covering it with white polyethylene sheeting in accordance with ASTM C171; the sheet must meet the minimum tensile‑strength requirements of that standard, and when a burlap backing is used either natural or synthetic burlap may be employed, synthetic burlap‑backed sheets being required to weigh eight point zero ounces per square yard.

Bridge approach slabs (Section 505) shall be water‑cured in accordance with Section 1001.3(p) (excluding provision 1001.3(p)3.a), and curing must continue for a minimum of seven days while the surface is protected by covers that are double the thickness of burlap.

Slurry seal (Section 482) shall be protected by prohibiting any traffic on the surface until directed, allowing the seal to completely cure to a firm condition that will prevent pick‑up of the mixture.

A controlled stockpile of steel‑slag fine aggregate (Sections 703 and 530) shall be completely soaked with water before or during stockpiling and then kept uniformly moist for a period of not less than six months; after this minimum cure representative samples are taken and the stockpile is approved only if the average total volumetric expansion measured by PTM No. 130 is less than 0.50 %. Any failure requires an additional minimum curing period of two months before retesting.

Long‑life concrete pavement (Section 530) shall have a quality‑control plan that specifies a method for curing and protecting the pavement joints during the curing period; the method must be intended to prevent moisture loss from the concrete in and adjacent to the joints while joint sawing is performed and thereafter. Expected performance and maintenance

Improper curing can generate a range of failures and distresses that are identified in the various pavement specifications. In Section 501—Plain or Reinforced Cement Concrete Pavements, any concrete temperature that rises above 160 °F during curing renders the slab defective; if at any time the high‑low thermometer falls between 40 °F and 35 °F the cure period must be extended by an additional day, and if it falls below 35 °F the concrete is considered defective, and the Representative will suspend concrete operations immediately when sufficient curing is not provided. Section 548—Unbonded Concrete Overlay treats concrete panels with cracking outside of the sawed joints as defective, requires that the rate of temperature change at the concrete surface does not exceed 40 °F within any one‑hour period, limits removal of curing covers to no more than ½ hour before sawing, and warns that removing insulating blankets too quickly can cause damage. Section 540—Bonded Concrete Overlay of Asphalt‑Surfac ed Pavement also mandates that the rate of temperature change at the concrete surface does not exceed 40 °F within any one‑hour period, requires insulation when air temperature is 45 °F or less, permits removal of the insulation only after the slab strength exceeds a minimum compressive strength of 3,000 psi, and states that allowing traffic before reaching that strength makes the pavement defective. Section 525—Concrete Pavement Partial‑Depth Repair specifies that curing temperature must be monitored at least hourly and that any change in curing temperature in excess of 40 °F within any one‑hour period renders the repair work defective; delamination observed after curing is considered a failure that must be replaced at no cost to the Department. Section 520—Pervious Concrete Pavement System requires that curing begin within 20 minutes of concrete discharge and that polyethylene sheeting remain in place for a minimum of 7 days; failure to do so can lead to surface mortar loss when rain strikes before the sheet is applied, damage from rollers bridging the slab before sufficient strength is achieved, and any mortar loss after rainfall may be repaired, removed, or replaced. Section 545—Bonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfac ed Pavements adds that a bond‑strength test average below 175 psi or any individual result under 160 psi makes the area defective, and surface imperfections such as honeycombing, rain damage, or an improper final finish are also considered defects. Section 344—Full Depth Reclamation requires curing until the 7‑day strength requirement is met; allowing heavy traffic during that period can damage the reclaimed layer, and chemically stabilized material tested to strengths greater than 900 psi may result in shrinkage cracking, which necessitates rework. Section 502—Roller Compacted Concrete Pavements defines acceptance criteria that the final average density of each lot cannot be less than 98 % of the maximum wet density and no individual test may be less than 96 %, that the 3‑day compressive strength must reach a minimum of 3,000 psi (with continuation of cure until the 7‑day cylinders also meet that value and the 28‑day cylinders achieve at least 3,500 psi), that any depth deficiency greater than ¼ inch renders the pavement defective, and that any lot identified as defective during curing must be removed and replaced. In addition, pervious concrete is vulnerable to damage if it is not protected from freezing throughout the seven‑day cure period, and under hot conditions a lack of sufficient hydration‑stabilizing admixture can cause premature set; both situations are addressed by cold‑weather and hot‑weather provisions in the quality‑control plan. For high‑friction surface treatment, cracks wider than ¼ inch that are over‑filled must be scarified after the binder cures to avoid a raised surface, exposure, separation, premature drying or hardening of the mixed binder impairs aggregate retention and bonding and requires removal and replacement, inadequate protection of adjacent travel lanes can allow loose aggregate to fall onto active lanes, and failure to observe the mandatory 30‑day cure period for new asphalt pavements or the 28‑day age limit for concrete repairs often leads to delamination, color discoloration, poor aggregate retention, or exposed bare pavement that must be re‑treated. Finally, for steel‑slag fine aggregate the principal distress in moist curing is excessive volumetric expansion; after at least six months of uniform moisture the stockpile must exhibit an average total volumetric expansion of less than 0.50 %, and if the measured expansion exceeds this limit the stockpile fails the expansion criterion and must remain in cure for a minimum of two additional months before retesting. For high‑friction surface treatments (Section 659) contractor equipment and traffic shall be kept off the treatment during curing; any binder that becomes contaminated must be removed and replaced at no additional expense, and if cracks are overfilled excess material shall be scarified after the binder has cured to make it flush with surrounding pavement. After the binder has cured a first aggregate cleanup is required before opening to traffic, followed by a second cleanup performed between fifteen and thirty days after placement. For RCC surface tolerance (Section 502 k) preventive maintenance includes protecting the RCC pavement from vehicular traffic for the duration of the curing period; monitoring is carried out by testing the finished surface with a twelve‑foot straightedge or crown template in successive positions advancing no more than five feet until the entire width is examined, and when the pavement surface varies by more than one‑quarter inch high points are removed by grinding with a self‑propelled diamond grinder or cutting tools while depressions exceeding one‑quarter inch are considered defective; testing and correction continue until no observable departures remain. For steel‑slag fine aggregate curing (Section 703) preventive maintenance limits the stockpile size to a maximum of thirty thousand tons, requires the material be fully soaked before or during placement and kept uniformly moist for not less than six months; after this period the Representative samples the pile and tests expansive characteristics according to PTM No. 130, accepting it only when average total volumetric expansion is less than zero point five percent, and if the expansion exceeds that limit curing must be continued for a minimum of two additional months before another sample is taken and retested. The QC plan (Section 530) must include the method proposed to cure and protect joints so that moisture loss from concrete in and adjacent to joints is prevented during the curing period and after joint sawing.
