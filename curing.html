Curing

Part of: Portland Cement Concrete is-subtopic-of
Curing is a topic under Portland Cement Concrete.

Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Field curing is the on‑site process of preserving moisture and controlling temperature of freshly placed concrete pavements by applying a curing material (e.g., liquid membrane‑forming compound, polyethylene sheeting, water misting or insulating blankets) within prescribed time limits after bleed‑water dissipation and maintaining it until the pavement reaches the required early strength or specified cure period. The procedure includes continuous monitoring to ensure temperature changes do not exceed 40 °F in any one‑hour interval and that all curing requirements (coverage rates, timing, protection from rain, and removal restrictions) are satisfied before traffic is allowed.

Purpose and applicability

Curing solves the problem of concrete losing moisture or experiencing rapid temperature changes that would otherwise prevent achievement of the required compressive strength before traffic opening, leading to premature opening, defective work, cracking, or loss of durability. For slip‑form paving (Section 501.3) and unbonded concrete overlays (Section 548.3) curing prevents moisture loss and rapid heat loss that could cause insufficient strength; a curing material must be applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and retained until the opening strength specified in the respective section is attained, and insulating blankets are required when ambient temperature is 40 °F or less, with removal limited to a surface‑temperature change of no more than 40 °F per hour. For roller‑compacted concrete (Section 502.3) curing prevents inadequate strength development caused by premature moisture loss after rolling; the surface must be kept moist immediately after final rolling and in‑field density testing, and a curing method must be maintained for at least 72 hours until a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi is developed. For bonded concrete overlays on asphalt (Section 540.3) curing prevents premature strength loss and potential cracking due to rapid cooling; curing material must be applied within thirty minutes after bleed‑water dissipates and retained until the opening strength is reached, insulating blankets are provided when ambient air is 45 °F or lower, and insulation may be removed only when temperature exceeds 45 °F or average compressive strength exceeds 3,000 psi, with surface‑temperature change not exceeding 40 °F per hour. Field curing of test cylinders (Section 704.1) solves the problem of obtaining reliable compressive‑strength data by maintaining cylinder temperature between 60 °F and 80 °F for the first 24 ± 2 hours using a PTM No. 611 tank or equivalent, thereby preventing strength loss from improper moisture or temperature control. In partial‑depth repairs (Section 525.3) curing prevents the repair from becoming defective when ambient conditions cause rapid temperature shifts; a white membrane‑forming compound must be applied with an atomizing spray before each application and insulation or heating provided if air temperature falls below 80 °F, while hourly monitoring ensures temperature change does not exceed 40 °F per hour. For pervious concrete pavement (Section 520.3) curing prevents premature loading that could compromise integrity; curing must begin within 20 minutes of discharge, the surface must be completely covered with polyethylene sheeting for a minimum of 7 days, and rollers or traffic are prohibited until that period has elapsed. In precast concrete products (Section 714.8) curing addresses the problem of units being stripped or handled before adequate strength is obtained; products must remain in their forms until at least 70 % of the 28‑day strength is achieved and then be kept in an environment of at least 50 °F during secondary cure until 80 % of the 28‑day minimum compressive strength indicated on the shop drawing is reached. For rapid‑set concrete patch repairs (Section 516.3) curing ensures that test cylinders develop a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi before opening to traffic, thereby preventing premature loading of under‑strength concrete. In bonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545.3) curing solves premature heat loss and inadequate strength development; a curing compound must be applied at a minimum rate of 1 gallon (± 0.1 gallon) per 125 sq ft, insulating blankets are required when forecast air temperature is 45 °F or less, and removal follows the same temperature‑change limits as above. For full‑depth reclamation (Section 344.3) curing solves insufficient strength development in chemically stabilized reclaimed material before traffic opening; the reclaimed layer must be kept damp by daily water application or an emulsified asphalt primer followed by a fog seal at a residual rate between 0.02 and 0.10 gallons per square yard, and a minimum seven‑day cure period is enforced. For roller‑compacted concrete acceptance (Section 502.3(m)) curing prevents acceptance of pavement that has not attained required strength; test cylinders are evaluated at 3 days and 7 days with a threshold of ≥3,000 psi allowing discontinuation of field cure, and a 28‑day test must show a minimum of 3,500 psi before the pavement is considered acceptable. In cold‑recycled asphalt base courses (Section 341.3) curing eliminates excess moisture that would jeopardize placement and durability; a minimum cure of at least 2 weeks for emulsified‑asphalt mixes or until average moisture content drops below 2 % for foamed‑asphalt mixes is required. For pervious concrete pavement systems (Section 520.2) curing prevents early‑age temperature‑related failures such as freezing; cold‑weather provisions must protect the concrete throughout a 7‑day period and hot‑weather plans must specify hydration‑stabilizing admixture additions in 5 °F increments. In high‑friction surface treatment (Section 659.3) curing prevents the binder from hardening before proper bonding, which would impair aggregate retention; a mandatory 30‑day cure of new asphalt pavement is required before HFST application, and equipment and traffic are prohibited during this period. For slurry seal (Section 482.3) curing solves premature traffic loading that could cause mixture pick‑up, so traffic is prohibited until the slab fully cures to a firm condition. In steel‑slag fine aggregate (Section 703.1) curing eliminates excessive volumetric expansion; stockpiles are limited to 30,000 tons, soaked and maintained in a uniformly moist condition for not less than six months, and only aggregates with an average total volumetric expansion of less than 0.50 % are accepted, while any batch that fails this criterion must undergo an additional two‑month cure before retesting. In long‑life concrete pavement joints (Section 530.3) curing prevents moisture loss from concrete at joint locations during and after joint sawing, which could degrade joint performance; a specific method is required to cure and protect joints throughout the curing period. In slip‑form cement concrete pavements (Section 501) curing should be initiated within thirty minutes after bleed‑water has dissipated and continued until the slab attains the strength required for opening to traffic as defined in Section 501.3(q). Normal curing may use any approved cover or membrane method provided the same method is maintained throughout unless the Representative directs a change; if the normal covering cannot be placed promptly, an intermediate monomolecular‑film curing compound must be applied before resuming normal curing. When the air temperature is above 40 °F and is forecast to remain above that level for at least four hours, the PAMS liquid membrane may be employed as the primary cure. A black membrane‑forming curing compound is appropriate only when the concrete will be overlaid with asphalt and will not be opened to traffic before the surface course is placed; otherwise another approved method must be selected.

For unbonded concrete overlays (Section 548) curing is appropriate after finishing and texturing provided it is applied within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and retained until the pavement reaches the strength required for opening to traffic as specified in Section 548.3(q). Insulating blankets are required when air temperature is forty °F or less and may be removed only when temperatures rise above that level or when compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, while ensuring surface‑temperature changes do not exceed forty °F per hour. When a white‑pigmented liquid membrane‑forming compound is specified it shall be applied uniformly at the minimum rate of one gallon per 150 sq ft for sections thicker than six inches (or per 125 sq ft for thinner sections) and may be reapplied after joint sawing. If any other curing cover is used, it must be removed from the joint area before cutting and replaced immediately afterward; removal time shall not exceed one‑half hour.

In roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502) curing is appropriate immediately after the final rolling pass and in‑field density testing; at that moment the surface must be kept moist until a water cure with a misting truck, white polyethylene sheeting, a curing compound, or cold‑weather curing is applied and maintained for at least seventy‑two hours while the pavement develops a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi. The pavement must remain protected from traffic throughout this period and may be opened to traffic only after the cure period described in Section 502.3(j) is complete.

For bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 540) curing is appropriate after finishing and texturing when curing materials are placed within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and retained until the slab reaches the strength required for opening to traffic as set out in Section 540.3(q). Insulating blankets should be provided if air temperature is forty‑five °F or less and may be removed when the air temperature exceeds forty‑five °F or when average compressive strength exceeds 3,000 psi, provided surface‑temperature changes do not exceed forty °F in any one‑hour period. When curing is performed with a method other than a white‑pigmented liquid membrane‑forming compound, the cover must be removed from the joint area before sawing and replaced immediately afterward; removal shall not exceed one‑half hour.

Field‑cured concrete test cylinders (Section 704) require curing whenever such specimens are prepared. The cylinders must be cured in accordance with PTM No. 611 using a tank or boxes that maintain the temperature adjacent to the cylinders between 60 °F and 80 °F for the first twenty‑four ± 2 hours, and a Bluetooth‑enabled recorder must log temperature at least hourly within a range of thirty °F to one‑twenty °F with an accuracy of one °F. If the maturity method is selected to estimate in‑place strength, the contractor shall provide maturity meters and PTM No. 640 temperature sensors, although casting cylinders per PTM 611 remains recommended as a backup.

In Class AA cement‑concrete partial‑depth repairs (Section 525) curing with a white membrane‑forming compound is appropriate for both the Modified mix and the Accelerated mix; the same atomizing spray tank with an adjustable nozzle and agitation before each application is used. For the Accelerated repair, curing must be protected when ambient air temperature drops below 80 °F by providing insulation or heating, and the curing temperature may not change by more than forty °F within any one‑hour period; exceeding that limit renders the work defective. When a rapid‑set concrete patching material is used, the appropriate method is to follow the manufacturer’s recommendations rather than the standard white‑compound procedure.

Curing of cement‑treated permeable base courses (Section 303) is appropriate immediately after compaction; the contractor shall apply a white‑membrane forming compound at a rate of one gallon per 100 sq ft ± 10% (or as directed) and keep it in place for twenty‑four hours. As an alternative, the contractor may cover the base course with white polyethylene sheeting and maintain that covering for the same period. If temperature drops below forty °F during the initial twenty‑four‑hour period, the cure must be extended an additional twenty‑four hours, and if temperature falls to thirty‑two °F within seventy‑two hours of placement the base course must be removed and replaced at no cost to the Department.

Concrete pavement patching (Section 516) requires curing for all patches in accordance with Section 501.3(l); a curing compound shall be applied to the fresh patch and may be either the standard white PAMS membrane or, where permitted, a Type 2 white pigmented liquid membrane‑forming compound. For rapid‑set concrete patch repairs, the cure schedule for quality‑control cylinders must follow the manufacturer’s recommendations before the pavement can be opened to traffic, and when the contract specifies an alternative compound it replaces the PAMS product rather than eliminating curing altogether.

For plain cement concrete base courses (Section 301) curing with a liquid membrane‑forming compound is permissible only when the black formulation is used and the surface remains inaccessible to traffic until the overlying surface course is placed; the white formulation shall not be allowed. If the black compound becomes unsatisfactory as a curing agent, its use must be discontinued and curing resumed by other methods as specified.

In roller‑compacted concrete base courses (Section 323) curing consists of applying emulsified asphalt after the RCC control joints have been saw‑cut and loose debris removed, and before any asphalt wearing course or surface treatment is placed. The emulsified asphalt must be applied uniformly with a void‑free finish using equipment approved by the Representative; curing compounds are expressly prohibited and therefore should never be used as an alternative method.

For roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502) field cure may be discontinued when three‑day cylinders reach at least 3,000 psi unless otherwise directed; if the three‑day result is below that value curing must continue and be retested at seven days, where it may be stopped when the seven‑day cylinders reach at least 3,000 psi; should the seven‑day strength still fall short, curing continues until twenty‑eight‑day cylinders verify a minimum of 3,500 psi, after which the pavement is accepted, otherwise the work is considered defective and must be removed or replaced.

Curing of the binder for high‑friction surface treatment (Section 659) is appropriate only after the underlying pavement satisfies its minimum age limits: new asphalt pavements must have completed a mandatory thirty‑day cure period and concrete surfaces must be at least twenty‑eight days old before HFST can be installed. During the binder’s own curing phase contractor equipment and traffic are prohibited, and the mixed binder may not be allowed to separate, dry, or harden in a way that would impair aggregate bonding; if either age threshold has not been reached the HFST treatment must be delayed rather than proceeding with curing.

Curing of fine aggregate (Section 703) is prescribed only for steel‑slag fine aggregate. The material must be placed in a controlled stockpile not exceeding 30,000 tons, fully soaked with water before or during stacking, and kept uniformly moist for a period of not less than six months; after this minimum cure period the stockpile may be sampled and accepted if the average total volumetric expansion measured by PTM No. 130 is less than 0.50 %. If the expansion criterion is not met, the stockpile must continue curing for a minimum of two additional months before retesting. For all other fine aggregates no curing requirement is specified. Required inputs

For concrete pavements covered by Sections 501.3, 548.3, 502.3(a), 540.3, 704.1(d), 525.3(g), 520.3 and 714.8, curing must be started within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation after finishing and texturing; a curing material such as a monomolecular film or white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene (PAMS) liquid membrane is applied uniformly over the entire surface at a minimum rate of one gallon per 150 sq ft for overlays thicker than six inches or one gallon per 125 sq ft for those six inches or less, or higher as required by the manufacturer, and the material must remain in place until the slab attains the strength needed for traffic opening. When ambient air temperature is forty degrees Fahrenheit or lower, adequate insulating blankets (white polyethylene sheeting, burlap‑backed, fiber‑backed, or polypropylene‑fiber‑backed) overlapping at least twelve inches and sealed on the sides shall be provided to prevent rapid heat loss; insulation may be removed only after the air temperature exceeds forty degrees Fahrenheit or when the average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi (or 3,000 psi for certain RCC pavements), and removal must not cause a surface‑temperature change greater than forty degrees Fahrenheit in any one‑hour period. If the forecasted air temperature is expected to fall below fifty degrees Fahrenheit, high‑low thermometers shall be placed on the slab; when readings are between forty and thirty‑five degrees Fahrenheit the cure period shall be extended an additional day, and any reading below thirty‑five degrees Fahrenheit renders the concrete defective. For accelerated‑strength concrete pavements the cure must be aided by insulation or heating, and slab temperature shall be monitored hourly to ensure no change exceeds forty °F in any one‑hour period; the air temperature at time of applying PAMS curing compound must be above forty °F and forecasted to remain above that level for four hours thereafter. For roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavement, the surface shall be kept uniformly moist for a minimum of seventy‑two hours after final rolling, with moisture supplied by water cure using a misting spray truck, soaking hoses, sprinkler system or other means, by covering with white polyethylene sheeting, or by applying a curing compound that forms a uniform solid opaque film; traffic may be allowed only after the pavement has reached at least 3,000 psi compressive strength. For concrete repair cylinders each batch shall be placed in six‑by‑twelve‑inch molds with tight‑fitting domed caps, the fresh mix must meet a slump target and stay within one‑and‑a‑half inches of that target while satisfying air‑content, density and temperature limits; the cylinders are to be field‑cured in a PTM No. 611‑approved tank or box capable of maintaining surface temperature between sixty and eighty degrees Fahrenheit for the first twenty‑four ± 2 hours, with a Bluetooth‑enabled digital recorder logging temperature at least hourly over a thirty‑°F to one‑hundred‑twenty °F range to ±1 °F accuracy, and, if the maturity method is used, appropriate sensors shall be installed. For Class AA cement concrete repairs a white membrane‑forming curing compound shall be applied with an atomizing spray tank whose nozzle is adjustable and whose solution is agitated before each use; when ambient air temperature falls below eighty degrees Fahrenheit insulation or heating must be provided, and the curing temperature shall be checked at least hourly to ensure no change exceeds forty °F in any one‑hour period, otherwise the work is defective. For pervious concrete pavement (Section 520) curing begins within twenty minutes after discharge; the surface shall be completely covered with polyethylene sheeting that is thoroughly secured at all exterior edges and interior laps to prevent wind removal, and the cover shall remain for a minimum of seven days. The mixture must meet void structure of twenty percent ± 5 %, hardened density not exceeding 130 lb/ft³ ± 5 lb/ft³, and an infiltration rate exceeding 200 in/hr; air temperatures may not fall below forty degrees Fahrenheit during the first twenty‑four hours and must exceed fifty degrees Fahrenheit at any time throughout the seven‑day curing period before traffic is permitted. For cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303) field curing shall commence immediately after compaction and may be performed either by applying a white membrane‑forming curing compound at about one gallon per 100 ft² (±10 %) or by covering with white polyethylene sheeting; the material shall remain for twenty‑four hours, and if the CTPBC temperature falls below forty degrees Fahrenheit during that period the cure time is extended an additional twenty‑four hours. For concrete patches (Section 516) a liquid membrane‑forming curing compound—either standard white PAMS or Type 2 white pigmented variant—shall be applied in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations for rapid‑set patching material and also used for QC cylinders. For plain cement concrete base course (Section 301) a black liquid membrane‑forming curing compound may be used only when the base will remain inaccessible to traffic until the surface course is placed; white compounds are prohibited, and use of the black compound must be discontinued if it becomes unsatisfactory as a curing agent, after which other specified methods shall be employed. For bonded concrete overlay (Section 545) the curing compound must be applied to all exposed surfaces within thirty minutes after bleed water has dissipated and retained until required strength is achieved; it is spread uniformly as a solid white opaque film at a minimum rate of one gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 ft² or greater if the manufacturer specifies, and when forecast air temperature is forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or less insulating blankets shall be provided and may be removed only after ambient temperature exceeds forty‑5 degrees Fahrenheit or average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, with surface temperature change not exceeding forty °F in any one‑hour period. Concrete placement is limited to a concrete temperature between sixty‑five and ninety degrees Fahrenheit and an existing surface temperature below one‑hundred‑twenty degrees Fahrenheit; fog‑misting or protective covers shall be used as needed. For full‑depth reclamation (Section 344) the material shall be kept damp by daily water application or by spraying an emulsified asphalt primer followed by a fog seal at a residual asphalt rate between 0.02 and 0.10 gallons per square yard, applied while the mix is at its optimum moisture content but not exceeding that optimum by more than three percent; curing continues until the seven‑day strength requirement is satisfied, with favorable site conditions (no rain, sunshine, low humidity, high temperature) allowing accelerated curing, otherwise strength gain may require from two or three days up to at least two weeks depending on material type and climate. For roller‑compacted concrete base courses (Section 323) after saw‑cutting control joints and removing loose debris a coating of emulsified asphalt shall be applied over the entire surface, providing a uniform void‑free layer placed with equipment approved not to damage or mar the compacted surface; traffic may be permitted prior to placement of the wearing course. For cold‑recycled asphalt base course (Section 341) curing shall last at least two weeks or until average moisture content falls below two percent, whether stabilized with emulsified or foamed asphalt; placement may proceed only if no freezing temperatures have occurred for twenty‑four hours and ambient air temperature is at least forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit and rising. For thin bonded concrete overlay (Section 541) the mix shall incorporate Portland cement concrete reinforced with synthetic or steel macrofibers, use No. 67 coarse aggregate when overlay depth is less than three inches, be designed to achieve a target chloride‑ion penetration classified as Low, Very low, or Negligible after a fifty‑six‑day (or shorter) curing period according to AASHTO T 277 or T 358, and meet specified resistivity or coulomb limits for Moderate classification. For high‑friction surface treatment (Section 659) the binder must be blended within ±2 % by volume and applied at a uniform thickness of fifty‑to‑sixty‑five mils; aggregate is placed at twelve‑to‑fifteen pounds per square yard, and the treatment may not be placed on new asphalt until after a mandatory thirty‑day cure period nor on concrete surfaces or repairs younger than twenty‑eight days; during binder curing no equipment or traffic is permitted and wind speeds must not exceed fifteen mph (gusts of twenty mph). For slurry seal (Section 482) the mixture shall be placed at an application temperature between fifty and one hundred twenty‑five degrees Fahrenheit, a fog coat of water limited to a maximum of 0.05 gallon per square yard must be applied immediately before spreading, and traffic is prohibited until the seal fully cures to a firm condition. For steel‑slag fine aggregate (Section 703) stockpiles shall not exceed thirty thousand tons, the material must be completely soaked with water before or during piling and kept uniformly moist for at least six months; after this period the stockpile is acceptable only if average total volumetric expansion measured by PTM No. 130 is less than 0.50 %. For long‑life concrete pavement (Section 530) curing shall protect joints and prevent moisture loss during and after joint sawing, be tied to a concrete temperature control plan activated when concrete temperatures exceed eighty degrees Fahrenheit at placement, and the mix must meet a plastic air content of seven percent ± 1.5 %; plant‑mixed concrete is used unless truck‑mixed concrete is approved for limited areas. For stabilized slabs (Section 679) field curing may be performed only when daytime air temperature is at least forty degrees Fahrenheit and the subgrade or base course is not frozen; deflection testing preceding curing must not occur if temperature exceeds seventy degrees Fahrenheit, the QC plan shall specify the curing period and opening‑to‑traffic time, and after grouting drill holes are sealed with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete patching material applied flush with the pavement surface. Field curing of pavement materials is governed by a series of design‑controlled actions that define when curing must begin, how long it must be maintained, the rates and types of curing agents, temperature and moisture tolerances, and the pre‑construction tests required to verify compliance. For slip‑form concrete pavements (Section 501.3(g)) curing shall start within 30 minutes after bleed water has dissipated and shall continue until the compressive‑strength level required for opening to traffic in Section 501.3(q) is achieved; any approved method may be used, but when sheet curing is selected the sheets must be wide enough to cover the surface and sides, overlapped at least 12 inches and sealed tightly, while burlap‑backed or fiber‑backed sheets shall be saturated with water before placement. If a liquid membrane such as PAMS is applied, air temperature must be above 40 °F at the time of application and forecast to remain above that level for four hours; the compound is sprayed in a fine mist at roughly one gallon per 150 ft², and any rain or film damage requires an immediate re‑coat of equal amount. In cold weather the pavement temperature shall not be less than 40 °F; if temperatures fall between 40 °F and 35 °F the cure period is extended one day, and a reading below 35 °F renders the concrete defective. For unbonded concrete overlays (Section 548.3) curing also must begin within 30 minutes after bleed‑water dissipation and continue until the strength required for traffic opening in Section 548.3(q) is reached; a white‑pigmented liquid membrane‑forming compound shall be applied at a minimum rate of one gallon (± 0.1 gallon) per 150 ft² for pavements greater than six inches thick and per 125 ft² for pavements less than or equal to six inches, or according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, whichever is greater, and may be reapplied in two equal passes with the second pass after sawing. When air temperature is 40 °F or less insulating blankets shall be provided and may be removed only when air temperature exceeds 40 °F or when average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, ensuring that surface temperature does not change more than 40 °F in any one‑hour period; for other curing methods a cover over a joint must be removed no longer than one‑half hour before sawing and replaced immediately after the cut. Roller‑compacted concrete (Section 502.3(a)) requires construction of a test section at least 50 feet long sized to the plan thickness and width prior to full‑scale paving to verify mix consistency, equipment performance, curing procedures and compressive strength. After final rolling and in‑field density testing the RCC surface shall be kept moist by water cure, white polyethylene sheeting, curing compound or cold‑weather curing; the selected method(s) shall be maintained for a minimum of 72 hours and until a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi is developed as determined by the Section 502.3(m)3 test. Water curing shall use a water truck equipped with misting spray nozzles or comparable means to provide uniform moisture without damaging the finished surface, and curing compound shall be applied immediately after rolling to produce a uniform solid opaque coverage. For bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavement (Section 540.3) curing material shall be applied within 30 minutes after bleed‑water dissipation and retained until the strength required for opening to traffic in Section 540.3(q) is achieved; when ambient air temperature is 45 °F or less insulating blankets shall be placed and may be removed only when air temperature exceeds 45 °F or when average compressive strength exceeds 3,000 psi, with removal performed so that surface temperature change does not exceed 40 °F in any one‑hour period. The timing of insulation application or removal may be governed by the maturity method described in Section 540.3(q), which uses accumulated degree‑hours at the slab top as a pre‑construction test to verify sufficient curing. Laboratory curing for quality control (Section 704.1(d)) shall employ PTM No. 611‑approved curing tanks and boxes capable of maintaining temperature between 60 °F and 80 °F for the first 24 ± 2 hours, with a Bluetooth‑enabled digital recorder logging temperature at least hourly within a 30 °F–120 °F range to an accuracy of 1 °F; cylinder molds shall be 6‑inch by 12‑inch with tight‑fitting domed caps as specified in PTM No. 611. If the maturity method is used, appropriate maturity meters and PTM No. 640 temperature sensors must be provided, and a field operation QC plan describing this equipment shall be submitted on Form CS‑704 at the pre‑construction conference or no later than three weeks before the initial concrete pour. Partial‑depth repairs of Class AA cement concrete (Section 525.3(g)) shall receive a white membrane‑forming curing compound applied in accordance with Section 516.3(j) using an atomizing spray tank equipped with an adjustable nozzle and agitated before each application; for accelerated repairs the same compound is applied, test cylinders are cured under identical conditions, insulation or heating shall be provided when ambient temperature drops below 80 °F, and curing temperature shall be monitored at least hourly so that no change exceeds 40 °F in any one‑hour period, otherwise the work is deemed defective. Rapid‑set concrete patches are to be cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. For pervious concrete pavement (Section 520.3) curing must start within 20 minutes after discharge and the entire surface shall be completely covered with polyethylene sheeting that is thoroughly secured at all exterior edges and interior laps to resist wind removal; this covering shall remain in place for a minimum of seven days, and the pavement may not be opened to vehicular traffic until those seven days have elapsed and ambient temperature has exceeded 55 °F at any time during the day. Precast concrete products (Section 714.8) are governed by a quality‑control plan prepared in accordance with ACI 308 that specifies required strength milestones and temperature limits; primary cure shall be maintained until the unit reaches at least 70 % of its 28‑day compressive strength, after which stripping time is set based on the product’s inherent characteristics or, when not shown on the drawings, by a reduced‑time/strength calculation approved in a QC‑Plan addendum sealed by a Pennsylvania‑registered professional engineer. Secondary curing shall be performed by an approved method while units are kept at no less than 50 °F until they achieve 80 % of the 28‑day minimum compressive strength indicated on the shop drawing, and after secondary cure storage temperature may not fall below 32 °F unless the unit‑air temperature differential is under 30 °F; units remain in storage until the full 28‑day minimum strength is obtained. Cement‑treated permeable base courses (Section 303.3(g)) shall be cured immediately after compaction either with a white membrane‑forming curing compound applied at a rate of one gallon per 100 ft² ± 10 % of the surface area or by covering with white polyethylene sheeting; the material shall remain for twenty‑four hours, and if the temperature of the CTPBC falls below 40 °F during that period the cure time must be extended an additional twenty‑four hours, while a drop to 32 °F within seventy‑two hours requires removal and replacement at no cost to the Department. Concrete pavement patches (Section 516.3) shall be cured with either the specified white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene (PAMS) liquid membrane‑forming curing compound or, where permitted, a Type 2 white pigmented liquid membrane‑forming curing compound; rapid‑set repairs shall follow the manufacturer’s recommendations, and traffic may be allowed only when quality‑control cylinders measured in accordance with PTM No. 604 reach at least one thousand pounds per square inch. Plain cement concrete base courses (Section 301.3) are limited to a black liquid membrane‑forming curing compound, which may be used only when the base course will not be accessible to traffic before placement of the surface course; if the compound becomes unsatisfactory it must be discontinued and other curing methods employed as specified. Bonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545.3(g)) shall have curing material applied within thirty minutes after bleed‑water dissipation and retained until the strength required for opening to traffic in Section 545.3(l) is achieved; when forecast air temperature is forty‑five °F or less insulating blankets shall be placed and may be removed only when air temperature exceeds forty‑five °F or when average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, with removal performed so that surface temperature change does not exceed forty °F in any one‑hour period. The curing compound shall be applied homogeneously at a minimum rate of one gallon (± 0.1 gallon) per 125 ft² or the greater manufacturer‑specified rate, may be placed in two equal passes with the second after sawing, and joint faces must be protected from exposure to the compound or cleaned before sealant installation. Full‑depth reclaimed material (Section 344.3(e)) shall remain in cure until the seven‑day strength requirement is satisfied; heavy traffic is prohibited during this period and the layer shall be kept damp by daily water application or by applying an emulsified asphalt primer followed by a fog seal at a residual rate of 0.02 to 0.10 gallons per square yard while the material is at optimum moisture content but not exceeding three percent. Proof rolling with a vehicle comparable to the heaviest expected traffic or verification of the seven‑day compressive strength (three hundred to five hundred psi) is required before opening to traffic, and three standard Proctor samples per three thousand‑square‑yard lot shall be wrapped, sealed in an airtight bag and cured for seven days. Roller‑compacted concrete base courses (Section 323.3(j)) are not cured with membrane compounds; instead emulsified asphalt must be placed over the RCC surface in accordance with Section 460.3(b) to form a uniform, void‑free coat using equipment approved by the Representative that does not damage or mar the compacted surface, and the pavement may be opened to local traffic only after the prescribed cure period is complete. Acceptance of roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502.3(m)) requires pre‑construction verification that the final average density is not less than ninety‑eight percent of the maximum wet density with no individual test below ninety‑six percent, that compressive‑strength cylinders molded per ASTM C 1435/PTM No. 604 achieve at least three thousand pounds per square inch at three days (or, if not met, at seven days) to permit cessation of field cure unless otherwise directed and reach a minimum of three thousand five hundred psi at twenty‑eight days, and that pavement depth measured in accordance with PTM No. 614 be within one‑quarter inch of the plan depth (deficient cores may average no more than one‑quarter inch). Cold‑recycled asphalt base courses (Section 341.3(a)) shall be cured for at least two weeks before any wearing or binder course is placed, and the average moisture content must be less than two percent; foamed‑asphalt stabilized CRABC shall also be cured until its average moisture content falls below two percent. Prior to placing the overlying course a minimum of three core samples each weighing at least 1,200 grams shall be dry‑cut or dry‑extracted in accordance with PTM No. 1, tested for moisture per PTM No. 749, and any resulting core holes shall be backfilled and compacted with CRABC material or an approved alternate. Thin bonded concrete overlays (Section 541.2) shall be designed to achieve a target chloride‑ion penetration classified as Low, Very low, or Negligible after a curing period of fifty‑six days or less in accordance with AASHTO T 277 or AASHTO T 358; acceptance is based on permeability test results that shall not exceed 2,800 coulombs per AASHTO T 277 Table 1 and shall not exceed 15.6 kΩ‑cm for four‑by‑eight‑inch cylinders or 12.3 kΩ‑cm for six‑by‑twelve‑inch cylinders per AASHTO T 358 Table 1, although a Moderate level may be accepted if these limits are met. Pervious concrete curing (Section 520) is governed by the prescribed Concrete Curing Material referenced in Section 711.1(a) or 711.1(b) and by a QC plan prepared, submitted and approved before any production begins; for cold‑weather conditions the design shall include provisions to protect the concrete from freezing throughout the seven‑day curing period as required by ACI 306.1, while for hot‑weather placement the design shall contain detailed plans specifying the amount of hydration‑stabilizing admixture to be added in five‑degree Fahrenheit increments to provide sufficient time for placing and finishing before initial set. High‑friction surface treatment (Section 659.3(e)) shall not be placed on new asphalt pavements until after a mandatory thirty‑day cure period and shall not be applied to concrete surfaces or repairs that are less than twenty‑eight days of age; during the cure period contractor equipment and traffic shall be prohibited, the mixed binder must remain protected from exposure, and the binder shall be uniformly mixed to within ± 2 % of the manufacturer’s volume specifications, applied at a uniform thickness of fifty‑to‑sixty‑five mils (twenty‑five‑to‑thirty‑two square feet per gallon), with aggregate spread onto the wet, uncured binder at a rate of twelve‑to‑fifteen pounds per square yard to achieve full coverage without visible binder. After the binder has cured surplus aggregate must be reclaimed by vacuum sweeping between fifteen and thirty days, and a calibrated skid test shall be performed between forty‑five and one‑hundred‑twenty days in accordance with ASTM E274 (AASHTO T 242). Curing and protecting covers shall be white polyethylene sheeting used in accordance with ASTM C171 that shall have a minimum tensile strength of seven point zero pounds per linear inch in both the machine direction and the cross‑direction; this tensile requirement serves as the acceptance criterion before the material is placed over fresh concrete or asphalt during pre‑construction curing. After cement‑treated permeable base course has cured, the Representative shall direct a full‑depth test hole to be dug or drilled at a rate of one per three thousand square yards of finished base; the measured depth shall be compared with the indicated design depth and if it is deficient by one‑half inch or more, or if the surface fails the tolerance criteria, the work shall be deemed defective and must be removed and replaced according to the approved plan, with additional longitudinal and transverse test holes required until the depth meets the specified requirement; any excavated holes shall then be backfilled with fresh CTPBC mixture. Steel‑slag fine aggregate used in asphalt wearing courses shall be limited to a maximum stockpile size of thirty thousand tons, shall be completely soaked with water before or during placement and thereafter maintained in a uniformly moist condition for a period of not less than six months; the Representative shall sample the pile and test it for expansive behavior using PTM No. 130, accepting the stockpile only when the average total volumetric expansion is less than 0.50 %; if this limit is not met the pile shall be cured for a minimum of two additional months before retesting, and the LTS may also require petrographic analysis per PTM No. 518 to verify durability. Long‑life concrete pavement (LLCP) specifications require a QC plan that includes a method for curing and protecting joints to prevent moisture loss from concrete in and adjacent to joints during and after joint sawing, a concrete temperature control plan that outlines actions when concrete temperatures exceed eighty degrees Fahrenheit at the point of placement, and it shall require that the first truck in each lot be sampled for plastic air with an acceptance limit of seven point zero percent ± 1.5 %; trucks not meeting this requirement shall be excluded. Slab stabilization (Section 679.3) requires a field QC plan that includes details of the curing period and open‑to‑traffic times; work may not start if daytime temperatures are below forty degrees Fahrenheit or if the subgrade or base course is frozen, and all curing‑related details are subject to Representative review and approval. Equipment and personnel

Preparation of the pavement surface begins with equipment specified for the various pavement types. For slip‑form concrete pavements the preparation may involve a fully energized, self‑propelled slip‑form paving machine equipped with side forms, automatic alignment controls and vibrating/tamping units as required by Section 501.3(g). Loose debris is removed by a power broom, vacuum truck or other approved means in accordance with Section 548.3. The subgrade or subbase must be clean of foreign material and uniformly moistened before any roller‑compacted concrete is placed, as stipulated in Section 502.3(a). Light equipment such as a spring‑tooth rake or equivalent and a light tractor are used to scarify the underlying soil to at least six inches in accordance with Section 520.3(c). Rubblized pavement/subbase is prepared by sprinkling water until it is readily absorbed, per Section 303.3. Hand tools and small vibratory compactors are employed to re‑compact any subbase disturbed during concrete patching preparation, as required by Section 516.3. Preparation also includes the use of a self‑propelled mechanical scarifier to remove existing surface, shot, sand or water blasting equipment to clear loose concrete and rust, and a clean compressed‑air source delivering at least 100 psi for final cleaning, as described in Section 545.3. In full‑depth reclamation the preparation of reclaimed material requires a Ready Mix Concrete Truck or a distributor/tanker equipped with a recirculating pump and agitation system to keep slurries from settling, while pulverization is performed beforehand, as required by Section 344.3(e). Cold recycled asphalt base course preparation calls for liquid‑metering equipment that must stay within ± 2.0 percent by weight of liquids, as specified in Section 341.3(a). High‑friction surface treatment preparation mandates the use of mechanical sweepers and high‑pressure air‑wash equipment delivering at least 180 cfm at 80 psi, shot‑blasting machines for concrete, and a hot compressed‑air lance to dry the pavement, as required by Section 659.3(e).

Placement of the concrete layer may be performed with the slip‑form paving machine that provides side forms, automatic alignment controls and vibrating/tamping units (Section 501.3(g)). An interlayer is placed by rolling out geotextile flat on the cleaned pavement and, when an asphalt interlayer is used, it is compacted with a rubber‑tire roller (Section 548.3). The concrete mix is produced by a batch‑type or continuous mixing plant, delivered by covered haul trucks and spread by a conventional or high‑density paver equipped with compaction devices (Section 502.3(a)). Incidental equipment such as wheelbarrows, shovels and scoops may be used during placement together with thermometers, air meters meeting AASHTO T 196/T 152 and slump cones meeting AASHTO T 119 for fresh‑mix evaluation (Section 704.1(d)). Truck mixers operating at the manufacturer’s speed of 70–100 drum revolutions discharge concrete through conveyors or direct discharge to a paver equipped with compaction tampers, a roller screed or vibrating screed (Section 520.3). An approved asphalt paver or mechanical spreader fitted with a screed, plate vibrator and fully automated sensors for profile and transverse grade control may be used for placement of cement‑treated permeable base courses (Section 303.3). Sufficient hauling units are required to keep concrete moving continuously during patching operations (Section 516.3). In full‑depth reclamation the reclaimed material is placed using a Ready Mix Concrete Truck or distributor/tanker equipped with a recirculating pump and agitation system, as described in Section 344.3(e). Cold recycled asphalt base course mixtures are placed with an asphalt paver as called for in Section 413,3(e)1 (referenced in Section 341.3(a)). High‑friction surface treatment binder is applied using calibrated containers and a low‑speed, high‑torque drill fitted with a helical stirrer to achieve binder proportions within ± 2 % by volume, and continuous mixers, metering pumps (heated if recommended) and equipment that can mix, meter, monitor and apply both binder and aggregate in one pass at least 2 000 sq yd/hr, as required by Section 659.3(e).

Compaction or consolidation of the slip‑form concrete is achieved with vibrating/tamping units for full‑width consolidation on the slip‑form machine (Section 501.3(g)). A rubber‑tire roller may be used to compact an asphalt interlayer (Section 548.3). Rollers meeting Section 108.05(c)3 are employed, and if a minimum 95 % field density is not achieved, a self‑propelled smooth dual steel‑drum roller having a minimum weight of eight tons is used together with steel‑drum or rubber‑tired rollers for final compaction (Section 502.3(a)). A maximum two‑ton smooth steel‑wheeled roller making one to four static passes, or other steel finishing equipment, provides the final surface finish (Section 520.3). Steel‑wheel power rollers weighing between eight and ten tons are required for consolidation of cement‑treated permeable base courses (Section 303.3); vibratory paver screed alone may not be used. Small vibratory compactors and a roller are employed after subbase work to achieve proper grade and slope in patching applications (Section 516.3). Full‑depth reclamation compaction is carried out with roller equipment whose passes are verified by a calibrated nuclear density gauge operated in direct transmission mode until the minimum density of at least 95 % of the laboratory compacted maximum density at optimum moisture content is achieved and no appreciable increase occurs, as required by Section 344.3(e). Cold recycled asphalt base course compaction relies on rollers as specified in Section 108.05(c) (Section 341.3(a)). Consolidation for high‑friction surface treatment is achieved by mechanically or hand‑spreading aggregate onto the wet binder at 12–15 lb per sq yd after the binder has been spread with a serrated squeegee to a uniform thickness of 50–65 mil (Section 659.3(e)).

Finishing is performed with a finishing machine equipped with a rubber‑tired wheel spreader and a transverse finishing unit positioned about twelve inches from the edge, while mechanical or long‑handled floats may be used for floating (Section 501.3(g)). Surface flatness is verified with a twelve‑foot straightedge swung from handles extending three feet beyond half the slab width, and tie‑bar pull‑out capacity is checked with a center‑pull hydraulic jack, load gauge and bearing ring rated to 12 000 lb or a one‑thirty‑second inch slip (Section 501.3(g)). Surface finishing and tolerance checks employ a self‑propelled diamond grinder or cutting tools together with a twelve‑foot straightedge (Section 502.3(a)). A ten‑foot straightedge is used to test the surface in stages, correcting any irregularities greater than one‑half inch (Section 303.3). Final strike‑off and consolidation for patching are performed with the equipment specified in Section 501.3(k), which includes standard finishing tools and texturing devices (Section 516.3). In full‑depth reclamation finishing is limited to shaping the surface within the specified irregularities before curing, while cold recycled asphalt base course finishing is performed during daylight hours without additional machinery, and high‑friction surface treatment finishing uses a serrated squeegee immediately after mixing to distribute a uniform binder thickness of 50–65 mil (Section 659.3(e)).

Curing may be accomplished by covering the slab and sides with white polyethylene sheets that overlap at least twelve inches and are sealed, or by applying a liquid membrane‑forming curing compound with a self‑propelled mechanical spreader and an atomizing spray equipped with a tank agitator and hood to lay a continuous film at one gallon per 150 sq ft (or not more than 0.015 gal per sq yd), as required by Section 501.3(g). An alternative monomolecular film is sprayed from a pressure tank with an adjustable nozzle, and cold‑weather work requires high‑low thermometers and insulating blankets when the air temperature is forty °F or less to prevent rapid heat loss (Section 548.3). At least one water truck equipped with fog nozzles may be used to apply a uniform mist, or alternatively white polyethylene sheeting or curing compound may be applied (Section 502.3(a)). The pavement must be covered within twenty minutes of discharge with polyethylene sheeting that is securely fastened at all edges (Section 520.3). Curing may also be performed with a white membrane‑forming curing compound applied at about one gallon per 100 ft² ±10 % or with white polyethylene sheeting, both maintained for twenty‑four hours (extended if temperature falls below forty °F) and protected from traffic for at least three days (Section 303.3). For patching, curing is achieved by applying either a white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene liquid membrane or the permitted Type 2 white pigmented liquid membrane forming curing compound as described in Section 501.3(l)1.c., and insulating blankets are required when ambient temperature is forecasted at forty‑five °F or below, with the curing compound applied uniformly at a minimum of one gallon per 125 ft² (±0.1 gal) to all exposed surfaces (Section 545.3). Full‑depth reclamation curing is maintained by daily water application with spray equipment or by applying an emulsified asphalt prime material followed by an asphalt fog seal or tack coat while the layer remains at optimum moisture content and does not exceed the 3 % limit, as required by Section 344.3(e). Heavy traffic must be prohibited for the seven‑day cure period (Section 344.3(e)). Cold recycled asphalt base course curing does not call for extra equipment but mandates a minimum cure time of at least two weeks for emulsified‑asphalt stabilized bases (or until the average moisture content is less than 2 percent) (Section 341.3(a)). High‑friction surface treatment requires that no equipment or traffic be allowed on the treatment during the curing period (Section 659.3(e)).

Testing includes verification of surface flatness with a twelve‑foot straightedge and tie‑bar pull‑out capacity checks using the center‑pull hydraulic jack, load gauge and bearing ring as described in Section 501.3(g). Field density testing employs direct‑transmission equipment required by ASTM C1040 together with compressive strength tests after a seventy‑two hour cure period (Section 502.3(a)). Test cylinders are formed using six‑inch by twelve‑inch molds with tight‑fitting domed caps (PTM No. 611) and later tested for compressive strength on a compression machine equipped with a capping device per PTM No. 604; curing of field‑cured cylinders is accomplished with a PTM No. 611 curing tank or boxes that maintain sixty to eighty degrees Fahrenheit for the first twenty‑four ± 2 hours, while a Bluetooth‑enabled digital recorder logs temperature at least hourly within thirty to one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit at plus‑minus one degree accuracy; when the maturity method is used, one or more maturity meters and PTM No. 640 temperature sensors are also required (Section 704.1(d)). Unit‑weight testing on each truck is performed according to ASTM C1688, three four‑inch diameter cores are taken for oven‑dry density and void content determination in accordance with ASTM C1754, and infiltration testing follows ASTM C1701 (Section 520.3). The finished surface of cement‑treated permeable base courses is tested with the same ten‑foot straightedge in staged passes to verify that irregularities do not exceed one‑half inch (Section 303.3). Compressive‑strength cylinder testing equipment per PTM No. 604 is used for patching, with cylinders cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations before traffic opening based on a minimum strength of one thousand psi (Section 516.3). Equipment specified for ASTM C1583 bond‑strength tests at seven days and cylinder compressive‑strength testing in accordance with PTM No. 604 is provided to verify the required three‑thousand‑six hundred psi strength before traffic opening (Section 545.3). In full‑depth reclamation a minimum of three standard Proctor samples per ASTM D1633 are prepared, sealed in plastic wrap inside airtight bags and cured for seven days before strength measurement with either an indirect tensile test (minimum 50 psi) or an unconfined compressive strength test (300 to 500 psi), as required by Section 344.3(e). Nuclear density readings are taken after each pass of the compaction equipment using a calibrated nuclear gauge operated in direct transmission mode, as specified in Section 344.3(e). Cold recycled asphalt base course testing employs a twelve‑foot straight edge or template for surface tolerance, density determination in accordance with AASHTO T 310 and moisture‑content measurement per PTM No. 749 (Section 341.3(a)). High‑friction surface treatment performance is verified by a calibrated skid test performed between forty‑five and one hundred twenty days using a smooth‑tire lock‑wheel apparatus in accordance with ASTM E274 (AASHTO T 242) (Section 659.3(e)). Work sequence

Before any curing can be applied, the pavement must have been fully finished—strike‑off and consolidation operations, floating, straightedge testing with any surface corrections, and the initial texture must all be completed so that finishing and texturing are done prior to curing (Section 501). For unbonded concrete overlays, the concrete surface must be finished and textured, bleed water must have dissipated, and curing materials must be applied within 30 minutes of that dissipation; in addition, every pre‑overlay repair, sweeping to remove loose debris, and placement of the chosen interlayer (geotextile or asphalt) must be completed as part of surface preparation (Section 548). For roller‑compacted concrete pavements, the RCC pavement must be fully rolled, in‑field density testing performed no later than 30 minutes after rolling, and the final average density requirements satisfied before keeping the surface moist for curing (Section 502). For bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements, the overlay must first be finished and textured, bleed water must have dissipated, and curing materials applied within 30 minutes of that dissipation; no construction‑related activity may start until the comprehensive QC Plan has been submitted for review and accepted by the Representative (Section 540). Field curing also requires an approved QC Plan—including a field‑operation QC Plan—before concrete placement begins, assignment of a qualified concrete technician and a PennDOT‑certified field testing technician, provision of calibrated thermometers, air meters and slump cones, supply of a curing tank or boxes capable of maintaining cylinder temperature between 60 °F and 80 °F for the first 24 ± 2 hours, and a digital data‑logging system that records temperature at least hourly within 30 °F–120 °F to an accuracy of 1 °F (Section 704). For pervious concrete pavement systems, the pervious concrete must be placed and fully finished with a maximum 2‑ton roller or steel troweling while keeping the voids open, exterior formed edges must be edged to a radius of not less than ¼‑inch, and curing may begin within twenty minutes of concrete discharge (Section 520). For precast concrete products, a QC Plan prepared in accordance with ACI 308 must be approved before any curing can start; the concrete is then cured and protected following that approved plan (Section 714). For cement‑treated permeable base courses, material must first be placed and fully compacted using steel‑wheel power rollers weighing between eight and ten tons, at least one pass completed without over‑compacting, and compaction finished within one hour of adding water to the aggregate and cement; curing may begin immediately thereafter (Section 303). For concrete pavement patching, the patch must be fully finished—including strike‑off, consolidation and texture work required for Type A, B or C patches—surface tolerances must be verified, and the placement date scribed into the fresh concrete along the shoulder edge before curing as prescribed in Section 501.3(l) (Section 516). For a plain cement concrete base course, the base course must not be accessible to traffic until the surface course has been placed; only then may a black liquid‑membrane forming curing compound be used (Section 301). For bonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements, the concrete must already have been finished and textured, bleed water must have dissipated, and curing materials applied within 30 minutes of that dissipation before any curing compound or insulating blankets are placed (Section 545). For full‑depth reclamation, pulverization of the existing roadway to the specified size and depth must be completed, mixing of reclaimed material with any required aggregates, RAP, stabilizing additive(s) and water must follow the mix design at the recommended moisture content, compaction must reach at least 95 % of the laboratory‑compacted maximum density at optimum moisture (with moisture not exceeding optimum by more than 3 %), and surface finishing must ensure irregularities do not exceed 1½ inches before curing can begin (Section 344). For roller‑compacted concrete base course, RCC control joints must first be saw‑cut, loose debris generated by the saw cutting must be cleared, and additional emulsified asphalt must be placed along those joints as specified; no curing compounds are permitted thereafter (Section 323). For cold‑recycled asphalt base course, the mixture must first be processed and mixed with the stabilizing agent, then uniformly spread, shaped and graded to the required lines, grades and depth before the curing step is initiated (Section 341). For bridge approach slabs, the sleeper slab must first be excavated after the subbase has been constructed (unless a geosynthetic stabilized subbase per the GSBA Standard Drawings is used), two layers of at least 4.0‑mil polyethylene sheeting must be placed as a bond breaker with a smooth surface, seams must run longitudinally and overlap a minimum of 12 inches, and manufacturer certification for these material requirements must be provided (Section 505). For thin bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavement, the contractor must submit concrete paving job‑mix formulas approved by the District Materials Engineer/Manager at least two weeks before commencing Portland Cement Concrete placement, and the producer must complete permeability testing to achieve a target chloride ion penetration of Low, Very low, or Negligible after 56 days or less in accordance with AASHTO T 277 or T 358 (Section 541). For high‑friction surface treatment, all surface‑preparation actions described in Section 659.3(e) must be finished before the binder can begin its curing phase: existing pavement markings, rumble strips and adjacent utilities must be covered or removed as directed; the pavement must be washed with a mild detergent, rinsed with potable water and dried with hot compressed air; cracks one‑quarter inch or wider must have loose material removed, be pre‑treated with mixed binder and allowed to gel before HFST is placed; new asphalt pavements may not receive HFST until after a mandatory 30‑day cure period, and concrete surfaces or repairs must be at least 28 days old (Section 659). Prior to curing of cement, mortar or grout, the water used for mixing or curing must conform to ASTM C1602 and be reasonably clean, free of vegetable matter, oil, acid, alkali, sugar, or any other substances injurious to the finished product (Section 720). Before a slurry seal can be allowed to cure, the existing pavement surface must be conditioned by sealing open joints and cracks with an acceptable crack sealer or emulsion, removing heavy paint buildup, protecting existing raised markers and replacing damaged ones; the mixture temperature must be controlled so that application temperature is not less than 50 °F and not more than 125 °F (Section 482). For steel‑slag fine aggregate, the material must first be graded and then placed in a controlled stockpile that does not exceed a maximum of 30,000 tons, and the aggregate must be completely soaked with water either before or during that stockpiling (Section 703). In long‑life concrete pavement projects, the contractor must develop and submit a written QC Plan for Department approval; the plan must detail how LTU assemblies will be anchored, how alignment will be measured before concrete placement, and, most importantly, the specific method that will be used to cure and protect joints during the curing period so moisture loss from concrete in and adjacent to joints is prevented (Section 530). For slab stabilization, qualified personnel must be confirmed as available and a crew supervisor with at least five years’ experience on similar projects must be assigned, together with three references for the supervisor and injection nozzle‑men; written documentation of these qualifications must be submitted to the Representative at least ten working days before work starts. A complete field QC plan that includes hole spacing, equipment lists, control methods, waste disposal, and the curing period must be prepared and cannot be acted upon until the Representative has reviewed and approved all submittals (Section 679). After finishing and texturing the concrete in the slip‑form paving method of plain or reinforced cement‑concrete pavements (Section 501), the curing material must be applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and then retained until the pavement attains the strength required for opening to traffic as specified in Section 501.3(q). Typical cures consist of covering the surface with white polyethylene sheeting that overlaps at least 12 inches and is sealed on all sides and edges, or applying a PAMS liquid membrane‑forming compound as a continuous film at about one gallon per 150 sq ft using fine‑mist spray equipment when the air temperature exceeds 40 °F and is forecast to remain above 40 °F for four hours. If normal curing must be delayed, an intermediate monomolecular film may be sprayed in a light fog and the surface re‑covered with the chosen cover; during cold weather high‑low thermometers are placed and the temperature must not drop below 40 °F, any reading between 40 °F and 35 °F requires extending the cure period by one day, and temperatures below 35 °F render the concrete defective. Insulating blankets may be used for slabs less than six inches thick when air is 45 °F or lower, but insulation must be removed without causing a surface‑temperature change greater than 40 °F per hour, and the Representative will suspend operations if adequate curing is not provided.

For unbonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 548) the same requirement to apply curing materials within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation applies, and the material remains until the strength required for traffic opening in Section 548.3(q) is achieved. When ambient temperature is 40 °F or less insulating blankets are placed to prevent rapid heat loss and may be removed only when temperature exceeds 40 °F or average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, with removal paced so that surface‑temperature change does not exceed 40 °F in any one‑hour period. A white‑pigmented PAMS liquid membrane‑forming curing compound is spread evenly over all exposed surfaces at a minimum of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 150 sq ft for pavements thicker than 6 inches or per 125 sq ft for pavements 6 inches or less, or at the greater manufacturer rate, and it may be applied in two equal coats—first before sawing and second immediately after joint cutting. For any other curing cover the material is removed only where a joint will be sawed, limited to a maximum of one‑half hour, and replaced as soon as the cut is made.

In roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502) after the final rolling pass and completion of in‑field density testing the RCC surface must be kept moist and a curing method selected; the chosen method—whether water cure with a misting‑equipped water truck, white polyethylene sheeting, curing compound, or cold‑weather curing—must then be applied immediately. The water cure must maintain a uniform moist condition without washing out or damaging the finished surface and must be continued for a minimum of 72 hours while the pavement develops a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi as required by Section 502.3(m)3; when those time and strength thresholds are met the curing period may end and traffic can be allowed.

The procedure for bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 540) mirrors that of Sections 501 and 548 but the temperature limit for insulating blankets is 45 °F. Insulating blankets are placed when ambient air temperature is 45 °F or less and may be removed once the air temperature exceeds 45 °F or when average compressive strength exceeds 3,000 psi, provided removal does not cause a surface‑temperature change greater than 40 °F in any one‑hour period; removal also may be governed by the maturity method after the minimum degree‑hours of curing have been accumulated at the top of the slab.

For testing and acceptance of cement concrete (Section 704) a curing tank conforming to PTM No. 611 together with curing boxes or equivalent equipment must be provided so that the temperature next to field‑cured cylinders is maintained between 60 °F and 80 °F for the first 24 ± 2 hours; a Bluetooth‑enabled digital recorder then logs the box temperature at least hourly within 30 °F–120 °F to an accuracy of 1 °F and supplies the data to the Representative. The cylinders are field‑cured in accordance with PTM No. 611 Section 12.2 for the specified curing period, and after curing of the placed concrete stops the QC cylinders may be moved to a preapproved secure area while continuing the same temperature control until compressive‑strength testing.

For Class AA cement‑concrete partial‑depth repairs (Section 525) the first step is to apply a white membrane‑forming curing compound as required in Section 516.3(j) using an atomizing spray tank with an adjustable nozzle and agitating the solution before each application; if ambient air temperature drops below 80 °F insulation or heating of the repair area must be provided. During curing the temperature must be controlled and monitored at least hourly so that the repair area does not experience a curing‑temperature change in excess of 40 °F within any one‑hour period, because any such change will cause the work to be considered defective; Rapid‑Set concrete patches are cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations.

In pervious concrete pavement systems (Section 520) the curing process must begin within twenty minutes after concrete discharge, the pavement surface is then completely covered with polyethylene sheeting that is thoroughly secured at all exterior edges and interior laps to prevent wind removal, and the cover is maintained for a minimum of seven days before any traffic loading is allowed.

For precast concrete products (Section 714) curing begins by developing an approved quality‑control plan in accordance with ACI 308; the units are kept in their forms for a primary cure until they have attained at least 70 % of the 28‑day strength, after which stripping time is set based on product characteristics and secondary curing follows using approved methods. After stripping, the precast units must be stored in an environment no lower than 50 °F and kept there until they reach a compressive strength of 80 % of the 28‑day minimum indicated on the shop drawing, and they may not be placed in temperatures below 32 °F after secondary cure unless the temperature differential is under 30 °F. Finally the units are stored until the full 28‑day minimum compressive strength is achieved.

For cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303) curing begins immediately after compaction; the contractor either applies a white membrane‑forming curing compound at a rate of 1 gallon per 100 sq ft ± 10 % of the surface area or covers the CTPBC with white polyethylene sheeting as directed, and the material is maintained for 24 hours, with an additional 24‑hour extension required if temperature falls below 40 °F during that period.

For bonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545) after finishing and texturing operations curing materials must be placed within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and remain until the pavement reaches the strength specified in Section 545.3(l); when forecast air temperature is 45 °F or less insulating blankets are provided to prevent rapid heat loss and may be removed once the air temperature exceeds 45 °F or the average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, provided removal does not cause a surface‑temperature change greater than 40 °F in any one‑hour period. Subsequently a curing compound is applied homogeneously to all exposed concrete surfaces at a minimum rate of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 sq ft or as the manufacturer recommends, whichever is greater; when conditions warrant the compound may be applied in two equal rates with the second application made immediately after sawing operations, while joint faces must be protected from exposure to the curing compound or cleaned before sealant installation.

For full‑depth reclamation (Section 344) after finishing the material is kept damp each day either by water or by applying an emulsified asphalt primer followed by a fog seal at a residual asphalt rate between 0.02 and 0.10 gallons per square yard while the layer is at optimum moisture content but not exceeding more than 3 %; this maintenance continues until the 7‑day strength requirement is met, during which heavy traffic is prohibited.

For roller‑compacted concrete base courses (Section 323) after saw cutting of the RCC control joints loose debris is removed and additional emulsified asphalt is applied along those joints as directed; no curing compounds are used, and a uniform void‑free layer of emulsified asphalt is placed over the entire RCC surface using approved equipment that does not damage the pavement, after which, once this cure period has elapsed, the pavement may be opened to local traffic before the asphalt wearing course or surface treatment is installed.

For acceptance testing of roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502) cylinders are molded in accordance with ASTM C 1435 and PTM No. 604 and tested at three days; if the 3‑day compressive strength is greater than or equal to 3,000 psi the field cure may be discontinued unless otherwise directed, otherwise curing continues until seven‑day cylinders are tested and, if that result is also greater than or equal to 3,000 psi, curing may again be stopped; if the 7‑day strength does not meet the threshold, curing proceeds to a 28‑day test which must reach a minimum compressive strength of 3,500 psi for the lot to be accepted.

For cold‑recycled asphalt base course stabilized with emulsified asphalt (Section 341) the material must cure for at least two weeks before the wearing or binder course is placed, whereas foamed‑asphalt‑stabilized CRABC cures until its average moisture content drops below 2 percent; prior to installing the overlying course a minimum of three random core samples each weighing at least 1,200 g are dry cut or extracted according to PTM No. 1 and their moisture contents measured in accordance with PTM No. 749, after which any core holes are back‑filled and compacted with CRABC material or an approved alternate.

For bridge approach slabs (Section 505) the curing sequence begins after the subbase is placed by excavating the area for the sleeper slab unless a geosynthetic stabilized subbase is used; two layers of at least 4.0 mil polyethylene sheeting are then laid as a bond breaker with longitudinal seams overlapped a minimum of 12 inches, and finally the approach slab is water‑cured for a minimum of seven days using covers of double‑thickness burlap. Staging and logistics

Field curing of pavement materials is subject to a variety of space, site‑access, weather and environmental constraints that differ by treatment. For Slip‑Form Paving Method (Section 501.3) the curing sheets or burlap must be long enough to cover each joint spacing, overlap at least twelve inches and be sealed tightly against the pavement edges; protective material must be ready before the initial set to keep rain off the fresh concrete. When white‑membrane compounds are used the air temperature must be above forty °F when applied and forecasted to stay above that level for at least four hours, any surface temperature below thirty‑five °F renders the work defective and temperatures between forty °F and thirty‑five °F require an extra day of cure. If the ambient temperature is expected to fall below fifty °F high‑low thermometers must be placed on the slab, insulation blankets are required when air is less than or equal to forty‑five °F for thin sections, and removal of insulation may not cause a surface‑temperature change greater than forty °F in any one‑hour interval. Emergency curing material must be kept on hand at all times.

For Unbonded Concrete Overlay of Concrete‑Surfacied Pavements (Section 548) insulating blankets are required when the air temperature is forty °F or less and may be removed only after the air temperature exceeds forty °F provided that the concrete surface temperature does not change more than forty °F within any one‑hour period. Both the geotextile fabric and the pavement surface must be kept below one hundred twenty °F before overlay placement, curing covers for joint sawing may be off no longer than a half hour, and construction traffic shall be avoided on the geotextile to prevent damage that could compromise the cure.

For Roller Compacted Concrete Pavements (Section 502) placement must be stopped if the ambient temperature away from artificial heat drops to forty °F or if the mixture temperature exceeds ninety °F after mixing; hard rain that could damage the surface is prohibited and light rain may be allowed only if no damage occurs. The evaporation rate must not exceed 0.20 lb/ft²·hr unless windbreaks or fog‑mist systems are used, and a water truck equipped with misting nozzles must be on site and available throughout paving and curing to keep the surface moist for at least seventy‑two hours until the pavement reaches a compressive strength of three thousand pounds per square inch. Sufficient space and access must be provided for the water‑truck, windbreaks and fog‑mist equipment, and traffic shall be barred from the slab for the entire curing duration.

For Bonded Concrete Overlay of Asphalt‑Surfacied Pavement (Section 540, modified from Section 501.3) curing must begin within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates and the curing material must remain until the concrete attains sufficient strength for traffic. If the air temperature is forty‑five °F or less adequate insulating blankets are required; they may be removed only when the air temperature exceeds forty‑five °F or when the average compressive strength exceeds three thousand pounds per square inch, and removal must not cause a surface‑temperature change greater than forty °F in any one‑hour period. Before placing the overlay the asphalt surface must be kept below one hundred twenty °F using fog‑misting, protective covers or blankets and no standing water may remain; curing covers at joint locations may be removed for up to a half hour prior to sawing.

Testing and Acceptance (Section 704) requires that field curing be performed in a location that can accommodate the required curing tank, curing boxes or equivalent equipment and provide a secure, easily‑accessible area for stored cylinders. The project office must have at least one hundred twenty square feet of floor space. Curing boxes or tanks must keep the temperature immediately adjacent to the field‑cured cylinders in the range sixty °F to eighty °F for the first twenty‑four ± two hours, and a digital system must be capable of recording temperatures from thirty °F to one hundred twenty °F with an accuracy of one °F. All equipment and storage areas must remain protected from the elements for the entire curing period, and any secure area where cylinders are relocated after cure must stay accessible for inspection.

Partial‑Depth Repair (Section 525) specifies that if the air temperature falls below eighty °F the crew must supply insulation or heating to keep the patch within acceptable limits; the curing temperature must be checked at least hourly and any change greater than forty °F in a one‑hour period renders the work defective. These requirements apply to both Class AA cement concrete (modified) and accelerated mixes.

Pervious Concrete Pavement System (Section 520) mandates that curing start within twenty minutes of discharge and that the surface be completely covered with polyethylene sheeting that is thoroughly secured at all exterior edges and interior laps so wind cannot lift it. Placement may not occur if projected air temperatures will fall below forty °F or drop lower any time during the subsequent twenty‑four‑hour period, and placement must be avoided when rain is forecast before final finishing and curing. The seven‑day cure cannot be considered complete for traffic opening unless ambient temperature exceeds fifty‑five °F at some point each day.

Precast Concrete Products (Section 714) require that secondary‑cured units be kept in an environment of at least fifty °F until they reach eighty percent of the twenty‑eight‑day compressive strength, and after secondary cure they may not be placed in an environment below thirty‑two °F unless the temperature differential between unit and air is less than thirty °F. Primary curing continues until units achieve seventy percent of the twenty‑eight‑day strength.

Cement Treated Permeable Base Course (Section 303) requires that cure begin immediately after compaction and that a white membrane forming compound be applied at a rate of one gallon per one hundred square feet ± ten percent or that white polyethylene sheeting remain in place for twenty‑four hours. If the CTPBC temperature falls below forty °F during that period the cure period is extended by an additional twenty‑four hours, and if it drops to thirty‑two °F within seventy‑two hours the material must be removed and replaced at no cost. Construction traffic is prohibited on the base for at least three days after placement and exposure to rain must be prevented because rain‑induced surface defects render the work defective and require removal and replacement.

Concrete Pavement Patching (Section 516) stipulates that before any concrete is placed the contractor must verify that adequate equipment, trained personnel and sufficient hauling units are on site, which limits the space and access available for curing. Excavated patch areas may not remain un‑patched for more than forty‑eight hours or over weekends or holidays, establishing a time constraint on when curing can begin. If rain occurs while the patch area is open an outlet must be excavated through the shoulder at the lowest point and any resulting shoulder damage repaired because moisture exposure would interfere with the curing of rapid‑set material, and cooling water, slurry and dust from saw cutting are prohibited from entering any lane opened to traffic.

Bonded Concrete Overlay (Section 545) permits concrete placement only when its temperature is between sixty‑five °F and ninety °F. If forecast air temperature is forty‑five °F or less insulating blankets are required; they may be removed when the air temperature exceeds forty‑five °F or when the average compressive strength exceeds three thousand six hundred pounds per square inch, provided removal does not cause a surface‑temperature change greater than forty °F in any one‑hour period. Curing materials must be applied within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates and kept on the pavement until the required strength is achieved, with fog‑misting, protective covers or blankets permitted to maintain proper temperature and moisture conditions.

Full‑Depth Reclamation (Section 344) mandates that during the seven‑day cure period the reclaimed layer be kept damp by applying water each day or by spraying an emulsified asphalt primer followed by a fog seal at a residual rate of 0.02 to 0.10 gallon per square yard, and moisture content may not exceed the optimum design level by more than three percent. Heavy traffic is prohibited for the full cure period, with only limited light vehicles allowed after the material reaches a stable condition. Curing speed is strongly influenced by weather; no rain, sunshine, low humidity and high temperature accelerate strength gain while less favorable conditions may require from two or three days up to at least two weeks.

For Roller Compacted Concrete (Section 502 acceptance) the only site or environmental factor that can limit curing is a short stoppage caused by weather or equipment failure, which reduces the lot size placed in that day and may delay or interrupt the field‑cure period; no other space, access or environmental constraints are specified.

Pervious Concrete (Section 520 weather) requires provisions to protect the concrete from freezing throughout the seven‑day curing period as required by ACI 306.1 when cold weather prevails, and hot weather calls for detailed plans indicating the amount of hydration‑stabilizing admixture to be added in five‑degree Fahrenheit increments to provide sufficient time for placement and finishing before initial set.

High Friction Surface Treatment (Section 659) demands that the treated lane remain free of contractor equipment or traffic until the binder has set, that placement be avoided when sustained wind exceeds fifteen mph or gusts reach twenty mph because higher winds can dry the binder, and that HFST not be installed on new asphalt until after a mandatory thirty‑day cure period nor on concrete repairs younger than twenty‑eight days.

Slurry Seal (Section 482) requires traffic to be kept off the surface until it has fully cured to a firm condition, restricting site access during this period, and the mixture must be placed at a temperature not less than fifty °F and not more than one hundred twenty‑five °F.

Steel‑slag Fine Aggregate (Section 703) limits the stockpile size to thirty thousand tons, requiring sufficient space to accommodate that limit; the material must be completely soaked with water before or during placement and then kept uniformly moist for not less than six months, making reliable water access essential regardless of weather, and if the expansion test after that period exceeds 0.50 % the stockpile must remain in a moist condition for a minimum of two additional months before retesting.

Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (Section 530) requires a concrete temperature control plan whenever temperatures exceed eighty °F at placement, and the quality‑control plan must specify how joints will be cured and protected to prevent moisture loss during and after joint sawing.

Slab Stabilization (Section 679) states that field curing may be delayed when ambient conditions fall outside the permitted range; work cannot start if daytime temperatures are below forty °F or if the subgrade or base course is frozen, and deflection testing must not be performed when air temperature is below forty °F, exceeds seventy °F, during spring‑thaw conditions, or when the subgrade is frozen. Quality control and acceptance

For curing of concrete pavements the inspector must verify that the curing material is applied after finishing and texturing and within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation (Section 501, Section 548, Section 540, Section 525). The material must remain in place until the slab or repair reaches the strength required for opening to traffic or for removal of formwork as specified for each pavement type. Coverage rates are prescribed by thickness: a minimum of one gallon per 150 square feet (±0.1 gallon) is required for pavements thicker than six inches, and one gallon per 125 square feet (±0.1 gallon) for pavements six inches or less, unless the manufacturer’s recommendation is greater (Section 548); for cement‑treated permeable base course the rate is one gallon per 100 square feet ±10% of the surface area (Section 303). Temperature control must be monitored at least hourly to ensure that the concrete never experiences a curing‑temperature change in excess of 40 °F within any one‑hour period (Section 501, Section 525, Section 548, Section 540). In cold weather the surface temperature shall not fall below forty degrees Fahrenheit; if the thermometer reads between forty and thirty‑five degrees Fahrenheit the cure period must be extended an additional day, and any reading below thirty‑five degrees Fahrenheit renders the concrete defective (Section 501). When the ambient air temperature is forty degrees Fahrenheit or less insulating blankets are required and may be removed only after the concrete surface temperature exceeds forty degrees Fahrenheit or the average compressive strength surpasses three‑thousand six hundred pounds per square inch, with removal performed so that the surface temperature change does not exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit in any one‑hour period (Section 548). For bonded concrete overlays insulating blankets are required when the air temperature is forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or less and may be removed only after the air temperature exceeds forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or the average compressive strength exceeds three thousand pounds per square inch, again limiting the surface temperature change to no more than forty degrees Fahrenheit per hour (Section 540). Roller‑compacted concrete pavements must be kept moist by an approved method—such as misting water trucks, white polyethylene sheeting, or a curing compound that forms a uniform opaque film—and each selected method must be maintained for at least seventy‑two hours while confirming that the pavement has attained a compressive strength of at least three thousand pounds per square inch (Section 502). For partial‑depth repairs a white membrane‑forming curing compound shall be applied with an atomizing spray tank having an adjustable nozzle and the solution agitated before each application; insulation or heating must be provided whenever ambient temperature drops below eighty degrees Fahrenheit, and any curing‑temperature change exceeding forty degrees Fahrenheit in one hour will cause the work to be considered defective (Section 525). Field‑cured cylinders are inspected by confirming that the curing tank and any curing boxes conform to PTM No. 611 and that the boxes maintain the concrete surface temperature in the range of sixty to eighty degrees Fahrenheit for the first twenty‑four ± two hours, recording the temperature at least every hour within a thirty to one‑hundred‑twenty degree Fahrenheit range to an accuracy of one degree Fahrenheit; when the maturity method is used, maturity meters and PTM No. 640 temperature sensors must be installed (Section 704). Precast concrete units must remain in their forms and retain primary cure until they reach at least seventy percent of the twenty‑eight‑day compressive strength, after which stripping time is set according to the product’s inherent characteristics; during secondary cure the units must be kept in an environment not lower than fifty degrees Fahrenheit until they have reached eighty percent of the twenty‑eight‑day minimum strength shown on the shop drawing, and they may not be stored at temperatures below thirty‑two degrees Fahrenheit unless the temperature differential with the unit is less than thirty degrees Fahrenheit; finally the units are stored until the full twenty‑eight‑day minimum compressive strength is achieved (Section 714). For concrete pavement patching the contractor must use either a Type 2 white pigmented liquid membrane‑forming curing compound or the specified white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene (PAMS) liquid membrane‑forming curing compound, and any rapid‑set concrete patching material shall be cured in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations (Section 516). After placement QC cylinders are to be cured and then tested for compressive strength, and the pavement may only be opened to traffic when the measured strength reaches at least one thousand pounds per square inch as required by PTM No. 604 (Section 516). For cement concrete base courses the inspector must verify that only a black liquid membrane‑forming curing compound is applied—white compound is prohibited—and confirm that the base course remains inaccessible to traffic until the surface course is placed; the inspector also must assess the compound’s performance and discontinue its use if it becomes unsatisfactory, switching to alternative curing methods as specified (Section 301). For bonded concrete overlays the inspection must ensure that curing materials are placed within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates and remain until the pavement reaches the strength required for traffic opening, that insulation may be removed only when the air temperature exceeds forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or when the average compressive strength of the concrete exceeds three‑thousand six hundred pounds per square inch, and that the surface temperature does not change more than forty degrees Fahrenheit in any one‑hour period because a higher rate will be considered defective; additionally the curing compound must be applied uniformly to give a solid white opaque film at a minimum of one gallon (± 0.1 gallon) per 125 square feet, with joint faces protected from the compound before sealant installation (Section 540). Full‑depth reclamation requires inspection for achievement of the seven‑day strength requirement verified by testing specimens; moisture condition must be checked to ensure it remains damp through daily water or emulsified‑asphalt prime/fog seal applied at a residual rate between 0.02 and 0.10 gallons per square yard without exceeding more than three percent above optimum moisture; heavy traffic must be prevented, and proof‑rolling with a vehicle comparable to the heaviest expected or a strength measurement must demonstrate sufficient cured strength before traffic is allowed (Section 344). For roller‑compacted concrete acceptance the inspector checks density—final lot average shall not be less than ninety‑eight percent of the maximum wet density and no individual test may be less than ninety‑six percent—and compressive strength on cylinders molded to ASTM C1435 where a three‑day result greater than or equal to three thousand pounds per square inch (or, if not met, a seven‑day result greater than or equal to three thousand psi) may allow the field cure to be discontinued; the twenty‑eight‑day cylinders must reach a minimum compressive strength of three thousand five hundred pounds per square inch, and pavement depth measured on cores is acceptable when the measured depth is within one‑quarter inch of plan depth (or, if the first core exceeds that tolerance, the average of three cores must be within one‑quarter inch) (Section 502). For cold‑recycled asphalt base courses cured with an emulsified‑asphalt stabilizing agent the contractor must allow curing for at least two weeks before installing a wearing or binder course, or continue until the average moisture content falls below two percent; the same moisture‑content limit applies to foamed‑asphalt stabilized material, and prior to placing the overlying course the contractor must randomly select and dry‑cut at least three core samples of at least one thousand two hundred grams each from throughout the project and determine their moisture content in accordance with PTM No. 749; any sample exceeding the limit requires backfilling and recompaction (Section 341). Bridge approach slabs must be water‑cured for a minimum of seven days and protected with covers having double thickness of burlap (Section 505). For thin bonded concrete overlays curing is verified by correlating degree‑hours of cure to compressive strength using the maturity method at ages of eight hours, one day, seven days, twenty‑eight days and fifty‑six days; permeability testing must demonstrate a target chloride ion penetration classified as Low, Very low or Negligible after a curing period of fifty‑six days or less, and if Moderate is accepted measured values may not exceed two thousand eight hundred coulombs on AASHTO T 277 or fifteen point six kilohms‑centimeter for four‑by‑eight inch cylinders and twelve point three kilohms‑centimeter for six‑by‑twelve inch cylinders on AASHTO T 358 (Section 541). The curing cover, which is white polyethylene sheeting, must be evaluated for its tensile strength; the minimum tensile strength in both the machine direction and the cross‑direction shall meet seven point zero pounds per linear inch as required by ASTM C171 (Section 711). When cement concrete, mortar or grout is cured the water used for mixing or curing shall conform to ASTM C1602 and be reasonably clean, free from vegetable matter, oil, acid, alkali, sugar, or other substances injurious to the finished product (Section 720). After a cement‑treated permeable base course has cured the Representative must inspect depth by digging one full‑depth test hole for each three thousand square yards of completed material and measuring actual thickness against the specified depth; any location where the measured depth is deficient by one‑half inch or more, where surface tolerance is not met, or where rain‑induced or improperly finished surface defects exist must be identified as defective, with additional longitudinal and transverse test holes placed until the depth meets requirements, and any full‑depth sections that remain defective are to be removed and replaced (Section 303). For slurry seal curing the surface must be inspected for a firm condition that will prevent pick‑up of the mixture before any traffic is allowed; traffic may not be permitted until directed once this firmness is achieved (Section 482). Steel‑slag fine aggregate cured in a controlled stockpile must be sampled after at least six months of uniform moist storage and tested for expansive characteristics according to PTM No. 130; the average total volumetric expansion shall be less than zero point five percent for approval, and if the expansion criterion is not met the stockpile must be cured for a minimum of two additional months before retesting; the LTS also evaluates fine‑aggregate quality by conducting petrographic analysis according to PTM No. 518 and any other tests necessary to demonstrate that required construction of acceptable durability can be achieved (Section 703). The acceptance criteria for curing of plain or reinforced cement‑concrete pavements placed by the slip‑form method (Section 501) require that the contractor place curing materials after finishing and texturing and no later than 30 minutes after bleed‑water has dissipated, and retain those materials until the pavement attains the strength required for opening to traffic. The same curing method shall be used throughout unless the Representative directs a change; any interruption or insufficient curing triggers an immediate suspension of concrete operations by the Representative. During cold weather the pavement temperature may not fall below 40 °F; hourly monitoring is required, and if the thermometer reads between 40 °F and 35 °F the cure period is extended one day. A temperature reading below 35 °F renders the work defective and requires removal and replacement of the affected concrete at no additional cost to the Department. For accelerated‑strength pavements insulation or heating must be provided and the pavement temperature may not change more than 40 °F in any one‑hour interval; exceeding that limit also makes the work defective, with correction as specified in Section 501.3(t).

For unbonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 548) curing is accepted only when curing materials are placed within 30 minutes after bleed water dissipates and remain until the pavement reaches the strength required for traffic opening. Insulating blankets may be used when air temperature is ≤40 °F but must be removed once the temperature exceeds 40 °F or the average compressive strength passes 3,600 psi, and removal must not cause a surface‑temperature change greater than 40 °F in any one‑hour period; exceeding that rate renders the concrete defective. When the white‑pigmented PAMS liquid membrane curing compound is used it must be applied evenly at a minimum of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 150 ft² for slabs thicker than 6 inches or per 125 ft² for slabs 6 inches or less; joint faces must be kept free of the compound before sealant placement. Failure to meet the rate or to protect joints is non‑conforming and requires re‑application of the proper amount and cleaning of joint faces. If curing covers are removed for joint sawing, they may be off for no longer than a half hour; otherwise the work must be corrected by reinstating the cover for the required duration.

For bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 540) the same 30‑minute placement and retention requirement applies as modified from Section 501.3(l). When air temperature is 45 °F or less, insulating blankets shall be provided and may be removed only after the air temperature exceeds 45 °F or the average compressive strength exceeds 3,000 psi, with removal performed so that the concrete‑surface temperature does not change more than 40 °F in any one‑hour period; exceeding this tolerance causes the slab to be considered defective and it must be corrected in accordance with the project specifications.

For bonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545) curing materials are likewise required within 30 minutes after bleed water dissipates and must remain until the pavement reaches the strength needed for traffic opening. The curing compound shall be spread uniformly at a minimum rate of 1 gallon (±0.1 gallon) per 125 ft², or greater than the manufacturer’s recommendation. Insulating blankets are to be used when forecast air temperature is 45 °F or less and may be removed once the air temperature exceeds 45 °F or the average compressive strength surpasses 3,600 psi, provided that removal does not cause a surface‑temperature change greater than 40 °F in any one‑hour period; otherwise the concrete is considered defective. Any curing‑related defect, such as exceeding the 40 °F per hour temperature‑change limit, must be removed and replaced in accordance with Section 545.3(n), which requires a minimum removal length of 6 feet between transverse joints unless directed otherwise by the District Executive.

Accelerated partial‑depth repairs classified as Class AA (Section 525) must be monitored at least hourly and may not experience a curing temperature change in excess of 40 °F within any one‑hour period; if such a change occurs, the work is considered defective.

For cement‑treated permeable base courses (Section 303) the white membrane curing compound shall be applied at a rate of 1 gallon per 100 ft² ±10 % and retained for 24 hours. If the CTPBC temperature drops below 40 °F during that period, the cure time must be extended an additional 24 hours. The work is deemed non‑conforming when the temperature falls to 32 °F within 72 hours of placement; at that point the contract requires removal and replacement of the CTPBC at no cost to the Department.

For plain cement concrete base courses (Section 301) a white liquid membrane curing compound is prohibited, while a black liquid membrane may be used only if the base course will not be opened to traffic before the surface course is placed; should the black compound later prove unsatisfactory, its use must be discontinued and curing resumed by other specified methods.

Full‑depth reclamation (Section 344) requires that the reclaimed layer remain damp by daily water application or an emulsified asphalt prime followed by a fog seal at a residual rate between 0.02 and 0.10 gallons per square yard, and that moisture content during curing may not exceed the optimum moisture content by more than 3 %. The material is cured until the 7‑day strength specified for the stabilizer is achieved—at least 50 psi indirect tensile strength for asphalt‑stabilized mixes or an unconfined compressive strength of 300 to 500 psi (or 280 to 500 psi when a three‑inch or greater overlay is used) for chemically stabilized mixes—and heavy traffic must be prohibited during this period. If the measured strength falls below these minima, the lot is deemed nonconforming and must be reworked by developing an approved new mix design, reclaiming the area with additional stabilizing material, and filling any core holes with an approved repair material; similarly, if moisture exceeds the 3 % limit or surface irregularities exceed ½ inch over a 10‑foot template, corrective compaction or reconstruction of a control strip is required.

Roller‑compacted concrete pavement (Section 502) must achieve a final average density not less than 98 % of the maximum wet density and no individual test may be less than 96 % of the maximum wet density; if these limits are not met, the lot must be cored and the cores tested at 28 days per AASHTO T 24, with any core that reaches a compressive strength of 3,500 psi allowing the material to remain in place, otherwise the pavement is defective. For compressive strength, 3‑day cylinders must be greater than or equal to 3,000 psi before field curing may be discontinued; if the 3‑day result is below that value, curing continues until 7‑day cylinders are tested and also must be greater than or equal to 3,000 psi, and finally 28‑day cylinders must achieve a minimum of 3,500 psi; failure to meet the 28‑day requirement renders the pavement defective. Pavement depth is measured with an apparatus conforming to PTM No. 614; a single core within ¼ inch of plan depth is acceptable, but if the first core exceeds that tolerance two additional cores are taken and the average must be within ¼ inch, otherwise the depth is defective. Any lot found defective in curing, surface tolerance, density, compressive strength or depth shall be removed and replaced unless written direction from the District Executive states otherwise.

Cold‑recycled asphalt base course (Section 341) is accepted only when the stabilized CRABC has been cured for at least 2 weeks or until its average moisture content is less than 2 %; compliance is verified by dry cutting or extracting a minimum of three random samples of at least 1,200 grams each and determining their moisture per PTM No. 749, and if any sample shows moisture above the 2 % limit the non‑conforming portion must be corrected by backfilling and compacting the core holes with CRABC material or an approved alternate material.

High‑friction surface treatment (Section 659) requires that the mixed binder be proportioned within ±2 % by volume, applied as a uniform film of 50‑65 mils thickness and covered with aggregate placed at 12‑15 lb/yd²; these dimensional tolerances constitute the acceptance criteria for the cured surface, which may not be exposed to traffic or equipment during the cure period and may only be installed after a mandatory 30‑day cure on new asphalt or a 28‑day age on concrete. If any binder becomes contaminated, separates, or cures in a way that impairs aggregate bonding, the contractor must remove and replace the affected binder at no additional expense to the Department, and any areas showing delamination, poor retention, or discoloration must be re‑prepared and retreated with HFST also at no extra cost.

Cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303) further requires that the installed depth not be deficient by ½ inch or more from the indicated depth, that surface tolerance be met, and that any rain‑induced or finish defects be repairable in the Representative’s opinion; if any of these conditions are present the work is deemed defective and the corrective action is to remove and replace the full depth at no additional cost to the Department unless written direction from the District Executive says otherwise.

Slab stabilization (Section 679) accepts panel displacement limited to a raise not exceeding 0.05 inch; any pavement raised beyond that must be ground back to the correct grade as specified in Section 514.3. Radial cracks spreading outward from grout injection holes require work to stop until the cause is determined and corrected, while transverse cracks between adjacent grout injection holes require replacement of the entire slab at no additional cost. After grouting each stabilized joint or crack must be retested; slabs that deflect 0.02 inch or more must be re‑grouted and retested, and if movement still exceeds the specified limit after two proper groutings the Representative may either accept the slab or direct removal and replacement with a full‑depth concrete patch; all drill holes are to be patched with rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete flush with the surrounding surface. Safety and environmental controls

During the curing phase of high‑friction surface treatment (HFST) specified in SECTION 659—HIGH FRICTION SURFACE TREATMENT, workers are instructed to minimize walking or standing on the wet binder because exposure to uncured material can create slip and contact hazards; simultaneously, no contractor equipment or traffic may be present in the treatment area while the binder cures, which prevents both worker and motorist exposure to the uncured surface. Any loose aggregate that falls onto an active adjacent travel lane must be removed immediately after traffic is temporarily held, thereby protecting the public from falling debris during curing. In the slurry seal work described in SECTION 482—SLURRY SEAL, traffic must be kept off the freshly placed slurry seal until it has reached a firm condition; premature vehicle movement can cause the uncured mixture to be picked up, creating a hazard for both the traveling public and any workers still in the area. The specification therefore directs that no traffic is allowed on the surface until directed, ensuring the cure is complete before exposure to loads. For slab‑stabilization operations covered by SECTION 679—SLAB STABILIZATION, workers must observe all normal safety rules in the operation of pressure pumps and other equipment, take extra precautions when handling hot asphalt hose and nozzle, and wear safety masks, protective gloves and heavy, tightly fitted clothing on the ankles, wrists and neck; the source does not identify any specific hazards to members of the public. Curing, protection, and handoff

For slip‑form pavements (Section 501), unbonded concrete overlays (Section 548) and bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavement (Section 540), the contractor must apply a curing material within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and retain it until the slab reaches the strength required for traffic opening as defined in each section. Protection may be provided by covering the surface and sides with an approved membrane such as white polyethylene sheeting that overlaps at least 12 inches, is secured to form a tight seal and is folded down over the full depth, or by applying a liquid membrane‑forming compound (for example PAMS) in a uniform opaque film. The compound is applied at a rate of one gallon per 150 sq ft when air temperature exceeds 40 °F and is forecast to remain above that level for four hours; for slabs thicker than 6 inches the rate is at least one gallon per 150 ft², and for slabs 6 inches or less it is at least one gallon per 125 ft², with a possible second coat immediately after joint sawing. When ambient temperature is 40 °F or lower (Section 548) or 45 °F or lower (Section 540), adequate insulating blankets are placed to prevent rapid heat loss and may be removed only when the air temperature rises above the respective threshold or when the average compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi (Section 548) or 3,000 psi (Section 540); removal must not cause a surface‑temperature change greater than 40 °F in any one‑hour period.

Roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Sections 502 and 323) require moisture to be supplied immediately after final rolling and in‑field density testing and maintained for a minimum of 72 hours until the pavement attains a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi. Moisture may be provided by a water truck equipped with misting spray nozzles, soaking hoses or sprinklers so that the surface stays uniformly wet without being washed out; alternative methods such as white polyethylene sheeting or a curing compound forming an opaque solid film are also acceptable. During this period the pavement is protected from vehicular traffic.

For Class AA partial‑depth repairs (Section 525), a white membrane‑forming curing compound is applied with an atomizing spray tank that has an adjustable nozzle, agitating the solution before each application. If ambient temperature drops below 80 °F, insulation or heating must be provided and the curing temperature is monitored at least hourly to keep any change within one hour from exceeding 40 °F; a temperature change greater than 40 °F in any one‑hour period renders the patch defective. Rapid‑Set patches are cured according to the manufacturer’s recommendations.

Pervious concrete pavement systems (Section 520) must begin curing within 20 minutes of concrete discharge by completely covering the surface with polyethylene sheeting that is thoroughly secured at all exterior edges and interior laps to resist wind removal; the cover remains in place for a minimum of 7 days. The pavement may not be opened to vehicular traffic until it has cured for at least 7 days during which the ambient temperature has exceeded 55 °F at any time of day, and the surface must be protected from freezing throughout the seven‑day period as required by ACI 306.1. Hot‑weather conditions are addressed by a detailed plan that specifies the amount of hydration‑stabilizing admixture to add in five‑degree Fahrenheit increments.

Precast concrete units (Section 714) are cured and protected according to an approved quality‑control plan based on ACI 308. Primary curing continues in the forms until the units reach at least 70 % of their 28‑day strength; after stripping, secondary curing proceeds by any approved method while the units are kept in a minimum 50 °F environment until they achieve 80 % of the 28‑day compressive strength shown on the shop drawing. Units may not be placed in an environment below 32 °F unless the temperature differential between the unit and the air is less than 30 °F. After curing, a protective coating—either boiled linseed oil or a penetrating sealer that does not discolor the concrete—is applied to barrier and inlet tops before shipment; if a curing compound was used for secondary cure, application of the coating must wait 14 days.

Cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303) is cured immediately after compaction either by applying a white membrane‑forming curing compound at a rate of one gallon per 100 sq ft ±10% or by covering it with white polyethylene sheeting; the material must remain in place for 24 hours and an additional 24 hours if temperature falls below 40 °F, and removal and replacement at no cost is required if temperature drops to 32 °F within 72 hours. Protection includes prohibiting construction traffic for at least three days and forbidding any overlying pavement during that time; after three days only equipment needed for the next layer may be allowed, and damaged or contaminated areas must be removed and replaced at no cost.

Concrete pavement patching (Section 516) requires that, after the final finish, a liquid membrane‑forming curing compound be applied uniformly over all exposed concrete surfaces. The compound may be the specified white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene (PAMS) or, where permitted, a Type 2 white pigmented curing compound, and it must be spread at a minimum rate of one gallon per 125 sq ft (or the greater of the manufacturer’s recommendation). Application may be made in two equal passes with the second pass immediately after any sawing; joint faces must be protected from the compound or cleaned before sealant installation. The work is protected from rain as required by Section 501.3(r), and traffic is not opened until the Representative confirms that compressive strength has reached at least 1,000 psi measured on test cylinders in accordance with PTM No. 604.

Full‑depth reclamation (Section 344) is cured by maintaining the reclaimed base in a damp condition, either through daily water application or by applying an emulsified asphalt primer followed by an asphalt fog seal at a residual asphalt rate between 0.02 and 0.10 gal per square yard while moisture content does not exceed more than 3 %. Curing continues until the seven‑day strength requirement is met. Heavy traffic is prohibited during the seven‑day cure period, appropriate traffic signs must be posted, and actual cure time may vary from a few days to at least two weeks depending on weather and material type.

Cold‑recycled asphalt base course (Section 341) must be protected from marring, distortion and other damage while exposed to construction equipment, and any such damage must be corrected immediately to the Representative’s satisfaction. When an emulsified‑asphalt stabilizing agent is used, the cured period shall be at least two weeks before a wearing or binder course is placed or until the average moisture content drops below 2 %; when a foamed‑asphalt stabilizing agent is used, curing continues until the average moisture content is less than 2 %.

Bridge approach slabs (Section 505) are water‑cured for a minimum of seven days using a cover of double‑thickness burlap as specified.

Thin‑bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavement (Section 541) is cured by applying the maturity method, correlating degree‑hours of cure to compressive strength according to PTM No. 640 at the prescribed intervals of 8 hours, 1 day, 7 days, 28 days and 56 days; the mix shall be designed to achieve a target chloride ion penetration classified as Low, Very low or Negligible after a curing period of 56 days or less in accordance with AASHTO T 277 or T 358. Concrete curing material requirements are further referenced in Sections 711.1 and 711.2(b).

High‑friction surface treatment (Section 659) shall not be subjected to contractor equipment or traffic during the binder cure; the mixed binder may not be exposed, separated, dried or otherwise hardened in a manner that would impair aggregate retention and bonding, and any contaminated binder must be removed and replaced at no additional cost. After the binder has cured all surplus aggregate is reclaimed with a vacuum sweeper before opening to traffic, and a second sweep of loose aggregate, including the shoulder area, is required between 15 and 30 days after placement; no HFST may be placed on new asphalt until after a mandatory 30‑day cure period or on concrete that is less than 28 days old.

Completed concrete pavements are protected by placing a white polyethylene sheet over the surface that conforms to ASTM C171 and possesses a minimum tensile strength of 7.0 lb per linear inch in both the machine direction and the cross‑direction, serving as the curing and protecting cover.

Steel‑slag fine aggregate (Section 703) is cured in a controlled stockpile that may not exceed 30,000 tons; the material is completely soaked with water before or during placement and then maintained in a uniformly moist condition for not less than six months. Representative samples are taken thereafter and the average total volumetric expansion must be less than 0.50 %; if the measured expansion exceeds this limit, curing must continue for an additional minimum period of two months before retesting.

Long‑life concrete pavement (Section 530) requires that the contractor’s quality‑control plan describe a method to cure and protect the concrete joints during the curing period so that moisture loss from the concrete in and adjacent to the joints is prevented both while the pavement cures and after joint sawing. Following grout injection, wooden plugs are removed and each drill hole is filled to full depth with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete patching material struck flush with the surrounding pavement; the QC plan must specify the required curing period and the time when the slab may be opened to traffic, and if directed by the Representative each stabilized joint or crack shall be retested after 24 hours before final acceptance. For plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 501) and related sections, work that has undergone curing may be opened to construction traffic or users only after the concrete has attained the early strength required in Table D for normal mixes, or 1,200 psi for accelerated mixes, as shown by cylinder compressive‑strength testing or the maturity method, provided all curing requirements have been satisfied, no second‑stage saw cuts have been made, a backer rod is installed near the surface and the joints are sealed; the District Representative then authorizes opening. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502) the pavement must be kept moist using an approved curing method for at least 72 hours and until a compressive strength of not less than 3,000 psi is attained; vehicular traffic must be excluded during that period and the pavement may be opened to construction traffic or users only once the cure period described in Section 502.3(j) is complete. For bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 540) curing materials must be applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and kept in place until the slab reaches the strength required for opening as defined in Section 540.3(q); insulating blankets may be removed when the air temperature exceeds 45°F or when the average compressive strength exceeds 3,000 psi, provided the rate of temperature change does not exceed 40°F within any one‑hour period, and only when these strength and temperature conditions are satisfied may construction traffic be allowed or the pavement be opened to users. For pervious concrete pavement systems (Section 520) curing must begin within twenty minutes, the surface must be completely covered with polyethylene sheeting and that cover maintained for a minimum of seven days; the pavement may not be opened to construction traffic or any users until the concrete has remained in cure for at least seven days and during each day the ambient temperature has exceeded 55°F. For cement‑treated permeable base courses (Section 303) construction traffic is prohibited for at least three days after placement; after the three‑day period only trucks and equipment needed to place the next layer or an adjacent lift may travel on it. For concrete pavement patching (Section 516) the work may be placed in construction traffic or opened to users only after the Representative confirms that the concrete has reached a minimum compressive strength of 1,000 psi as demonstrated by testing QC cylinders according to the manufacturer’s curing recommendations and PTM No. 604. For cement‑concrete base courses where a black liquid‑membrane forming curing compound is used, the base must remain inaccessible to traffic until the overlying surface course is placed; at that point the work may carry construction traffic or be opened to users (a white curing compound is not permitted). For bonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545) curing material must be applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and retained until the concrete reaches the strength required for opening; the Representative may permit construction traffic or opening only when cylinder testing shows a compressive strength of at least 3,600 psi per PTM No. 604 and the bond strength satisfies the minimum specified in Section 545.3(e). For full‑depth reclamation (Section 344) heavy traffic is prohibited for the full seven‑day cure period; after the base is completed a proof roll with a vehicle comparable to the heaviest expected can permit limited use, and light traffic (vehicles under 5 tons) may be allowed about two hours after construction finishes provided the material has reached a stable condition, while unlimited traffic is not permitted until the seven‑day strength requirement is satisfied. For roller‑compacted concrete base courses (Section 323) when the specified cure period has elapsed the pavement may be opened to local traffic before the asphalt wearing course or surface treatment is placed; the curing step does not permit use of curing compounds and requires a uniform, void‑free application of emulsified asphalt as prescribed in Section 460.3(b) using equipment approved by the Representative that will not damage the RCC surface. For high‑friction surface treatments (Section 659) contractor equipment and traffic must remain absent until the binder has fully cured; after the curing period is complete, surplus aggregate must be reclaimed with a vacuum sweeper before the surface may be opened to construction traffic or public users. For slurry seals (Section 482) the surface may not be opened to construction traffic or any users until the contractor receives direction that the seal has fully cured and achieved a firm condition that will prevent the mixture from being picked up by tires; only after this confirmation can traffic be permitted. Expected performance and maintenance

During field curing of plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements (Section 501), insufficient coverage of the curing material triggers immediate suspension of work and is classified as a defective condition, while failure to protect joints permits moisture loss that must be corrected; any drop of the high‑low thermometer below 35°F renders the concrete defective, and a temperature change exceeding 40°F within one hour also makes the work defective, and rain striking a freshly applied curing film before it has dried sufficiently requires immediate re‑application of the coating. In unbonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 548), removal of insulation must be performed so that the concrete surface temperature does not change more than 40°F within any one‑hour period, otherwise the concrete may be considered defective, and curing covers removed for joint sawing may not remain off for longer than a half hour lest the joint area suffer inadequate protection. For bonded concrete overlay of asphalt‑surfaced pavement (Section 540), allowing the overlay to cool faster than a 40°F drop within any one‑hour period will render the slab defective, and insufficient curing before traffic loading can lead to premature failure because the pavement must retain its curing cover until it reaches the required strength for opening to traffic; cracks that develop at or within 1.5 feet of a planned joint location, whether before or during sawing, are treated as distress that must be addressed. In concrete pavement partial‑depth repair (Section 525), a temperature change exceeding 40°F within any one‑hour period in the patch makes the repair work defective and must be avoided by proper application of a white membrane‑forming curing compound and hourly temperature monitoring, and delamination of the repaired area requires replacement at no cost to the Department. For pervious concrete pavement systems (Section 520), improper curing can cause loss of surface mortar and damage to the hardened pavement; if the polyethylene sheet is not placed within twenty minutes of discharge, removed for longer than sixty minutes after an interruption, or exposed to rainfall before final finishing, moisture infiltration may strip surface mortar that must be repaired or replaced, and rollers may not bridge the pavement before a minimum of seven days—or before the Representative confirms sufficient strength—to avoid damage. In cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303), inadequate temperature control during curing is distressing: if the CTPBC temperature falls below 40°F during the required twenty‑four‑hour cure, the contractor must extend the cure by another twenty‑four hours, and if the temperature drops to 32°F within seventy‑two hours of placement, the material must be removed and replaced at no cost; maintaining the curing compound or white polyethylene covering for the full twenty‑four‑hour period is essential. In plain cement concrete base course (Section 301), loss of effectiveness of the black liquid membrane‑forming curing compound constitutes a failure; the compound must be discontinued and another method applied if it becomes unsatisfactory, and use of the prohibited white liquid membrane‑forming curing compound would also constitute a failure to meet the curing requirements. In bonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545), concrete becomes defective when the surface temperature changes faster than 40°F in any one‑hour period, when bond strength fails to meet a minimum average of 175 psi and at least 160 psi per test, or when rain, an improper final finish, or honeycombing produce surface defects that cannot be repaired. In full‑depth reclamation (Section 344), failure to achieve the required seven‑day strength triggers rework of the lot and may require a new mix design or additional compaction if density targets are not met, chemically stabilized material that tests above 900 psi may develop shrinkage cracking and must be repaired, and allowing heavy traffic before the cure period is complete can cause damage, so trucks must be kept off the reclaimed base until strength is verified. In roller‑compacted concrete pavement (Section 502), curing defects are identified when any density test falls below ninety‑six percent of the maximum wet density or when the lot average is less than ninety‑eight percent, when three‑day cylinder strengths are under three thousand psi or twenty‑eight‑day strengths are under three thousand five hundred psi, and when core depth exceeds the plan by more than one quarter inch; any such defect requires removal and replacement of the lot. In high‑friction surface treatment (Section 659), applying the binder before the mandatory thirty‑day cure period for new asphalt or before twenty‑eight days for concrete can cause the mixed binder to become exposed, separate, dry, or otherwise harden in a way that impairs aggregate retention and bonding; cracks wider than one quarter inch that are overfilled may leave cured binder that must be scarified, loose aggregate that falls onto active travel lanes during curing must be removed, and after the cure interval common distresses such as poor aggregate retention, delamination, or color discoloration may appear and require removal, preparation, and retreatment. After curing cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303), a full‑depth test hole is required for each three thousand square yards; if the measured slab thickness is short by one half inch or more, fails surface tolerance, or exhibits rain‑induced or improperly finished surface defects, the work is considered defective and must be removed and replaced. In fine aggregate stockpiles of steel‑slag (Section 703), material must remain uniformly moist for at least six months, and if the average total volumetric expansion measured according to PTM No. 130 exceeds the acceptance limit of 0.50 percent, the stockpile fails and must undergo an additional two‑month cure before retesting; excessive expansion is the primary distress observed. In slab stabilization (Section 679), radial cracks that spread outward from grout injection holes indicate poor quality or improper methods, transverse cracks forming between adjacent injection holes may require full‑slab replacement, pavement raised more than the allowable zero point zero five inch tolerance must be ground back to grade, any vertical movement observed during pumping mandates an immediate stop of the operation, and a stabilized joint or crack that still deflects zero point zero two inch or more after curing requires re‑grouting and retesting. For slip‑form paving (Section 501.3), the contractor must apply the chosen curing material within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and then maintain the cover until the slab reaches the strength required for traffic opening; hourly temperature checks are required and any curing‑temperature change exceeding 40°F in a one‑hour span makes the work defective, while if the high‑low thermometer falls between 40°F and 35°F the cure period must be extended by an additional day and a reading below 35°F also renders the concrete defective; when rain or drying threatens the film the curing compound must be reapplied at the specified rate of 1 gallon per 150 sq ft for white PAMS before it is damaged, failure to do so requiring replacement of the affected pavement as a cure‑defect. For unbonded concrete overlays (Section 548), curing material must be applied within 30 minutes after bleed water dissipates and retained until the concrete reaches the strength required for traffic opening; insulating blankets shall be placed when ambient temperature is 40°F or lower and removed only after air temperature exceeds 40°F or average compressive strength surpasses 3,600 psi, ensuring that surface temperature does not change more than 40°F in any one‑hour period while temperature readings are taken during removal; the white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene liquid membrane‑forming curing compound must be spread uniformly to give a solid opaque cover at a minimum rate of 1 gallon per 150 sq ft for pavements thicker than 6 inches (or per 125 sq ft for ≤ 6 inches), may be applied in two equal passes with the second after sawing, joint faces must be protected from the compound or cleaned before sealant installation, and any curing cover removed to allow saw cutting may expose only the joint and must be replaced within one‑half hour. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502), immediately after final rolling and in‑field density testing the RCC surface shall be kept uniformly moist using an approved method—water cure with a misting water truck, white polyethylene sheeting, curing compound applied to give an opaque film, or cold‑weather curing as referenced in Section 501.3(l)—and the chosen method shall be maintained for at least 72 hours until the pavement reaches a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi before traffic is allowed; routine monitoring of moisture condition and periodic strength checks are required, preventive maintenance includes having a water truck equipped with misting spray nozzles or the appropriate sheeting/compound on site and shielding the slab from vehicular loads for the entire cure period, and if the surface dries or is damaged during curing the same curing treatment should be reapplied or repaired as directed by the Representative. For bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavement (Section 540), a comprehensive QC plan shall monitor concrete temperature and schedule application of curing materials within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates, keeping those materials in place until the slab reaches the strength needed for traffic opening; when ambient air is forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or less insulating blankets must be installed and then removed once the air temperature rises above forty‑five degrees Fahrenheit or when average compressive strength exceeds 3,000 psi, ensuring that removal does not cause a surface temperature change greater than forty degrees Fahrenheit in any one‑hour period, the maturity method may be used to verify required degree‑hours before full insulation removal, and any curing cover removed for joint sawing must not exceed a half‑hour exposure. For testing and acceptance (Section 704), preventive maintenance shall include calibrating air meters a maximum of two weeks before placement and recalibrating them every two weeks during concrete work, as well as calibrating scales, balances and the compression machine at least once per year and whenever the machine is relocated; curing boxes or equivalent equipment must maintain a temperature adjacent to field‑cured cylinders in the range of 60°F to 80°F for the first 24 ± 2 hours, while a Bluetooth‑enabled digital system shall record temperature at least every hour within 30°F to 120°F to an accuracy of 1°F and provide real‑time data to the Representative, and if any equipment such as the compression machine becomes out of tolerance or malfunctions it must be returned to working order within 24 hours or a backup unit supplied. For partial‑depth concrete repairs (Section 525), curing shall be performed with a white membrane‑forming curing compound applied by an atomizing spray tank that has an adjustable nozzle and whose solution is agitated before each application; if ambient air temperature falls below 80°F insulation or heating shall be provided to keep the repair area within the required range, the curing temperature must be monitored at least hourly so that any change does not exceed 40°F in a one‑hour period, exceeding this threshold renders the work defective, and any delaminated repair areas identified during resounding must be replaced at no cost to the Department. For pervious concrete pavement systems (Section 520), curing must begin within 20 minutes of discharge and the surface must be completely covered with polyethylene sheeting that is thoroughly secured at all edges and laps so wind cannot remove it; the sheet shall remain in place for a minimum of 7 days while the pavement gains strength, and if the covering is removed for any reason the same sheet must be reinstated within 60 minutes to avoid interruption, rain occurring before final finishing requires placement to stop, a construction joint to be formed and polyethylene applied, and after curing the concrete shall be inspected for loss of mortar at the surface and, if necessary, repaired or replaced at the Representative’s discretion; infiltration testing performed after curing will identify any areas that fail to achieve the required 200 inches per hour rate, which then must be removed and replaced. For precast concrete products (Section 714), preventive maintenance and monitoring shall require keeping the concrete in its forms and preserving the primary cure until it reaches at least 70% of the 28‑day strength, then performing secondary curing in a minimum 50°F environment until the units attain at least 80% of the 28‑day minimum compressive strength; after secondary cure the units shall not be placed in temperatures below 32°F unless the differential between the units and the air temperature is less than 30°F, and they should remain stored until the full 28‑day minimum compressive strength is achieved. For cement‑treated permeable base course (Section 303), curing should begin immediately after compaction using a white membrane‑forming curing compound applied at a rate of 1 gallon per 100 sq ft ± 10% or, alternatively, a white polyethylene sheet, and the curing material must be maintained for 24 hours; the contractor must monitor the CTPBC temperature during this period and, if it falls below 40°F, extend the cure by an additional 24 hours, while a drop to 32°F within 72 hours of placement requires removal and replacement at no cost to the Department, after curing the surface should be protected from construction traffic for at least three days with any damaged or contaminated areas removed and replaced at no cost, and the work should be protected against rain. For concrete pavement patching (Section 516), the contractor must use the specified liquid membrane‑forming curing compound or an approved Type 2 white pigmented alternative and follow the manufacturer’s recommendations for rapid‑set patching materials; the quality‑control program shall include curing test cylinders and monitor their compressive strength until they reach the minimum required 1,000 psi before the Representative authorizes opening to traffic, and if any cured patch later shows cracking or spalling the defective work must be removed and replaced at no additional cost. For plain cement concrete base course (Section 301), a black liquid membrane‑forming curing compound may be used only when the base is not accessible to traffic before the surface course is placed; discontinue use of the compound if it becomes unsatisfactory as a curing agent and resume curing by other methods as specified, which constitutes the required maintenance for the curing process. For bonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 545), curing materials must be applied within 30 minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and left in place until the pavement reaches the compressive strength required for traffic opening; when forecast air temperature is 45°F or less insulating blankets shall be provided and may be removed only after the temperature rises above 45°F or when measured compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi, with removal performed so that surface temperature does not change more than 40°F in any one‑hour period; curing compound must be spread uniformly at a minimum of 1 gallon per 125 ft² (±0.1 gallon) and joint faces must be kept free of the compound before sealant installation, bond strength shall be monitored by ASTM C1583 testing at seven days and the area accepted only if the average of three tests is at least 175 psi and each individual test is not less than 160 psi, failure to meet these limits or exposure to a temperature‑change rate above the specified 40°F per hour renders the work defective and requires removal and replacement as prescribed. For full‑depth reclamation (Section 344), the chemically stabilized reclaimed base shall be kept damp by applying water each day or by spraying an emulsified asphalt primer followed by a fog seal at a residual rate between 0.02 and 0.10 gal/yd² while ensuring moisture does not exceed more than 3%; heavy traffic is prohibited for the seven‑day cure period, light vehicles may be permitted after proof rolling with a vehicle similar to the heaviest expected and confirming no damage at speeds under ten miles per hour, any observed damage being repaired immediately; strength monitoring continues until the seven‑day strength requirement is satisfied, and if acceptance criteria are not met the area must be reworked and core holes filled with an approved repair material. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements (Section 502) acceptance, curing shall be monitored by testing field‑cured cylinders at three, seven and twenty‑eight days; if the three‑day compressive strength test result is greater than or equal to 3,000 psi (or likewise for the seven‑day result) the field cure may be discontinued unless otherwise directed, otherwise curing continues until the twenty‑eight‑day test shows a minimum compressive strength of 3,500 psi; density shall also be verified so that the final average is not less than 98% of the maximum wet density and no individual test is less than 96% of the maximum wet density, and when cores are taken any core holes shall be filled with RCC material or a rapid‑set concrete patching material; if the pavement is found defective during curing it must be removed and replaced. For cold‑recycled asphalt base course (Section 341), emulsified‑asphalt‑stabilized CRABC shall be cured for at least two weeks before placing the wearing or binder course, or foamed‑asphalt‑stabilized material shall be cured until the average moisture content is less than 2%; thereafter a minimum of three randomly selected samples per PTM No. 1, each at least 1,200 g, shall be dry cut or extracted and their moisture content determined, and any core holes identified during sampling must be back‑filled and compacted with CRABC material or an approved alternate to maintain the cured condition. For long‑life concrete pavement (Section 530), the QC plan shall specify a concrete temperature control plan that is activated when concrete temperatures exceed 80°F at placement and shall detail a method to cure and protect joints during the curing period so moisture loss from concrete in and adjacent to joints is prevented; for daily placements exceeding 700 sq yd, LTU vertical and horizontal alignment shall be verified within 18 hours by an acceptable nondestructive verification method, providing ongoing monitoring that supports proper joint performance during curing. For high‑friction surface treatment (Section 659), preventive maintenance for the curing phase requires keeping all equipment and traffic off the HFST until the binder has fully cured, observing the mandatory 30‑day cure period for new asphalt pavements and ensuring concrete surfaces are at least 28 days old before application; the contractor must monitor binder mixing to stay within ±2 % of the manufacturer’s volume specifications, remove any contaminated binder, and after curing reclaim surplus aggregate between 15 to 30 days post‑placement while prohibiting reuse of reclaimed material, and any areas showing poor aggregate retention, delamination or color discoloration shall be prepared and retreated with HFST at no additional cost, a calibrated skid test being performed between 45 and 120 days after completion to verify performance. For steel‑slag fine aggregate (Section 703), preventive maintenance consists of limiting the stockpile size to a maximum of 30,000 tons, completely soaking the material with water before or during stockpiling, and maintaining the pile uniformly moist for a period of not less than six months; a Representative shall monitor the cured stockpile by sampling and testing its expansive characteristics according to PTM No. 130, acceptance requiring an average total volumetric expansion of less than 0.50%, and if the test exceeds that limit corrective repair shall be to continue curing for a minimum of two additional months and then retest. For slab stabilization (Section 679), preventive monitoring includes performing deflection testing only when air temperature is between 40°F and 70°F and using visual verification of pumping when temperatures fall outside that range; joints or cracks showing a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more shall be stabilized, re‑grouted if necessary, and retested after 24 hours, any panel displacement exceeding the 0.05 inch allowable tolerance shall be corrected by grinding to grade, repairs required during curing involve patching full‑depth drill holes with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete material, grinding raised pavement back within tolerance, stopping work and correcting causes if radial cracks appear from injection holes, and replacing an entire slab at no extra cost when transverse cracks develop between adjacent grout injection holes.
