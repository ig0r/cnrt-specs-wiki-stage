Joint Rehabilitation

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Joint Rehabilitation is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
joint-rehabilitation
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Joint rehabilitation is a preservation treatment applied to rigid Portland‑cement concrete pavements when joint distress threatens load transfer and water‑tightness, aiming to restore structural continuity and extend service life[1]. The process begins with full‑depth spall repair followed by cleaning of remaining minor spalls (typically ≤ 2 in.), diamond grinding to shape a sealant reservoir that meets the required shape factor, installation of dowel‑bar load‑transfer devices as needed, and placement of a rubberized sealant to re‑establish a watertight joint[1][2]. It is limited to early‑stage distress on pavements with a stable subgrade and functional drainage, requires that patched area not exceed ten percent of the surface unless justified, and its success is verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing[1].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Joint rehabilitation is applied to concrete pavements when joint distress threatens load transfer and water‑tightness [1][2]. The treatment is triggered by observable spalling of the joint concrete, deterioration or failure of transverse, longitudinal, expansion, or pavement‑shoulder joints, early cracking, loss of seal integrity, or a joint geometry that no longer provides an adequate sealant reservoir [1]. When these conditions are present, any required full‑depth spall repairs are performed first, after which the remaining small spalls—typically two inches or less—are cleaned and resealed [1][2]. The joint is then shaped, often following diamond grinding, to achieve the proper shape factor that defines an appropriate sealant reservoir width, especially where the existing joint has become as wide as or wider than the design requirement [1][2]. A new sealant is placed to re‑establish a water‑tight joint and restore effective load transfer, thereby preventing further faulting, spalling, water infiltration, or loss of structural performance [1][2]. By addressing these distress mechanisms the treatment extends the service life of the pavement [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 394)

Joint rehabilitation in concrete pavements is undertaken to repair joints that have become spalled or otherwise failed and to re‑establish their load‑transfer capability. The work typically follows diamond grinding so that the sealant reservoir can be reshaped correctly, after which all transverse joints that have failed are rebuilt and every longitudinal and shoulder joint is cleaned and resealed. By constructing new sealant reservoirs and applying fresh sealant, the treatment restores the structural continuity of the slab, halts further joint decay, and thereby prolongs the useful life of the pavement when an overlay is not planned. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Distress characterization and causes

Joint distress in concrete pavements is fundamentally driven by a combination of load‑related and climate/materials‑related mechanisms. Repeated heavy traffic loading generates cracking at mid‑panel locations and overloads transverse joints, especially when the ability of the joint to transfer loads has been compromised. At the same time, water infiltration through inadequate or failed drainage systems penetrates the pavement, carrying chlorides and incompressible particles that accelerate deterioration of both the concrete matrix and the joint sealants. Weak subgrade or subbase support further undermines load transfer, leading to faulting and uneven settlement that magnifies joint movement. Frost action, aging concrete with reduced compressive strength, low air content, and deteriorating aggregate also contribute to loss of structural capacity. In addition, excessive shrinkage cracking associated with high early‑strength (HES) concrete exacerbates joint deterioration, while crushed or otherwise degraded joint material—often more severe toward the slab bottom than near the surface—reduces the effectiveness of the joint as a water barrier and load‑transfer element.

These mechanisms interact to erode the joint’s ability to accommodate thermal expansion and contraction, resulting in seal failure, increased crack propagation, and further loss of support. Effective joint rehabilitation therefore must address each underlying cause: restoring full subgrade support through slab stabilization, reestablishing proper drainage with a continuous pavement base drain, reconstructing sealant reservoirs and resealing joints, installing dowel bar retrofits to regain load transfer at mid‑panel cracks and transverse joints, and avoiding the use of HES concrete that predisposes the slab to shrinkage cracking. By mitigating water infiltration, reinforcing load‑transfer capacity, and ensuring a stable supporting foundation, joint rehabilitation can halt the acceleration of distress and extend the service life of the pavement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90)

Applicability and treatment selection

Joint rehabilitation is appropriate for existing Portland‑cement concrete (rigid) pavements that are being restored rather than overlaid, provided the pavement remains structurally sound as verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing and only minor joint distress is evident while overall serviceability is satisfactory. The treatment can be applied when failed joints have been identified and the total area of concrete patching—including the joint work—does not exceed ten percent of the pavement surface unless a cost‑effectiveness justification for exceeding that limit is supplied. Successful implementation also requires site conditions of a stable underlying subgrade and a functional drainage system that prevents water, chlorides, or incompressible materials from infiltrating the structure, allowing sealant reservoirs to function effectively. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Joint rehabilitation should be limited to situations where the pavement exhibits only early‑stage joint distress and where the required concrete patching remains a small fraction of the surface. When the amount of patching needed exceeds ten percent of the total area—excluding existing patches that are still in good condition—a cost‑effectiveness analysis must be performed, and if the analysis does not support preservation, an alternative such as full‑depth patching or overlay should be selected. The treatment is also inappropriate when the dominant distress is load‑related or stems from climate‑induced material durability problems; in those cases structural improvement, material replacement, or a concrete or asphalt overlay is required rather than merely fixing joints. If an overlay is planned, joint rehabilitation is unnecessary because the new layer demands a uniformly supporting base; any joints that have lost load transfer or are crushed must instead be removed and repaired through full‑depth patching or other appropriate methods. Likewise, when joint performance has progressed beyond minor distress—evidenced by faulting, poor load transfer, or compromised support—the pavement requires slab stabilization, dowel‑bar retrofits, jacking, or full‑depth concrete patching rather than rehabilitation. Additionally, joint cleaning, sealing, or rehabilitation must be performed only after diamond grinding, which creates the proper sealant reservoir shape and protects the new sealant. Finally, verification with Falling Weight Deflectometer testing is required to confirm that all failed joints have been addressed; if verification fails, alternative preservation or rehabilitation techniques such as diamond grinding, overlay, or full‑depth patching must be employed. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Condition assessment and timing

Determining the need for joint rehabilitation begins with a systematic visual survey that records and quantifies all observable distress such as cracking, spalling, loss of sealant, or early‑stage joint failure. The quantities of each type of distress are tabulated to establish severity thresholds. When visual cues indicate faulting, poor load transfer, or any sign that joint performance is compromised, the engineer proceeds with targeted testing. Concrete cores are extracted both adjacent to suspect joints and at randomly selected locations to evaluate subbase or subgrade conditions and to assess the depth and extent of deterioration beneath the pavement surface. Deflection testing, typically using a Falling Weight Deflectorometer (FWD), together with fault‑level measurements, confirms loss of structural support at specific joint locations. Complementary non‑destructive techniques—including ground‑penetrating radar, friction and roughness assessments, MIRA evaluations, MIT‑Scan 2 for dowel bar alignment, MIT‑Scan T2 for slab thickness, and tensile bond testing—provide additional insight into joint integrity, dowel condition, and underlying material performance. The combined results of these destructive and non‑destructive investigations identify precisely which joints require cleaning, sealing, replacement, or slab stabilization, while also verifying that the subgrade remains stable and drainage functions properly before any rehabilitation work is undertaken. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Joint rehabilitation is indicated once the condition of concrete‑pavement joints progresses beyond normal wear and corrective action such as spall repair, cleaning, or resealing becomes necessary. [1] The work is performed after any required spall repairs at a joint have been completed and specifically targets joints that still contain small unrepaired spalls no larger than two inches. [2] It is applied when transverse joints have failed but have not yet been repaired; in this situation all failed transverse joints are rehabilitated while non‑failed longitudinal and shoulder joints receive cleaning and resealing to restore function. [1] The earliest sign of joint distress—minor cracking or loss of sealant—is also a trigger for rehabilitation even though overall serviceability may still be satisfactory, because intervening at this stage yields the greatest cost effectiveness and helps extend pavement life when an overlay is not planned. [1] Rehabilitation should follow diamond grinding so that the sealant can be placed in a properly shaped reservoir and avoid damage to the sealant. [1] Any full‑depth patching associated with joint rehabilitation must include repair of all failed joints verified by inspection or Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, and the total patched area should not exceed ten percent of the pavement unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 394)

Materials and repair details

Joint rehabilitation on rigid pavements begins with a condition‑based assessment that dictates whether each transverse joint is fully reconstructed with load‑transfer devices or simply cleaned and resealed; the same cleaning and sealing operation is applied to longitudinal and pavement‑shoulder joints to reestablish continuity and water tightness. [1] Prior to any joint work, all spalls are repaired, and any remaining spalls two inches or less are cleaned and sealed as part of the preparation. [2] For each joint a sealant reservoir is cut to the width and depth prescribed for the specific joint spacing, centered on the existing opening, and immediately flushed with water; a tape bond‑breaker is placed on the bottom of the reservoir but must not extend more than one‑eighth inch up the face. [2] When joints are wider than standard, the cavity is cleaned to the required depth using a water blaster capable of at least ten gallons per minute at 7,000 psi and joint backing material is installed to achieve the needed depth; all vertical faces are media‑blasted and then blown clean with 100 psi compressed air before sealant application. [2] Load transfer is provided by dowel bars that run the full embedded length of the joint, are coated for corrosion protection, and are bonded to the existing concrete with a binder selected from epoxy, polyester, or vinylester systems. [1] The design must specify new pavement‑joint items according to whether skewed or non‑skewed joint baskets are required, and must follow the joint‑spacing requirements of the concrete pavement type—perpendicular joints spaced at 30 ft for jointed reinforced concrete pavements and at 15 ft for jointed plain concrete pavements. [1] Two joint configurations are addressed: Type 1 for existing saw‑cut joints and Type 2 for existing metal‑plate joints, the latter requiring removal of the plate portion within the new reservoir while leaving no more than one‑quarter inch of plate protruding. [1][2] All rehabilitated joints receive appropriate seals, with a rubberized joint‑sealing material prescribed in the specifications being placed into the reservoir after it is formed and cleaned. [1][2] When joint rehabilitation is incorporated into a concrete full‑depth patch, new concrete replaces every failed joint, dowel‑bar retrofits may be included, and the work is verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing; the patched area may not exceed ten percent of the pavement surface unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 394)

The success of joint rehabilitation hinges on the inherent properties of the concrete slab and its aggregates, with rapid‑polishing aggregates such as Vanport Limestone requiring alternatives to conventional diamond grinding because grinding removes the mortar layer and quickly degrades skid resistance [1]. Concrete compressive strength and air content also influence load‑carrying capacity of repaired joints, and any visible durability issues such as frost damage or aggregate degradation further limit treatment effectiveness [1]. Existing pavement conditions set practical limits on the scope of rehabilitation; all failed transverse or longitudinal joints must be identified and confirmed by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, and the total area to be patched is generally constrained to ten percent of the pavement unless a cost‑benefit analysis justifies exceeding that threshold [1]. A stable subgrade and a functional drainage system are prerequisite conditions so that water, chlorides, or incompressible materials cannot infiltrate the joint after repair, and early intervention at the first signs of minor joint distress yields the most cost‑effective results [1]. When no asphalt overlay is planned, diamond grinding after joint cleaning and sealing is required to achieve the proper sealant reservoir shape factor that allows a continuous rubberized sealant bond without damage; if an overlay is intended, the concrete joints are sawed and sealed to provide a sound bonding surface for the new layer [1]. Load transfer across repaired joints must be restored by installing dowels in every patched joint and fully bonding them along their embedded length with compatible anchoring binders such as epoxy, polyester, or vinylester, with coated or high‑performance dowel bars preferred for long‑term service [1]. Compatibility of the sealant system with joint geometry demands that the rubberized sealant form a continuous reservoir matching the specified shape factor and be appropriate for the joint type—whether saw‑cut or metal‑plate—to ensure durable adhesion [1]. Prior to sealant placement, the joint must be thoroughly cleaned of oil, moisture, and other contaminants; media blasting of all vertical faces is required using compressed air at approximately 100 psi, and when water blasting is employed the equipment must deliver a minimum of ten gallons per minute at seven thousand psi [2]. A tape bond breaker may be placed on the bottom of the sealant reservoir but must not extend more than one‑eighth inch up the reservoir face, and any existing steel plate within the new reservoir must be removed or limited to a protrusion of no more than one‑quarter inch [2]. Crushed or deteriorated joint material must be removed before dowel placement, and the spacing and alignment of newly formed joints should replicate that of adjacent slabs to maintain consistent load transfer and ride quality [1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 394)

Treatment procedures

Joint rehabilitation begins after any spall repairs are finished, with all unrepaired spalls two inches or smaller cleaned and sealed. The existing joint is then sawed to form a sealant reservoir of the required width and depth, centered on the joint opening, and immediately flushed with water; a tape bond‑breaker is placed at the bottom of the reservoir, ensuring it does not extend more than one‑eighth inch up the face. For joints that are already as wide as the reservoir or for Type 2 work, the joint surface (and any steel plate protruding into the reservoir, limited to a quarter‑inch) is thoroughly cleaned—using water blasting at ten gallons per minute and 7,000 psi if needed—and joint backing material is added to achieve the proper depth; all vertical faces are media‑blasted and blown with 100 psi compressed air to remove oil, moisture and other contaminants before applying the sealant as specified. The procedure does not prescribe a separate curing step beyond following the sealant’s own requirements. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 394)

Joint rehabilitation is scheduled only after any required spall repairs have been completed and after diamond grinding has been performed, a sequence that ensures the sealant reservoir attains the proper shape factor and prevents damage to existing sealant [1][2]. The work then proceeds with thorough cleaning of the joint, followed by sawing the sealant reservoir to the prescribed dimensions [2]. Immediately after sawing, the reservoir is flushed with water using a high‑capacity water‑blaster capable of at least 10 gpm at 7,000 psi, and the joint faces are media‑blasted with a compressed‑air stream of roughly 100 psi to remove oil, moisture, and other contaminants [2]. Before sealant placement, a tape bond breaker is positioned on the bottom of the reservoir to protect the sealant from adhesion to the substrate [2]. The joint surface must be dry and free of contaminants prior to applying the sealant, which may require allowing sufficient time for drying after flushing [2]. Across the guides, no explicit traffic control measures are mandated for joint rehabilitation, indicating that standard site safety practices apply but no additional restrictions are required [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 394)

Quality and workmanship

A satisfactory joint rehabilitation is considered achieved when the damaged transverse or expansion joint has been thoroughly cleaned and a new sealant reservoir constructed to the shape‑factor requirements set out in Publication 408 Section 521, with the reservoir cut to the dimensions shown on the standard drawings [1][2]. The bond‑breaker tape placed at the bottom of the reservoir must not extend more than one‑eighth inch above the reservoir face, and any steel plate retained within the reservoir may protrude no more than one‑quarter inch [2]. If water blasting is employed, the equipment must be capable of delivering a minimum of ten gallons per minute at 7,000 psi, after which the joint faces are media‑blasted and then cleaned with a 100 psi compressed‑air stream so that they are free of oil, moisture, and other contaminants before resealing [2]. Resealing is performed using the specified rubberized sealing material while the pavement exhibits only early‑stage joint distress, indicating that the underlying subgrade remains stable and drainage functions properly [1]. All failed joints on the rigid pavement must be repaired and their structural performance verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing [1]. The cumulative area of concrete patching, including joints, may not exceed ten percent of the total pavement surface unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided [1]. When no asphalt overlay follows the repair, diamond grinding shall be applied to achieve the required smooth ride [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 394)

Performance and service life

Joint rehabilitation restores the sealing capacity of transverse and expansion joints by first cleaning the joint surfaces, then forming new sealant reservoirs and applying a rubberized joint sealant. When performed at the earliest signs of distress—before spalling or faulting becomes extensive—the treatment blocks water and chloride ingress, improves load transfer across slab edges, and thereby preserves pavement condition and durability. In practice the procedure is often incorporated into concrete full‑depth patching; all failed joints identified through Falling Weight Deflectometer testing are repaired, which raises overall ride quality and maintains functional performance. The scope of joint rehabilitation is generally limited to ten percent of the total pavement area unless a cost‑effective justification can be demonstrated, making it a targeted preservation activity. Because it restores effective joint seals and load transfer, the treatment extends the expected service life of the concrete pavement, especially when applied to sections that are just beginning to exhibit minor joint distress. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Joint rehabilitation most often focuses on repairing failed transverse joints while also cleaning and resealing longitudinal and pavement‑shoulder joints to preserve overall joint integrity. The principal distresses that can recur after treatment include loss of load transfer, joint crushing or deterioration, faulting, and the development of shrinkage cracks when high‑early‑strength concrete is used. In addition, tie‑bars or bolts may shift in partially set patch material, especially if traffic loads the adjacent lane before the repair has hardened, and sealants can become compromised by water infiltration, chlorides, or incompressible debris. These failures are largely driven by inadequate dowel anchoring and bonding, selection of unsuitable high‑early‑strength mixes, insufficient tie‑bar design for longer patches, and premature traffic exposure on fresh concrete. External conditions such as an unstable subgrade, ineffective drainage, and delayed resealing—allowing joints to progress beyond minor distress— further exacerbate faulting and loss of load transfer, often necessitating more extensive repairs if left unattended. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90)

Monitoring and follow-up

After joint rehabilitation is completed, the repaired joints must be verified through post‑treatment testing. The standard practice is to conduct Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing to confirm that all failed joints have been successfully repaired and that the pavement meets the required structural criteria. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Additional maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation is warranted when the joints that have been treated begin to show any early distress. The appearance of spalls, loss of seal integrity, faulting between adjacent slabs, reduced load‑transfer capability, or visible cracking in the concrete are clear indicators even if overall pavement serviceability still appears satisfactory. An inspection that identifies failed or faulted joints together with a Falling Weight Deflectometer test that reveals a reduction in structural capacity within the joint zone confirms the need for further action. Moreover, when the amount of concrete patching required to address the distress approaches or exceeds ten percent of the total pavement surface, the area should be slated for additional rehabilitation unless a cost‑effectiveness analysis demonstrates otherwise. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)
