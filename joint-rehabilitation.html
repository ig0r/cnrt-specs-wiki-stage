Joint Rehabilitation

Part of: Pavement Preservation is-subtopic-of
Joint Rehabilitation is a topic under Pavement Preservation.
joint-rehabilitation
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Joint rehabilitation is a preservation technique applied to existing Portland‑cement concrete pavements whose joints have become spalled, widened, or otherwise failed, compromising load transfer and permitting water intrusion [1][2]. The treatment cleans joint faces, repairs spalls up to two inches, reshapes the joint—typically after diamond grinding—to form a proper sealant reservoir, installs dowel‑bar retrofits or other load‑transfer devices as required, and places a rubberized sealant with a tape bond breaker while ensuring a stable subgrade and functional drainage [1][2]. By re‑establishing a water‑tight joint and restoring structural continuity across slab edges, joint rehabilitation halts further deterioration and extends the service life of the pavement when an overlay is not planned [1].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Joint rehabilitation is applied to concrete pavements when joints show distress that undermines load transfer and permits water intrusion [1][2]. The distress typically manifests as spalled joint concrete, widening of the joint beyond the required sealant reservoir dimensions, failure of transverse, longitudinal, expansion, or pavement‑shoulder joints, and early cracking that reduces seal integrity [1][2]. Such conditions allow water to infiltrate the pavement structure, leading to further faulting, additional spalling, loss of structural performance, and accelerated deterioration [1]. Joint rehabilitation therefore targets these compromised joints by cleaning the joint faces, repairing spalls up to two inches, restoring the proper joint shape—often after diamond grinding—to achieve the correct shape factor for a sealant reservoir, and installing new sealant with a tape bond breaker where required [1][2]. By re‑establishing a water‑tight joint and restoring load transfer capacity, the treatment prevents further cracking and extends the service life of the pavement [1].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 403)

Joint rehabilitation in concrete pavements is undertaken to repair joints that have become spalled or otherwise failed and to re‑establish their load‑transfer capability. The work typically follows diamond grinding so that the sealant reservoir can be reshaped correctly, after which all transverse joints that have failed are rebuilt and every longitudinal and shoulder joint is cleaned and resealed. By constructing new sealant reservoirs and applying fresh sealant, the treatment restores the structural continuity of the slab, halts further joint decay, and thereby prolongs the useful life of the pavement when an overlay is not planned. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Distress characterization and causes

Joint distress in concrete pavements is fundamentally driven by a combination of load‑related and climate/materials‑related mechanisms. Repeated heavy traffic loading generates cracking at mid‑panel locations and overloads transverse joints, especially when the ability of the joint to transfer loads has been compromised. At the same time, water infiltration through inadequate or failed drainage systems penetrates the pavement, carrying chlorides and incompressible particles that accelerate deterioration of both the concrete matrix and the joint sealants. Weak subgrade or subbase support further undermines load transfer, leading to faulting and uneven settlement that magnifies joint movement. Frost action, aging concrete with reduced compressive strength, low air content, and deteriorating aggregate also contribute to loss of structural capacity. In addition, excessive shrinkage cracking associated with high early‑strength (HES) concrete exacerbates joint deterioration, while crushed or otherwise degraded joint material—often more severe toward the slab bottom than near the surface—reduces the effectiveness of the joint as a water barrier and load‑transfer element.

These mechanisms interact to erode the joint’s ability to accommodate thermal expansion and contraction, resulting in seal failure, increased crack propagation, and further loss of support. Effective joint rehabilitation therefore must address each underlying cause: restoring full subgrade support through slab stabilization, reestablishing proper drainage with a continuous pavement base drain, reconstructing sealant reservoirs and resealing joints, installing dowel bar retrofits to regain load transfer at mid‑panel cracks and transverse joints, and avoiding the use of HES concrete that predisposes the slab to shrinkage cracking. By mitigating water infiltration, reinforcing load‑transfer capacity, and ensuring a stable supporting foundation, joint rehabilitation can halt the acceleration of distress and extend the service life of the pavement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90)

Applicability and treatment selection

Joint rehabilitation is appropriate for existing Portland‑cement concrete (rigid) pavements that are being restored rather than overlaid, provided the pavement remains structurally sound as verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing and only minor joint distress is evident while overall serviceability is satisfactory. The treatment can be applied when failed joints have been identified and the total area of concrete patching—including the joint work—does not exceed ten percent of the pavement surface unless a cost‑effectiveness justification for exceeding that limit is supplied. Successful implementation also requires site conditions of a stable underlying subgrade and a functional drainage system that prevents water, chlorides, or incompressible materials from infiltrating the structure, allowing sealant reservoirs to function effectively. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Joint rehabilitation should be limited to situations where the pavement exhibits only early‑stage joint distress and where the required concrete patching remains a small fraction of the surface. When the amount of patching needed exceeds ten percent of the total area—excluding existing patches that are still in good condition—a cost‑effectiveness analysis must be performed, and if the analysis does not support preservation, an alternative such as full‑depth patching or overlay should be selected. The treatment is also inappropriate when the dominant distress is load‑related or stems from climate‑induced material durability problems; in those cases structural improvement, material replacement, or a concrete or asphalt overlay is required rather than merely fixing joints. If an overlay is planned, joint rehabilitation is unnecessary because the new layer demands a uniformly supporting base; any joints that have lost load transfer or are crushed must instead be removed and repaired through full‑depth patching or other appropriate methods. Likewise, when joint performance has progressed beyond minor distress—evidenced by faulting, poor load transfer, or compromised support—the pavement requires slab stabilization, dowel‑bar retrofits, jacking, or full‑depth concrete patching rather than rehabilitation. Additionally, joint cleaning, sealing, or rehabilitation must be performed only after diamond grinding, which creates the proper sealant reservoir shape and protects the new sealant. Finally, verification with Falling Weight Deflectometer testing is required to confirm that all failed joints have been addressed; if verification fails, alternative preservation or rehabilitation techniques such as diamond grinding, overlay, or full‑depth patching must be employed. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Condition assessment and timing

Determining the need for joint rehabilitation begins with a systematic visual survey that records and quantifies all observable distress such as cracking, spalling, loss of sealant, or early‑stage joint failure. The quantities of each type of distress are tabulated to establish severity thresholds. When visual cues indicate faulting, poor load transfer, or any sign that joint performance is compromised, the engineer proceeds with targeted testing. Concrete cores are extracted both adjacent to suspect joints and at randomly selected locations to evaluate subbase or subgrade conditions and to assess the depth and extent of deterioration beneath the pavement surface. Deflection testing, typically using a Falling Weight Deflectorometer (FWD), together with fault‑level measurements, confirms loss of structural support at specific joint locations. Complementary non‑destructive techniques—including ground‑penetrating radar, friction and roughness assessments, MIRA evaluations, MIT‑Scan 2 for dowel bar alignment, MIT‑Scan T2 for slab thickness, and tensile bond testing—provide additional insight into joint integrity, dowel condition, and underlying material performance. The combined results of these destructive and non‑destructive investigations identify precisely which joints require cleaning, sealing, replacement, or slab stabilization, while also verifying that the subgrade remains stable and drainage functions properly before any rehabilitation work is undertaken. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Joint rehabilitation should be initiated when the condition of concrete pavement joints has progressed beyond normal wear to a point where corrective actions such as spall repair, cleaning, or resealing become necessary [1]. The treatment is required once all spalling at a joint has been repaired; any remaining minor spalls no larger than about two inches are cleaned before the sealant reservoir is cut and sealed [2]. Rehabilitation is also indicated when transverse joints have failed and have not yet been repaired, prompting rehabilitation of all such failed transverse joints while non‑failed longitudinal and shoulder joints receive cleaning and resealing to restore function [1]. The guides recommend applying joint rehabilitation at the earliest sign of joint distress—when minor cracking or loss of sealant first appears—even if overall pavement serviceability remains satisfactory, because intervening at this stage offers the greatest cost effectiveness and helps extend pavement life when an overlay is not planned [1]. In a concrete‑pavement preservation program, joint rehabilitation follows diamond grinding so that the sealant can be placed in a properly shaped reservoir and avoid damage [1]. When full‑depth patching is required as part of joint rehabilitation, all failed joints must be repaired and verified by inspection or Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, and the total patched area should not exceed ten percent of the pavement unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided [1].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 403)

Materials and repair details

Joint rehabilitation on rigid pavements starts with a condition‑based assessment of each joint and proceeds either with full reconstruction of failed transverse joints or with cleaning and resealing of joints that have not failed, including longitudinal and pavement‑shoulder joints to restore continuity and water tightness. [1] Full reconstruction involves rebuilding the joint and installing load‑transfer devices in the form of dowel bars that extend the full embedded length of the joint and are bonded to the existing concrete with a binder selected from epoxy, polyester or vinylester systems, after which the dowels are coated and sealed to protect against corrosion. [1] The design must specify new pavement‑joint items according to whether skewed or non‑skewed joint baskets are required, as illustrated on drawing RC‑26M, and must observe the joint‑spacing requirements of the concrete pavement type—perpendicular joints spaced at 30 ft for jointed reinforced concrete pavements and at 15 ft for jointed plain concrete pavements. [1] For each rehabilitated joint a sealant reservoir is cut to the prescribed width and depth, centered on the joint, and a tape bond‑breaker is placed at the bottom of the reservoir with its upper edge not extending more than one‑eighth inch above the face of the reservoir. [2] Where the existing joint depth is insufficient, joint backing material is installed to achieve the required reservoir depth, and any steel plate intersecting a Type 2 reservoir is removed except for up to one‑quarter inch that may remain protruding. [2] The reservoir is then filled with the rubberized joint‑sealing material prescribed in Sections 512 and 521 of Publication 408, after media blasting and high‑pressure air cleaning; when a water blaster is used it must have a minimum capacity of ten gallons per minute at 7,000 psi. [1][2] Two joint configurations are addressed: Type 1 for existing saw‑cut joints and Type 2 for existing metal‑plate joints, each receiving the appropriate sealant reservoir and sealing procedure. [1][2] When joint rehabilitation is combined with a concrete full‑depth patch, new concrete replaces every failed joint, the work is verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, and the patched area may not exceed ten percent of the pavement surface unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided; dowel‑bar retrofits or other concrete‑pavement‑restoration techniques may be incorporated. [1] Prior to sealing, any remaining spalls up to two inches are removed and the joint is cleaned by media blasting or high‑pressure air cleaning to ensure proper adhesion of the sealant. [2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 403)

The performance of joint rehabilitation is controlled by the properties of the existing concrete slab and its aggregates, notably that aggregates prone to rapid polishing such as Vanport Limestone require alternative surface treatments rather than conventional diamond grinding [1]. Concrete compressive strength and air content also influence the ability of repaired joints to sustain loads, with higher strength and appropriate air voids supporting better load transfer [1]. The extent of existing distress limits the feasible scope of patching; guidelines generally restrict concrete pavement patching to no more than ten percent of the total area unless a cost‑benefit justification is provided, and all failed joints must be identified and confirmed by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing before repair [1]. When an overlay is not planned, diamond grinding after joint cleaning and sealing is required to achieve the proper sealant reservoir shape factor and to protect the fresh rubberized sealant from damage; if an asphalt overlay will be placed, the concrete joints are sawed and sealed to provide a sound bonding surface for the new layer [1]. Restoring load transfer across repaired joints depends on installing dowels in every patched joint and fully bonding them along their embedded length with compatible anchoring binders such as epoxy, polyester, or vinylester, with coated or high‑performance dowel bars preferred for durability [1]. All crushed or deteriorated joint material must be removed prior to dowel placement to ensure proper engagement of the dowels and sealant [1]. Compatibility between the sealant system and joint geometry is essential; the rubberized sealant must form a continuous reservoir that conforms to the required shape factor, and its performance varies with joint type—saw‑cut or metal‑plate—requiring appropriate sealant selection [1]. The bond breaker tape used at the bottom of the reservoir may not extend more than one‑eighth inch up the face, and any steel plate within the reservoir is limited to a maximum protrusion of one‑quarter inch to allow adequate sealant adhesion [2]. All vertical faces of the joint must be media‑blasted with compressed air at approximately one hundred pounds per square inch, free of oil, moisture, or other contaminants, before sealing, and water blasting should meet a minimum flow of ten gallons per minute at seven thousand psi to achieve proper cleaning [2]. A stable underlying subgrade and a functional drainage system are prerequisite conditions; water, chlorides, or incompressible materials must be prevented from infiltrating the joint to maintain long‑term performance [1]. Joint rehabilitation is most cost‑effective when applied at the first signs of minor joint distress, allowing the treatment to extend service life before extensive cracking develops [1]. The technique is applicable only to restoration of existing concrete joints and not to projects that involve overlaying with asphalt without prior joint preparation, emphasizing the need for proper sequencing of grinding, sawing, sealing, or overlay placement according to project scope [1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 403)

Treatment procedures

Joint rehabilitation begins after any spall repairs are finished; all small unrepaired spalls (two inches or less) are cleaned and sealed, then the joint sealant reservoir is cut to the required width and depth, centered on the existing opening, and flushed with water. A tape bond‑breaker is placed at the bottom of the reservoir, limited to a maximum protrusion of one‑eighth inch, and if the joint is wider than needed, it is thoroughly cleaned—using a water blaster of at least ten gallons per minute at 7 000 psi when required—and any necessary backing material is installed. Immediately before applying sealant, the vertical faces are media‑blasted and then blown clean with compressed air at 100 psi to remove oil, moisture and other contaminants; for Type 2 joints the portion of the existing steel plate within the reservoir is removed, allowing no more than a quarter inch to remain protruding. The sealant is then placed according to Section 501.3(n). [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 403)

Joint rehabilitation is scheduled after the completion of diamond grinding to obtain the proper shape factor for the sealant reservoir and to avoid damaging any existing sealant [1]. The work also follows the finalization of all spall repairs at the joint, with any remaining small spalls cleaned and sealed before rehabilitation begins [2]. The prescribed sequence then requires cutting a sealant reservoir to the width and depth indicated for the existing joint spacing using a saw, installing a tape bond breaker that does not exceed a one‑eighth inch rise, flushing the joint with water, media‑blasting the vertical faces, and finally cleaning them with a high‑pressure air stream before applying the sealant [2]. Required equipment includes a concrete saw for reservoir formation, a water blaster capable of at least ten gallons per minute at seven thousand pounds per square inch, compressed‑air tools delivering roughly one hundred pounds per square inch at the source, and tape bond breakers meeting the specified rise limitation [2]. The guidelines emphasize that all joint surfaces must be free of oil, moisture, or other contaminants prior to sealant placement, which is achieved by the water flush and subsequent high‑pressure air cleaning steps [2]. No explicit environmental thresholds such as temperature or humidity limits are stipulated in the sources, but the requirement for dry, clean surfaces implicitly restricts work to conditions where moisture can be removed effectively [2][1]. Likewise, the references do not prescribe particular traffic control measures or lane closures for joint rehabilitation activities [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 403)

Quality and workmanship

A satisfactory joint rehabilitation is achieved when the damaged transverse or expansion joint has been thoroughly cleaned and a new sealant reservoir constructed that conforms to the shape‑factor requirements specified in Publication 408 Section 521, after which the joint is resealed with the prescribed rubberized sealing material [1]. The preparation of the joint must include setting back any bond‑breaker tape so that it does not extend more than one‑eighth inch above the reservoir face and limiting any existing steel plate intrusion to no more than a quarter inch into the sealant cavity [2]. Cleaning is required to reach the specified depth using a water blaster capable of delivering at least ten gallons per minute at seven thousand pounds per square inch, or an equivalent method, and all vertical faces must be media‑blasted then cleared with a one hundred‑psi compressed‑air stream that leaves no oil, moisture, or other contaminants before sealant placement [2]. The work should be performed while the pavement exhibits only early‑stage joint distress, indicating that the underlying subgrade remains stable and drainage functions properly [1]. Every failed joint on the rigid pavement must be repaired and its structural performance verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing [1]. The total area of concrete patching, including joints, may not exceed ten percent of the overall pavement surface unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided, and if no asphalt overlay follows the repair, diamond grinding shall be applied to achieve the required smooth ride [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 403)

Performance and service life

Joint rehabilitation restores the sealing capacity of transverse and expansion joints by first cleaning the joint surfaces, then forming new sealant reservoirs and applying a rubberized joint sealant. When performed at the earliest signs of distress—before spalling or faulting becomes extensive—the treatment blocks water and chloride ingress, improves load transfer across slab edges, and thereby preserves pavement condition and durability. In practice the procedure is often incorporated into concrete full‑depth patching; all failed joints identified through Falling Weight Deflectometer testing are repaired, which raises overall ride quality and maintains functional performance. The scope of joint rehabilitation is generally limited to ten percent of the total pavement area unless a cost‑effective justification can be demonstrated, making it a targeted preservation activity. Because it restores effective joint seals and load transfer, the treatment extends the expected service life of the concrete pavement, especially when applied to sections that are just beginning to exhibit minor joint distress. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Joint rehabilitation most often focuses on repairing failed transverse joints while also cleaning and resealing longitudinal and pavement‑shoulder joints to preserve overall joint integrity. The principal distresses that can recur after treatment include loss of load transfer, joint crushing or deterioration, faulting, and the development of shrinkage cracks when high‑early‑strength concrete is used. In addition, tie‑bars or bolts may shift in partially set patch material, especially if traffic loads the adjacent lane before the repair has hardened, and sealants can become compromised by water infiltration, chlorides, or incompressible debris. These failures are largely driven by inadequate dowel anchoring and bonding, selection of unsuitable high‑early‑strength mixes, insufficient tie‑bar design for longer patches, and premature traffic exposure on fresh concrete. External conditions such as an unstable subgrade, ineffective drainage, and delayed resealing—allowing joints to progress beyond minor distress— further exacerbate faulting and loss of load transfer, often necessitating more extensive repairs if left unattended. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–90)

Monitoring and follow-up

After joint rehabilitation is completed, the repaired joints must be verified through post‑treatment testing. The standard practice is to conduct Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing to confirm that all failed joints have been successfully repaired and that the pavement meets the required structural criteria. [1]

[1] — Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Additional maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation is warranted when the joints that have been treated begin to show any early distress. The appearance of spalls, loss of seal integrity, faulting between adjacent slabs, reduced load‑transfer capability, or visible cracking in the concrete are clear indicators even if overall pavement serviceability still appears satisfactory. An inspection that identifies failed or faulted joints together with a Falling Weight Deflectometer test that reveals a reduction in structural capacity within the joint zone confirms the need for further action. Moreover, when the amount of concrete patching required to address the distress approaches or exceeds ten percent of the total pavement surface, the area should be slated for additional rehabilitation unless a cost‑effectiveness analysis demonstrates otherwise. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 89–90), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)
