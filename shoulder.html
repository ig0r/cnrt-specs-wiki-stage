Shoulder

Part of: Pavement Structure is-subtopic-of
Shoulder is a topic under Pavement Structure.
shouldershoulders
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-29

A shoulder is the paved edge of a roadway that extends laterally from the traveled way to provide additional structural support and functional space. It reduces edge stresses in the pavement, serves as an emergency stopping area, accommodates bicycles and vehicle turning movements, and maintains drainage by extending a feather‑pass grind and preserving cross‑slope continuity. Constructed as full‑depth concrete on rigid pavements or as widened asphalt types on flexible pavements, shoulders feature joint spacing limited to twenty feet, slab thicknesses from four to eighteen inches, and a safety edge formed at 26–40° to the extended cross‑slope, all to satisfy functional, structural, durability, safety and serviceability objectives [1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Shoulder” also include the following subtopics: Asphalt Shoulder, Concrete Shoulder, Lateral Support, and Shoulder Types.

Asphalt Shoulder and Concrete Shoulder serve as the primary structural implementations that provide lateral support by transferring edge loads into the main pavement slab. [1][2] Specialized types function within the broader category of Shoulder Types, which define the specific configurations required for flexible or rigid roadways based on traffic volume and geometric constraints. [1] The effectiveness of this side restraint is quantified through Lateral Support parameters, ensuring the shoulder maintains structural integrity under heavy truck loads and turning movements. [1]

Purpose and design objectives

The shoulder is incorporated into pavement designs to supply lateral structural support at the edge of the traveled way, reducing edge stresses that would otherwise cause cracking, rutting and other deterioration of the main carriageway. By providing a minimum three‑foot Type 6 shoulder width or a two‑foot widening into the pavement, the design ensures sufficient transverse capacity to accommodate occasional heavy‑truck loads when the Design Hourly Volume exceeds 150 and to support bicycles or turning movements at intersections. For new rigid‑pavement projects full‑depth concrete shoulders are required, with joint spacing limited to 20 ft, while flexible pavements receive a 2‑ft widening plus an appropriate shoulder type where drainage problems are anticipated. The shoulder also serves as a low‑maintenance area for emergency stopping and can function as a temporary lane on high‑speed facilities or as a paved surface for stopped vehicles. In addition to structural functions, the shoulder addresses drainage performance by extending a feather pass at least two feet onto it, directing water away from the travel lane and preventing ponding that could impair vehicle stability. The design limits any slope deviation on the shoulder to no more than one‑eighth inch over a twelve‑foot length, preserving cross‑slope continuity and avoiding depressions that would compromise control, especially when construction activities are suspended. When work is temporarily halted, the shoulder continues to provide a temporary drainage path, maintaining the roadway in satisfactory condition throughout the suspension period. Rehabilitation of existing concrete roads rebuilds or widens shoulders to retain required lateral width, maintain bridge clearance, and preserve slab deflection control, with options such as Roller Compacted Concrete offering high compressive strength for low‑volume rural interstates where a dedicated incident management lane is not justified. [1][2]

Shoulders are required to meet a comprehensive set of functional, structural, durability, safety and serviceability objectives that parallel those of the adjacent traveled way. [1][2] Functionally the shoulder must provide an emergency stopping area, accommodate bicycles where anticipated with a smooth‑surface type such as 1‑F, 1‑S or 1‑SP, and support vehicle turning movements by extending pavement at intersections. [1][2] It must also ensure continuous drainage by extending a feather‑pass grind at least two feet onto the shoulder for a minimum length of one hundred feet, preserving the roadway cross‑slope and limiting any depression or slope misalignment to no more than one‑eighth inch over any twelve‑foot segment. [2] The functional width of the shoulder shall preserve the existing lane width, incorporate a typical two‑foot widening of the travel lane into the shoulder for flexible pavements, and satisfy bridge clearance requirements. [1] A Safety Edge constructed from the same asphalt mix or specified paved‑shoulder material shall be provided with a profile angle between 26 and 40 degrees measured from the extended cross‑slope, automatically transitioning at intersections, driveways and guide‑rail sections. [2] Structurally the shoulder must supply lateral support that resists edge loads, thereby reducing edge stresses in the pavement. [1] Full‑depth plain cement concrete or roller‑compacted concrete shoulders are required on Interstate and other limited‑access freeways, while where drainage problems, heavy truck volumes or flexible pavements exist appropriate Type 2, Type 3 or suitable Type 1 variants may be selected. [1] When roller‑compacted concrete is used its thickness shall be designed in accordance with PennDOT procedures to fall between four and eighteen inches, achieving a compressive strength of approximately 4,000 to 10,000 psi and capable of supporting traffic volumes up to an average daily traffic of 2,000 vehicles at speeds of 55 mph or less. [1] For rigid‑pavement shoulders the subgrade is first prepared to the elevation of the proposed base course by removing surplus material and scarifying no more than four inches where required, after which a minimum compacted layer of No. 2A aggregate three inches thick is placed directly on the subgrade followed by a four‑inch compacted No. OGS layer, each compacted to a non‑movement condition and within one‑half inch of the design elevation. [2] All transverse and longitudinal contraction joints shall be accurately marked, sawed, cured and protected in accordance with standard drawings, with control joints aligned with those of the adjacent mainline pavement and longitudinal joints cleaned and sealed to maintain continuity. [1][2] Durability is achieved by matching the shoulder material to the existing pavement type—concrete shoulders where the existing shoulder is concrete—and by providing continuous base drainage and a properly prepared subgrade/subbase capable of sustaining compaction. [1][2] RCC shoulders must be placed on a prepared subgrade/subbase, with all longitudinal and pavement‑shoulder joints cleaned and sealed, and control joints aligned with those of the adjacent mainline pavement. [1] Concrete edge slump shall be controlled so that the deviation between lane and shoulder does not exceed one‑quarter inch, and longitudinal joint finishing tolerances shall be within a quarter‑inch measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge. [2] Compaction is considered satisfactory when the material will not move under the compaction equipment; any exposed No. 2A subbase left longer than sixty days or used as a haul road must be retested and corrected if irregularities exceed one‑half inch. [2] Safety objectives include provision of bicycle‑tolerable rumble strips and edge‑line rumble strips, as well as transverse texture over the full shoulder width using rectangular grooves three‑thirty‑seconds to three‑sixteenths of an inch wide and one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths of an inch deep to deliver the required skid resistance. [1][2] When the exposed shoulder serves speeds of 35 mph or higher a diamond‑ground textured surface shall be provided, and for all shoulders an open‑graded texture suitable for low‑speed travel is required. [1] Guide‑rail and drainage structures shall be adjusted or replaced as needed to maintain safety, and the Safety Edge angle of 26–40 degrees further reduces the risk of vehicle run‑off. [1][2] Serviceability is ensured by maintaining the designed cross‑slope in accordance with Publication 13 of the Design Manual Part 2, preserving effective shoulder width as shown on design charts, and keeping elevations within the specified tolerances so that depressions or misalignments greater than one‑eighth inch over twelve feet do not occur. [1][2] Proper drainage is sustained by the feather‑pass extension and continuous base drainage, while joint integrity is protected through cleaning, sealing and alignment of longitudinal and transverse joints throughout the design life. [1][2] Together these functional, structural, durability, safety and serviceability criteria ensure that shoulders perform their intended roles while protecting pavement condition and roadway users over the design life.

Scope and applicability

Shoulders are suitable for both flexible (asphalt‑concrete) and rigid (Portland‑cement‑concrete) pavements because they provide lateral support that reduces edge stresses from edge loads. [1] For new construction or reconstruction with a rigid pavement, full‑depth concrete shoulders are required, with Interstates and other limited‑access freeways using full‑depth sections while arterials, collectors and local roads employ Type 2 concrete shoulders; joint spacing shall not exceed twenty feet. [1] When the pavement is flexible, the roadway is widened at least two feet into the shoulder area and the remaining width is built with a specified shoulder type, using the same asphalt mixes as the traveled way—including conventional and reclaimed‑asphalt‑paving‑stone blends such as 4.75 mm NMAS—provided that density requirements are met by cores or acceptable rolling patterns for thin layers. [1][2] Type 1, 1‑F, 1‑S or 1‑SP shoulders are mandated on all Interstates, other limited‑access freeways and arterials, on routes with serious drainage problems, on two‑lane roads where the design hourly truck volume exceeds one hundred fifty, or where turning movements or tight curves require shoulder accommodation; Type 3 or a surface material identical to the pavement may be used on collectors and local roads. [1] For flexible pavements on collectors and local roads, shoulders may consist of the same surface material as the main pavement or use Type 1 variants, and during widening the existing structure may be extended into the shoulder by one foot when average daily traffic is five thousand vehicles or less (or ADT ≤ 500) and by two feet when traffic exceeds those thresholds. [1] In rehabilitation projects for rigid pavements, concrete shoulders may be provided to supply lateral support or a low‑maintenance edge, with the condition that joint spacing does not exceed twenty feet and that new concrete matches existing shoulder type unless the existing shoulder is concrete, in which case a concrete shoulder is required; all longitudinal joints are cleaned and sealed. [1] Where excavation or scarifying of an existing shoulder on Interstates, limited‑access freeways or arterials is necessary, Type 6 (or its 6‑F, 6‑S, 6‑SP variants) shoulders may be installed with a minimum width of three feet when only the first few feet adjacent to the pavement are distressed; if work is limited to cleaning and patching, a Type 7 shoulder is specified. [1] Roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) may serve as an alternate concrete shoulder adjacent to reinforced or plain cement‑concrete pavements when average daily traffic does not exceed two thousand vehicles and the speed limit is fifty‑five miles per hour or lower; RCC mixes use standard cement, aggregate and water with a low water‑to‑cement ratio and dense gradation, require no forms or reinforcing steel, and must have control joints aligned with those of the adjacent concrete pavement. [1][2] For low‑volume rural Interstates and expressways carrying two‑way ADT less than ten thousand vehicles where an incident‑management lane is not needed, a widened concrete slab not exceeding fourteen feet may be provided together with a partial asphalt or RCC right shoulder; the slab is striped at twelve feet and the outside lane may be thirteen feet wide when tied concrete shoulders are used. [1] During new construction or replacement where a full‑width shoulder is required, the shoulder can be built up to at least the elevation of each base‑course layer either before that layer is placed or concurrently with its compaction, using the same coarse and fine aggregate materials and compaction methods as specified for the base course; for rigid pavements this includes a compacted No. 2A aggregate layer of minimum three inches followed by a No. OGS aggregate layer of minimum four inches. [2] Plain cement‑concrete shoulders constructed with Class AA cement concrete, together with standard joint‑sealing material, curing covers or compounds, admixtures, dowels, expansion‑joint filler and longitudinal joint material, satisfy the requirement for a durable load‑bearing edge; RCC may be used as an alternative under the same incidental construction provisions. [2] Shoulders also serve miscellaneous applications such as patching, road widening, driveway adjustments and parking‑lot extensions, and may be placed in small quantities not exceeding five hundred tons in a continuous operation or as shallow layers of one inch thickness or 110 lb/ft², with acceptance based on non‑movement or optimum rolling when density is not critical. [2] When work is temporarily suspended for an extended period, a shoulder may be installed as a temporary drainage element to protect the roadway and maintain traffic flow, provided that appropriate storage and protection of materials are observed. [2]

When the anticipated conditions on a roadway threaten the performance of a conventional shoulder, designers turn to alternative treatments. Serious drainage problems, design hourly truck volumes exceeding about 150 vehicles, and geometric constraints such as ramp radii or horizontal curves sharper than roughly 12°‑30′ prompt extension of the main pavement into the shoulder or selection of higher‑type shoulders (e.g., Type 1, 1‑F, 1‑S) rather than a standard shoulder [1]. Traffic volumes also drive the decision: if average daily traffic is greater than five thousand vehicles or the adjusted truck‑bus‑recreational vehicle count exceeds five hundred, the widening required for rehabilitation must be carried into the shoulder by one to two feet, making a conventional shoulder unsuitable; conversely, on roads with ADT of five thousand or fewer and ADTT of five hundred or less, a lower‑type shoulder such as Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S, 6‑SP, a recycled shoulder, or a narrow three‑foot concrete shoulder may be provided when only the first few feet adjacent to the travel lane are distressed [1]. For concrete pavements, roller‑compacted concrete is limited to roadways with ADT of two thousand or less and posted speeds of fifty‑five miles per hour or lower; if the speed limit exceeds this value, or if ride quality will be evaluated using the International Roughness Index, RCC should be avoided because its surface texture may not meet IRI requirements, and any exposed RCC on roads with speed limits of thirty‑five miles per hour or higher requires diamond grinding—situations where an alternative shoulder construction is preferred [1]. The widened concrete slab option that incorporates an asphalt or RCC shoulder is constrained to a maximum overall width of fourteen feet and is appropriate only for low‑volume rural interstates and expressways with two‑way ADT under ten thousand and without a dedicated incident‑management lane; projects exceeding these limits must adopt a conventional full‑width concrete design or a tied concrete shoulder [1]. Finally, when a curb, sidewalk, or guide‑rail face is located directly at the pavement edge, the safety‑edge treatment normally applied to shoulders is omitted and an alternative edge condition is required, making another design approach preferable to a shoulder safety edge [2].

Design inputs and requirements

Shoulder design calls for information on the pavement material—rigid pavements require full‑depth concrete shoulders while flexible pavements are widened two feet into the shoulder and may use other specified types. The road classification (Interstate, arterial, collector or local) and anticipated traffic characteristics such as truck volumes exceeding a Design Hourly Volume of 150 and expected shoulder usage at tight‑radius ramps or turning movements must be known. Site conditions that could affect drainage are also required, since serious drainage problems trigger the use of higher‑type paved shoulders. The source does not specify any separate environmental, geotechnical, or existing‑condition data beyond these functional inputs. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 60–61)

The design of road shoulders is governed by a comprehensive set of Penn DOT publications, specifications, standard drawings and contract requirements that together define the geometric, structural and performance criteria for both rigid and flexible pavements [1][2]. All new construction or reconstruction projects must satisfy the cross‑slope limits prescribed in Publication 13 Design Manual Part 2, with any inability to meet those limits requiring a design exception under Publication 10X Appendix P [1]. Geometric definitions and the selection of shoulder families such as 1‑F, 1‑S, 1‑SP, 6‑F, 6‑S and 6‑SP are set out in Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards (RC‑25M) [1]. For projects with a rigid pavement structure a Portland Cement Concrete shoulder is mandatory, while flexible‑pavement projects must be widened an additional two feet into the shoulder except on inside shoulders of Interstates and other limited‑access freeways where full‑depth concrete shoulders are required [1][2]. Concrete shoulders are further classified by traffic and drainage conditions: Type 2 concrete shoulders are used on arterials, collectors and local roads, whereas Type 1 (or its variants) is specified when serious drainage problems exist, the design hourly truck volume exceeds 150, or turning or bicycle use is anticipated [1]. Joint spacing for concrete shoulders may not exceed twenty feet, and for roller‑compacted concrete shoulders the joint layout must align with the adjacent mainline concrete and follow a spacing of fifteen to twenty feet for slabs under eight inches thick or a spacing equal to 2.5 times the pavement thickness for thicker sections as required by Publication 408 Specification Section 658 [1][2]. RCC shoulders are permitted only on roadways with an average daily traffic of two thousand vehicles or less and a posted speed limit of fifty‑five miles per hour or lower, and if the exposed surface will serve speeds of thirty‑five miles per hour or higher it must be diamond‑ground [1]. Structural thickness for concrete shoulders ranges from four to eighteen inches and is determined using the standard Penn DOT concrete pavement design procedures in Chapter 8, with a maximum slab width of fourteen feet and lane striping required at twelve feet; tied concrete shoulders may employ a thirteen‑foot outside lane striped at twelve feet on low‑volume rural Interstates and expressways whose two‑way average daily traffic is under ten thousand vehicles [1]. When widening a roadway the structural layer must be extended into the shoulder by two feet if the current average daily traffic exceeds five thousand (or the adjusted truck‑vehicle traffic exceeds five hundred); otherwise a one‑foot extension is allowed, and the entire shoulder may be paved to the same design as the traveled way [1]. Minimum base depths are specified as five inches on arterials and four inches on collectors and local roads, with recycled asphalt permissible for base or surface courses provided those depth requirements are met; base‑course construction follows Section 310.3(b)(1) and is reinforced by Section 350.3, requiring shoulders to be built up and compacted across their full width to at least the elevation of each base‑course layer either before placement or simultaneously with each layer’s compaction [2]. Concrete shoulder construction must comply with Section 501.3 for plain cement‑concrete using Class AA cement concrete per Section 704, and roller‑compacted concrete shoulders are governed by Sections 502.3/502.2, including requirements for joint sealing, curing, dowels, expansion filler and longitudinal joint material [2]. Slip‑form paving of concrete shoulders demands that all transverse and longitudinal contraction joints at the pavement‑shoulder interface be accurately marked, sawed with guided equipment equipped with blade guards and water cooling, and cured or protected as prescribed; edge slumping where concrete meets the shoulder may not exceed one‑quarter inch measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge, and longitudinal joints must meet the same tolerance [2]. Transverse texture grooves on shoulders must be between three‑thirty‑seconds and three‑sixteenths of an inch wide and from one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths of an inch deep across the full shoulder width [2]. For flexible pavements, design and repair are controlled by Section 450.3; when a pothole extends into a shoulder excavation is limited to the roadway edge, and if the shoulder has an asphalt base it is filled with an asphalt wearing course compacted in the same manner as the main carriageway, otherwise suitable fill from the adjacent pavement is used, with all repaired edges sealed using emulsified asphalt, Class TACK, NTT or the PG binder employed in the pavement (e.g., PG 64S‑22) [2]. A safety edge is required on paved shoulders, constructed of the same material as the main carriageway with an extruded wedge profile forming a 26‑to‑40° angle measured from the extended cross‑slope, and it may not be placed on organic material or where a curb, sidewalk or guide rail directly overlies the edge [2]. Compaction methods are prescribed in Section 108.05(c) (items 3.a, 3.b, 3.e or 4), and a non‑movement condition must be achieved as defined in Section 206.3(b); for rigid pavements the subbase consists of a minimum three‑inch compacted layer of No. 2A aggregate over the prepared subgrade topped by at least four inches of No. OGS aggregate, each layer meeting surface‑tolerance limits and tested to within one‑half inch of design thickness, with traffic prohibited on OGS subbase and exposure exceeding sixty days or winter shutdowns triggering retesting and protective overlay before the paving season ends [2]. Acceptance criteria depend on shoulder density: critical shoulders are accepted by core testing in accordance with Section 413.3(j)4.a using approved nuclear (PTM No. 402) or electrical‑impedance (PTM No. 403) gauges calibrated per ASTM D2950, while non‑critical shoulders may be accepted on the basis of a non‑movement condition or an optimum‑rolling pattern observed by the Inspector‑in‑Charge with the contractor’s certified asphalt field technician [2]. All shoulder work must conform to the contract documents and standard drawings for PCC and RCC shoulders, and any damage incurred during transport or placement must be repaired at no cost to the department; continuous pavement‑base drainage must be maintained, guide‑rail and drainage structures adjusted or replaced as needed, required shoulder widths preserved, and bridge clearance constraints respected throughout construction [1][2].

Design methods and criteria

The guides require that any new construction or reconstruction project employing a rigid‑pavement structure provide Portland Cement Concrete shoulders, the thickness of which is determined by the PennDOT concrete pavement design procedures that relate traffic loading and material strength to slab depth [1]. The concrete mix for these shoulders is limited to a water‑cement ratio of approximately 0.30 to 0.40 in order to achieve compressive strengths ranging from about 4,000 to 10,000 psi [1]. For sections thinner than eight inches the control joints are spaced at fifteen to twenty feet, while for thicker slabs the spacing is calculated as the slab thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet, and under no circumstances may joint spacing exceed twenty feet [1]. Roller‑Compacted Concrete shoulders are permitted only where the average daily traffic does not exceed two thousand vehicles and the posted speed limit is fifty‑five miles per hour or less [1]. When the existing pavement is flexible, the design widens the traveled way by two feet into the shoulder and the remaining width is constructed according to a designated shoulder type selected from full‑depth concrete (Type 2) or various Type 1 configurations based on road classification, anticipated drainage problems, traffic volumes that exceed a Design Hourly Volume of 150, and specific intersection or curve conditions [1]. The effective shoulder width is taken from the design criteria charts provided in the guides, and additional guidance for rumble strips and bicycle accommodations is incorporated as required [1]. The 3R guidelines reinforce the concrete joint spacing limit of twenty feet and prescribe shoulder types according to roadway class and existing conditions, requiring Type 6 (or its variants) shoulders on interstates, limited‑access freeways, and arterials where excavation or scarifying of the existing shoulder is necessary, with a minimum three‑foot width applied when only the near edge of a high‑type paved shoulder shows distress [1]. Type 7 shoulders are specified for situations in which only cleaning and patching are needed, and when widening an existing pavement the guides direct that if average daily traffic exceeds five thousand vehicles or adjusted truck traffic (ADT multiplied by the percentage of trucks, buses, and recreational vehicles) exceeds five hundred, the new structure is extended two feet into the shoulder; otherwise a one‑foot extension is used, although districts may elect to pave the entire shoulder to match the widened section [1]. The guides also allow recycled asphalt material to be incorporated in the base or surface courses of Type 6 and Type 7 shoulders, with minimum recycled base depths of five inches on arterials and four inches on collectors and local roads [1]. A concrete shoulder may be designed as a widened slab up to fourteen feet wide and striped at twelve‑foot intervals, with any remaining width paired with a Type 1 asphalt or RCC right shoulder for low‑volume rural interstates and expressways where the two‑way ADT is below ten thousand vehicles, while tied concrete shoulders can provide a thirteen‑foot outside lane also striped at twelve feet to limit slab deflection as the passing lane retains conventional twelve‑foot lanes [1]. For such widened lanes the appropriate Load Transfer Coefficient (J) from Table 8.2 for “widened lane with asphalt or RCC shoulders” is applied in the design calculations [1]. The construction sequence for shoulders may involve building up and compacting the shoulder across its full width to at least the elevation of each base‑course layer before that layer is placed, or rolling the shoulder concurrently with compaction of successive base‑course layers so that the edge matches the vertical profile of the pavement [2]. Following subgrade preparation a well‑moistened aggregate subbase is placed in courses, and for both rigid and flexible shoulders a minimum compacted depth of three inches of No. 2A aggregate is laid directly on the subgrade followed by at least four inches of No. OGS aggregate, with no single layer exceeding eight inches unless granulated slag is used, in which case the limit is reduced to four inches [2]. Compaction proceeds from the sides toward the centre and must achieve a non‑movement condition as specified in the guidelines [2]. When concrete shoulders are required the concrete is placed and struck off without damaging the prepared surface, travel on that surface being restricted to the immediate area ahead of the paver; transverse and longitudinal contraction joints are accurately marked with tacks or approved methods, sawed using guided equipment equipped with blade guards, water‑cooling and depth control, then cured and protected in accordance with the relevant sections [2]. The acceptance criteria for shoulder density depend on whether the section is classified as critical: non‑critical shoulders may be accepted by demonstrating non‑movement or by an optimum‑rolling pattern measured with approved nuclear or electrical impedance gauges such as Troxler 3411B, Troxler 4640B, Campbell MC‑2, Seaman MC‑2, TransTech PQI 300/301, or the Troxler PaveTracker™, whereas critical shoulders require pavement cores that are tested for bulk specific gravity and density with acceptance based on percent‑theoretical‑density calculations outlined in the guidelines [2]. Surface finishing includes a feather‑pass diamond grinding operation that extends at least two feet from the pavement edge into the shoulder for a minimum length of one hundred feet, preserving the existing cross‑slope and limiting any depression or slope misalignment to no more than one‑eighth inch over a twelve‑foot straightedge, thereby ensuring proper drainage and meeting NGCS texture requirements [2]. A safety edge may be installed on the outer edge of asphalt shoulders using the same asphalt material as the roadway; a paver‑mounted device extrudes a wedge‑shaped profile whose final angle, measured from the extended pavement cross‑slope, must lie between twenty‑six and forty degrees, with daily measurements taken after compaction and paving halted until compliance is restored [2]. Potholes or abrupt depressions in the shoulder are repaired using manual asphalt‑patching procedures identical to those used for the traveled way: for depressions three inches deep or less a minimum one‑square‑foot area is cut, sound material excavated, tack coat applied to the bottom and vertical faces, and an asphalt wearing course placed and compacted with a steel‑wheeled roller until the surface matches surrounding grade; if an asphalt base already exists only the wearing course is placed [2]. For deeper depressions exceeding three inches after the tack coat cures an asphalt binder course is laid in lifts not exceeding three inches uncompacted depth, each lift being compacted with a mechanical tamper before the top wearing layer is applied, and edges are sealed with appropriate emulsified or tack asphalt, while excavation never extends beyond the roadway edge [2].

Structural section and dimensions

The shoulder may be provided as a concrete pavement, an asphalt pavement, or a recycled‑material base course, and each option carries its own thickness and joint requirements. When the shoulder is concrete, control joints may not exceed twenty feet in spacing; if the concrete slab is less than eight inches thick the joints are placed between fifteen and twenty feet apart, while a slab eight inches or thicker requires joint spacing equal to 2.5 times the slab thickness expressed in feet. Wearing‑course thickness for concrete shoulders can range from four inches up to eighteen inches. Minimum overall shoulder width is three feet for Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or 6‑SP configurations where only the first few feet adjacent to the main carriageway are distressed. Widened lane provisions allow a concrete shoulder extension of one to two feet accompanied by an asphalt shoulder, and full‑depth asphalt shoulders are also permitted as an alternative pavement type. If average daily traffic exceeds five thousand vehicles or the annual average truck traffic is greater than five hundred, any pavement widening must be continued into the shoulder by two feet; where traffic volumes are lower the required extension is one foot. Recycled‑material base courses placed on the shoulder must have a minimum depth of five inches on arterial facilities and four inches on collector or local roads. The shoulder is built up and compacted across its entire width so that its finished surface matches or exceeds the elevation of each underlying base‑course layer, and this buildup may be performed before placement of the base course or concurrently with each compaction pass. Beneath the shoulder a subbase consisting of a No. 2A aggregate course must be compacted to at least three inches on the prepared subgrade, and over that a No. OGS aggregate course is required to be compacted to a minimum of four inches; these thicknesses apply to both rigid and flexible pavement designs. The guides do not prescribe any separate treated‑soil thickness for the shoulder. Where a concrete slab forms the shoulder, its thickness may be selected between four and eighteen inches, consistent with the wearing‑course range. No explicit edge‑support or reinforcement dimensions are provided for shoulders in either source. [1][2]

Materials and mixture design

The shoulder material is selected according to the type of main pavement, with specifications differing for rigid and flexible structures [1][2]. When the primary pavement is a rigid system, the adjacent shoulder shall be constructed of Portland‑cement concrete, and full‑depth concrete shoulders are required on Interstate and other limited‑access freeways [1]. Concrete shoulders may be conventional or roller‑compacted; the latter requires a mix of Portland cement, a dense aggregate gradation whose maximum particle size is less than one inch, water, and only limited air‑entraining agents and admixtures, with a water‑to‑cement ratio typically about 0.40 but capable of being reduced to as low as 0.30, producing a dry, no‑slump consistency comparable to damp gravel and targeting compressive strengths in the range of four thousand to ten thousand pounds per square inch, while control joints shall be spaced no more than twenty feet apart [1][2]. For flexible pavements the design widens the traveled way two feet into the shoulder before constructing the designated shoulder type, although no additional material‑property requirements are imposed for that widened portion [1]. When an asphalt‑type shoulder is specified, reclaimed asphalt concrete may be employed either as a recycled base course for Type 6, 6‑F, or 6‑S shoulders or as an asphalt surface course for Type 7 shoulders on any roadway, and cold‑mixed recycled bases must achieve minimum thicknesses of five inches on arterial roads and four inches on collector and local streets [1]. Asphalt shoulder repairs require the placement of an asphalt wearing course as the surface layer; if the defect depth exceeds three inches a binder course is also required, placed in lifts not greater than three inches uncompacted, and all patches are to be primed with a tack coat of emulsified asphalt, Class TACK or NTT, or the same performance‑grade binder used in the main pavement such as PG 64S‑22 [2]. Where an existing shoulder already contains an asphalt base, the same wearing‑course material is used, while non‑asphalt shoulders are filled with suitable excavated material compacted to a stable condition [2]. New construction or reconstruction may involve removal of surplus material, scarification of the subgrade to the required depth, and placement of an aggregate base‑course material when existing graded material is insufficient; such base course is placed and compacted in accordance with standard specifications and used to backfill excavated shoulder areas to the proposed base elevation [2]. The completed asphalt shoulder then receives an asphalt prime coat followed by an asphalt surface treatment as prescribed [2]. Concrete shoulders are specified as plain cement concrete of Class AA, accompanied by appropriate joint‑sealing material, curing compounds, admixtures, and a compatible base course; joint reinforcement may consist of deformed or plain bar dowels, premolded expansion‑joint filler, and longitudinal joint material to achieve the required structural performance, with roller‑compacted concrete shoulders conforming to the applicable criteria [2][1]. The subbase for both rigid and flexible pavement shoulders consists of a No. 2A aggregate layer placed directly on the prepared subgrade to a minimum compacted depth of three inches, overlain by a No. OGS aggregate layer compacted to at least four inches; aggregates must retain sufficient moisture to prevent segregation during stockpiling, hauling, and placement and to limit additional water needed for compaction, and the subbase should remain well‑moistened from placement through compaction [2].

Structural details and interfaces

Shoulders may be constructed of cast‑in‑place concrete, roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) or asphalt, and their structural detailing follows the same principles applied to the main carriageway while incorporating specific joint and reinforcement provisions for each pavement type. For conventional cast‑in‑place concrete shoulders the control joints must be placed at intervals not exceeding twenty feet and aligned with the joint pattern of the adjacent mainline pavement [1]. When the shoulder is built as RCC the same alignment rule applies, but the spacing follows standard RCC criteria: slabs thinner than eight inches require joints spaced between fifteen and twenty feet, while for thicker slabs the spacing equals the slab thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet [1]. Neither concrete nor RCC shoulders are required to contain steel reinforcement, dowel bars, or other load‑transfer devices, and RCC shoulders can be placed without forms [1]. One guide, however, specifies that plain cement‑concrete shoulders must provide both transverse and longitudinal contraction joints, with deformed and plain‑bar dowels installed in accordance with Section 709.1(a) to achieve load transfer across these joints, together with premolded expansion‑joint filler, longitudinal joint material, and an appropriate sealing material selected from the options listed in Section 705.4 [2]. The centreline of each transverse joint is to be precisely marked with tacks or an approved method before sawing, and the cuts are made using guided equipment equipped with a blade guard, water‑cooling system, and cut‑depth control; after cutting the joints are cured and protected as required by Sections 501.3(i) and (j) [2]. When a widened concrete lane is paired with an asphalt or RCC shoulder, a load‑transfer coefficient J, as given in Table 8.2, must be used to represent the interaction between the widened slab (maximum fourteen feet wide, striped at twelve feet) and the adjacent shoulder type [1]. For asphalt‑surfaced shoulders, pothole repairs that extend into the shoulder are performed with the same wearing‑course material as the roadway and compacted in an identical manner; if the shoulder lacks an asphalt base a suitable fill is placed, compacted, and the perimeter of the repair sealed with emulsified asphalt, tack coat, NTT sealant or the PG binder used for the pavement, without requiring additional load‑transfer devices beyond this edge sealing [2]. The safety edge constitutes the structural edge treatment of an asphalt shoulder; it is placed with the same asphalt mix used for the roadway (or the paved shoulder when specified) by a paver‑mounted device that extrudes a wedge‑shaped profile whose slope must fall between 26° and 40° measured from the extended pavement cross‑slope, a geometry verified each day after final compaction, and it provides an automatic transition into adjoining features such as intersections, driveways, guide‑rail sections or other obstructions without the need for extra joints or reinforcement [2]. Rehabilitation work on concrete or RCC shoulders requires cleaning and sealing all longitudinal joints as well as pavement‑shoulder joints, but does not introduce additional reinforcement or special transition connections [1]. Consequently, the structural detailing of a shoulder consists of appropriately spaced control joints that match the mainline pattern, optional application of a load‑transfer coefficient for widened‑lane configurations, provision of transverse and longitudinal contraction joints with dowels where required by some guides, a safety edge for asphalt shoulders, routine joint sealing, and no dedicated reinforcement or forms beyond those specified.

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

The shoulder incorporates a continuous pavement‑base drain that conveys moisture away from the traveled way, providing reliable water removal under varying climate and environmental conditions [1]. In addition, subgrade drains are installed beneath the subbase after the subgrade is finished but before any subbase material is placed, with their length sized to adequately drain the entire wet area of the shoulder [2]. The trench that houses these drains is formed with a bottom slope of at least one‑quarter inch per foot across both pavement and shoulder zones, directing runoff toward the drainage system [2]. Paved‑shoulder classifications such as Type 6 or Type 7 are selected to supply appropriate structural capacity while supporting the required drainage performance [1]. After subbase placement a conventional diamond‑grinding feather pass is applied at least two feet onto the adjacent shoulder and extended for a minimum of one hundred feet, preserving the existing cross‑slope and limiting any depression or misalignment to no more than one‑eighth inch over a twelve‑foot straightedge so that water is directed off the traveled way onto the shoulder without ponding [2]. All longitudinal joints and the interfaces between pavement and shoulder are cleaned and sealed during construction to protect the structure from moisture infiltration [1]. Subbase material on the shoulder may not be placed on soft, muddy, or frozen ground; the aggregate mix must contain sufficient inherent moisture to remain cohesive during handling and to minimize additional water needed for compaction, and the subbase is kept well‑moistened throughout placement and compaction [2]. If exposure to weather exceeds sixty calendar days—or if the subbase remains uncovered during a winter shutdown—the subbase must be retested for depth and surface irregularities, repaired as required, and proof‑rolled before any pavement layer is installed; unstable or moisture‑damaged sections are scarified, blended with new material, recompacted, and re‑verified to meet prescribed tolerances [2]. Where existing guide‑rail or drainage structures intersect the shoulder, they are inspected and, if inadequate for prevailing environmental loads, adjusted or replaced to maintain effective water management [1]. Concrete shoulders are used when the existing shoulder material is concrete; otherwise the new shoulder matches the existing type, preserving consistent performance across differing moisture and temperature regimes [1].

Verification and expected performance

The completed shoulder is inspected by confirming that every construction step called for in the specifications has been carried out to the required depths and elevations, that all excess material has been removed, and that scarifying does not exceed four inches. The shaped surface and any low spots are filled with graded or aggregate‑base material and compacted to the bottom of the proposed base course until the material exhibits a non‑movement condition under the rollers as defined in Section 108.05. The backfilled shoulder must achieve the prescribed aggregate‑base depth, receive an asphalt prime coat per Section 461.3 and the surface treatment required by Section 480.3, thereby satisfying performance and constructability criteria. Density compliance is verified using a dual approach. When density is non‑critical, the Inspector‑in‑Charge may approve the shoulder by applying the non‑movement or optimum‑rolling pattern method used for other low‑depth courses. When density is critical, pavement cores are extracted and tested for bulk specific gravity and density in accordance with Section 413.3(j)4.a, with calibrated gauges and documentation under inspector supervision, ensuring contractual density specifications are met. The Safety Edge on the shoulder must be constructed with the same asphalt mix as the main roadway and its final angle measured from the extended pavement cross‑slope must fall between 26° and 40°. The mat on the shoulder is compacted according to project specifications, and mixture and density lot acceptance samples are taken to verify composition, Gmm verification, and core density tolerances; any deviation triggers corrective action. A follow‑up test hole is dug to the full subbase depth at each density test location; the measured depth must be within ½ inch of the design elevation. If the depth deviates by more than this amount, the subbase is deemed defective and must be scarified, blended with additional material, and recompacted. Each aggregate layer—minimum three inches for No. 2A and four inches for No. OGS—is compacted to a non‑movement condition with overlapping passes from the sides toward the center, and surface tolerances are checked against the limits in Section 210.3(c). Damage to underlying geotextile or deviations from specified layer thicknesses are corrected before pavement placement. Finally, the shoulder is subjected to the surface‑tolerance test prescribed in Section 501.3(o) for “other than mainline pavement.” Satisfying those tolerances confirms that the shoulder complies with design specifications, constructability standards, performance expectations, and overall project requirements. The design guidance specifies that a roller‑compacted concrete shoulder should achieve an approximate twenty‑year service life, with its ability to resist cracking and other forms of distress derived from the high compressive strength of the mix—typically in the range of four thousand to ten thousand pounds per square inch—and from proper joint spacing. The durability of such a concrete shoulder is confirmed through laboratory compressive‑strength tests on core samples and by monitoring joint performance over time, which together serve as the primary indicators of distress resistance. Because RCC surfaces tend to be rougher than conventional Portland cement concrete or asphalt pavements, the ride‑quality expectation for these shoulders is evaluated with the International Roughness Index, and projects that rely on IRI‑based payment incentives generally avoid specifying RCC shoulders. For paved shoulders constructed of asphaltic material, performance is judged by the ability to retain the prescribed safety‑edge geometry throughout service life; the edge must be formed as a wedge whose slope lies between 26 degrees and 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope, and this angle is verified at the start of each paving day after final compaction, with any deviation prompting corrective action before work resumes. The same asphaltic shoulders must also meet density acceptance criteria; compacted cores are taken and tested in accordance with PTM No. 715 (or, when required, PTM No. 716) to determine percent compaction, providing a quantitative measure of the shoulder’s structural quality. Additional material‑quality checks include laboratory verification that the mix satisfies specified asphalt‑content limits and sieve‑passing requirements, as well as Gmm verification testing, all of which together constitute the metric set for assessing durability, distress resistance, and ride quality of the paved shoulder. The combined geometric and material criteria outlined in the guides therefore serve as the performance metrics by which a shoulder’s service life, resistance to distress, and contribution to overall ride comfort are measured.
