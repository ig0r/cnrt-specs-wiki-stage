Shoulder

Part of: Pavement Structure is-subtopic-of
Shoulder is a topic under Pavement Structure.
shouldershoulders
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-26

The shoulder is a paved edge of the roadway that supplies lateral structural support to the main carriageway, reducing edge stresses and slab deflection while also providing an emergency stopping area, a low‑maintenance surface for occasional heavy‑truck loads, bicycles, turning movements and temporary lanes[1]. It is designed to match the material of the adjacent pavement—full‑depth concrete on rigid pavements or a widened flexible pavement with specified shoulder types—and must incorporate joint spacing not exceeding twenty feet, minimum clear widths (three feet for Type 6 variants), appropriate cross‑slope and continuous base drainage to control water at the edge[1][2]. Construction requires a prepared subgrade with compacted aggregate layers, open subgrade drains sloped at least one‑quarter inch per foot, and strict tolerances on edge slumping and joint alignment to ensure durability, safety and serviceability throughout its design life[2].

Purpose and design objectives

The shoulder is incorporated into pavement designs to supply lateral structural support at the edge of the traveled way, thereby reducing edge stresses that would otherwise lead to cracking, rutting, or excessive slab deflection in the main carriageway. [1] To provide this support, guidelines require concrete shoulders with joint spacing limited to 20 ft and a minimum three‑foot width for Type 6 shoulder types; flexible pavements may receive a two‑foot widening into the pavement where drainage problems are anticipated. [1] The cross‑section also must maintain a minimum longitudinal slope of one‑quarter inch per foot on the trench bottom across both carriageway and shoulder, establishing a gravity flow that removes water from the wet area. [2] Subgrade drains are required to remain open from construction through final acceptance and be extended sufficiently to drain the wet area, preventing moisture accumulation at the pavement edge. [2] By offering a low‑maintenance paved surface for emergency stopping, accommodating occasional heavy‑truck loads when the Design Hourly Volume exceeds 150, supporting bicycles or turning movements at intersections, and serving as a temporary lane on high‑speed facilities, the shoulder fulfills functional performance needs that cannot be satisfied by the traveled way alone. [1] For new rigid‑pavement projects full‑depth concrete shoulders are mandated, while rehabilitation of existing concrete roads may involve rebuilding or widening the shoulder—potentially using Roller Compacted Concrete—to retain required lateral width, preserve bridge clearance, and control slab deflection where an incident management lane is not justified. [1]

The shoulder is required to satisfy a comprehensive set of functional, structural, durability, safety and serviceability objectives that enable it to act as an integral component of the pavement system throughout its design life. Functionally the shoulder must serve as an emergency lane, provide a stable platform for vehicles that leave the traveled way, accommodate bicycles and support turning movements at intersections. Where bicycle traffic is anticipated, the surface shall be one of the smooth‑surface types (1‑F, 1‑S or 1‑SP) to furnish a low‑profile riding surface, while the general shoulder texture must deliver adequate skid resistance through transverse rectangular grooves whose width ranges from three‑thirty‑seconds to three‑sixteenths of an inch and depth from one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths of an inch. The shoulder width shall preserve the functional width of the existing roadway, incorporate a typical two‑foot widening of the travel lane into the shoulder for flexible pavements, and satisfy bridge clearance requirements. Structurally the shoulder must provide lateral support that resists edge loads, thereby reducing edge stresses on the adjacent pavement. Full‑depth concrete shoulders are mandated on Interstate and other limited‑access freeways, while where drainage problems, heavy truck volumes or flexible pavements exist, appropriate type selections such as Type 2, Type 3 or suitable Type 1 variants shall be employed; roller‑compacted concrete may be used with a thickness selected between four and eighteen inches to achieve compressive strengths of roughly 4,000–10,000 psi and accommodate traffic volumes up to an ADT of 2,000 at speeds of 55 mph or less. The shoulder is built across its full width to at least the elevation of each base‑course layer, either prior to placement of the base course or concurrently as each layer is compacted, and it incorporates a layered aggregate base consisting of a minimum three‑inch compacted No. 2A aggregate directly on the subgrade topped by at least four inches of No. OGS aggregate, both compacted to a non‑movement condition. Where required, a Class 4 Type A geotextile is installed beneath the base layers and protected from damage, with any tears repaired at no cost. Durability is achieved by matching the shoulder material to the existing type—concrete shoulders where the existing shoulder is concrete—and by providing continuous base drainage; roller‑compacted concrete shoulders must be placed on a prepared subgrade or subbase capable of sustaining compaction, and all longitudinal and pavement‑shoulder joints are to be cleaned and sealed with control joints aligned with those of the adjacent mainline pavement. Serviceability of the shoulder is further ensured by maintaining strict surface tolerances, such that the elevation at the lane‑shoulder interface and any longitudinal joint may not deviate more than one‑quarter inch when measured with a straightedge, and the finished shoulder depth must be within a half‑inch of the design profile; deficiencies beyond these limits require corrective actions such as scarification, re‑blending and recompaction. Safety objectives include provision of bicycle‑tolerable rumble strips and edge‑line rumble strips, installation of a safety edge on the outermost side compacted to a depth between 1½ and five inches and shaped at an angle of 26° to 40° from the pavement cross‑slope to create a slip‑resistant slope for errant vehicles, and adjustment or replacement of guide‑rail and drainage structures as needed. When the exposed shoulder serves speeds of 35 mph or higher, a diamond‑ground textured surface shall be provided; for lower speeds an open‑graded texture suitable for low‑speed travel is required, and the shoulder surface must retain adequate skid resistance through the specified transverse grooves. The cross‑slope of the shoulder must conform to the minimum requirements set out in Publication 13, Design Manual Part 2, and any deviation requires a design‑exception request; proper drainage and preservation of effective shoulder width as shown on design charts further support long‑term serviceability. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

Shoulders are appropriate on both flexible (asphalt‑concrete) and rigid (Portland‑cement‑concrete) pavements whenever a paved edge is needed to improve structural condition or performance, because they provide lateral support that reduces edge stresses from edge loads. For new construction or reconstruction involving a rigid pavement the guide requires full‑depth concrete shoulders, which may be plain cement concrete or roller‑compacted concrete placed together with the lane slabs; joint spacing shall not exceed 20 ft and all longitudinal joints must be cleaned and sealed within a quarter‑inch tolerance. When a rigid‑pavement shoulder is used the subgrade must first receive a minimum compacted three‑inch layer of No. 2A aggregate followed by at least a four‑inch layer of No. OGS aggregate, and the concrete mix must satisfy Class AA specifications and include the prescribed dowels, expansion‑joint filler, joint‑sealing materials, curing compounds, with edge slumping limited to one‑quarter inch. In new flexible‑pavement projects the roadway is widened two feet into the shoulder area and the remaining width is constructed using a specified shoulder type; the shoulder employs the same aggregate courses and thicknesses as the adjacent pavement structure. If an asphalt mixture is selected for a flexible shoulder, acceptance may be based on non‑movement or optimum‑rolling pattern methods for continuous placements up to 500 tons when density control is not critical, such as in patching, road widening, driveway adjustments, parking lots, or other miscellaneous items approved by the Representative; however, shoulders that require critical density control must be accepted through pavement core testing. Type 1, 1‑F, 1‑S or 1‑SP shoulders are mandated on all Interstates, other limited‑access freeways and arterials, on routes with serious drainage problems, on two‑lane roads where the design hourly truck volume exceeds 150, or where the shoulder must accommodate turning movements or tight curves; Type 3 shoulders—or a surface material identical to the pavement—may be used on collectors and local roads. For Interstates, limited‑access freeways and arterials that require excavation or scarifying of an existing shoulder, Type 6 (or its 6‑F, 6‑S, 6‑SP variants) shoulders may be installed with a minimum width of three feet when only the first few feet adjacent to the pavement are distressed; if work is limited to cleaning and patching, a Type 7 shoulder is specified. On collectors and local roads with flexible pavements, shoulders can be constructed from the same surface material as the pavement or using Type 1 (or its variants); during widening the existing structure may be extended into the shoulder by one foot when average daily traffic is 5 000 vehicles or less (ADTT ≤ 500) and by two feet when traffic exceeds those thresholds. Roller‑compacted concrete shoulders are permitted as the final wearing course adjacent to reinforced or plain cement‑concrete pavements where tied shoulders are not required, provided the roadway carries an average daily traffic of 2 000 vehicles or less and a speed limit of 55 mph or lower; the RCC mix uses standard cement, aggregate and water with a low water‑to‑cement ratio (typically 0.40) and dense aggregate gradation, and control joints in the shoulder must align with those of the adjacent concrete pavement. A widened‑lane approach may be employed on concrete pavements where a slab no wider than 14 ft is provided together with a partial asphalt or RCC right shoulder; this configuration is intended for low‑volume rural Interstates and expressways that carry two‑way ADT less than 10 000 vehicles and have minimal need for an incident‑management lane, with the concrete slab striped at twelve feet and the remaining width serving as an effective shoulder. When work on a roadway is suspended for a sustained or indefinite period, temporary shoulders may be constructed to provide suitable drainage as part of the overall roadway drainage system. [1][2]

When drainage problems are anticipated, designers may avoid a conventional shoulder and instead extend the pavement structure into the shoulder or select a higher‑type shoulder such as Type 1, 1‑F, or 1‑S [1]. Similarly, if truck traffic exceeds a design hourly volume of about one‑hundred fifty vehicles, or geometric conditions such as tight ramp radii and curves sharper than 12°–30′ are present, the same alternative approaches are preferred [1]. Traffic volumes also drive the decision; when average daily traffic is greater than five thousand vehicles or the adjusted truck‑bus‑recreational vehicle count exceeds five hundred, widening into the shoulder by one to two feet is required, making a standard shoulder unsuitable [1]. Conversely, on low‑volume roads with ADT of five thousand or fewer and ADTT of five hundred or less, the widening can be omitted and an alternative shoulder type such as Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S, 6‑SP, a recycled shoulder, or a narrow three‑foot concrete Type 6 shoulder may be provided, especially when only the first few feet adjacent to the travel lane are distressed [1]. For concrete pavements, roller‑compacted concrete is limited to roadways with ADT of two thousand or less and posted speeds of fifty‑five miles per hour or lower; if speed limits exceed this value or the project will be evaluated using International Roughness Index measurements, RCC should be avoided and another shoulder construction chosen [1]. Moreover, any exposed RCC surface on a roadway with a speed limit of thirty‑five miles per hour or higher requires diamond grinding, so where that finishing is impractical an alternative shoulder design is preferred [1]. The widened concrete slab option with an asphalt or RCC shoulder is constrained to a maximum overall width of fourteen feet and is only appropriate for low‑volume rural interstates and expressways with two‑way ADT under ten thousand and without a dedicated incident‑management lane; projects exceeding these limits must adopt a conventional full‑width concrete design or a tied concrete shoulder [1]. In situations where the pavement edge meets a curb, sidewalk, or a guiderail whose face lies directly over the edge, a safety edge on the shoulder is not required because the existing structure provides the necessary treatment, prompting use of an alternative edge design instead of a dedicated shoulder safety edge [2]. Likewise, for non‑wearing layers such as base, leveling, or scratch courses, the specification omits the safety edge, indicating that another edge design approach is preferred over constructing a shoulder safety edge [2].

Design inputs and requirements

Shoulder design calls for information on the pavement material—rigid pavements require full‑depth concrete shoulders while flexible pavements are widened two feet into the shoulder and may use other specified types. The road classification (Interstate, arterial, collector or local) and anticipated traffic characteristics such as truck volumes exceeding a Design Hourly Volume of 150 and expected shoulder usage at tight‑radius ramps or turning movements must be known. Site conditions that could affect drainage are also required, since serious drainage problems trigger the use of higher‑type paved shoulders. The source does not specify any separate environmental, geotechnical, or existing‑condition data beyond these functional inputs. [1]

Shoulder design is governed by a comprehensive set of Penn DOT publications, specifications, standard drawings, and contract‑document requirements that together control geometry, surface texture, material placement, compaction, drainage, and performance verification. All new construction or reconstruction projects must satisfy the cross‑slope criteria established in Publication 13 Design Manual Part 2, with any inability to meet those criteria requiring a design exception under Publication 10X Appendix P [1]. Geometric definitions and shoulder type selections are prescribed in Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards, which delineate allowable families such as 1‑F, 1‑S, 1‑SP, 6‑F, 6‑S, and 6‑SP, while the guides also reference Type 6 or Type 7 paved shoulders where appropriate [1]. For rigid‑pavement projects a Portland Cement Concrete shoulder is mandatory, whereas flexible‑pavement projects must be widened an additional two feet into the shoulder except on inside shoulders of Interstates and other limited‑access freeways, which require full‑depth concrete shoulders [1]. Concrete shoulder joints may not exceed twenty feet in spacing, and for roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) shoulders the joint layout must align with the adjacent mainline concrete and follow a spacing rule of fifteen to twenty feet for sections under eight inches thick or a spacing equal to 2.5 times the pavement thickness for thicker slabs as specified in Publication 408 Specification Section 658 [1]. RCC shoulders are permitted only on roadways with an average daily traffic of two thousand vehicles or less and a posted speed limit of fifty‑five miles per hour or lower; if the exposed surface will serve speeds of thirty‑five miles per hour or higher it must be diamond‑ground [1]. Structural thickness for concrete shoulders ranges from four to eighteen inches and is determined using the standard Penn DOT concrete pavement design procedures, with a maximum slab width of fourteen feet and lane striping required at twelve feet; tied concrete shoulders may employ a thirteen‑foot outside lane striped at twelve feet on low‑volume rural Interstates and expressways whose two‑way average daily traffic is under ten thousand vehicles [1]. When widening a roadway the structural layer must be extended into the shoulder by two feet if the current average daily traffic exceeds five thousand (or the adjusted truck‑vehicle traffic exceeds five hundred); otherwise a one‑foot extension is allowed, and the entire shoulder may be paved to the same design as the traveled way [1]. Minimum base depths are prescribed as five inches on arterials and four inches on collectors and local roads, and recycled asphalt may be used for base or surface courses provided those depth requirements are met [1]. Rumble‑strip design follows Publication 13 Chapter 12 and Publication 72M RC‑22M, while all shoulder work must maintain continuous pavement‑base drainage, adjust or replace guide‑rail and drainage structures as needed, preserve required shoulder widths, and respect bridge clearance constraints [1]. The slip‑form paving specification limits edge slippage at the lane‑shoulder interface to a quarter‑inch and requires longitudinal joint tolerances between pavement and shoulder not to exceed a quarter‑inch when measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge, and transverse texture on shoulders must consist of rectangular grooves ranging from three‑thirty‑seconds to three‑sixteenths of an inch in width and from one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths of an inch in depth [2]. Surplus material on existing shoulders is removed to the bottom of the proposed base course or to a directed depth, scarification may not extend more than four inches below the existing grade, and low spots are filled and compacted to the required base‑course elevation using aggregate base as prescribed, with any voids backfilled and compacted until no movement under equipment is observed [2]. Subgrade drain outlets must remain open through final acceptance and the trench bottom—including the shoulder area—must maintain a minimum slope of a quarter‑inch per foot, while geotextiles required for shoulders must be Class 1 material (Section 212.3(b)) or, for subbase construction, a Class 4 Type A geotextile as defined in Section 210.3(b) [2]. The subbase for shoulders mirrors that of the adjacent rigid pavement: a minimum compacted depth of three inches of No. 2A aggregate placed directly on the prepared subgrade, overlain by at least four inches of No. OGS aggregate [2]. Concrete shoulders are governed by the Standard Drawing together with Section 501.3 for plain cement‑concrete (PCC) shoulders and Section 502.3 for RCC shoulders; PCC shoulders must satisfy the Class AA concrete criteria of Section 704, and all joint sealing, curing, admixture, dowel, expansion‑joint filler, and longitudinal joint materials must be selected in accordance with the relevant sections of the specifications [2]. Slip‑form paving of concrete shoulders requires accurate marking, sawing, and protection of transverse and longitudinal contraction joints as detailed in Sections 501.3(i) and (j). Geometric sequencing for all shoulder types mandates that shoulders be built up and compacted across their full width to at least the elevation of each base‑course layer, either before the base course is placed or concurrently while each layer is being compacted [2]. For flexible‑pavement shoulders the safety edge requirement specifies that the outer edge be constructed with the same material as the roadway (or a paved shoulder if specified), compacted to a depth between one and a half inches and five inches, formed to an angle of 26°–40° measured from the extended cross‑slope, and approved by the Representative; this edge device is omitted where curbs, sidewalks, or guide rails are present [2]. Performance verification follows the Department’s mat‑density acceptance criteria, with critical shoulders requiring core testing as specified and non‑critical shoulders accepted using an optimum‑rolling pattern; density measurements are made with an approved nuclear or electrical impedance gauge calibrated according to ASTM D2950 and documented per PTM 402/403, while surface tolerances for “Other than Mainline Pavement” shoulders are verified using the testing procedures set forth in Section 501.3(o) [2].

Design methods and criteria

Shoulder design follows a series of procedural rules that differ according to pavement type and traffic conditions, requiring Portland Cement Concrete shoulders for new rigid‑pavement projects and mandating a two‑foot widening of the traveled way into the shoulder when the existing pavement is flexible. [1] The concrete mix for such shoulders is limited to a water‑cement ratio of approximately 0.30 to 0.40 in order to achieve compressive strengths between about 4,000 and 10,000 psi. [1] Control joint spacing for concrete shoulders is governed by empirical limits: joints may not be spaced more than twenty feet, sections thinner than eight inches receive joints every fifteen to twenty feet, and for thicker slabs the spacing equals the slab thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet. [1] For Roller‑Compacted Concrete (RCC) shoulders less than eight inches thick, control joints are likewise placed at fifteen to twenty foot intervals, while thicker RCC sections use a spacing obtained by multiplying the pavement thickness by 2.5 feet. [1] RCC shoulders are permitted only where average daily traffic does not exceed two thousand vehicles and the posted speed limit is fifty‑five miles per hour or less. [1] When traffic volumes exceed five thousand ADT or adjusted truck traffic exceeds five hundred, the new structure extends two feet into the shoulder; otherwise a one‑foot extension is used, with districts optionally paving the entire shoulder to match the widened carriageway. [1] The selection of shoulder type—ranging from full‑depth concrete (Type 2) to various Type 1 configurations—is based on road classification, anticipated drainage issues, Design Hourly Volume greater than 150, and specific intersection or curve conditions, with effective shoulder widths taken from design criteria charts. [1] Recycled asphalt material may be incorporated in the base or surface courses of Type 6 and Type 7 shoulders, requiring a minimum recycled‑base depth of five inches on arterials and four inches on collectors and local roads. [1] For widened concrete slabs not exceeding fourteen feet in width, the slab is striped at twelve‑foot intervals and may be paired with an asphalt or RCC right shoulder; load transfer for such configurations uses the appropriate Load Transfer Coefficient (J) from Table 8.2 for a widened lane with asphalt or RCC shoulders. [1] Construction quality control begins with an edge‑slump test in which a twelve‑foot straightedge is drawn across the six‑inch zone adjacent to the pavement edge before the concrete loses plasticity; measured slumps must not exceed one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes and one‑quarter inch between a lane and its adjoining shoulder. [2] Repairs of depressions are performed by manual asphalt patching: Type A addresses depressions three inches or less deep through vertical cutting, cleaning, tack coating, placement of an asphalt wearing course and compaction with a steel‑wheeled roller, while Type B is used for deeper depressions and adds an uncompacted binder lift not exceeding three inches before the top wearing layer. [2] When potholes extend into a shoulder that already has an asphalt base, the shoulder portion is filled with wearing‑course material and compacted in the same manner as the main carriageway; if no asphalt base exists, suitable excavated fill is placed and the edges are sealed with emulsified or tack asphalt. [2] Construction proceeds by removing surplus material down to the bottom of the proposed base course, scarifying existing shoulder areas up to four inches below grade, reshaping and compacting them to the required bottom elevation, and backfilling any voids with aggregate base that is shaped and compacted until it no longer moves under rollers. [2] Subgrade drainage trenches are installed after subgrade completion but before subbase placement; the trench bottoms are kept on a minimum slope of one‑quarter inch per foot through both pavement and shoulder zones, and Class 1 geotextile may be used where specified. [2] Pavement cores or approved nuclear or electrical impedance gauges—such as Troxler Model 3411B/4640B, Campbell Pacific MC‑2, Seaman MC‑2, TransTech PQI models 300 or 301, and Troxler PaveTracker™—are employed to verify density for critical shoulder sections in accordance with PTM procedures and ASTM D2950, while non‑critical shoulders may be accepted by a non‑movement method when course thickness is less than one inch or weight does not exceed 110 pounds per square yard. [2] Plain cement concrete shoulders are constructed following the Standard Drawing and Section 501.3, with care taken not to damage the prepared surface during placement; joints are sawed with guided equipment that includes a blade guard, water‑cooling and depth control, then cured and protected as required. [2] Roller‑compacted concrete shoulders follow the details of Section 502.3, and may be built up and compacted for full width to or above the elevation of each base‑course layer before placing the base course, or built up and rolled in tandem with compaction of successive layers, ensuring that the final vertical profile matches the adjacent pavement. [2]

Shoulders are required to provide lateral support to pavement under edge loads, reducing edge stresses compared with an unsupported condition [1]. The geometric design must satisfy the same minimum cross‑slope criteria applied to the main carriageway, as defined in the relevant design manual, and superelevations shall be checked and corrected; if compliance cannot be achieved a formal design‑exception request is required [1]. Edge slumping at the lane‑shoulder interface is limited to a quarter‑inch measured over a six‑inch zone adjacent to the pavement edge, and the longitudinal joint between main slab and shoulder shall not deviate more than a quarter‑inch when surveyed with a twelve‑foot straightedge [2]. The surface of the shoulder must be textured across its full width with rectangular transverse grooves whose width ranges from three‑thirty‑seconds to three‑sixteenths of an inch and depth from one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths of an inch, providing adequate skid resistance [2]. For rigid‑pavement projects the shoulder shall be constructed of Portland cement concrete, with full‑depth concrete shoulders mandated on Interstates and other limited‑access freeways and Type 2 concrete shoulders used on arterials, collectors and local roads; flexible‑pavement projects require widening the traveled way an additional two feet into the shoulder except on inside shoulders of limited‑access facilities, with specific shoulder types selected based on drainage concerns, a design hourly truck volume greater than 150, or intersection geometry tighter than 12°–30′ [1]. Concrete joint spacing shall not exceed twenty feet, and when rehabilitating concrete pavements the new shoulder must match the existing material; where only the first few feet adjacent to the lane are distressed a minimum three‑foot width of Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or 6‑SP may be provided [1]. Structural extension into the shoulder shall be two feet when the average daily traffic exceeds five thousand vehicles or when the ADT‑truck factor exceeds five hundred; otherwise a one‑foot extension is required, with full paving of the shoulder recommended in high‑turning zones [1]. Recycled‑asphalt base courses are permitted for shoulders, with a minimum depth of five inches on arterials and four inches on collectors and local roads [1]. RCC (roller‑compacted concrete) shoulders may be employed only where traffic volume does not exceed an average daily traffic of two thousand vehicles and the posted speed limit is fifty‑five miles per hour or less; if the surface will be exposed to speeds of thirty‑five miles per hour or greater it must be textured by diamond grinding [1]. Design thickness for concrete shoulders ranges from four to eighteen inches, with compressive strength typically between four‑thousand and ten‑thousand psi, and control joints shall be spaced fifteen to twenty feet for sections thinner than eight inches or at a spacing equal to 2.5 times the pavement thickness in feet for thicker sections; joint spacing must also comply with the general limit of twenty feet [1]. The subgrade beneath the shoulder must be prepared and compacted to support the intended pavement structure, and the subbase follows the same dimensional and compaction standards as the main pavement, requiring a minimum three‑inch compacted layer of No. 2A aggregate directly on the prepared subgrade followed by four inches of No. OGS aggregate, each compacted to a non‑movement condition; the finished subbase elevation must be within half an inch of design depth, with deficiencies of half an inch or more requiring scarification, blending and recompaction [2]. A Class 4 Type A geotextile shall be installed before placing the subbase, and where drainage is required sub‑grade drains must remain open through construction until final acceptance, with trench bottoms—including the shoulder portion—maintained at a minimum slope of one quarter inch per foot; drains are to be installed prior to subbase placement and may be protected by Class 1 geotextile where specified [2]. Shoulder material density must be verified by pavement cores when critical, while non‑critical placements may be accepted based on achieving a non‑movement condition or an optimum‑rolling pattern provided that placement depth does not exceed one inch, unit weight does not exceed 110 lb/yd² and continuous placement quantities do not exceed five hundred tons [2]. The shoulder shall incorporate a Safety Edge constructed of the same asphalt material as the roadway or specified paved shoulder; this edge must be compacted to a minimum depth of one‑and‑a‑half inches and may not exceed a total depth of five inches, with its final face inclined between twenty‑six and forty degrees measured from the extended pavement cross‑slope, and any sharp or ragged sections shall be removed immediately after placement [2]. Fill widening shall be used to retain proper shoulder widths, bridge under‑clearances must be verified, longitudinal and pavement‑shoulder joints cleaned and sealed, and guide rails and drainage structures adjusted or replaced as necessary; a continuous base drain is required where indicated [1]. The design assumes that the specified traffic volumes, speed limits, and geometric thresholds are representative of future conditions, and compliance with all enumerated material specifications, geometric criteria, joint tolerances, drainage provisions, and construction procedures constitutes the demonstration of safety and reliability; explicit safety factors or probabilistic reliability targets are not prescribed beyond meeting these deterministic thresholds [1][2].

Structural section and dimensions

The shoulder must be provided with a minimum clear width of three feet when it is classified as Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or 6‑SP and only the first few feet adjacent to the main pavement are distressed. When a lane is widened onto a concrete shoulder the concrete portion may extend one to two feet beyond the existing edge and must be paired with an asphalt shoulder; full‑depth asphalt shoulders are also permitted. If the average daily traffic exceeds five thousand vehicles or the annual average truck traffic exceeds five hundred, any pavement widening is required to continue into the shoulder by two feet, whereas lower traffic volumes call for a one‑foot extension. Concrete shoulders are limited to control joint spacings that never exceed twenty feet; for concrete thicknesses under eight inches joints may be placed between fifteen and twenty feet apart, while for thicknesses of eight inches or greater the spacing equals two point five times the slab thickness expressed in feet. The concrete wearing course itself may range from four to eighteen inches thick. Recycled‑material base courses placed on shoulders must be at least five inches deep on arterial facilities and four inches deep on collector or local roads. Prior to constructing the shoulder any excess material is stripped down to the bottom of the proposed base course, with scarifying permitted only to a depth of four inches below the existing grade; deeper cuts are backfilled with aggregate base‑course material and compacted to the same elevation. The shoulder must be built up and compacted across its full width to at least the elevation of each underlying base‑course layer, either before the base course is placed or simultaneously as each layer is compacted, thereby matching or exceeding the height of the pavement structure beneath it. A safety‑edge treatment is required on the shoulder; its depth shall be no less than one and a half inches and shall not exceed five inches. The aggregate composition of the shoulder follows the same minimum compacted thicknesses as the pavement, with a No. 2A aggregate layer placed directly on the subgrade to a minimum depth of three inches and a No. OGS aggregate layer placed atop that to a minimum depth of four inches, regardless of whether the adjacent pavement is rigid or flexible. The guides do not prescribe separate dimensional requirements for subbase, treated‑soil, slab, edge‑support, or reinforcement within the shoulder structure. [1][2]

The shoulder thickness is taken from the standard PennDOT concrete pavement design procedures and may be any value between four and eighteen inches, selected to satisfy the anticipated compressive strength of the rapid‑curing concrete (RCC) mix. When a recycled asphalt base is employed beneath the shoulder, the base must be at least five inches thick on arterial routes and four inches on collector or local roads. Joint spacing for concrete shoulders is limited to twenty feet; in practice slabs less than eight inches thick receive control joints spaced between fifteen and twenty feet, while slabs eight inches or thicker are provided with joints spaced at a distance equal to 2.5 times the slab thickness expressed in feet. All shoulder joints must line up with the joint pattern of the adjacent mainline pavement. In widening projects the shoulder is extended into the new lane by two feet when the current average daily traffic exceeds five thousand vehicles or when the adjusted daily truck‑traffic (ADTT) exceeds five hundred, and by one foot otherwise. Because RCC shoulders are permitted where tied shoulders are not required, no additional tie‑in treatment beyond matching joint alignment is mandated, and the source does not prescribe specific cross‑slope or transition geometry for the shoulder. Materials and mixture design

The shoulder material is chosen to correspond with the type of main pavement, so that projects built on a rigid‑pavement structure specify Portland‑cement concrete shoulders while flexible‑pavement projects first widen the traveled way two feet into the shoulder and then apply the designated shoulder type [1][2]. Full‑depth concrete shoulders are required on Interstate and other limited‑access freeways, and any concrete joints shall be spaced no more than twenty feet apart to control cracking [1]. When roller‑compacted concrete is selected, the mix must consist of Portland cement, a dense aggregate gradation whose maximum particle size is less than one inch, water, and only limited air‑entraining agents or other admixtures; the water‑to‑cement ratio should be about 0.40 but may be reduced to as low as 0.30, producing a dry, no‑slump consistency comparable to damp gravel, and the resulting pavement is expected to achieve compressive strengths between four thousand and ten thousand pounds per square inch [1]. Plain cement concrete shoulders are specified as Class AA cement concrete together with the appropriate joint sealing material, curing covers or compounds, required admixtures, dowels, expansion‑joint filler and longitudinal joint material as referenced in the applicable sections [2]. For asphalt‑based shoulders reclaimed asphalt concrete may be used either as a recycled base course for Type 6, 6‑F, or 6‑S shoulders or as an asphalt surface course for Type 7 shoulders on any roadway, with cold‑mixed recycled bases required to be at least five inches thick on arterial roads and four inches thick on collector and local streets [1]. The permanent shoulder structure is built on a prepared subgrade using an aggregate base course defined by the specifications, typically a No. 2A aggregate layer placed directly on the subgrade to a minimum compacted thickness of three inches overlain by a No. OGS aggregate layer at least four inches thick; both layers must be kept sufficiently moist to prevent segregation and allow compaction without adding excess water, and the completed subbase is compacted to the non‑movement condition prescribed for the project [2]. Where indicated, a Class 1 geotextile shall be installed between the subgrade and the aggregate base to provide separation or reinforcement as required by the design criteria [2]. If an asphalt wearing course is needed for shoulder repairs, the repair must use either the existing base asphalt or a new wearing‑course mix compacted in the same manner as the main pavement, with patch edges sealed using an emulsified tack coat of Class TACK or NTT or a binder of comparable performance grade such as PG 64S‑22; for repairs deeper than three inches a binder course is placed in lifts whose uncompacted thickness does not exceed three inches and finished with a one‑and‑a‑half inch wearing course, while any required prime coat or surface treatment follows the mix designs referenced in the applicable sections [2].

Shoulders are designed by selecting a material type—rigid‑pavement concrete for high‑volume facilities or reclaimed asphalt concrete for flexible‑pavement contexts—and proportioning the mixture to satisfy structural, durability, constructability and performance objectives [1][2]. For rigid‑pavement shoulders the mix is kept very dry, with a water‑to‑cement ratio typically between 0.30 and 0.40, producing a damp‑gravel consistency that has essentially no slump; aggregates are densely graded and limited to one inch or less in size, air entrainment may be provided when needed, and admixtures are used only as required [1]. This proportioning yields compressive capacities appropriate for the anticipated loads and permits design thicknesses ranging from four to eighteen inches as dictated by structural calculations [1]. The concrete is placed with a paver, compacted with rollers, and does not require forms, reinforcement or dowel bars; control joints are cut to match the spacing and alignment of the adjacent mainline pavement and joint spacing on the shoulder itself is never allowed to exceed twenty feet [1]. When the existing roadway is flexible‑pavement type, shoulders are classified as Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or 6‑SP and are constructed with reclaimed asphalt concrete for base or surface layers, with a minimum recycled‑base depth of five inches on arterials and four inches on collectors and local roads [1]. If the average daily traffic exceeds five thousand vehicles or the adjusted daily truck traffic exceeds five hundred, the structural layer of a widening project is extended into the shoulder by two feet to provide additional support [1]. The shoulder subbase is built from specified aggregates—No. 2A placed directly on a prepared subgrade to at least three inches compacted and topped with No. OGS to at least four inches—so that each layer does not exceed eight inches, or four inches when granulated slag is used [2]. Materials are mixed with water using acceptable methods, ensuring sufficient moisture to prevent segregation during stockpiling, hauling and placing while limiting additional water added during compaction [2]. When the quantity of subbase material exceeds 1,500 tons a Subbase Delivery Plan detailing equipment, delivery and placement methods must be submitted for review [2]. Compaction is required to achieve the non‑movement condition defined in Section 206.3(b), thereby providing the structural stability, durability, constructability and performance expected of the shoulder [2].

Structural details and interfaces

Concrete and roller‑compacted concrete shoulders are required to incorporate control joints that limit cracking; for cast‑in‑place concrete the transverse joints must be spaced at intervals not exceeding twenty feet and aligned with the joint pattern of the adjacent mainline pavement, while RCC shoulders follow the same alignment rule but use spacing of fifteen to twenty feet for slabs thinner than eight inches and a spacing equal to the slab thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet for thicker slabs [1]. All transverse contraction joints on plain cement‑concrete shoulders must be sealed at both ends, and when a new shoulder is placed next to an existing lane the existing transverse joint is saw‑cut to remove any mortar that may have entered it; the joints are accurately marked at their centers and cut with guided, water‑cooled saws equipped with blade guards and depth‑control devices before being cured and protected as specified [2]. Longitudinal joints between shoulder and pavement are inspected for a maximum vertical tolerance of one‑quarter inch and edge slumping is limited to no more than a quarter‑inch difference between lane and shoulder surfaces, ensuring a smooth transition across the interface [2]. One guide states that no steel reinforcement, dowel bars, or other load‑transfer devices are called for in either concrete or RCC shoulders, allowing an RCC shoulder to be placed without forms; another guide requires clean deformed or plain‑bar dowels staked in advance and mechanically inserted tie bars positioned at intervals of every 500 feet on straight alignments and every 100 feet through horizontal curves, with each tie bar located at least twenty‑four inches from any transverse joint to transfer loads across all joints [1][2]. The only explicit load‑transfer provision mentioned by one source is the use of a load‑transfer coefficient J (as given in Table 8.2) when a widened concrete lane is paired with an asphalt or RCC shoulder, whereas the other source treats the shoulder as an integral component of the slab system and relies on the dowels and tie bars to provide the necessary load transfer across joints [1][2]. Rehabilitation and construction practices require cleaning and sealing all longitudinal joints as well as pavement‑shoulder joints; premolded expansion‑joint filler, the longitudinal joint material specified in Section 705.2, and the sealing material prescribed in Section 705.4 are installed to protect the joints from intrusion and deterioration [2][1]. For paved (asphalt) shoulders a safety edge is required as the sole structural element; it is formed with the same asphalt mix using a paver‑mounted extruder that creates a wedge shape at least 1.5 inches deep but not exceeding five inches total thickness, and after compaction the edge slope must lie between 26° and 40° measured from the extended pavement cross‑slope, with the safety edge omitted where a curb, sidewalk or guide rail terminates the pavement [2]. Roller‑compacted concrete shoulders are constructed per the plans without additional joint specifications, and no forms are required for RCC shoulder placement, reinforcing that the primary structural detailing consists of appropriately spaced control joints, routine joint sealing, and, where specified, reinforcement or load‑transfer devices appropriate to the shoulder type [1][2].

Shoulder interfaces begin with subgrade preparation that includes placement of a Class 4 Type A geotextile, followed by a compacted No. 2A aggregate course at least three inches thick and a No. OGS layer to a minimum depth of four inches, establishing a stable base for subsequent layers [2]. The subbase is then placed in uniform lifts not exceeding eight inches of compaction (or four inches when granulated slag is used), with any subbase laid narrower than the full pavement width trimmed neatly at its edges before adjoining sections are installed, thereby preserving continuity across the shoulder‑mainline interface [2]. For flexible‑pavement projects the existing carriageway structure is extended into the shoulder a prescribed distance—two feet when average daily traffic exceeds five thousand vehicles or truck trips exceed five hundred, and one foot for lower volumes—and this widened lane may be paved with the same material as the main pavement to create a seamless transition [1]. When the project employs rigid‑pavement design, Portland cement concrete shoulders are required on interstates and other limited‑access freeways, using Type 1, 1‑F, 1‑S or 1‑SP configurations where functional needs such as drainage or truck traffic dictate, and concrete shoulder joints are spaced no more than twenty feet apart with control joints aligned to those of the adjoining mainline [1]. During rehabilitation the shoulder surface is placed in the same material as the adjacent pavement, and reclaimed asphalt may be incorporated into a recycled base course for Type 6, 6‑F or 6‑S shoulders provided a minimum depth of five inches on arterials (four inches on collectors and local roads), ensuring material compatibility across the interface [1]. All longitudinal joints and the joint between pavement and shoulder are cleaned and sealed, with tack coat applied where asphalt is placed, to secure a durable bond that resists water infiltration and load‑transfer issues [1][2]. Load‑transfer devices such as deformed or plain bar dowels are installed across concrete joints at the shoulder‑mainline junction, and premolded expansion‑joint filler and longitudinal joint material are placed in accordance with base‑course requirements to maintain structural continuity [2]. Joint tolerances at the lane‑shoulder line are limited to a quarter‑inch deviation, verified by positioning a twelve‑foot straightedge perpendicular to the joint, while transverse or crack terminations at the shoulder edge are sealed before any construction equipment or traffic enters the area, thereby preserving interface integrity [2]. The first saw cuts in the shoulder are aligned with the dominant joints of the adjacent pavement so that cracking patterns remain coordinated across the transition, and all joint ends are scarified, moistened and blended with fresh material to create a continuous section without a distinct joint, using the same moisture‑control practices applied to the main pavement [2]. A one‑inch leveling course is provided for full‑depth asphalt shoulders to correct cross‑slope and produce a smooth transition, while a continuous base drain beneath the shoulder prevents water accumulation that could compromise the interface between subgrade and pavement layers [1]. When prefabricated base drains intersect the shoulder, the interface is treated as a distinct functional zone; the drain core must provide at least six percent open area on its pavement‑side face and three percent on its shoulder side, and the surrounding geotextile must allow unobstructed inflow across no less than fifty percent of its pavement‑side face and twelve percent of its shoulder‑side face, with the geotextile wrapped completely around the core and spot‑bonded to prevent deformation under load [2]. Guide rails, drainage structures and other adjacent features are adjusted or replaced as necessary to maintain proper alignment and function at the shoulder interface, ensuring that ancillary infrastructure does not disrupt the pavement‑shoulder continuity [1]. The outermost edge of an asphalt shoulder incorporates a safety edge constructed from the same mix as the roadway, placed at a depth of at least one and a half inches but not more than five inches and finished with a cross‑slope angle between twenty‑six and forty degrees, providing a durable interface that enhances vehicle stability and reduces pavement damage [2]. Where the shoulder meets curbs, gutters or other structures, the wearing course is laid slightly higher than the adjoining surface and sealed with hot or approved emulsified asphalt over a minimum twelve‑inch width to create a watertight joint, thereby protecting the interface from moisture ingress [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

The shoulder incorporates a continuous pavement‑base drain that conveys moisture away from the traveled way, ensuring reliable water removal under varying climate and environmental conditions.[1] Across the shoulder face, prefabricated pavement‑base drains are installed, each required to provide at least 3 % open core area on the shoulder side and 6 % on the pavement side, while the overlying geotextile must permit unobstructed inflow through a minimum of 12 % of its area on the shoulder side and 50 % on the pavement side.[2] Depending on project requirements, shoulders are classified as Type 6 or Type 7 paved shoulders to deliver appropriate structural capacity and drainage performance.[1] A permanent subgrade drainage system is placed beneath the subbase after the subgrade has been prepared but before subbase placement, with outlet ends left open throughout construction until final acceptance of the shoulder.[2] These subgrade drains are extended sufficiently to remove water from any wet zones and are installed in trenches sloped a minimum of one‑quarter inch per foot across both pavement and shoulder sections to promote proper runoff.[2] The shoulder’s dedicated drainage system is required to meet the same performance criteria as the main carriageway, ensuring comparable effectiveness in managing water under all environmental conditions.[2] All longitudinal joints and the interfaces between pavement and shoulder are cleaned and sealed during construction to protect the structure from moisture infiltration.[1] Where existing guide‑rail or drainage structures intersect the shoulder, they are inspected and, if inadequate for prevailing environmental loads, are adjusted or replaced to maintain effective water management.[1] Concrete shoulders are used when the existing shoulder material is concrete; otherwise the new shoulder matches the existing type, preserving consistent performance across differing moisture and temperature regimes.[1] When pipe drainage is employed, perforated pipes must provide a minimum inlet area of 1.4 in² per linear foot, with hole diameters not smaller than three‑sixteenths inch and not larger than three‑eighths inch, and must be capable of an infiltration rate of ten gallons per minute per linear foot.[2] If construction is temporarily suspended, the contractor must protect the roadway from moisture by providing suitable drainage through temporary shoulders, opening existing culverts, inlets and parallel ditches, and installing any required temporary drainage structures until permanent shoulder work can be completed.[2]

Shoulders are required to conform to the same minimum cross‑slope standards that govern the traveled way, and any necessary superelevation adjustments must be verified; when those geometric criteria cannot be satisfied a design‑exception request is mandated. In addition, the concrete edge of a shoulder may not deviate more than one‑quarter inch within the six‑inch zone adjacent to the pavement before the concrete sets, and the longitudinal joint joining the shoulder to the main slab must also be limited to a quarter‑inch tolerance measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge. For asphalt shoulders a safety edge constructed from the same material as the adjoining pavement shall be provided on the outer side, with a depth of at least 1.5 inches but not exceeding five inches and an installed cross‑slope angle between 26 and 40 degrees measured from the extended pavement cross‑slope, except where a curb, sidewalk or guide rail directly abuts the edge. Concrete shoulders must be provided with control joints spaced no more than twenty feet, and when distress is confined to the first few feet adjacent to the travel lane a minimum three‑foot width of Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or 6‑SP shoulder is required. The structural build‑up of a shoulder mirrors that of the adjoining pavement, beginning with a compacted layer of No. 2A aggregate at least three inches thick placed directly on the prepared subgrade, followed by a compacted layer of No. OGS aggregate at least four inches thick. Shoulders may be built up and fully compacted across their entire width to an elevation equal to or greater than each base‑course layer before that layer is placed, or they may be rolled in step with the compaction of successive base‑course layers so that the final shoulder elevation meets or exceeds the corresponding pavement layer. Where a concrete traveling way is used, full‑depth concrete shoulders are required on Interstates and other limited‑access freeways, while Type 2 concrete shoulders are specified for arterials, collectors and local roads; flexible pavements call for a two‑foot widening of the roadway into the shoulder (except inside shoulders on limited‑access highways) with the remaining effective shoulder width derived from design charts. Types 1, 1‑F, 1‑S and 1‑SP shoulders are mandated on all Interstates and arterials, in locations where drainage problems are serious, on two‑lane roads whose truck Design Hourly Volume exceeds 150, and wherever traffic is expected to use the shoulder at tight‑radius ramps, curves sharper than 12°‑30′, or as a turning lane near intersections; rumble strips may be added for bicycle tolerance and edge‑line applications. Recycled asphalt base courses are permissible on shoulders provided they achieve minimum depths of five inches for arterials and four inches for collectors and local roads. Shoulder pavement classification must employ Type 6 or Type 7 paved shoulders where required, constructed as full‑depth asphalt shoulders; when concrete shoulders are provided they should be widened one to two feet and paired with an asphalt shoulder, with any necessary cross‑slope correction applied and guide rail and drainage structures adjusted to maintain proper support and runoff. When widening an existing pavement, a two‑foot extension into the shoulder is mandated if average daily traffic exceeds five thousand vehicles or the truck/bus/recreational vehicle factor exceeds five hundred; otherwise a one‑foot extension suffices, although full‑width paving is strongly recommended at locations of heavy turning movements such as driveway entries and intersections. Roller‑compacted concrete shoulders are allowed only on roads with an average daily traffic of 2,000 vehicles or fewer and a posted speed limit of 55 mph or less, serving as the final wearing course where a tied shoulder is not required; thickness follows PennDOT procedures (typically four to eighteen inches), control joints must align with those of the adjacent mainline concrete pavement, and if the RCC surface is exposed on roads with speed limits of 35 mph or higher it must be textured by diamond grinding. When shoulders are incorporated into a widened concrete slab, the total slab width may not exceed fourteen feet; the slab is striped at twelve feet and the remaining portion functions as an effective shoulder when combined with a Type 1 asphalt or RCC shoulder, while tied‑concrete shoulders may employ a thirteen‑foot‑wide outside lane striped at twelve feet to limit slab deflection. Smooth, bicycle‑friendly surfaces are required wherever cycling is anticipated, ensuring continuity of ride quality across the shoulder and main carriageway; any pothole repair that extends into the shoulder must be compacted so that the repaired surface conforms to the grade of the surrounding pavement, with excavation limited to the roadway edge, thereby preserving seamless vehicle access. All rehabilitation or overlay work shall continue the existing material type—concrete shoulders are retained where the current shoulder is concrete, otherwise the new shoulder should match the existing type—while preserving proper shoulder width and verifying bridge under‑clearances. Verification and expected performance

The completed shoulder is verified through a coordinated series of inspections that confirm every prescribed construction step has been performed and that the finished element satisfies density, dimensional, texture, and edge‑treatment requirements. First, any surplus material is removed to the base‑course level, existing or backfilled areas are scarified no deeper than four inches below grade, and then shaped and compacted to the bottom elevation of the proposed base course. Aggregate base is placed to the depth indicated in Section 350.3, and excavated zones are backfilled with aggregate base material and compacted with rollers as required by Section 108.05; compaction is considered satisfactory only when the material does not move under the equipment. The subbase is checked for proper placement of each No. 2A layer to a minimum compacted depth of three inches and each OGS layer to four inches, verification that the Class 4, Type A geotextile remains intact, and compliance with elevation tolerances; compaction must achieve a non‑movement condition and any deficiency of half an inch or more triggers scarification, blending, recompaction and retesting. After the base is verified, the asphalt prime coat and surface treatment are applied in accordance with Sections 461.3 and 480.3. The safety edge is constructed from the same asphalt material with a minimum thickness of 1.5 inches and a maximum total depth of five inches, and its finished face must form an angle between 26° and 40° measured from the extended pavement cross‑slope; exposed outer edges are worked smooth immediately after placement. Density acceptance follows the procedures used for other low‑criticality asphalt works: when density is not critical the Inspector‑in‑Charge may accept by non‑movement or by an optimum rolling pattern measured with a calibrated nuclear or electrical impedance gauge and documented on the appropriate forms, while shoulders requiring critical density are tested by extracting cores within one day of placement and having the laboratory determine bulk specific gravity and density against theoretical maximum values. All results are compared to the project’s acceptance criteria and any failure triggers corrective action before paving resumes. Performance is further verified by macro‑texture testing for segregation or flushing, checking that curb seals create watertight joints, and applying the surface‑tolerance test defined in Section 501.3(o) to evaluate texture, smoothness and dimensional tolerances. When every measured dimension, compaction value, edge finish, density result and surface‑tolerance outcome meets the specified thresholds, the shoulder is deemed compliant with constructability, performance and design consistency requirements.
