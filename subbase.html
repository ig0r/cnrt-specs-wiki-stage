Subbase

Part of: Pavement Structure is-subtopic-of
Subbase is a topic under Pavement Structure.
subbase
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-29

The subbase is a well‑compacted, highly permeable granular layer—usually No. 2A coarse aggregate—placed directly on a prepared, sound subgrade to isolate the pavement structure from weak or unstable soils [1][2]. By providing an even, non‑moving platform that rapidly conveys water away by gravity, it preserves the bearing capacity of the underlying subgrade and mitigates moisture‑induced distresses such as frost heave, rutting and premature cracking [1][2]. Its stiffness, thickness and loss‑of‑support factors are combined with those of the base layer to achieve the effective modulus required for the overall pavement system [1].

PennDOT publications covering “Subbase” also include the following subtopics: 2A Subbase.

The 2A Subbase supplies the granular material and compaction standards that define the physical properties of a subbase layer. [2] Its specified aggregate characteristics and placement procedures ensure the formation of a firm bearing plane with an effective modulus of subgrade reaction suitable for rigid pavements. [1][2]

2A Subbase

Purpose and design objectives

The subbase is introduced primarily to compensate for a native subgrade that cannot reliably sustain traffic loads when moisture accumulates, erosion or pumping occurs, or the soil is soft, wet, frozen, or otherwise unstable [1][2]. By providing a well‑compacted, non‑moving layer of uniformly graded coarse aggregate placed directly on the subgrade, the subbase isolates the pavement structure from weak soils and creates an aggregate platform that supports applied loads [2]. The material is required to be highly permeable so that water can flow through it rapidly, and the slope of the subbase layer is maximized to increase the rate of gravity drainage within the granular media, thereby removing water by gravity and preserving the bearing capacity of the underlying subgrade [1]. Removal of water reduces loss of subgrade support and increases the resistance of the overlying base to traffic‑induced deflections, which in turn mitigates frost‑heave distress, rutting, and premature cracking [1]. Different subbase types possess distinct modulus values, allowing designers to select a treated permeable base or dense asphalt base placed over unbound aggregate, or to use No. 2A coarse aggregate, to meet the required load‑bearing capacity for the intended service life and traffic demand [1][2]. The effective stiffness of the pavement system is obtained by combining the k‑values of the base and subbase, with a loss‑of‑support factor applied to correct for potential erosion or pumping of the subbase material [1]. Typical designs evaluate subbase thicknesses such as a 6‑inch unbound granular layer in roller‑compacted concrete pavements to achieve an economical balance between structural performance and material usage while maintaining a final surface grade within plus or minus ½ inch of the design elevation [1][2]. When integrated with underdrain systems and positive drainage, the subbase contributes to a higher drainage coefficient (C_d = 1.1) used for performance prediction, further enhancing overall drainage quality and long‑term pavement durability [1].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59), Ch. 8 (pp. 160–167); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 231–232), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 438–447)

The subbase must fulfill a suite of interrelated objectives that together provide a reliable foundation for the pavement system. Functionally it is required to act as a highly permeable, firm and even supporting plane that rapidly conveys water away by gravity while limiting the migration of fines from the subgrade into the overlying layers. Structurally the material shall be placed only on a prepared, sound subgrade after all soft, wet or otherwise unsuitable soil has been removed, and its thickness must be built to the design elevation within a tolerance of plus‑or‑minus one‑half inch; any deficiency of half an inch or more renders the layer defective. Some drainability may be sacrificed to provide a more stable work platform while the pavement layers are being built. The required aggregate is No. 2A coarse material, or No. OGS where specified, placed in lifts not exceeding six inches, except when installed inside expanded geocell cells taller than six inches, where the lift may be one to two inches greater; each lift must be compacted to a non‑movement condition as defined by the applicable specification, with no shoving or wave motion observed under rolling pressure. The final surface grading shall meet grade and contour tolerances of plus‑or‑minus one‑half inch while remaining smooth enough for subsequent paving. Durability is achieved by keeping the subbase dry, maintaining proper moisture content from mixing through placement to avoid segregation, protecting the layer from traffic during construction, and limiting exposure; when left uncovered for more than sixty days or exposed to winter conditions it must be retested, proof‑rolled and repaired or replaced as necessary. Keeping the subbase dry preserves the support provided by the underlying subgrade and prevents moisture‑induced distresses such as rutting, cracking and frost heave, thereby protecting long‑term performance. Safety considerations are addressed by ensuring that the subbase attains a stable, non‑movement condition before any further work, eliminating the risk of settlement under load, and by prohibiting traffic—including construction traffic—over subbases constructed with No. OGS aggregate. Serviceability is reflected in the strict control of elevation, thickness, surface smoothness and tolerance, which together guarantee a uniform ride quality throughout the design life and maintain the pavement’s intended performance; for high‑traffic facilities where projected equivalent single axle loads exceed five million ESALs, the subbase may only be credited toward structural capacity if its condition and drainage capability have been verified by investigation. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

Subbase is considered appropriate for both rigid and flexible pavement systems, as well as for shoulders, provided that the underlying subgrade has been properly prepared and does not contain soft, muddy or frozen zones. [2] In rigid‑pavement structures such as Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement and Roller Compacted Concrete, an aggregate layer placed on the prepared subgrade supplies drainage and a stable platform for the overlying slab. [1] For Interstate and Expressway concrete pavements the design typically requires an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) resting on a No. 2A subbase, and this configuration may also be used in rehabilitation treatments such as 3R and other CPR projects when the original construction employed open‑graded subbase, ATPB or CTPB and positive outlet drainage can be achieved. [1] Open‑graded subbase is prohibited for new concrete pavements; only ATPB or CTPB are allowed when a drainage layer is required. [1] Low‑volume local and collector roads with average daily traffic of 2 000 vehicles or less that use roller‑compacted concrete commonly assume a six‑inch unbound granular subbase beneath the slab to meet structural support requirements for a twenty‑year design life with a one percent annual traffic growth rate. [1] Flexible‑pavement overlays on non‑interstate highways may incorporate a subbase in the structural analysis when the projected 18‑kip ESALs are below five million and site investigations confirm that the material is of acceptable quality and provides adequate drainage; on Interstate projects or where ESALs exceed five million without verified subbase condition, the subbase should be excluded from the overlay design. [1] When a treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate is used together with an underdrain system that ensures positive drainage, a drainage coefficient of Cd = 1.1 may be applied in life‑cycle calculations. [1] In rigid‑pavement projects that employ precast concrete slab installations or full‑depth rubblizing, a granular subbase of No. 2A coarse aggregate is placed to the required elevation, compacted in lifts not exceeding six inches for backfill applications and eight inches for general placement, and then shaped to a firm, even surface within plus‑or‑minus one‑half inch tolerance before the new slabs are set. [2] Flexible pavements and shoulders may also use an open‑graded subbase consisting of coarse aggregate that contains at least seventy‑five percent crushed particles with three fracture faces; this OGS layer is placed over a minimum three‑inch compacted No. 2A course and must be at least four inches thick. [2] Granulated slag may be used as a substitute subbase material provided each lift does not exceed four inches (or six inches when fully compacted) and the material meets prescribed density, size and composition limits; aggregate manufactured from steel slag is expressly prohibited. [2] Subbase design frequently incorporates geotextile separation, using Class 4 Type A fabric for general subgrade stabilization or Class 4 Type C when stabilizing fine‑graded subgrades or reinforcing GRS abutments, with the fabric overlapped at least two feet, secured, and covered before any equipment contacts it, and a minimum of six inches of subbase is placed on the geotextile prior to compaction. [2] Where special rolling reveals permanent deflection greater than one‑half inch but not more than three inches, the deficient subgrade is undercut to a depth of one foot; if permanent deflection exceeds three inches, the undercut may extend up to three feet, after which selected borrow material is backfilled, a Class 4 Type A geotextile is laid, and at least six inches of subbase is placed and compacted. [2] Throughout all applications the subbase must be kept well‑moistened, continuously compacted, and constructed with a positive slope to promote drainage. [2]

A subbase is not the preferred solution when project constraints conflict with several specified limits, such as the prohibition of open‑graded material for concrete pavements, which requires a cement‑treated or asphalt permeable base instead. [1] If a drainage layer cannot guarantee a positive outlet, designers replace the subbase function with a dense base equipped with longitudinal edge drains. [1] The structural suitability of a subbase also depends on its stiffness relative to the underlying roadbed; the ratio of the subbase modulus to the roadbed resilient modulus must not exceed four, and exceeding this threshold mandates adjustment of layer composition or adoption of an alternative design. [1] When bedrock is encountered within ten feet of the subgrade surface over a significant length, its effect on the k‑value must be considered, prompting selection of a different approach. [1] If a treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system that assures positive drainage cannot be provided, the subbase option is abandoned in favor of a design that relies solely on unbound aggregate; this also applies to rehabilitation projects where existing pavement has shown poor drainage and no substantial drainage upgrades are planned. [1] Existing subbases that are in poor condition—particularly on Interstate highways—are omitted from structural evaluation, leading designers to depend on other layers or different design methods. [1] On non‑Interstate routes where a subbase is absent and projected traffic exceeds five million 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads, the subbase cannot be counted unless a dedicated investigation verifies its quality and drainability; otherwise an alternative design must be employed. [1] When the existing subgrade is soft, muddy or frozen, specifications require correction before any subbase can be placed, making a design that treats or stabilizes the subgrade directly preferable. [2] If the project requires more than 1,500 tons of subbase material, a Subbase Delivery Plan must be prepared and approved; failure to satisfy this requirement drives selection of an alternative structural layer. [2] Construction traffic is prohibited on a subbase constructed with No. OGS aggregate, and when early vehicle loads are anticipated designers often opt for a different load‑bearing solution. [2] Any subbase that would remain exposed over winter without an asphalt or concrete protective layer must be proof‑rolled and may need to be removed, indicating that a pavement system not reliant on an unprotected subbase should be employed. [2] Aggregates derived from steel slag and recycled‑concrete aggregate are expressly prohibited for use as a subbase, and granulated slag may be used only if it conforms to the limits set out in Section 350; when these materials are the only coarse options, designers must substitute a base course or another granular material. [2] Coarse aggregate containing deleterious constituents such as clay, silt, vegetation, reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide in amounts that could impair performance must be excluded from the subbase and replaced with a more appropriate material. [2]

Design inputs and requirements

The design of a subbase layer is governed by both state and national specifications, including PennDOT publications such as Publication 72M for headwall protection and Publication 13M for pavement‑base drain sizing, as well as the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures that require inputs like ESALs, serviceability limits, concrete strength, modulus values and drainage coefficients [1]. Subbase material must be highly permeable, a requirement demonstrated through gradation testing and reinforced by specifications that call for maximizing subbase slope to promote gravity drainage, which together act as project constraints influencing cost and performance [1][2]. For Interstate and Expressway rigid pavements the structural base is required to consist of an asphalt or cement‑treated permeable base (ATPB or CTPB) over a 2A subbase, and the use of open‑graded subbase is expressly prohibited, a rule echoed in both the state guidance and the Base Courses specifications [1][2]. Seasonal subgrade reaction moduli are prescribed at 15 000 psi for spring thaw, 30 000 psi for normal conditions and 50 000 psi for frozen conditions, with an additional constraint that the ratio of subbase modulus to roadbed soil resilient modulus shall not exceed four, and loss‑of‑support factors from Table 8.1 are applied when determining the weighted k‑value [1]. Drainage performance is expressed through a drainage coefficient Cₙ of 1.0 for pavements built directly on unbound aggregate, increasing to 1.1 when a treated permeable base or dense asphalt base overlies unbound aggregate and an under‑drain system providing positive drainage is installed; rehabilitation projects that retain existing drainage revert to the standard Cₙ of 1.0 [1]. Local and collector reinforced concrete designs incorporate a six‑inch unbound granular subbase, with thickness inputs used by design software together with assumptions of a 20‑year service life, 1 % annual traffic growth and standard PennDOT subgrade resilient modulus values to satisfy performance criteria such as limiting slab cracking to fifteen percent at the end of design life [1]. Project constraints for overlay designs limit the use of existing subbase: on Interstate projects the subbase is omitted from structural evaluation because of potential poor condition, and on non‑Interstate routes it is excluded when projected 18‑kip ESALs exceed five million unless a specific investigation confirms acceptable quality and drainage [1]. Installation must follow the Base Courses specifications, particularly Section 350.3, which incorporates equipment standards of Section 108.05(c), subgrade preparation requirements of Section 210 and moisture‑control mixing rules; prior to placement the existing subgrade is prepared as required and any soft, wet or otherwise unsuitable material must be removed [2]. When a shipment exceeds 1,500 tons a Subbase Delivery Plan must be submitted to the Representative for approval, ensuring that material quantities are documented and verified before placement [2]. All subbase material is placed over a Class 4 Type A geotextile installed in accordance with Section 212.3(d); the fabric may be secured with steel pins as described in Section 212.2 and must be fully covered by back‑dumped subbase before any equipment contacts it, preventing wind uplift of the fabric [2]. A minimum of six inches of approved material is required before compaction, and the overall depth must meet tolerances set out in Section 210.3(c); a deficiency of half an inch or more constitutes a defect that requires scarifying, refilling and recompacting [2]. The aggregate used shall comply with the coarse‑aggregate specifications of Section 703.2; open‑graded subbase material must contain at least 75 % crushed particles each exhibiting three fracture faces, and its absorption may not exceed 3.0 % for Type A or 3.5 % for Type B as determined by AASHTO T85 [2]. Steel slag is prohibited; granulated slag may be used only as a subbase material per Section 350 and must contain no more than 20 % non‑slag, have a dry rodded density of 80 lb/ft³ or less, be sized to a maximum of 50 mm, and limit the fraction passing the No. 100 sieve to 20 % [2]. All aggregate sources must appear on Bulletin 14 or receive LTS approval, with quality verified by petrographic analysis (PTM 518) and the prescribed absorption test [2]. Placement is executed in lifts not exceeding eight inches thick, reduced to four inches when granulated slag is used; each lift must be compacted to a non‑movement condition as required by Section 206.3(b) [2]. For No. 2A aggregate the minimum compacted depth per lift is three inches, while OGS requires four inches; lifts for rubblized pavement are limited to six inches and must be rolled until no shoving or wave motion occurs under the roller [2]. When subbase is placed in expanded geocell cells it must be overfilled so that the compacted surface extends at least one inch above the cell top, the drop height of material may not exceed three feet, and lift thickness may be increased by one to two inches when cell heights exceed six inches; compaction still follows Section 350 [2]. The finished subbase surface must be shaped to a firm, even grade within plus‑or‑minus ½ inch of the required contour [2]. Traffic, including construction traffic, is prohibited on OGS subbase, and any subbase exposed for more than 60 days requires retesting; winter protection or proof rolling is mandated by Sections 310.3(g) and 206.3(a) [2]. All work is performed under the supervision of the Representative, who may waive requirements only when alternative treatments are specified and approved [2].

Design methods and criteria

The subbase is defined by selecting a highly permeable granular material whose particle‑size distribution governs its permeability and by shaping the layer to provide the greatest practical slope so that gravity can rapidly drain water through the aggregate[1]. Drainage design for base drains and outlet spacing follows the procedure outlined in PennDOT Publication 13M Design Manual Part 2 Chapter 10 Section 10.3.D[1]. For structural analysis the subbase is treated together with the base layer, and a weighted‑average loss‑of‑support factor is computed from the depths of each layer using LS₁·D₁/(D₁+D₂)+LS₂·D₂/(D₁+D₂)[1]. The resulting effective subgrade reaction modulus (k) is then adjusted by selecting an elastic modulus for the base of 15,000 psi under spring‑thaw conditions, 30,000 psi for normal summer/fall conditions, or 50,000 psi for frozen winter conditions, while ensuring that the ratio of subbase to soil resilient modulus does not exceed four[1]. Different effective k‑values are generated for each subbase type and depth, with additional modifications applied when the pavement is located near a rigid foundation[1]. In reinforced concrete pavement design for low‑volume roads the subbase is incorporated through the American Concrete Pavement Association’s StreetPave12 software, which assumes a six‑inch unbound granular layer in combination with the subgrade and uses an empirical resilient‑modulus model that defines MRSG as 1,500 times the CBR value to compute slab thickness for a twenty‑year design life[1]. Subbase construction begins by removing existing concrete and excavating unsuitable or soft material until the required subgrade elevation is reached, discarding any wet, soft, or otherwise unsatisfactory material before placement of the granular aggregate[2]. The selected No. 2A coarse aggregate is placed and compacted in accordance with Section 703.2 (and referenced Section 350), and the prepared surface is shaped to a firm, even plane that remains within plus‑or‑minus ½ inch of the design grade and contour[2]. A rolled‑out geotextile is laid on the subgrade with overlapping sheets securely fastened, and subbase material is back‑dumped and spread ahead of equipment to the full design depth while maintaining a positive slope for drainage and ensuring that machinery never rides directly on the fabric[2]. When expanded‑geocell cells are used, the subbase is placed within those cells after geotextile installation, overfilled so that the compacted surface extends at least one inch above the top of the cell, and the drop height of material is limited to three feet to protect the cell walls[2]. The subbase is laid in uniform lifts whose thickness depends on the aggregate type; for No. 2A aggregate each compacted lift must be at least three inches thick, for No. OGS aggregate a minimum of four inches, and no lift shall exceed eight inches in compacted depth, with granulated slag limited to four inches[2]. When geocell cells taller than six inches are employed, lift thickness may be increased by one to two inches beyond the cell height, while lifts of other materials remain within the prescribed limits[2]. All lifts are compacted from the sides toward the centre until a non‑movement condition specified in Section 206.3(b) (or Section 350/350.3 where applicable) is achieved, avoiding shoving or wave motion under the roller[2]. After each lift is compacted a depth test hole is excavated; if the measured depth differs from the intended depth by half an inch or more, the lift is classified as defective and must be scarified, blended with additional material, and recompacted[2]. Any wheel ruts that develop during construction are filled with additional subbase to preserve the designed profile[2].

The subbase shall consist of a highly permeable granular material that allows gravity‑drainable water to be conveyed rapidly and possesses void spaces large enough to prevent capillary rise. [1] To enhance drainage the longitudinal slope of the subbase layer is maximized, and any drain outlets located in mowable areas are protected by appropriate headwalls. [1] Where wet subgrade conditions or cut‑to‑fill transitions occur, lateral underdrains are encouraged to assure reliable removal of water. [1] In structural analysis the subbase is treated together with the base course and an effective modulus of subgrade reaction is computed using a weighted‑average loss‑of‑support factor that reflects each layer’s thickness and LS value. [1] Seasonal elastic moduli for the base are prescribed as 15 000 psi for spring thaw, 30 000 psi for normal summer/fall, and 50 000 psi when frozen, but the ratio of subbase resilient modulus to that of the underlying soil shall not exceed four. [1] Only asphalt‑ or cement‑treated permeable bases may serve as drainage layers; open‑graded subbases are prohibited in interstate and expressway designs that specifically call for a 2A subbase beneath an ATPB or CTPB layer. [1] For rigid‑pavement design the overall drainage coefficient Cd is set to 1.1 when a treated permeable base or dense asphalt base is placed over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system and positive drainage, reverting to 1.0 when such measures are absent or in rehabilitation projects lacking significant drainage improvements. [1] A minimum six‑inch unbound granular subbase is mandated for reinforced concrete pavement designs that assume a twenty‑year service life, a one percent annual traffic growth, and define performance thresholds through terminal serviceability values of 2.5 for collector roads with a ninety‑percent reliability target and 2.0 for local roads with an eighty‑five‑percent reliability requirement. [1] In overlay design the existing subbase on Interstate projects is assumed non‑structural and excluded from the effective structural number calculation because it may be in poor condition; on non‑Interstate routes the subbase is also omitted when projected 18‑kip ESAL traffic exceeds five million unless a specific investigation confirms adequate quality and drainage. [1] The subbase must be placed on a properly prepared subgrade that is free of soft, wet, frozen or otherwise unsuitable material, and it must be kept sufficiently moist to avoid segregation during handling. [2] Prior to placement a Class 4 Type A geotextile is required beneath the subbase, and the subgrade stability limits require permanent deflection not to exceed one‑half inch for existing ground or one inch for raised embankments; exceeding these limits mandates undercutting and replacement with approved fill. [2] The design calls for a minimum thickness of six inches before compaction, and the finished surface must lie within plus‑or‑minus ½ inch of the specified grade and contour. [2] Placement is carried out in compacted lifts that generally shall not exceed eight inches; when granulated slag is used each lift is limited to four inches, although a fully compacted six‑inch lift is permissible under Section 206.3(b). [2] For No. 2A aggregate the minimum compacted depth per lift is three inches and for open‑graded subbase material it is four inches; backfill lifts are capped at six inches and must be rolled until a non‑movement condition is achieved with no shoving or wave motion observed. [2] Compaction density requirements follow those of other pavement layers—typically ninety‑seven percent of maximum dry density for deeper embankments and one hundred percent when within three feet of the final subgrade elevation. [2] When the subbase is installed in geocell cells it must be over‑filled so that the compacted surface rises at least one inch above the cell top, material drops onto the cells may not exceed three feet, and lift thickness may be one to two inches greater than the cell height if the cells are taller than six inches. [2] Material quality standards for open‑graded subbase require at least seventy‑five percent crushed particles with a minimum of three fracture faces and absorption limits of three point zero percent for Type A or three point five percent for Type B, while granulated slag may contain no more than twenty percent non‑slag material, must have a dry rodded density not exceeding eighty pounds per cubic foot, be uniformly sized to a maximum of fifty millimetres, and fines may not exceed twenty percent passing the No. 100 sieve; all aggregates must be free of deleterious substances such as clay, shale, glassy particles or reactive minerals. [2] For projects exceeding one‑five hundred tons a Subbase Delivery Plan detailing equipment and methods must receive approval before work commences. [2]

Structural section and dimensions

Across the guides, a six‑inch unbound granular subbase is prescribed for reinforced concrete pavement designs, and when a supported edge such as a curb‑and‑gutter or widened lane is provided the required slab thickness may be reduced by one inch, although no other layer dimensions are specified [1]. The subbase must be placed in compacted lifts whose individual thickness does not exceed eight inches; if granulated slag is used each lift is limited to four inches unless the entire depth is compacted in a single six‑inch lift as permitted [2]. For No 2A aggregate, each lift must achieve at least three inches of compacted depth, while for No OGS aggregate the minimum per‑lift compaction is four inches [2]. The total subbase thickness is controlled by Section 210.3(c), and any shortfall of one‑half inch or more from the specified depth renders the work defective [2]. When the subbase is installed within expanded geocell cells, it must be overfilled so that after compaction its surface lies at least one inch above the top of the cell, and if the cell height exceeds six inches the compacted lift may be permitted to be one to two inches thicker than the cell height [2]. Over a Class 4 Type A geotextile a minimum uncompacted thickness of six inches is required before compaction can commence [2]. For backfilling excavated sections after rubblizing, No 2A subbase material is used in lifts not exceeding six inches and compacted by rolling until no movement occurs before the next lift is placed [2]. The guides do not provide additional dimensional requirements for pavement, base, treated‑soil, slab (beyond the one‑inch reduction), shoulder, edge‑support, or reinforcement beyond what is noted above [1][2].

Materials and mixture design

The subbase shall be defined by the material type, required thickness, loss‑of‑support factor (where applicable), elastic modulus, and any prescribed mixture proportions. [1] Acceptable material types include an unbound granular No 2A aggregate, an open‑graded subbase (OGS), granulated slag, or a treated base such as an asphalt‑treated permeable base (ATPB) or cement‑treated permeable base (CTPB); the latter must be proportioned in accordance with the ATPB or CTPB specifications rather than using open‑graded mixes. [1][2] The coarse aggregate used for unbound subbases must meet durability and cleanliness criteria and may be selected from approved stone, durable gravel, blast‑furnace slag, granulated slag, or lightweight aggregate, with all aggregates required to be free of deleterious substances such as clay, silt, reactive chert, or iron sulfide. [2] When an OGS is specified, at least 75 % of the material shall consist of crushed particles possessing three fracture faces and its water absorption may not exceed 3.0 % for Type A applications (3.5 % for Type B). [2] Granulated slag, which may be used only as a subbase material, must have a dry rodded density of no more than 80 lb/ft³, a maximum particle size of 50 mm, not more than 20 % passing the 150‑µm sieve, and any non‑slag component is limited to 20 % by weight. [2] For treated bases the designer shall assign an appropriate loss‑of‑support factor to adjust the effective k‑value for potential erosion or pumping, and shall provide the elastic modulus to be used under the relevant seasonal condition; the ratio of this subbase elastic modulus to the resilient modulus of the underlying soil shall not exceed four. [1] The design shall state the thickness of the layer in inches; a six‑inch thickness is the standard practice for unbound granular subbases, while placement shall be carried out in compacted lifts that do not exceed eight inches in depth (four inches when granulated slag is used). [1][2] A No 2A layer must achieve a minimum compacted depth of three inches and an OGS layer at least four inches; backfill with No 2A aggregate shall be placed in lifts not greater than six inches and rolled until a non‑movement condition is attained. [2] Throughout handling, stockpiling and placement the material must contain sufficient moisture to prevent segregation, and compaction shall be performed to the non‑movement condition so that the finished subbase surface is firm, even and within ±½ inch of the required grade and contour. [2]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–167); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 231–232), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 430–447), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–655)

The design process starts by classifying the intended sub‑base type because each type provides a characteristic elastic modulus that contributes to the pavement’s effective k‑value. For rigid pavements the preferred configuration is an asphalt or cement‑treated permeable base over a 2A sub‑base, and the combined layer is assigned elastic moduli of roughly 15 000 psi during spring thaw, 30 000 psi under normal conditions, and 50 000 psi when frozen. Designers compute an overall support factor by weighting individual layer thicknesses with a loss‑of‑support (LS) factor that reflects potential erosion or pumping, and this weighted average guides the selection of the most economical thickness while still meeting structural requirements. The modulus of the selected sub‑base is limited to no more than four times the resilient modulus of the underlying roadbed soil to avoid an overly stiff interface. Material composition is primarily No. 2A coarse aggregate, with optional inclusion of No. OGS or granulated‑slag material where specified, and only contract‑authorized blends are used. Sufficient moisture must be present in the blend to keep it homogeneous during handling, prevent segregation, and minimize additional water addition during compaction. Prior to placement any soft, wet, or otherwise unsuitable subgrade material is removed to provide a stable foundation. The aggregate is placed in uniform lifts whose thicknesses are controlled: each lift of No. 2A must be compacted to at least three inches and no more than six inches, while No. OGS requires a minimum of four inches; when granulated slag is employed the overall layer depth may not exceed four inches, and in all cases the total compacted depth of any layer shall not exceed eight inches. Each lift is compacted from the edges toward the centre by rolling until a non‑movement condition is achieved, with shoving or wave motion prohibited under the roller. Highly permeable granular mixes are specified so that water can move freely through the voids and be removed by gravity, while the larger inter‑particle spaces also suppress capillary suction that could otherwise draw moisture upward into the pavement structure. Particle‑size gradation is used in the field as a practical proxy for permeability, with standards such as those employed by PennDOT guiding selection. Although maximum drainability is desirable for durability and performance, designers may intentionally reduce it to obtain a more stable work platform, especially when a coarser mix provides better bearing capacity. The sub‑base is placed on the steepest feasible slope to accelerate gravity drainage, and where positive drainage cannot be achieved with an open‑graded material, dense bases equipped with edge drains are specified instead. After compaction the finished sub‑base surface is shaped to a firm, even plane that lies within plus or minus one‑half inch of the designed grade and contour, thereby delivering the required structural support, durability, constructability, and long‑term pavement performance. By balancing high permeability for durability with sufficient stiffness, thickness optimization, LS adjustments, moisture control, lift limits, and compaction criteria, the final mixture satisfies structural, durability, constructability, and performance requirements. Structural details and interfaces

Subbase installation requires a series of coordinated joints, reinforcement measures, load‑transfer provisions and transition details. When a geocell system is employed, each cell panel must be aligned with its neighbor and the welded perimeter edges overlapped; the adjoining sections are then secured with mechanical fasteners that meet the manufacturer’s specifications and are capable of maintaining direct contact between panels until the pavement layer is placed. Reinforcement is achieved by driving anchors through the geocell to a depth of at least twelve inches below its bottom, after which they are removed prior to compaction, providing load transfer to the underlying subgrade. The filled cells are over‑filled so that the finished compacted surface sits at least one inch above the geocell, creating a smooth transition into the pavement. Beneath the subbase a geotextile layer is required. The fabric must be laid flat without wrinkles, with adjacent rolls overlapped by no less than two feet and, where necessary, fastened in place with steel pins to prevent wind uplift. The geotextile should remain exposed for no more than two weeks, and construction equipment may not drive on it until the subbase fully covers it. In areas of undercut, a transition slope not steeper than an eight‑horizontal‑to‑one‑vertical ratio is constructed at both the beginning and end, onto which a Class 4 Type A geotextile is placed over the backfill. At least six inches of subbase material are then laid on this geotextile before compaction. The subbase itself is placed by back‑dumping to the full design depth ahead of all equipment, spread uniformly and compacted in accordance with Section 350.3 while maintaining a positive slope for drainage. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 168–193)

The design of the subbase‑subgrade interface is treated as a distinct construction item that must be capable of bearing the compaction loads imposed by the overlying pavement, and both layers are verified in the design phase for adequate support without excessive deformation. Prior to subbase placement the subgrade surface is uniformly compacted to provide a stable platform on which the interface treatments are applied. When a base course overlies the subbase, the two layers are considered as a single composite for determining the effective modulus of subgrade reaction, with their individual thicknesses summed and a weighted‑average loss‑of‑support factor calculated from the LS values and depths of each layer. Seasonal elastic moduli for the combined base‑subbase system are prescribed at 15 000 psi during spring thaw, 30 000 psi under normal conditions, and 50 000 psi when frozen, and the ratio of subbase modulus to the resilient modulus of the roadbed soil is limited to four. The selected subbase material must be compatible with drainage requirements; only asphalt‑treated porous base or cement‑treated porous base are permitted as drainage layers, while open graded subbase is expressly prohibited, and if positive outlet drainage cannot be achieved a dense base equipped with longitudinal edge drains shall be provided. A Class 4 Type A geotextile is installed over the prepared subgrade or back‑filled borrow material, spread without wrinkles, secured, and overlapped by at least twelve inches (or two feet for rolled fabric), extending six inches beyond the perimeter of any installed geocell. For geocell systems the welded ends are aligned with a minimum twelve‑inch overlap, mechanically fastened, and anchored so that each anchor projects at least twelve inches below the cell bottom. Subbase material is then placed over the fabric by back‑dumping and spreading ahead of equipment to the full design depth while maintaining a positive slope for drainage, with a minimum six‑inch layer spread on the geotextile before compaction; when cells are used the fill is over‑filled so that the compacted surface lies at least one inch above the top of the geocell. Placement drops shall not exceed three feet and material is placed in lifts not greater than six inches, especially in backfilling voids beneath rubblized concrete slabs, with each lift compacted by rolling until no movement occurs and shoving or wave motion are avoided. A fifty‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller is used to seat the rubblized slab, and satisfactory seating is confirmed when vertical deflection under the roller is less than three‑quarters of an inch. All compaction must achieve the densities specified for the subbase material (e.g., No. 2A) before any paving so that the overlying pavement rests on an undisturbed, properly compacted layer. Where the subbase does not extend the full pavement width, its edges are neatly trimmed to provide a clean joint with adjacent sections, and transition zones at undercuts or repaired subgrades are shaped with slopes no steeper than eight horizontal to one vertical to ensure smooth load transfer and protection of underground utilities. For shoulder sections that incorporate control joints in the concrete slab, joint spacing and alignment are required to match those of the adjoining mainline slab, maintaining continuity across the interface. Drainage, environment, and accessibility

The subbase is placed over a properly prepared subgrade from which all soft, wet, muddy or frozen material has been removed [2]. A highly permeable granular material is required so that water can flow freely by gravity and avoid capillary retention [1]. The granular component must consist of well‑graded No. 2A coarse aggregate whose absorption does not exceed three percent for Type A or three‑point‑five percent for Type B, and which either is naturally freeze‑thaw resistant (absorption under two percent) or meets soundness limits of sixteen percent sodium‑sulfate loss or seven percent freeze‑thaw loss [2]. The aggregate is laid over a correctly installed Class 4, Type A geotextile that must be covered within two weeks to protect it from moisture and wind lift [2]. A positive slope is provided on the subbase surface so that water drains away by gravity, and where the subgrade includes an undercut or repair a drainage path is excavated and backfilled with No. 57 aggregate before the geotextile and at least six inches of compacted subbase are placed [1][2]. Lateral underdrains are installed in wet subgrade areas and at cut‑to‑fill transitions to provide additional drainage capacity, and all drain outlets are protected against crushing or plugging to retain long‑term functionality [1]. Where a positive outlet can be achieved, a dedicated permeable drainage layer such as an asphalt‑treated porous base (ATPB) or cement‑treated porous base (CTPB) is used; otherwise a dense base with edge drains is employed, and open‑graded subbase is prohibited [1]. During placement the material is kept well‑moistened—sufficient to prevent segregation yet not excessive—and compacted when its moisture content lies between three percent below optimum and the optimum value; any material wetter than optimum is dried by disking or scarifying [2]. The subbase is spread, back‑dumped and compacted to full design depth, achieving a firm, even surface within plus‑or‑minus one‑half inch of the required grade [2]. If exposure to weather exceeds sixty calendar days, the layer must be retested for irregularities and repaired before pavement installation [2]. The design incorporates seasonal variations by assigning elastic moduli of fifteen thousand psi for spring thaw conditions, thirty thousand psi for normal summer/fall conditions, and fifty thousand psi for frozen winter conditions, with a limit that the subbase resilient modulus not exceed four times that of the underlying soil without reduction and a loss‑of‑support factor applied to account for moisture‑induced erosion or pumping [1]. For new rigid‑pavement projects the subbase consists of a treated permeable base or dense asphalt base placed over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system that provides positive drainage, and the overall drainage coefficient used in design is increased to 1.1 to reflect superior moisture control; projects lacking such treatment retain the default coefficient of 1.0 [1]. In overlay designs the subbase is assumed to provide adequate drainage and all drainage coefficients are set to 1.0, and the subbase may be omitted from structural evaluation on Interstates unless a specific investigation confirms its quality and drainability; for non‑Interstate facilities with design traffic exceeding five million 18‑kip ESALs, the subbase may also be excluded unless verified [1].

Subbase layers are required to be placed on a clean, stable foundation after removal of any soft, wet, or otherwise unsuitable material, and the aggregate used shall be No 2A coarse aggregate as specified for structural support [2]. The finished subbase surface must be shaped to the designed grade and contour within a tolerance of plus‑or‑minus one‑half inch, and it must maintain a positive longitudinal slope that is maximized to promote rapid gravity drainage through the highly permeable granular material [1][2]. Drainage outlets located in mowable areas shall be protected by appropriate headwalls to provide safe maintenance access and prevent blockage or crushing of the drainage system [1]. When the subbase is installed within expanded geocell cells, it must be overfilled so that the compacted top lies at least one inch above the cell rim, providing a fine‑grading margin and protecting the cell during traffic and paving heat; anchors securing the geocell must extend a minimum of twelve inches below the bottom of the cell and leave sufficient length above for later removal [2]. The material drop height is limited to three feet to avoid damage or displacement of cell walls, and equipment may not be operated on unfilled cells until they have been overfilled with loose subbase; for cells taller than six inches, compaction lifts may exceed the cell height by one to two inches [2]. If a geotextile underlies the subbase, adjacent fabric rolls must overlap by at least two feet and remain protected from direct equipment contact until fully covered, with exposure limited to no more than two weeks [2]. During placement the subbase is back‑dumped and spread ahead of all equipment to full design depth while maintaining the positive slope, and any wheel ruts that develop are filled with additional subbase to preserve the designed profile [2][1]. Compaction shall follow the procedures specified in Section 350.3, ensuring a firm and even surface that prevents fluid‑induced loss of support which could otherwise create surface undulations detrimental to ride quality for users [2][1]. By adhering to these geometric tolerances, drainage provisions, protective measures for outlets, geocell and geotextile systems, and compaction standards, the subbase supports structural integrity, safe access for maintenance personnel, and a smooth riding surface that meets user expectations [1][2].

Verification and expected performance

The Representative confirms compliance by performing density tests at each prescribed location and then excavating a full‑depth test hole to verify that the finished subbase depth is within ±½ in of the design elevation. Compaction must reach the non‑movement condition defined in Section 206.3(b), achieved with overlapping passes from the sides toward the centre, and surface tolerances are inspected according to Section 210.3(c). If any test hole shows a deficiency of ½ in or more, the area is classified as defective; it must be scarified to three inches, blended with additional No. 2A coarse aggregate, re‑compacted, and retested until the allowable tolerance is met. Prior to slab placement, the bedding surface is examined using a straight‑edge at least ten feet long together with a depth gauge of at least six feet set to the slab thickness; any deviation greater than 1⁄8 in over the length of a slab requires correction. The final subbase must present a firm, evenly compacted layer of No. 2A aggregate shaped to the required grade and contour within ±½ in, with traffic restrictions observed for aggregates designated No. OGS and any required proof‑rolling completed, thereby ensuring constructability, performance, and consistency with the project specifications.
