Aggregate Interlock

Part of: Load Transfer is-subtopic-of
Aggregate Interlock is a topic under Load Transfer.
aggregate-interlock
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Aggregate interlock is the condition in which full‑depth cracks are introduced across a rigid concrete slab yet the broken pieces remain mechanically locked together through their coarse aggregates, thereby preserving load transfer and overall pavement stability [1]. It is achieved by employing controlled variable‑force impact equipment that creates continuous cracks while avoiding surface spalling, punch holes, or shattering, followed by reseating the fragments with a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller (four to seven rubber wheels inflated to at least 100 psi) making multiple passes—at least three for Crack‑and‑Seat and five for Break‑and‑Seat methods [1]. The approach also mandates strict clearances from utilities and structures, limits saw‑cut lengths to 20 ft (or depth within ±1 in of two‑thirds slab thickness), and requires visual inspection plus falling‑weight deflectometer testing to verify that interlock is retained and the fractured layer meets the specified elastic modulus criteria [1].

Purpose and design objectives

The fracture process must be executed so that the broken concrete slabs continue to lock together through their aggregates, because loss of that interlock would compromise the pavement’s ability to transfer loads and remain stable. Therefore, equipment is required to produce continuous full‑depth cracks while maintaining aggregate interlock between pieces, ensuring the fractured slab system can still support traffic without excessive movement or shattering. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–415)

For the fracture-and-seat treatment described in Section 522.3, aggregate interlock must be preserved as a functional and structural requirement; equipment must be capable of delivering a variable force that creates continuous full‑depth cracks across the width while keeping the pieces interlocked, and it may not cause extensive surface spalling, punch holes, or shatter the pavement. The seating operation must use a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic tire roller with at least four rubber‑tired wheels inflated to no less than 100 psi, applying the prescribed number of passes (at least three for Crack‑and‑Seat and at least five for Break‑and‑Seat) so that vertical deflection under the roller indicates non‑movement of the substrate, thereby satisfying serviceability and safety objectives. Additionally, fracture work must avoid damaging nearby utilities, bridge decks, or other structures, further supporting durability and safety goals. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–415)

Scope and applicability

Aggregate interlock is appropriate for rehabilitating existing rigid (jointed) concrete pavements using full‑depth slab fracturing and subsequent seating, such as the Crack & Seat or Break & Seat methods. The construction plan must employ equipment that can impact the pavement with a variable force and be controlled to produce continuous full‑depth cracking across the entire width while preserving aggregate interlock between pieces; sonic vibratory equipment may only be used when the fracturing will not cause soil liquefaction of the subgrade. Prior to fracturing, any overlying asphalt must be removed and slabs saw‑cut to a maximum length of 20 feet (or to within ±1 inch of two‑thirds slab depth for Break & Seat), after which the fractured pieces are reseated with a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic tire roller. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–415)

When the existing concrete slab cannot be fractured without violating the proximity limits for underground utilities or structures—specifically when culverts, cross‑pipes, or utility sleeves lie within 10 ft horizontally and 10 ft from the slab bottom, when the pavement is within 40 ft of bridge or tunnel approaches, or when a fixed object such as a manhole is less than 3 ft away—the fracture‑and‑seat method that depends on aggregate interlock must be abandoned in favor of an alternative design. Likewise, sonic vibratory equipment may only be employed if the fracturing will not cause soil liquefaction of the subgrade; otherwise another technique must be selected. Additionally, any equipment that would produce extensive surface spalling, punch holes, or shatter the pavement is prohibited, prompting a different approach. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–415)

Design inputs and requirements

Aggregate interlock in existing concrete pavement rehabilitation is governed by the requirements set out in Section 522.3 Construction of the Rigid Pavements chapter. The specification mandates that fracturing equipment must be capable of variable‑force impact to create continuous full‑depth cracks while maintaining aggregate interlock and expressly prohibits tools that cause extensive surface spalling, punch holes, or shatter the slab; it also limits operations near utilities, structures, and fixed objects. After cracking, a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller with four to seven rubber wheels inflated to at least 100 psi must be used, applying a rolling pattern that provides at least three passes for Crack‑and‑Seat or five passes for Break‑and‑Seat methods to ensure proper seating and preservation of interlock. Finally, verification of the fractured layer’s elastic modulus must follow AASHTO T256 and ASTM D4694/D4695/D5858 procedures. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–415)

Design methods and criteria

Aggregate interlock is ensured by following the fracture‑and‑seat procedures set out in Section 522.3: a controlled variable‑force impact equipment must be used to produce full‑depth cracks while preserving interlock, preceded by sawcutting slabs to no more than 20 ft length and to within ±1 in of two‑thirds slab depth; a minimum 1,000‑foot test section is established to fine‑tune the breaking pattern, after which fractured slabs are seated with a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic tire roller applying at least three passes for the Crack and Seat method or five passes for the Break and Seat method, and the resulting elastic modulus of the broken concrete layer is verified by falling weight deflectometer testing and back‑calculated with approved software such as MODULUS or EVERCALC in accordance with AASHTO T256, ASTM D4694, D4695 and D5858. [1]

For slab‑fracturing work the design must preserve aggregate interlock, so equipment must be capable of impacting the pavement with a variable force and the force and point of impact must be controlled to produce continuous full‑depth cracking across the full width while maintaining aggregate interlock between pieces; equipment that will cause extensive surface spalling, punch holes in the pavement, or cause shattering of the pavement may not be used. These criteria are set out in Section 522.3 of the Rigid Pavements specifications to ensure proper load transfer after fracturing. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–415)

Structural details and interfaces

Aggregate interlock is achieved by creating continuous full-depth cracks across the pavement width using equipment whose force and impact point are controlled so that the broken pieces remain interlocked; before fracturing all joint sealants must be removed and any exposed steel plates in transverse joints taken out, and existing slabs are sawcut to no more than 20 ft in length across the lane. Fracturing is prohibited within 10 ft of utilities or 40 ft of structure approaches (and 3 ft of fixed objects), ensuring that the interlocking fragments are not compromised by nearby features. After cracking, a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller must seat the fractured slab with multiple passes to secure the interlocked pieces before overlay. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–415)

Design of the interfaces for aggregate interlock during slab fracturing requires that the impact force be controlled so that continuous full‑depth cracks are produced while preserving interlock between the broken pieces. Fracturing must be avoided within ten feet horizontally and ten feet from the bottom of the slab at culverts, cross pipes, and utility sleeves, within forty feet of bridge or tunnel approaches, and within three feet of any fixed object; in those prohibited zones the pavement is instead fully saw‑cut to isolate segments. The saw cuts are limited to a maximum length of twenty feet across the lane width and are made to a depth of plus or minus one inch of two‑thirds of the slab thickness, producing full‑depth panels that are later segmented into pieces approximately five feet longitudinally by six feet transversely. This approach ensures that aggregate interlock is maintained where cracking occurs while providing controlled, small‑scale interfaces around adjacent structures and utilities. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–415)

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Aggregate interlock must be preserved by using equipment that delivers a variable force and avoids spalling, punching holes, or shattering the concrete while producing continuous full‑depth cracks across the entire width; sawcutting is required on slabs longer than twenty feet with a depth of plus or minus one inch of two‑thirds of the slab thickness across the lane width, but slabs shorter than twenty feet need no cut. Fracturing operations must stay at least ten feet horizontally and ten feet vertically from any culvert, cross pipe, or utility sleeve, at least forty feet from bridge approaches, decks, tunnels, or other structures, and at least three feet from fixed objects such as manholes or inlets; where fracturing is prohibited, the uncracked panels must be sawcut to full depth into five‑foot longitudinal by six‑foot transverse segments. Seating must be performed with a minimum fifty‑ton pneumatic tire roller meeting specified tire pressure and wheel count, and rollers may not be used under wet conditions or on vibratory types; traffic is prohibited on the fractured and seated surface except for limited crossing to access ramps. All of these geometric clearances, equipment specifications, and traffic controls are required to ensure that aggregate interlock is maintained while protecting safety and accessibility during pavement rehabilitation. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–415)

Verification and expected performance

The completed aggregate interlock is verified first by a visual check that the full‑depth cracks are continuous across the width and that all cracks are visible on the top surface, confirming that the fracturing equipment has maintained interlock between pieces. After seating, the roller pass is considered satisfactory only when vertical deflection under the 50‑ton pneumatic tire roller can be observed visually indicating no movement of the substrate, and any non‑movement condition triggers base repair before proceeding. Performance is then confirmed with falling weight deflectometer testing on a minimum one‑thousand‑foot test section (and on at least three such lots per lane mile) to back‑calculate the elastic modulus of the fractured layer and ensure it falls within the range specified in Table A; if the average modulus exceeds the upper limit, the breaking pattern must be adjusted and retested. Compliance with project requirements is demonstrated by submitting the fracturing plan, equipment list, test results, and any corrective actions to the Representative for approval before continuing work. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–415)

For slab‑fracturing projects the performance tied to aggregate interlock is that the fractured and seated concrete must retain sufficient structural stiffness, which is judged by an average elastic modulus of the fractured layer staying within the range specified in Table A for Section 522.1; this stiffness is measured with falling weight deflectometer (FWD) testing at intervals of 50 feet or less using a 9,000‑pound drop weight and back‑calculated according to AASHTO T256, ASTM D4694, D4695, D5858 and approved software; the final seating is deemed satisfactory when visual observation shows no vertical deflection of the substrate under the roller, confirming that aggregate interlock has been maintained. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 413–415)
