Aggregate Interlock

Part of: Load Transfer is-subtopic-of
Aggregate Interlock is a topic under Load Transfer.
aggregate-interlock
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Aggregate Interlock is a slab‑fracture rehabilitation strategy that preserves load transfer and overall pavement stability by producing continuous full‑depth cracks across the entire width of a concrete slab while keeping the broken aggregate pieces interlocked so they continue to act together under traffic loads [1]. The method requires variable‑force impact equipment that avoids extensive surface spalling, punched holes, or shattering, and follows with seating using a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller to maintain the interlock and prevent substrate movement [1]. Performance is verified through visual inspection of surface cracks and falling‑weight deflectometer testing to confirm that the fractured slab meets prescribed elastic modulus limits, ensuring the required load‑transfer reliability of the interlocked aggregate [1].

Purpose and design objectives

Aggregate Interlock is required to solve the problem of preserving load transfer and slab stability after a concrete pavement has been fractured; the design calls for equipment that creates continuous full‑depth cracks across the entire width yet keeps the aggregate pieces interlocked so the fractured slabs can still act together under traffic loads. By maintaining this interlock, the pavement retains structural integrity and avoids excessive movement or failure of individual fragments during and after seating. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

To satisfy its functional role of load transfer, the fracturing operation must produce a continuous full‑depth crack while maintaining aggregate interlock between pieces; structurally it must avoid any equipment that causes extensive surface spalling, punched holes, or shattering of the pavement so that the interlocked aggregate remains intact. Durability is addressed by requiring that all cracks be visible on the top surface and that the seated slab exhibit no substrate movement as shown by visual observation of vertical deflection under the roller. Safety objectives include protecting adjacent utilities, bridge decks, and other structures by prohibiting damage during fracturing and seating. Serviceability is verified through post‑seating FWD testing to confirm the fractured concrete layer meets the prescribed elastic modulus range. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Scope and applicability

For rehabilitation of existing rigid (concrete) pavements using slab‑fracture methods, aggregate interlock is appropriate when the pavement is fractured with equipment that can apply a variable force and produce continuous full‑depth cracks without causing extensive surface spalling, punching holes, or shattering, thereby preserving interlock between pieces; the subsequent seating operation must employ a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller to compact the broken slabs while maintaining this interlock. The approach is limited to crack‑and‑seat or break‑and‑seat applications on concrete slabs where subgrade conditions permit use of sonic vibratory equipment only if liquefaction will not occur, and all work must follow the prescribed test sections and rolling patterns. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

When site conditions prevent maintaining aggregate interlock—such as when culverts, cross pipes or utility sleeves lie within 10 feet horizontally and 10 feet from the slab bottom, when approaches to structures, bridge decks or tunnels are within 40 feet, when fixed objects like manholes are within 3 feet, when sonic vibratory equipment would risk soil liquefaction of the subgrade, or when only equipment that would cause extensive surface spalling, punch holes, or shatter the pavement is available—the prescribed fracture‑and‑seat method cannot be used and an alternate design approach must be adopted; similarly, if satisfactory fracture results are not obtained (e.g., elastic modulus outside the specified range), operations must cease and an alternate action plan submitted. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Design inputs and requirements

Under Section 522.3 of the Rigid Pavements chapter, slab‑fracturing work must employ equipment that can apply a variable force to produce continuous full‑depth cracks while maintaining aggregate interlock between pieces and may not cause extensive surface spalling, punch holes, or shatter the pavement; after fracturing, seating is required with a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller whose tires are inflated to at least 100 psi and that makes at least three passes for Crack‑and‑Seat or five passes for Break‑and‑Seat to ensure proper interlock; the fractured slab’s performance is then verified on a minimum 1,000‑foot test section using falling weight deflectometer testing performed in accordance with AASHTO T 256, ASTM D4694, D4695 and D5858, and the elastic modulus must fall within the range specified in Table A before production fracturing may continue. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Design methods and criteria

Aggregate interlock is preserved by following the construction procedures outlined in Section 522.3: equipment must be capable of delivering a variable‑force impact to produce continuous full‑depth cracks while maintaining interlock, and a minimum of one 1,000‑foot‑long test section is established to fine‑tune the fracturing pattern; after cracking, a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic tire roller seats the pieces with the required number of passes, and the resulting fractured slab is verified by falling weight deflectometer testing (using a 9,000‑pound drop weight at ≤50‑foot intervals) whose back‑calculated elastic modulus is obtained with approved software such as MODULUS or EVERCALC per AASHTO T 256, ASTM D4694, D4695 and D5858. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

For slab‑fracturing operations the design criteria require that the fracturing equipment be capable of delivering a variable, controlled force so that continuous full‑depth cracking across the entire width is achieved while maintaining aggregate interlock between pieces; any equipment that would cause extensive surface spalling, punch holes, or shatter the pavement must not be used, and the resulting fractured units must stay within the size limits set out in Table A, with final seating considered satisfactory only when visual inspection shows no substrate movement, thereby ensuring the required load‑transfer reliability of the interlock. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Structural details and interfaces

Aggregate Interlock for slab fracturing requires that the impact equipment be controlled so that continuous full‑depth cracks are produced across the entire width while preserving interlock between aggregate pieces; any existing joint seals must be removed and any exposed reinforcing steel or steel plates in transverse joints must be taken out to avoid interference with the interlocking action. No special load‑transfer devices are prescribed, but sawcutting may be performed to limit slab length to 20 ft where required. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

To preserve aggregate interlock at the interfaces, the fracturing equipment must be capable of delivering a variable force that creates continuous full‑depth cracks across the slab while maintaining aggregate interlock between pieces. Fracturing is prohibited within 10 feet horizontally and 10 feet from the bottom of the slab of any culvert, cross pipe or utility sleeve, within 40 feet of bridge, tunnel or other structure approaches, and within 3 feet of fixed objects such as manholes; in those zones the pavement must instead be full‑depth sawcut into small segments. Where fracturing is not allowed, the uncracked panels are sawcut to full depth forming 5‑foot by 6‑foot pieces to control the interface geometry before any further treatment. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

To preserve aggregate interlock during slab fracturing the design must provide adequate drainage by installing pavement base drains and outlets before any cracking is performed. Moisture conditions are controlled by prohibiting roller seating when the surface is wet and by covering the fractured and seated pavement so that it is not exposed to freezing temperatures, while visual water application is limited to inspection only. Climate limits are observed by restricting falling‑weight deflectometer testing to air temperatures between 45 °F and 90 °F and by avoiding sonic vibratory equipment when it could cause soil liquefaction of the subgrade. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Aggregate interlock must be preserved by using impact equipment that can vary force but shall not cause extensive surface spalling, punch holes, or shattering of the pavement; pre‑fracturing operations must keep all sawcuts no longer than twenty feet across a lane and cut to a depth of plus or minus one inch of two‑thirds slab depth. Fracturing may not be performed within ten feet horizontally and ten feet from the bottom of culverts, cross pipes, or utility sleeves, nor within forty feet of approaches to structures, bridge decks, or tunnels, and must stay at least three feet from any fixed object such as a manhole or inlet. Prohibit traffic on the roadway after the pavement has been fractured and seated except for crossing over the fractured lane to gain access to ramps, and remove all exposed reinforcing steel or steel plates before seating. Seating shall use a minimum fifty‑ton pneumatic tire roller with at least three passes for Crack and Seat (or five passes for Break and Seat) to ensure continuous contact without vibratory rollers. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Verification and expected performance

The completed aggregate interlock is verified by first submitting a pavement fracturing plan and equipment list for approval, ensuring the chosen impact equipment can produce continuous full‑depth cracks while maintaining aggregate interlock between pieces; a minimum of one 1,000‑foot‑long test section is then established to confirm the fracture pattern, and visual inspection must show all cracks on the surface. After fracturing, seating is performed with a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller that meets the gross mass requirement and tire‑pressure specifications, applying a rolling pattern that provides at least three passes for Crack and Seat or at least five passes for Break and Seat methods. Performance compliance is demonstrated through Falling Weight Deflectometer testing conducted per AASHTO T 256, ASTM D4694, D4695, and D5858, with the average elastic modulus of the fractured concrete layer required to fall within the range specified in Table A; any deviation triggers adjustment of the breaking pattern and retesting. Ongoing compliance is maintained by testing at least three 1,000‑foot lots per lane mile, submitting FWD results within seven calendar days for Representative approval, and confirming that seating produces no visible substrate movement before proceeding to the final asphalt overlay. [1]

[1] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)
