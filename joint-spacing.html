Joint Spacing

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Joint Spacing is a topic under Rigid Pavement.
joint-spacingjoint-spacing-requirementsslab-lengthslab-lengthsjoint-placement
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

Joint spacing is the intentional placement of transverse and longitudinal joints at prescribed intervals within concrete pavements so that shrinkage, temperature‑induced expansion, and load‑transfer movements are confined to defined planes rather than developing as random cracks, thereby maintaining structural integrity, durability and traffic safety[1][2]. The required joint layout is developed in a project plan—or an approved layout when none is shown—before concrete placement, and the joints are created by saw‑cutting to approximately one‑third slab thickness to provide the intended crack‑control planes[1][2]. Spacing limits vary with pavement type and slab thickness—for example, reinforced concrete pavements may require 30 ft intervals while plain concrete slabs often use 15 ft, and overlay panels are limited to a maximum dimension of 18 times the overlay thickness with an aspect‑ratio not exceeding 1.5—to ensure functional load transfer, prevent uncontrolled cracking, and keep longitudinal joints out of wheel paths[1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Joint spacing is employed to control the development of uncontrolled cracks in concrete pavements by providing designated locations where cracks can be initiated, thereby supporting the intended service life of the pavement. It also serves to control where contraction and expansion occur so that the slab cracks in a predictable pattern rather than developing random, uncontrolled cracking; locating transverse and longitudinal joints at specified intervals and sawing them promptly creates designated planes for shrinkage movement and load transfer, preserving pavement integrity and durability. In precast concrete pavements joint spacing is used to accommodate the slab’s response to temperature changes and to control cracking by providing defined locations where movement can occur without damaging the structure. For unbonded concrete overlays joint spacing is required to keep overlay panels within dimensional limits that would otherwise lead to premature or random cracking and to keep longitudinal joints out of wheel paths; when a project plan does not specify spacing, a joint layout plan must be developed and approved before concrete placement so that panel sizes stay within the recommended limits and avoid shapes prone to early cracking. In bonded concrete overlays joint spacing is prescribed to limit panel size so that stresses do not cause premature or random cracking, especially under traffic loads; panels shall not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness and their aspect ratio shall not exceed 1.5, longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths, and the layout must be developed and sawed at the proper time to create controlled joints that prevent random cracking of the slab. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–474)

Joint spacing in concrete pavement systems is prescribed to satisfy a range of performance objectives including functional load transfer, structural integrity, durability against cracking, safety for traffic, and serviceability. For concrete shoulders used in rigid‑pavement rehabilitation joint spacing shall not exceed 20 ft; this limit is intended to provide lateral support to the existing pavement and keep the shoulder relatively maintenance‑free, thereby meeting structural integrity and durability objectives [1]. In jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) the design calls for 30‑ft joint spacing with perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, whereas jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) requires 15‑ft joint spacing with the same joint orientation and load‑transfer devices, and these provisions satisfy the Publication 72M load‑transfer objective while supporting functional load transfer, structural integrity, durability against cracking, safety for traffic, and serviceability [1]. When a single lane of at least 500 ft is being reconstructed new joints shall be aligned with existing joints and an intermediate joint must be placed if the existing joint spacing exceeds 20 ft in order to meet current joint‑spacing requirements [1]. For roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavement thinner than 8 in control joints shall be spaced between 15 ft and 20 ft, while for RCC thicknesses of 8 in or greater the spacing shall equal the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 ft, a rule that provides durability by initiating crack locations and functional/structural alignment with adjacent concrete mainline shoulders [1]. In plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements covered by Section 501 longitudinal joints shall be planned so that they never cross a driving lane or conflict with final pavement markings, thereby ensuring safe traffic flow; transverse contraction joints shall be spaced at least ten feet apart to limit cracking and preserve durability; each transverse joint shall be centered over dowel bars within ±1 in to provide proper load transfer, satisfying structural performance; and contraction joints shall be sawed to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness and a width of 1/8 in to deliver controlled movement and acceptable surface smoothness for serviceability [2]. For full‑depth precast/prestressed concrete pavement slabs addressed in Section 529 the width of any transverse joint shall not exceed ½ in and shall be at least ¼ in, while longitudinal joints shall not exceed ¾ in; these limits control expansion‑contraction movement, limit vertical differential to protect structural integrity, and provide adequate load transfer across joints for durability and safety [2]. The mix design for continuous prestressed precast paving systems must account for ambient average daily temperatures and exposure conditions at the project site in order to determine appropriate panel size and expansion‑joint locations, thereby ensuring that the pavement can expand and contract without distress [2]. In unbonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements (Section 548) joint spacing shall follow the locations shown on the project plans or, when none are provided, a department‑approved joint layout must be prepared before concrete placement to control slab movement and limit random cracking; longitudinal joints shall be kept out of wheel paths for safety; panel dimensions shall be limited so that no dimension exceeds 18 times the overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter side ratio shall not exceed 1.5, requirements intended to meet structural durability and serviceability, and any concrete panels that crack outside the sawed joints are considered defective [2]. In bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements (Section 540) joint spacing shall follow the layout shown on the project plans or an approved joint layout plan when none is provided; panel dimensions shall be limited so that their maximum dimension does not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter side ratio shall not exceed 1.5; longitudinal joints shall be avoided in wheel paths, thereby providing structural control of cracking, durability against premature failure, safety by keeping joints out of traffic‑bearing zones, and serviceability through consistent panel geometry [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–474)

Scope and applicability

Joint spacing is appropriate for concrete shoulders that are installed as part of a rigid‑pavement rehabilitation without widening; in this context the joint spacing shall not exceed 20 ft (section 2.6) [1]. It is also applied when reconstructing Portland cement concrete pavements over lengths of at least 500 feet, where the design follows Publication 72M RC‑21M for jointed reinforced concrete pavement with a 30‑foot joint spacing or RC‑27M for jointed plain concrete pavement with a 15‑foot joint spacing; both require perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals [1]. When only a single lane of 500 feet or more is reconstructed, the new joints should be aligned with existing joints and an intermediate joint must be placed if the existing joint spacing exceeds 20 ft to satisfy the current joint‑spacing requirements shown on RC‑27M [1]. For widening or adding lanes to existing uniform‑depth rigid pavements built since 1945, transverse joints in the new section must follow the pattern of the original pavement; if the original joint spacing is 20 ft or less, the widened slabs are laid out with the same length, whereas if the original spacing exceeds 20 ft the addition shall be placed as plain cement concrete with joint spacing set between a minimum of 10 ft and a maximum of 17 ft, inserting intermediate joints as needed to keep slab lengths roughly equal [1]. When placing an unbonded concrete overlay over existing Portland cement concrete pavement, the joints are laid out like those of a new concrete pavement and do not need to line up with the underlying transverse joints; the required spacing is determined by the type and thickness of the concrete being placed as referenced in Table 10.10 [1]. For roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) that may be used as concrete shoulders, temporary pavements such as median crossovers or widening areas, and as the final wearing course or base course on roadways with an average daily traffic ≤ 2000 and a speed limit ≤ 55 mph, control joints are provided when required; RCC sections less than 8 inches thick should have joints spaced every 15 to 20 ft, while RCC sections 8 inches or greater use a spacing obtained by multiplying the pavement thickness by 2.5 (feet) [1]. Joint spacing is also appropriate for plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements constructed with the slip‑form paving method, where transverse or longitudinal contraction joints are required; in new multilane pavements transverse joints are cut perpendicular to the centerline and dowel bars or load‑transfer units are installed, and tie‑bars must be kept at least 24 inches away from a dowelled contraction joint [2]. In these slip‑form projects the joints are spaced as indicated on the project plans and special provisions, and when no spacing is shown a Department‑approved joint layout plan must be developed and laid out before concrete placement [2]. Joint spacing applies to unbonded concrete overlays placed over existing concrete‑surfaced pavements; overlay panels are sized according to lane geometry and concrete placement width, must be laid out prior to placement in accordance with the project plans or an approved layout plan, and panel dimensions shall not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness with a longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio not exceeding 1.5, while longitudinal joints should be avoided in wheel paths [2]. For bonded concrete overlays of Class AA cement concrete placed over existing asphalt‑surfaced rigid pavements, joint spacing follows the project plans or an approved layout plan, longitudinal joints shall not be located in wheel paths, and the same panel size limits (maximum dimension ≤ 18 times overlay thickness and aspect ratio ≤ 1.5) apply [2]. Joint spacing is further applied during joint rehabilitation of rigid concrete pavements after spall repairs; a sealant reservoir is sawn to the width and depth indicated for the existing joint spacing, centered on the joint opening, flushed with water, fitted with a tape bond breaker that must not extend more than 1/8 inch up the reservoir face, and sealed after media blasting the vertical faces at 100 psi; this procedure is used for joints whose spalls are 2 inches or less, and existing wide joints may require cleaning to the required depth, water‑blasting with a minimum capacity of 10 gpm at 7,000 psi, adding backing material as needed, and removal of any steel plate within the reservoir with no more than 1/4 inch protruding [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–474)

In the concrete‑pavement patching context, when the deteriorated area extends roughly 500 feet and the remaining intact pavement between intermittent patches is ten feet or less, the preferred solution shifts from patch‑by‑patch joint‑spacing adjustments to complete removal and reconstruction of the entire section. If a full‑width reconstruction of all adjacent lanes is required for five hundred feet or more, the design must follow the standards that prescribe either thirty‑foot joint spacing for jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) or fifteen‑foot joint spacing for jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP), rather than relying on existing joint layouts. For a single‑lane reconstruction of five hundred feet or more, new joints should line up with existing ones, but an intermediate joint must be inserted whenever the pre‑existing joint spacing exceeds twenty feet to satisfy current joint‑spacing requirements. In these situations the choice of pavement type may also consider that reinforcement in JRCP does not add additional strength. When the existing pavement’s original transverse joint spacing is greater than 20 feet, the widening or lane addition should not simply copy that spacing; instead the design shall use Jointed Plain Cement Concrete (JPCP) and adjust the joint spacing to a minimum of 10 feet and a maximum of 17 feet, adding intermediate joints as needed so slab lengths are roughly equal. When the original transverse joint spacing is 20 feet or less, the widening may retain the same slab length as the existing pavement, making the standard joint‑spacing approach appropriate. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–96)

Design inputs and requirements

For concrete shoulders added in a rigid‑pavement rehabilitation that are intended for lateral support or low‑maintenance purposes, the joint spacing shall not exceed 20 ft as required by Chapter 2 Section 2.6 B.1.a. When a concrete pavement is reconstructed, the reinforced‑concrete design (RC‑21M) requires a joint spacing of 30 ft with perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, whereas the plain‑concrete design (RC‑27M) requires a joint spacing of 15 ft with the same orientation and load‑transfer details. If only one lane is reconstructed for a length of 500 ft or more, new joints must align with existing joints and an intermediate joint shall be placed when the existing joint spacing exceeds 20 ft to satisfy the RC‑27M requirement. All reconstruction work must also comply with Publication 408 specifications (e.g., Sections 516 and 519). For widening or lane additions, transverse joints on the added section shall be aligned with those on the original pavement; when the existing transverse joint spacing is 20 ft or less, the widened portion shall use slabs of the same length as the original, while if the existing spacing exceeds 20 ft the design shall employ Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement (JPCP) and adjust the joint spacing so that each slab length is at least 10 ft and no more than 17 ft, adding intermediate joints as needed to keep slab lengths approximately equal. Unbonded concrete overlays are governed by RC‑20M, which requires the overlay joints to be constructed in the same manner as joints in new concrete pavements; the transverse joints of the overlay do not have to line up with existing pavement joints and no attempt should be made to match them, with the actual spacing determined by the type and thickness of the concrete as shown in Table 10.10 of the PCCP guidelines. For roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements, control joints in shoulders shall align with the adjacent concrete mainline and follow the spacing rules set out in RC‑27M; for temporary median crossovers and roadways limited to an ADT of ≤ 2000 and a speed limit of ≤ 55 mph, either the publication may be referenced or a thickness‑based rule applied: RCC sections less than 8 inches thick shall have control joints spaced 15 ft to 20 ft, while sections 8 inches or greater shall have spacing equal to 2.5 times the pavement thickness in feet. Under Section 501.3 of the Rigid Pavements specifications, transverse joints shall be placed at least ten feet apart and a transverse joint shall not be constructed within ten feet of another transverse joint; this restriction also applies after any construction interruption longer than thirty minutes. Tie‑bars must not be installed within twenty‑four inches of a dowelled contraction joint and must not be placed within thirty inches of a transverse joint. When pavement is removed and replaced, the minimum length between transverse joints shall be ten feet for reinforced cement concrete pavements or six feet for plain cement concrete pavements. Section 529.3 limits full‑depth precast concrete pavement slabs to a maximum length of fifteen feet (twenty feet if prestressed) and a minimum length of six feet, with slab widths determined by the longitudinal joint layout shown on the shop drawings; transverse joints shall be between one‑quarter inch and one‑half inch wide and longitudinal joints shall not exceed three‑quarters inch. The placement of expansion joints shall be based on thermal‑expansion calculations using the coefficients in the AASHTO Mechanistic‑Empirical Pavement Design Guide, and for continuous prestressed precast systems the mix design must account for ambient average daily temperatures and exposure conditions at the project site to determine appropriate panel size and expansion joint locations. For unbonded concrete overlays, Section 548.3 requires that joint spacing follow the layout shown on the project plans and special provisions, and if no joint spacing is indicated a joint‑layout plan shall be developed and accepted by the Department before concrete placement; longitudinal joints shall be avoided in wheel paths and panel dimensions shall not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness, with the longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio not exceeding 1.5, allowing only slight deviations to match adjacent structures or prevent premature cracking. For bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements, Section 540.3 likewise mandates that joint spacing follow the distances shown on the project plans and any special provisions, with a department‑approved joint layout plan required when spacing is not indicated; panel dimensions shall not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio shall not exceed 1.5, and all joints shall be cut only with a saw using a green‑cut blade to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness and a minimum width of one‑eighth inch. Joint rehabilitation work is governed by Section 513 within Section 500, which requires that a sealant reservoir be cut to the width and depth specified for the existing joint spacing, that a tape bond breaker not extend up the reservoir face more than one‑eighth inch, that water blasting equipment have at least a ten‑gallon‑per‑minute flow at seven thousand pounds per square inch when cleaning wide joints, and that vertical faces be media‑blasted followed by compressed‑air cleaning at one hundred pounds per square inch before sealing as required by Section 501.3(n). [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–474)

Design methods and criteria

In reconstruction projects where all adjacent traffic lanes of at least 500 feet are rebuilt with Portland cement concrete, the design follows Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards and applies RC‑21M for jointed reinforced concrete pavement, which prescribes a 30‑foot transverse joint spacing with perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals. For jointed plain concrete pavement under the same reconstruction conditions, RC‑27M requires a 15‑foot transverse joint spacing together with the same joint characteristics. When only one lane of at least 500 feet is reconstructed, new transverse joints must be aligned with existing joints and an intermediate joint shall be provided whenever the pre‑existing spacing exceeds 20 feet in order to satisfy the current spacing criteria set out in RC‑27M. For widening or lane‑addition projects, transverse joints are required to align with those on the original pavement; if the original joint spacing is 20 feet or less, slabs of the same length as the original are used. If the original joint spacing exceeds 20 feet, the design shall employ jointed plain concrete pavement and adjust the joint spacing to a minimum of 10 feet and a maximum of 17 feet, inserting intermediate joints as necessary so that slab lengths in the widened portion are approximately equal. For unbonded concrete overlays, joint spacing is selected from Table 10.10 based on the type and thickness of the existing pavement rather than by an explicit equation, and the joints are placed using the same procedures prescribed for new concrete pavements in Publication 72M RC‑20M. In roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements, joint spacing follows a thickness‑based rule: when slab thickness is less than 8 inches, control joints are spaced between 15 feet and 20 feet; when slab thickness is 8 inches or greater, the spacing is obtained by multiplying the pavement thickness expressed in feet by 2.5. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs, the width of any transverse joint between slabs shall be a minimum of 1/4 inch and shall not exceed 1/2 inch along its entire length, while longitudinal joints must not exceed 3/4 inch. The placement of joints for full‑depth precast or prestressed slabs is governed by the layout shown on the project plans; if no joint spacing is indicated, a joint‑layout plan must be prepared and accepted by the Department before concrete placement. For bonded and unbonded concrete overlays of existing pavements, joint spacing likewise follows the layout indicated on the project plans, and when none is provided a department‑approved joint layout plan shall be developed; panel dimensions are limited to not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness and the ratio of the longer to shorter panel dimension shall not exceed 1.5. The FHWA HIPERPAV software or an equivalent approved program may be used to assist in developing the project‑specific joint layout and sawing window, although the primary procedure remains plan‑based development and departmental review. Saw cutting is the only acceptable method for creating joints, performed with a green‑cut saw to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness. For existing concrete pavements where longitudinal grooving is employed to control joint spacing, grooves shall be 3/32 inch wide with a tolerance of plus 1/16 inch, spaced 3/4 inch center‑to‑center, and cut to at least a depth of 3/16 inch; grooves may not be placed within 6 inches of the outside edge or the centreline pavement joint, may not overlap, and must not be made with flailing equipment. After grooving, all slurry or residue must be removed in a continuous operation without allowing it to flow across traffic lanes or into drainage facilities, and the surface must be cleaned before reopening to traffic. [1][2]

In rehabilitation projects that provide concrete shoulders for lateral support of a rigid pavement, the joint spacing of those concrete shoulders shall not exceed 20 ft [1]. When reconstructing all adjacent lanes for 500 feet or more of Portland cement concrete, the design must follow Publication 72M RC‑21M, which requires a 30‑foot joint spacing together with perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, or alternatively RC‑27M, which requires a 15‑foot joint spacing with the same joint orientation, dowel bar coating and sealing [1]. For reconstruction of only one lane over the same length, new joints must be aligned with existing joints and an intermediate joint must be placed whenever the existing joint spacing exceeds 20 feet to satisfy the current joint spacing requirements of RC‑27M [1]. In widening and lane addition projects, transverse joints in the new section shall be aligned with the existing pavement; if the original transverse joint spacing is 20 feet or less, the widened slabs shall have the same length as on the original pavement, whereas when the original spacing exceeds 20 feet the design shall use jointed plain concrete pavement and adjust the joint spacing to a minimum of 10 feet and a maximum of 17 feet [1]. For unbonded concrete overlays, joints shall be placed in the same manner as joints in new concrete pavements and need not align with existing transverse joints; the joint spacing shall be selected based on the type and thickness of the concrete pavement being constructed [1]. Under roller‑compacted concrete provisions, control joints for RCC pavements less than 8 inches thick should be spaced 15 feet to 20 feet, while for RCC pavements 8 inches or greater the spacing shall equal the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet; additionally, for shoulders the control joints should match the spacing and alignment of the adjacent concrete mainline [1]. When the slip‑form paving method is used for plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements, joint spacing must be controlled so that a new transverse joint is never placed within 10 feet of an existing transverse joint [2]. If defective pavement is removed and replaced, the replacement work must retain at least a 10‑foot length between transverse joints for reinforced cement concrete pavements or at least a 6‑foot length for plain cement concrete, thereby preserving the required spacing [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements, joint spacing shall follow the project plans and special provisions; if no joint spacing is indicated, a joint layout plan must be developed and accepted by the Department before placement of concrete, and longitudinal joints are to be avoided in wheel paths [2]. For bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements, joint spacing shall also follow the project plans and special provisions, with the same requirement for a pre‑approved layout plan when none is indicated and avoidance of longitudinal joints in wheel paths [2]. In both overlay types, maximum panel dimensions should not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness, and the ratio of the longer panel dimension to the shorter panel dimension should not exceed 1.5 [2]. Sawing is the only acceptable technique for creating concrete overlay joints, with each saw cut extending to a depth of D/3 and a minimum width of 1/8 inch [2].

Structural section and dimensions

Joint spacing requirements for concrete shoulders in rigid‑pavement rehabilitation projects are limited to a maximum of 20 ft, as stipulated in the Pavement Policy Manual.[1] For reconstruction of jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) the manual requires a 30‑foot joint spacing, while jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) is limited to a 15‑foot joint spacing; if only one lane is reconstructed and the existing joint spacing exceeds 20 ft, an intermediate joint must be installed to meet current requirements.[1] When widening or adding lanes, transverse joint spacing must follow the original pavement: if the existing transverse joints are spaced at 20 ft or less, new slabs shall match those lengths, whereas when the original spacing exceeds 20 ft the widened portion shall use JPCP with joint spacing adjusted to a minimum of 10 ft and a maximum of 17 ft, adding intermediate joints as needed.[1] For roller‑compacted concrete pavement, control joints in sections less than 8 in thick shall be spaced between 15 ft and 20 ft, while for sections 8 in or thicker the spacing is calculated by multiplying the thickness in inches by 2.5 to obtain a spacing in feet; the manual provides no dimensions for base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab thickness, edge‑support, or reinforcement.[1] Under the slip‑form paving method, transverse joints may not be placed within 10 ft of another transverse joint and longitudinal joints shall run parallel to and within 12 in of the final pavement markings unless the construction drawings specify otherwise; for non‑standard slab lengths reinforcement tie bars must be located at least 24 in from any dowelled contraction joint, and no other explicit dimensions are given for base, subbase, treated‑soil, shoulder, edge‑support or additional reinforcement.[2] In full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs the transverse joint gap between adjacent slabs shall not exceed 1/2 inch and shall be at least 1/4 inch along its entire length, while each longitudinal joint may not exceed 3/4 inch in width; again no dimensions are prescribed for base, subbase, treated‑soil, shoulder, edge‑support or other reinforcement.[2] For both bonded and unbonded concrete overlays the maximum panel dimension shall not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the ratio of the longer side to the shorter side shall not exceed 1.5, with no explicit dimensional requirements for underlying pavement, base, subbase, treated‑soil, shoulder, edge‑support or reinforcement.[2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–474)

Where a reconstruction segment extends five hundred feet or more, the designer must first perform a pavement design analysis to decide whether a thicker slab depth is required [1]. For jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) the standard calls for thirty‑foot perpendicular joints with coated dowel bars, while jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) uses fifteen‑foot perpendicular joints with coated dowel bars; if only one lane is rebuilt, the new joints must line up with existing joints and an intermediate joint shall be placed whenever the existing spacing exceeds twenty feet [1]. Tie bars or tie bolts are required only for concrete patches longer than sixty‑five feet, and they are installed along longitudinal joints as shown on the shop drawings for continuous precast sections greater than sixty‑five feet [1][2]. For roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements thicknesses may be any value between four inches and eighteen inches as determined using the current PennDOT concrete pavement design procedures, and the source does not provide guidance on layer configurations, cross slopes, transitions, or tie‑ins for RCC pavements [1]. Joint spacing for RCC is derived from the selected thickness: when the RCC slab is less than eight inches thick, control joints should be placed at intervals of fifteen feet to twenty feet; when the slab is eight inches or greater, the joint spacing equals the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet [1]. Full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slabs are cast with a maximum length of fifteen feet (twenty feet if prestressed) and a minimum length of six feet, and each slab is made one quarter inch thicker than the nominal design thickness to provide sacrificial concrete; these dimensions satisfy the joint spacing limits set out in Section 529.3(c) [2]. The transverse joint width between slabs must not exceed one half inch and must be at least one quarter inch along its full length, while longitudinal joints may not exceed three quarters inch; joint locations are laid out during surveying to align with these spacing rules and with existing pavement joints [2]. Cross‑slope continuity after installing precast slabs is achieved by diamond grinding the surface because the delivered slab elevation is set at least one quarter inch higher than surrounding pavement [1]. Cross‑slope and elevation control for slab placement are obtained by fine‑grading the compacted bedding material with a laser‑controlled screeding device to keep surface variation within required tolerances before slab placement [2]. Any vertical drop greater than two inches between new and existing pavement is bridged with a temporary asphalt wedge transition [2].

Materials and mixture design

Joint spacing specifications shall identify the concrete material type and any associated joint treatments required for each pavement category. For jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) the specification must state a transverse‑joint spacing of 30 feet, require coated dowel bars and appropriate sealants at those joints, and shall be based on the standard Portland cement concrete defined in Publication 408 Section 704. For jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) the specification must state a transverse‑joint spacing of 15 feet, also require coated dowel bars and appropriate sealants, and likewise shall use the standard PCC mix. When the work involves widening or lane addition, the material to be used shall be Class AA cement concrete unless an exemption is granted; widening of JPCP shall be constructed with plain cement concrete, while JRCP may be widened with either reinforced or plain cement concrete. The transverse‑joint layout for the new section shall follow the original pavement: if the existing transverse‑joint spacing is 20 feet or less, the slabs in the addition shall be the same length as on the existing pavement; if the existing spacing exceeds 20 feet, the joint spacing shall be adjusted to a minimum of 10 feet and a maximum of 17 feet with intermediate joints added as needed so that slab lengths are approximately equal. Joint spacing specifications for rehabilitation require placing a tape bond breaker on the bottom of the sealant reservoir and limiting the tape to extending no more than 1/8 inch up the reservoir face. The joint must be cleaned and sealed with the sealing material referenced in Section 501.3(n) after media blasting the vertical faces and cleaning them with a compressed‑air stream of 100 psi at the source; if water blasting is used, the blaster shall have a minimum capacity of 10 gallons per minute at 7,000 psi. When the existing joint width meets or exceeds the required reservoir width, joint backing material shall be placed to the depth required for the sealant reservoir, and any steel plate within the new reservoir may protrude no more than 1/4 inch. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–96); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 403)

In concrete‑pavement patching, joint spacing is dictated by the pavement type; a jointed reinforced concrete pavement calls for 30-foot spacing with perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and proper seals, whereas a jointed plain concrete pavement uses 15-foot spacing with the same joint details, and when only one lane is rebuilt new joints must line up with existing ones and an extra joint shall be added if the existing spacing exceeds 20 feet [1]. The concrete mix for these joints follows the standard PCC specifications (Publication 408 Section 704) and employs an optimized aggregate gradation; high early‑strength concrete should be avoided because of its potential for excessive shrinkage cracking, while accelerated concrete may be used when a rapid traffic opening is required, achieving strength in four to seven hours compared with the normal mix that reaches traffic‑opening strength in twenty-four to forty-eight hours [1]. For long‑term durability high‑performance dowel bars are recommended when the repaired pavement will remain in service for more than twenty years [1]. In widening or lane additions on rigid pavements the design shall use Class AA cement concrete unless an exemption is granted, and the transverse joint layout must follow the original pavement; if the existing transverse joint spacing is twenty feet or less the new slabs are constructed to the same length as the originals, whereas if the existing spacing exceeds twenty feet the widening shall be placed with jointed plain concrete pavement using a joint spacing no shorter than ten feet and no longer than seventeen feet, adding intermediate joints as needed so slab lengths remain roughly equal [1]. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs joint spacing is controlled by limiting joint widths while using a concrete mix that meets the required structural strengths; the mix design must be proven by trial batch testing to achieve at least 4,925 psi compressive strength and 650 psi flexural strength and must incorporate thermal‑expansion data for the project’s average daily temperatures so that panel size and expansion‑joint locations are appropriate [2]. Transverse joints shall not exceed 1/2 inch in width and shall be a minimum of 1/4 inch along their entire length, and longitudinal joints shall not exceed 3/4 inch, thereby providing the durability, constructability, and performance needed for the pavement system [2].

Structural details and interfaces

Joint spacing is defined differently for each pavement type and construction method. For concrete shoulders that are part of rehabilitation projects the joints shall not exceed 20 ft and no reinforcement, load‑transfer devices, transitions, connections or other structural details are prescribed [1]. In concrete pavement patching, jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) requires transverse joints spaced at 30 ft, placed perpendicular to traffic flow and provided with coated dowel bars and appropriate seals as required by RC‑21M; jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) uses 15‑ft perpendicular joints with coated dowel bars and seals in accordance with RC‑27M [1]. Load transfer at all transverse patch joints shall be achieved with dowels that are fully bonded along their embedded length and anchored to the existing slab using epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders [1]. When a single lane is reconstructed for lengths of 500 ft or more, the new joints shall align with the existing joints and an intermediate joint shall be placed if the existing spacing exceeds 20 ft [1]. Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only when a concrete patch exceeds 65 ft in length [1]. For widening or lane‑addition projects the transverse joints shall be aligned with those of the existing pavement; if the original transverse joint spacing is 20 ft or less, the added slabs shall have the same length as the original, whereas if the original spacing exceeds 20 ft the joint spacing in the addition shall be limited to a minimum of 10 ft and a maximum of 17 ft with intermediate joints added as needed [1]. Widening of JPCP shall be constructed with plain cement concrete, while JRCP may be widened with either reinforced or plain cement concrete, and the new portion shall be tied to the existing pavement to provide the required connection between old and new sections [1]. Roller‑compacted concrete pavements are designed without forms, steel reinforcement, or dowel bars, so no reinforcement or load‑transfer devices are required; control joints may be provided and, when specified, must be placed on shoulders to align with the adjacent mainline concrete joints, with spacing of 15 ft to 20 ft for RCC less than 8 in thick and a spacing calculated by multiplying pavement thickness (in inches) by 2.5 to obtain feet for RCC 8 inches or greater [1].

In slip‑form paving of rigid pavements the joint layout consists of transverse contraction joints and longitudinal joints that are coordinated with load‑transfer units (LTU) and reinforcement; transverse joints must be sawed perpendicular to the centreline across the full pavement width and dowel bars placed as shown on the Standard Drawings, while LTU assemblies are secured before paving and aligned so that vertical tilt or horizontal skew does not exceed 0.25 in at each end, longitudinal translation does not exceed 1 in and horizontal translation does not exceed 1 in [2]. Longitudinal joints are provided with tie bars as directed; for non‑standard slab lengths no tie bar may be installed within 24 in of a dowelled contraction joint and one tie bar is eliminated adjacent to any joint where cracks have opened 1/8 in or greater, and the longitudinal joints must be parallel to and within 12 in of final pavement markings unless otherwise detailed [2]. Reinforcement mesh used in slip‑form paving shall be clean, free of rust, dirt, oil, grease or other foreign substances, positioned on approved supports before concrete placement and fully embedded in the concrete [2].

For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs joint spacing is controlled by prescribed joint widths: the width of any transverse joint between slabs shall not exceed 1/2 in and must be at least 1/4 in along its entire length, while the width of longitudinal joints shall not exceed 3/4 in [2]. Load transfer across transverse joints is achieved with cast load‑transfer dowels (or narrow‑mouth surface slots) placed as shown on the shop drawings and coated with a bond breaker, and tie bars are installed along longitudinal joints for continuous sections greater than 65 ft [2]. Each joint must be sealed and each slab provided with grout ports—at least four per slab—to inject dowel and bedding grouts; where a temporary vertical drop‑off greater than 2 in exists an asphalt wedge transition is required before traffic opening [2].

In unbonded concrete overlays transverse expansion joints shall be identified and marked in the existing pavement, precut in the plastic concrete before sawing, and two full‑depth saw cuts made on each side of the precut joint following the underlying expansion joint with the intervening concrete removed; joint spacing shall follow the project plans or a Department‑approved layout when none are shown, longitudinal joints shall be avoided in wheel paths, and saw cut depth must be no less than D/3 within 12 in of the pavement edges and no less than D/4 elsewhere, after which both transverse and longitudinal joints are sealed [2]. Panel dimensions are limited to a maximum of 18 times the overlay thickness and an aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5, and transitions at the overlay limits shall be constructed as indicated; where such transitions overlie existing treated base or concrete pavement a geotextile debonding layer shall be placed, while no reinforcement is required for the overlay [2].

For bonded concrete overlays joint spacing must follow the layout shown on the project plans or, if none is indicated, a Department‑approved joint layout plan prepared and laid out before concrete placement; longitudinal joints are to be avoided in wheel paths and panel dimensions may not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness nor have a longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio greater than 1.5 [2]. Joints are created exclusively by sawing after the concrete has hardened sufficiently, using a green‑cut saw to a depth of D/3 and a minimum width of 1/8 in; early‑entry saws are recommended, and sawing is omitted if a crack occurs within 1.5 ft of the intended joint location, with joints not sealed at this stage [2]. Transitions at the overlay limits shall be constructed as shown and, where they overlie existing treated base or concrete pavement, a geotextile debonding layer must be placed; no reinforcement is required for the overlay [2].

Joint rehabilitation requires constructing a sealant reservoir sized to the prescribed joint spacing and centered on the existing joint opening, with a tape bond breaker placed at the bottom of the reservoir that may not extend up the face more than 1/8 in; the joint, whether narrow or wide, is cleaned to the required depth, water‑blasted using equipment capable of at least 10 gallons per minute at 7,000 psi, and all vertical faces are media‑blasted and cleared with compressed air at 100 psi before sealing as specified [2]. For Type 2 rehabilitation any portion of an existing steel plate within the new reservoir is removed, allowing no more than a 1/4 in protrusion of the plate into the reservoir [2].

Longitudinal grooving of existing concrete pavement requires cutting grooves that are 3/32‑in wide with a tolerance of plus 1/16 in, spaced 3/4 in centre‑to‑centre and at least 3/16 in deep; grooves must not be placed within 6 in of either the outside edge or the centreline pavement joint, overlapping grooves are prohibited, flailing type equipment shall not be used, and grooving on bridge decks is not permitted [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–474)

Joint spacing is a primary tool for managing the interface between new concrete elements and existing pavement structures. For concrete shoulders added during rehabilitation, the joint spacing shall not exceed 20 ft, limiting the distance between joints in those shoulders and controlling the interface with the existing pavement structure. When full‑width reconstruction of adjacent traffic lanes that are at least 500 feet long is undertaken, joint spacing of 30 ft is required for Jointed Reinforced Concrete Pavement (JRCP) and 15 ft for Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement (JPCP); both options also require perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals as specified in Publication 72M. If only one lane of at least 500 feet is reconstructed, the new joints should be aligned with existing joints and an intermediate joint must be placed when the existing joint spacing exceeds 20 ft to satisfy current joint‑spacing requirements. In widening or adding lanes to an existing rigid pavement, transverse joints in the added section shall be aligned with those on the original pavement; where the original transverse joint spacing is 20 ft or less, slabs are laid to the same length as the original, while where the original spacing exceeds 20 ft the widening uses JPCP and adjusts joint spacing to a minimum of 10 ft and a maximum of 17 ft, incorporating intermediate joints so that slab lengths in the widened portion are approximately equal. For roller‑compacted concrete pavements, joint spacing is defined on the plan sheets; when control joints are required, shoulder joints should match the spacing and alignment of the adjacent concrete mainline, and the spacing requirements shall be specified on the plan sheets. In RCC pavements less than 8 inches thick, control joints are spaced between 15 ft and 20 ft; for RCC pavements 8 inches or greater, joint spacing equals pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet unless other values are designated in Publication 72M, RC‑27M. For bonded concrete‑on‑concrete overlays the interface is designed to be structural, requiring that a bond between overlay and existing slab be achieved so that the two act as one load‑carrying unit. For unbonded overlays the old slab is deliberately separated by a fine‑graded minimum 1‑inch asphalt (or geotextile) layer, creating a non‑bonded platform; it is not necessary to match transverse joints in the overlay with those in the existing pavement, and joint spacing for the overlay depends on the type and thickness of concrete being constructed. Joint spacing is achieved by placing longitudinal joints parallel to and within 12 inches of final pavement markings and ensuring they do not traverse a driving lane. Transverse contraction joints are cut perpendicular to the centerline across the full width, centered over dowel bars with a tolerance of ±1 inch, and shall never be placed within 10 ft of another joint. When new concrete abuts adjacent pavement, shoulders or structures, the dominant joints are marked within 48 hours and the first saw cuts in the adjoining element are aligned to those dominant joints to promote coordinated cracking. Load‑transfer units and dowel bar locations are secured and accurately marked before paving so that saw cuts fall directly over the dowel centers. The width of any transverse joint between slabs must not exceed 1/2 inch and must be a minimum of 1/4 inch along its entire length, while the width of longitudinal joints must not exceed 3/4 inch. Joint spacing for an unbonded concrete overlay is laid out according to the project plans; if the plans do not specify joint locations, a joint layout plan must be prepared and approved by the Department before concrete placement. The layout should keep longitudinal joints out of wheel paths and limit panel dimensions so that no dimension exceeds 18 times the overlay thickness and the aspect‑ratio (longer side divided by shorter side) does not exceed 1.5, with the ratio as close to 1.0 as feasible; slight deviations from nominal panel sizes may be permitted to align joints with adjacent pavement or structures, thereby ensuring proper interface continuity. Slab length should be adjusted to match transverse joints between slabs with existing, adjacent transverse joints as much as possible, and precast slab transverse joint locations should be laid out in a manner that will allow for expansion and contraction at the existing and proposed joints. Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Joint spacing for full‑depth precast slabs must be planned to accommodate site climate by having the mix design account for ambient average daily temperatures and exposure conditions so that panel size and expansion joint locations are appropriate, and the slab layout must include blockouts for proposed drainage structures. Transverse joints are limited to a maximum width of 1/2 inch with a minimum of 1/4 inch along their entire length, while longitudinal joints may not exceed 3/4 inch. [2]

For rehabilitation projects that do not involve pavement widening, any concrete shoulder provided shall have its joint spacing limited so that it does not exceed 20 ft, establishing the sole geometric constraint for Joint Spacing in this context. For full‑width concrete reconstruction, the reinforced concrete design (RC‑21M) calls for 30‑foot joint spacing and requires joints to be perpendicular, equipped with coated dowel bars and appropriate seals to maintain load transfer and safety, while the plain concrete design (RC‑27M) calls for 15‑foot joint spacing with the same perpendicular orientation, coated dowel bars and seals. When only a single lane is rebuilt for 500 feet or more, the new joints should be aligned with the existing joint pattern, and if the pre‑existing spacing is greater than 20 ft an intermediate joint must be placed to satisfy the current spacing criteria. For widening or lane additions, transverse joints shall be aligned to match those on the original pavement; when the original transverse joint spacing is 20 ft or less, the widened section shall use slabs of the same length as the original, whereas when the original spacing exceeds 20 ft the design shall adjust the joint spacing to a minimum of 10 ft and a maximum of 17 ft, incorporating intermediate joints as necessary so that slab lengths in the widened portion are approximately equal. In roller‑compacted concrete pavement, control joints for slabs thinner than 8 inches shall be spaced 15 feet to 20 feet, while slabs 8 inches thick or greater shall use a spacing equal to the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet. Longitudinal joints must be constructed parallel to and within 12 inches of the final pavement markings and shall never traverse a driving lane or conflict with those markings. Transverse joints are required to extend across the full pavement width perpendicular to the centerline and may not be placed within 10 ft of another transverse joint. For non‑standard slab lengths, tie bars used in longitudinal joints shall not be installed within 24 inches of a dowelled contraction joint, thereby keeping joints clear of vehicle travel paths. The width of any transverse joint between slabs must not exceed 1/2 inch and must be at least 1/4 inch along its entire length; the width of longitudinal joints must not exceed 3/4 inch; and the finished vertical differential across any joint must not exceed 1/4 inch, with any excess up to 1/2 inch requiring diamond grinding before traffic can be allowed. Panel dimensions are derived from lane geometry so that the aspect ratio (longer dimension divided by shorter dimension) is as close to 1.0 as feasible and shall not exceed 1.5, and overall panel size shall not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness. Longitudinal joints shall be avoided in wheel paths for both bonded and unbonded concrete overlays, addressing accessibility and safety considerations. Where project plans do not specify joint spacing, a joint layout plan approved by the Department must be prepared before concrete placement. For joint rehabilitation, the sealant reservoir is to be cut to the width and depth shown on standard drawings and centered on the existing joint opening; a tape bond breaker may not extend more than 1/8 inch up the reservoir face; water‑blasting used to clean wide joints must have at least 10 gpm flow at 7,000 psi; and vertical faces are media‑blasted then cleaned with compressed air of 100 psi. Verification and expected performance

In the Pavement Policy Manual, section 4.2 Concrete Pavement Patching, compliance of the finished joint spacing is established by measuring the actual distance between transverse joints and confirming that it equals the prescribed spacing for the pavement type—30‑foot joint spacing for Jointed Reinforced Concrete Pavement (JRCP) as required by RC‑21M or 15‑foot joint spacing for Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement (JPCP) as required by RC‑27M—and by verifying that each joint is perpendicular, incorporates coated dowel bars and has appropriate seals. For lane‑only reconstructions that extend 500 feet or more, the new joints must be aligned with existing joints and an intermediate joint must be placed whenever the existing spacing exceeds 20 feet so that current spacing requirements are satisfied; meeting these measured values and alignment criteria demonstrates constructability, expected performance, and consistency with project requirements. In section 4.8 Widening and Lane Additions, verification of the completed joint spacing requires confirming that transverse joints in the widened or added lane are aligned with those on the existing pavement and that slab lengths conform to the applicable limits. When the original transverse joint spacing is 20 feet or less, the new slabs must be the same length as the originals; when the original spacing exceeds 20 feet, the new joint spacing must be no shorter than 10 feet and no longer than 17 feet, with intermediate joints added as needed so that slab lengths in the widened section are approximately equal, and the addition must be tied to the existing pavement. For slip‑form concrete pavements (Section 501), the completed joint layout is checked by confirming that longitudinal joints are parallel to the final pavement markings and lie no more than twelve inches from those markings unless the drawings specify otherwise, and that transverse joints are sawed directly over the marked dowel‑bar centers with a tolerance of plus or minus one inch. Prior to concrete placement the dowel locations must be accurately marked and any load transfer units secured, and after placement the actual dowel positions are inspected to ensure that vertical tilt or horizontal skew does not exceed 0.25 inch at each end, longitudinal translation is no greater than one inch, and horizontal translation is also limited to one inch. The contractor must also verify that no transverse joint is placed within ten feet of another joint and that all joints are cut to the required depth of one‑third slab thickness and width of 1/8 inch. For full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete pavement slabs (Section 529), verification begins with confirming that the surveyed locations of all longitudinal joints match the layout shown on the shop and working drawings, and after slab placement each transverse joint is measured to ensure its width does not exceed 1/2 inch and is at least 1/4 inch along its full length; any joint falling short of the 1/4‑inch minimum must be sawcut to obtain a minimum of 1/4 inch, and every longitudinal joint shall remain no wider than 3/4 inch. For unbonded concrete overlays (Section 548), joints are verified by confirming that they are placed exactly as shown on the project plans or special provisions; when the plans provide no spacing a joint layout plan must be prepared, approved by the Department, and all joints laid out before concrete placement while ensuring longitudinal joints are not located in wheel paths. The panel dimensions defined by those joints are then checked so that no dimension exceeds eighteen times the overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter side ratio does not exceed 1.5, and any slight deviation from nominal dimensions is only acceptable if the Representative permits it to match adjacent structures or avoid problematic panel shapes. For bonded concrete overlays (Section 540), verification follows the same approach of checking the spacing against the project plans or a Department‑approved joint layout plan set out before placement; longitudinal joints shall be avoided in wheel paths and each panel shall be no larger than eighteen times the overlay thickness with an aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5, any slight deviations must be authorized by the Representative, and compliance is further confirmed by ensuring that joints are cut only with a saw to a depth of D/3 and a minimum width of 1/8 inch. For longitudinal grooving of existing concrete pavement (Section 510), verification consists of measuring each groove to confirm that the width is 3/32‑inch with a tolerance of plus 1/16 inch, that the center‑to‑center spacing is 3/4‑inch, and that the depth meets at least 3/16‑inch as required; locations are also checked to ensure no groove lies within six inches of the outside edge or the centerline pavement joint and that grooves do not overlap, with continuous removal of any slurry or residue observed before traffic is allowed.
