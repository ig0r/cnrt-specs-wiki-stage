Joint Spacing

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Joint Spacing is a topic under Rigid Pavement.
joint-spacingjoint-spacing-requirementsslab-lengthslab-lengthsjoint-placement
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Joint spacing is the deliberate placement of transverse, longitudinal or contraction joints in rigid concrete pavements to control where uncontrolled cracking will occur, thereby supporting the intended service life and structural performance of the slab. By prescribing specific maximum distances—such as 30 ft for jointed reinforced concrete pavement, 15 ft for jointed plain concrete pavement, and thickness‑based limits for roller‑compacted concrete—and by requiring minimum separations between adjacent joints, the design ensures reliable load transfer, durability and safety while preventing random cracking through early saw‑cutting of the joints. The spacing rules also dictate alignment with existing joints, limit panel dimensions (no side exceeding 18 × overlay thickness and aspect ratios ≤ 1.5), and place longitudinal joints away from wheel paths to maintain serviceability and traffic safety [1] [2].

Purpose and design objectives

Joint spacing is employed to control the development of uncontrolled cracks in concrete pavements by providing designated locations where cracks can be initiated, thereby supporting the intended service life of the pavement. [1] In concrete‑pavement reconstruction, the required joint placement criteria are 30 feet for jointed reinforced concrete pavement (RC‑21M) and 15 feet for jointed plain concrete pavement (RC‑27M); when a single lane longer than 500 feet is being reconstructed, an intermediate joint must be placed if the existing joint spacing exceeds 20 feet so that current joint‑spacing requirements are satisfied. [1] For roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements, control joints on shoulders must align with the spacing and alignment of the adjacent concrete mainline, while for temporary median crossovers or low‑traffic roadways the spacing follows either Publication 72M or the rule that RCC slabs less than 8 inches thick receive joints every 15 feet to 20 feet and slabs 8 inches or greater receive joints spaced at a distance equal to the slab thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet. [1] Joint spacing in rigid concrete pavements is also prescribed to control crack development and provide reliable load transfer, so the design limits how close joints may be placed; transverse joints must not be within 10 feet of another transverse joint and the minimum length between transverse joints is 10 feet for reinforced cement concrete or 6 feet for plain cement concrete. [2] Early saw‑cutting of joints is required to prevent random cracking, which helps maintain long‑term performance of the slab. [2] For unbonded concrete overlays the spacing requirement is intended to control cracking and preserve long‑term performance by limiting slab size; maximum panel dimensions should not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness and the length of the longer panel dimension divided by the shorter panel dimension should not exceed 1.5, with longitudinal joints avoided in wheel paths. [2] The same limits on panel dimensions and aspect ratio apply to bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavement, and a layout plan must be developed and approved when joint locations are not shown on the plans; saw cutting is required as soon as the concrete can be cut without excessive raveling to prevent random cracking and premature failure of the slab. [2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–462)

Joint spacing for concrete shoulders employed in rigid‑pavement rehabilitation shall not exceed 20 ft; this limitation is intended to provide lateral support to the existing pavement and to keep the shoulders relatively maintenance‑free, thereby satisfying structural integrity and durability objectives [1]. For jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) the design calls for a 30‑foot joint spacing with perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, which meets the Publication 72M load‑transfer objective and supports functional load transfer, structural integrity, durability against cracking, safety for traffic and serviceability of the pavement [1]. Jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) requires a 15‑foot joint spacing with the same joint orientation and load‑transfer devices, fulfilling the same set of functional, structural, durability, safety and serviceability objectives as described for JRCP [1]. When reconstructing a single lane that is 500 feet or longer, new joints shall be aligned with the existing joints and an intermediate joint must be placed if the existing joint spacing exceeds 20 ft, in order to meet current joint‑spacing requirements and preserve structural performance and serviceability [1]. In roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavement less than 8 in thick, control joints shall be spaced between 15 ft and 20 ft; for RCC thicknesses of 8 in or greater the spacing shall equal the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 ft, a rule that provides crack‑location control (durability) and aligns shoulder joints with the adjacent concrete mainline to satisfy functional and structural objectives [1]. Longitudinal joints shall be placed parallel to and within 12 in of the final pavement markings, and longitudinal joints shall be avoided in wheel paths; this arrangement prevents a joint from traversing a driving lane and thereby supports safety as well as serviceability [2]. Transverse joints shall not be located within 10 ft of another transverse joint; maintaining this minimum separation helps control cracking and contributes to structural performance and durability objectives [2]. When load‑transfer units are used, dowel‑bar locations must be accurately marked so that sawed transverse joints fall over the centre of the dowels, ensuring proper load transfer and enhancing durability [2]. Tie bars may not be installed within 24 in of a dowelled contraction joint, limiting interference with joint action and maintaining serviceability of the pavement [2]. Panel dimensions shall be limited so that no dimension exceeds eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and the ratio of the longer panel dimension to the shorter shall not exceed 1.5; these limits are intended to prevent premature cracking and thus satisfy functional, structural, durability and serviceability objectives [2]. If project plans do not indicate joint spacing, a joint‑layout plan must be developed and accepted by the Department before concrete placement, ensuring that the resulting joint configuration meets all required performance objectives [2]. Joints shall be created only by sawing once the concrete has sufficiently hardened to prevent excessive raveling; this practice helps avoid random cracking and supports durability and serviceability goals [2].

Scope and applicability

Joint spacing is employed for concrete shoulders that are added during rigid‑pavement rehabilitation when no widening is performed, and the spacing shall not exceed 20 ft (section 2.6) [1]. For reconstruction of Portland cement concrete pavements whose length is at least 500 feet, joint spacing follows Publication 72M RC‑21M for jointed reinforced concrete pavement with a 30‑foot spacing or RC‑27M for jointed plain concrete pavement with a 15‑foot spacing, and the design requires perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals (section 4.2) [1]. When only one lane of a concrete pavement of 500 feet or more is reconstructed, the new joints are to be aligned with existing joints and an intermediate joint must be provided if the existing joint spacing exceeds 20 feet in order to meet the current joint‑spacing requirements shown on RC‑27M (section 4.2) [1]. In widening or lane‑addition projects on uniform‑depth rigid pavements constructed since 1945, transverse joints in the new portion shall follow the original pattern; if the original spacing is 20 feet or less, the added slabs are laid out with the same length, whereas if the original spacing exceeds 20 feet the addition shall be placed as plain cement concrete with joint spacing limited to a minimum of 10 feet and a maximum of 17 feet, inserting intermediate joints as needed to keep slab lengths roughly equal (section 4.8) [1]. For unbonded concrete overlays placed over existing Portland cement concrete pavement, the joints are laid out in the same manner as for new concrete pavements, need not line up with the underlying transverse joints, and the required spacing is determined by the type and thickness of the concrete being placed as shown in Table 10.10 (section 4.9) [1]. Roller‑compacted concrete may be used as a concrete shoulder, temporary pavement, or as the final wearing or base course on roadways with an average daily traffic of 2 000 vehicles or less and a speed limit of 55 mph or lower; control joints are provided when required, with spacing of 15 to 20 feet for sections thinner than 8 inches and a spacing equal to the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 (feet) for sections 8 inches thick or greater (section 4.11) [1]. For plain or reinforced cement‑concrete pavements, joint spacing is applied on new multilane slabs and on sections being widened by placing transverse contraction joints that extend across the full slab width; no transverse joint may be located within 10 feet of another transverse joint, and longitudinal joints shall run parallel to and within 12 inches of final pavement markings unless otherwise detailed (Section 501) [2]. The same plain or reinforced cement‑concrete treatment is used when load‑transfer units such as dowel bars are installed and when slip‑form paving equipment is employed for continuous placement of the concrete slab (Section 501) [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays on existing concrete‑surfaced pavements, joint spacing follows the distances shown on the project plans; if the plans do not specify spacing a department‑approved joint layout plan must be prepared before concrete placement, and the design limits require that no panel dimension exceed 18 times the overlay thickness and that the ratio of the longer to shorter panel dimension shall not exceed 1.5, with longitudinal joints avoided in wheel paths (Section 548) [2]. Bonded concrete overlays applied over existing asphalt‑surfaced pavements as Class AA cement concrete use joint spacing that also follows the project plans or an approved plan when none are shown; panel dimensions shall not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter side ratio shall not exceed 1.5, longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths, and saw cutting with a green‑cut saw to a depth of D/3 and a minimum width of 1/8 inch is the only acceptable method (Section 540) [2]. Joint rehabilitation on rigid concrete pavements after spall repairs employs joint spacing that matches the existing joint configuration; unrepaired spalls two inches or less are cleaned and sealed before a sealant reservoir is cut to the width and depth prescribed for the existing joint spacing, and for Type 1 rehabilitation the joint may be widened using a water blaster of at least 10 gpm at 7,000 psi and placing a tape bond breaker that does not extend more than 1/8 inch up the reservoir face before sealing; Type 2 uses the same steps but removes any existing steel plate within the reservoir, allowing no more than ¼ inch of plate to protrude (Section 513) [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–462)

In the concrete‑pavement patching context, if the deteriorated area extends roughly 500 feet and the remaining intact pavement between intermittent patches is ten feet or less, the preferred solution shifts from patch‑by‑patch joint‑spacing adjustments to complete removal and reconstruction of the entire section. When a full‑width reconstruction of all adjacent lanes is required for five hundred feet or more, the design must follow standards that prescribe either thirty‑foot joint spacing for jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) or fifteen‑foot joint spacing for jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP), rather than relying on existing joint layouts. For a single‑lane reconstruction of five hundred feet or more, new joints should line up with existing ones, but an intermediate joint must be inserted whenever the pre‑existing joint spacing exceeds twenty feet to satisfy current joint‑spacing requirements. When the existing pavement’s original transverse joint spacing is greater than 20 feet, widening or lane addition shall use Jointed Plain Cement Concrete (JPCP) and adjust the joint spacing to a minimum of 10 feet and a maximum of 17 feet, adding intermediate joints as needed so slab lengths are roughly equal. When the original transverse joint spacing is 20 feet or less, widening may retain the same slab length as on the original pavement, making the standard joint‑spacing approach appropriate. In slip‑form paving, if concrete placement cannot be continuous for at least half an hour, a regular joint‑spacing plan is set aside and the contract requires constructing a transverse joint using a bulkhead rather than relying on the usual contraction spacing. Because the code limits the placement of new transverse joints to more than ten feet from any other transverse joint, if that distance cannot be maintained another joint configuration such as a bulkhead or an aligned expansion joint is preferred. Where expansion joints are indicated—or when a widened pavement must line up with existing expansion joints—the design calls for placing transverse expansion joints, where indicated, instead of the standard spaced‑joint arrangement. For unbonded concrete overlays, the prescribed joint‑spacing plan is considered unsuitable if the maximum panel dimensions should not exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness or if the length of the longer panel dimension divided by the shorter panel dimension should exceed 1.5; under those geometric limits a different panel layout must be adopted to prevent premature cracking. The Representative may allow slight deviations from nominal panel dimensions in order to match joints in adjacent pavement or structures, or to avoid constructing panels with dimensions or shapes that would otherwise be subject to premature cracking. Longitudinal joints should be avoided in wheel paths. [1][2]

Design inputs and requirements

Joint spacing for concrete shoulders added during a resurfacing, restoration, or rehabilitation project that does not involve widening shall not exceed 20 ft, as required by Chapter 2 Section 2.6 B.1.a for shoulder elements used for lateral support or low‑maintenance purposes [1]. The Department also requires that control joints in shoulders of roller‑compacted concrete pavements align with the adjacent concrete mainline and follow the spacing rules set out in Publication 72M RC‑27M; where a temporary median crossover or roadway is limited to an average daily traffic of 2,000 vehicles and a speed limit of 55 mph, either the publication may be referenced or a thickness‑based rule applied, with sections less than 8 inches thick having control joints spaced 15 ft to 20 ft and sections 8 inches or greater having spacing equal to 2.5 times the pavement thickness in feet [1]. When concrete pavement is reconstructed, joint spacing is governed by Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards: the reinforced‑concrete design (RC‑21M) requires a 30‑foot joint spacing with perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, whereas the plain‑concrete design (RC‑27M) requires a 15‑foot joint spacing with the same orientation and load‑transfer details [1]. If only one lane is reconstructed for a length of 500 ft or more, new joints must align with existing joints and an intermediate joint shall be placed when the existing joint spacing exceeds 20 feet to satisfy the RC‑27M requirement; all such work must also comply with Publication 408 specifications (e.g., Sections 516 and 519) [1]. For widening or lane‑addition projects, the PCCP Guidelines subsection 4.8 require transverse joints on the added section to be aligned with those on the original pavement; when the existing transverse joint spacing is 20 ft or less, the widened portion shall use slabs of the same length as the original, while if the existing spacing exceeds 20 ft the design shall employ JPCP and adjust the joint spacing so that each slab length is at least 10 ft and no more than 17 ft, adding intermediate joints as needed to keep slab lengths approximately equal [1]. Unbonded concrete overlays are governed by Publication 72M RC‑20M, which mandates that overlay joints be constructed in the same manner as joints in new concrete pavements; the transverse joints of the overlay do not have to line up with existing pavement joints and no attempt should be made to match them, with the actual spacing determined by the type and thickness of the concrete as shown in Table 10.10 of the PCCP guidelines [1]. Joint spacing for rigid concrete pavements is governed by Section 501.3 of the Rigid Pavements specifications; transverse joints may not be placed within 10 ft of another transverse joint, and when pavement is removed and replaced the distance between transverse joints must be at least 10 ft for reinforced cement concrete or 6 ft for plain cement concrete [2]. Tie bars must be located no closer than 24 inches to a dowelled contraction joint and any additional tie bars may not be installed within 30 inches of a transverse joint [2]. All transverse and longitudinal contraction joints must be sawed, cured and protected in accordance with Sections 501.3(i) and (j), and saw cutting is the only acceptable method for forming joints, with cuts made to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness and a minimum width of 1/8 inch once the concrete has hardened sufficiently [2]. Joint spacing for unbonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements is governed by Section 548.3(i); joints shall be placed as shown on the project plans or special provisions, and if the plans do not specify spacing a joint layout must be prepared and approved by the Department before concrete placement, with panel dimensions generally not exceeding eighteen times the overlay thickness and the ratio of the longer panel dimension to the shorter not exceeding 1.5, while longitudinal joints shall be avoided in wheel paths [2]. Similarly, under Section 540 for bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavement, joint spacing must follow the project plans or special provisions, and when no spacing is shown a joint layout plan must be approved before placement; panel dimensions shall generally not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio shall not exceed 1.5, and longitudinal joints shall be avoided in wheel paths [2]. Joint rehabilitation work must be performed in accordance with the Standard Drawings that prescribe the width and depth of the sealant reservoir based on the existing joint spacing; the reservoir is to be cut to those dimensions, a tape bond breaker may be placed at the bottom of the reservoir but shall not extend up the face more than 1/8 inch, and any steel plate remaining in the reservoir may protrude no more than 1/4 inch [2]. Cleaning of joints shall use water blasting with a blaster having at least 10 gallons per minute flow at 7,000 psi, or compressed‑air cleaning with a stream of 100 psi measured at the source, after which sealing follows the requirements of Section 501.3(n) [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–462)

Design methods and criteria

Reconstruction of all adjacent traffic lanes that are at least 500 feet long with Portland cement concrete follows the Publication 72M Roadway Construction Standards, using jointed reinforced concrete pavement designed according to RC‑21M which prescribes a thirty‑foot joint spacing with perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals, and jointed plain concrete pavement designed according to RC‑27M which prescribes a fifteen‑foot joint spacing with the same joint characteristics. [1] When only one lane of at least 500 feet is reconstructed, the new transverse joints shall be aligned with existing joints and an intermediate joint shall be provided whenever the pre‑existing spacing exceeds twenty feet in order to satisfy the current spacing criteria set out in RC‑27M. [1] Designers must also specify quantities for New Pavement Joint items as either skewed or non‑skewed joints. [1] For widening projects or lane additions, transverse joints shall be aligned with those on the original pavement; if the original joint spacing is twenty feet or less, the widened section shall use slabs of the same length as the original, whereas when the original spacing exceeds twenty feet the design shall employ jointed plain concrete pavement and adjust the joint spacing to a minimum of ten feet and a maximum of seventeen feet, inserting intermediate joints as necessary so that slab lengths in the widened portion are approximately equal. [1] In unbonded concrete overlays the joint spacing is selected from Table 10.10 based on the type and thickness of the existing pavement rather than by an explicit equation, and the joints are placed using the same procedures prescribed for new concrete pavements in Publication 72M RC‑20M. [1] For roller‑compacted concrete pavements the joint spacing follows a thickness‑based rule: when slab thickness is less than eight inches control joints are spaced between fifteen feet and twenty feet, and when slab thickness is eight inches or greater the spacing is obtained by multiplying the pavement thickness expressed in feet by 2.5. [1] Temporary median crossovers, shoulders, or low‑volume roads may use joint layouts taken from Publication 72M RC‑27M or matched to adjacent concrete mainline joints. [1] In the slip‑form paving method transverse joints may not be placed within ten feet of another transverse joint; when an existing pavement is widened the new joints should be aligned with existing joints or, if alignment is not possible, also kept at least ten feet away, and the centre of each sawed contraction joint must be marked so that it lies within ±1 inch of the dowel‑bar centreline. [2] The cut for a transverse joint shall be made to a depth equal to one‑third of slab thickness (D/3) with a width of 1⁄8 inch. [2] For unbonded concrete overlays joints are spaced as indicated on the project plans and special provisions; if no joint spacing is indicated a joint layout plan must be developed and accepted by the Department before concrete placement, while avoiding longitudinal joints in wheel paths, and panel dimensions are generally limited to no more than eighteen times the overlay thickness with the longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio not exceeding 1.5 and an aspect ratio as close to 1.0 as feasible. [2] The FHWA HIPERPAV software or a similar program approved by the Department may be used to determine the project‑specific sawing window. [2] The bonded concrete overlay provisions adopt a comparable approach: when plans do not already specify spacing a joint layout plan must be accepted by the Department, the layout shall avoid longitudinal joints in wheel paths and generally limit panel dimensions so that no side exceeds eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio does not exceed 1.5. [2] Sawing is the only acceptable method for creating joints; it should be performed with a green‑cut saw to a depth of D/3 and a minimum width of 1⁄8 inch as soon as the concrete has hardened enough to prevent excessive raveling, and HIPERPAV or similar approved software may be used in determining a project‑specific sawing window. [2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–462)

For rehabilitation projects that provide concrete shoulders as lateral support, the joint spacing of those shoulders shall not exceed 20 ft. [1] When reconstructing all adjacent lanes for a length of 500 feet or more of Portland cement concrete, the design must follow Publication 72M RC‑21M, which requires a 30‑foot joint spacing together with perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals; alternatively, compliance with RC‑27M requires a 15‑foot joint spacing with the same joint orientation, dowel bar coating and sealing. [1] For reconstruction of only one lane over the same length, new joints must be aligned with existing joints and an intermediate joint shall be placed whenever the existing joint spacing exceeds 20 feet in order to satisfy the current joint spacing requirements of RC‑27M. [1] In widening or lane‑addition projects, transverse joints in the new section shall be aligned with those on the existing pavement; if the original transverse joint spacing is 20 feet or less, the widened slabs shall have the same length as on the original pavement, whereas when the original spacing exceeds 20 feet the design shall employ jointed plain concrete pavement and adjust the joint spacing to a minimum of 10 feet and a maximum of 17 feet. [1] For unbonded concrete overlays, joints are to be placed in the same manner as joints in new concrete pavements and need not align with existing transverse joints; the selected joint spacing shall be based on the type and thickness of the concrete pavement being constructed as referenced in Table 10.10. [1] Under the roller‑compacted concrete provisions, control joints for RCC pavements less than 8 inches thick should be spaced 15 feet to 20 feet, while for RCC pavements 8 inches or greater the spacing shall equal the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet; shoulders shall have control joints that match the spacing and alignment of the adjacent concrete mainline. [1] When the slip‑form paving method is used for plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements, transverse construction or contraction joints must be spaced at least ten feet apart, and no new transverse joint may be placed within ten feet of any existing transverse joint, including those required after a concreting interruption or when widening an existing pavement. [2] For unbonded concrete overlays on concrete‑surfaced pavements, the joint layout must follow the plan drawing or special provisions; if the plans do not specify spacing, a department‑approved joint layout shall be prepared before concrete placement, and longitudinal joints should be kept out of wheel paths. Additionally, panel dimensions are limited so that the longest dimension does not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the aspect ratio shall not exceed 1.5, although slight deviations may be permitted to align with adjacent joints or avoid shapes prone to premature cracking. [2] For bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements, the joint layout must follow the project plans or a department‑approved layout, longitudinal joints shall be avoided in wheel paths, and panel dimensions are limited to a maximum side length of eighteen times the overlay thickness with an aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5. Joints shall be created only by sawing with a green‑cut saw to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness and a minimum width of 1/8 inch, and cutting must occur early enough to prevent random cracking. [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 61–62), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–99); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 355–462)

Structural section and dimensions

Joint spacing for concrete shoulders used in rigid‑pavement rehabilitation shall not exceed 20 ft. For jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) the required transverse joint spacing is 30 ft as stipulated by RC‑21M, whereas jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) requires a 15‑ft spacing per RC‑27M. When only one lane is reconstructed and the existing joint spacing exceeds 20 ft, an intermediate joint must be placed to satisfy current joint‑spacing requirements. For widening or lane‑addition work the transverse joint spacing follows the original pavement: if the existing transverse joints are spaced at 20 ft or less, the new slabs are laid with the same length as those on the original pavement; when the original spacing exceeds 20 ft, the widened portion shall be constructed as JPCP with a minimum joint spacing of 10 ft and a maximum of 17 ft, adding intermediate joints as needed so that slab lengths in the widened portion are approximately equal. In roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavement whose thickness is less than 8 inches, control joints shall be spaced between 15 ft and 20 ft; for RCC pavement 8 inches or greater thick, joint spacing shall be calculated by multiplying the pavement thickness in inches by 2.5 to obtain a spacing in feet. The Pennsylvania specification requires that any new transverse joint on a rigid concrete pavement be placed at least ten feet from an existing transverse joint, and no transverse joint may be constructed within ten feet of another transverse joint. For bonded concrete overlays the layout shall limit each panel so that its longest dimension does not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the ratio of the longer to shorter panel dimensions shall not exceed 1.5; saw joints are to be cut to a depth equal to one‑third of the slab thickness and to a minimum width of 1/8 inch. No other dimensional requirements for base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab thickness, edge‑support, or reinforcement are prescribed in either source with respect to joint spacing. [1][2]

For reconstruction segments that are five hundred feet or longer, the designer must first perform a pavement design analysis to decide whether a thicker slab depth is required [1]. In jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) the standard calls for thirty‑foot perpendicular joints with coated dowel bars, while jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) uses fifteen‑foot perpendicular joints with coated dowel bars; if only one lane is rebuilt, the new joints must line up with existing joints and an intermediate joint shall be placed whenever the existing spacing exceeds twenty feet [1]. Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only for concrete patches longer than sixty‑five feet [1]. For roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements, thicknesses may be any value between four inches and eighteen inches as determined using the current PennDOT concrete pavement design procedures [1]. Joint spacing for RCC is derived from the selected thickness: when the RCC slab is less than eight inches thick, control joints should be placed at intervals of fifteen feet to twenty feet; when the slab is eight inches or greater, the joint spacing equals the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet [1]. Cross‑slope continuity after installing precast slabs is achieved by diamond grinding the surface because the delivered slab elevation is set at least one quarter inch higher than surrounding pavement [1]. The source does not provide guidance on layer configurations, cross slopes, transitions, or tie‑ins for RCC pavements [1]. For a bonded concrete overlay, joint spacing shall follow the panel‑size limits that require panels not to exceed eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and the ratio of the longer to the shorter panel dimension shall not exceed 1.5, with longitudinal joints avoided in wheel paths; joints are to be spaced as shown on the project plans or, if none are provided, a joint layout plan approved by the Department must be prepared [2]. Transitions are constructed at the overlay limits as indicated on the plans, and where an existing treated base or concrete pavement underlies the overlay a geotextile debonding layer is placed [2].

Materials and mixture design

Joint spacing specifications shall identify the concrete material type and any associated joint treatments required for each pavement category. For jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) the specification must state a transverse‑joint spacing of 30 feet, require coated dowel bars and appropriate sealants at those joints, and shall be based on the standard Portland cement concrete defined in Publication 408 Section 704; for jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) the specification must state a transverse‑joint spacing of 15 feet, also require coated dowel bars and appropriate sealants, and likewise shall use the standard PCC mix. When the work involves widening or lane addition, the material to be used shall be Class AA cement concrete unless an exemption is granted; widening of JPCP shall be constructed with plain cement concrete, while JRCP may be widened with either reinforced or plain cement concrete. The transverse‑joint layout for the new section shall follow the original pavement: if the existing transverse‑joint spacing is 20 feet or less, the slabs in the addition shall be the same length as on the existing pavement; if the existing spacing exceeds 20 feet, the joint spacing shall be adjusted to a minimum of 10 feet and a maximum of 17 feet with intermediate joints added as needed so that slab lengths are approximately equal. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–96)

In the concrete‑pavement patching context (section 4.2), joint spacing is dictated by the pavement type; a jointed reinforced concrete pavement calls for 30‑foot spacing with perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and proper seals, whereas a jointed plain concrete pavement uses 15‑foot spacing with the same joint details. When only one lane is rebuilt, new joints must line up with existing ones and an extra joint shall be added if the existing spacing exceeds 20 feet. The concrete mix for these joints follows the standard PCC specifications (Publication 408 Section 704) and employs an optimized aggregate gradation; high early‑strength concrete should be avoided because of its potential for excessive shrinkage cracking, while accelerated concrete may be used when a rapid traffic opening is required, achieving strength in four to seven hours, compared with the normal mix that reaches traffic‑opening strength in twenty‑four to forty‑eight hours. For long‑term durability, high‑performance dowel bars are recommended when the repaired pavement will remain in service for more than twenty years. For widening or lane additions on rigid pavements (section 4.8), the design shall use Class AA cement concrete unless an exemption is granted, and the transverse joint layout must follow the original pavement. If the existing transverse joint spacing is twenty feet or less, the new slabs are constructed to the same length as the originals; if the existing spacing exceeds twenty feet, the widening shall be placed with jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) using a joint spacing that is no shorter than ten feet and no longer than seventeen feet, adding intermediate joints as needed so slab lengths remain roughly equal. Plain cement concrete is required for JPCP widenings while jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) may use reinforced or plain cement concrete, ensuring the material choice aligns with the joint configuration. Structural details and interfaces

Joint spacing varies with pavement type and the specific work being performed; for concrete shoulders that are part of rehabilitation projects, the joints shall not exceed 20 ft [1]. In concrete pavement patching, jointed reinforced concrete pavement (JRCP) must be laid with transverse joints spaced at 30 ft, perpendicular to traffic flow, and equipped with coated dowel bars and appropriate seals as required by RC‑21M; jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) shall use 15‑ft perpendicular joints with coated dowel bars and seals in accordance with RC‑27M [1]. Load transfer at all transverse patch joints must be provided by dowels that are fully bonded along their embedded length and anchored to the existing slab with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders [1]. Longitudinal tie bars or tie bolts are required only when a concrete patch exceeds 65 ft in length [1]. When a single lane is reconstructed for lengths of 500 feet or more, the new joints shall align with the existing joints, and an intermediate joint shall be placed if the existing spacing exceeds 20 ft [1]. For widening or lane‑addition projects, transverse joints shall be aligned with those of the existing pavement; when the original transverse joint spacing is 20 ft or less, the added slabs shall have the same length as the original; when the original spacing exceeds 20 ft, the joint spacing in the addition shall be limited to a minimum of 10 ft and a maximum of 17 ft, with intermediate joints added as needed [1]. Widening of JPCP must use plain cement concrete, whereas JRCP may be widened with either reinforced or plain cement concrete, and the new portion shall be tied to the existing pavement to provide the required connection between old and new sections [1]. Roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements are designed without forms, steel reinforcement, or dowel bars, so no reinforcement or load‑transfer devices are required; control joints may be provided, and when they are specified they must be placed on shoulders to align with the adjacent mainline concrete joints [1]. For RCC less than 8 inches thick, control joint spacing shall be 15 ft to 20 ft; for RCC 8 inches or greater, joint spacing shall be calculated by multiplying the pavement thickness (in inches) by 2.5 to obtain feet [1]. For rigid concrete pavements constructed by the slip‑form method, transverse contraction joints are laid across the full width and aligned with dowel bars or load‑transfer units (LTUs) that are staked in advance; LTU assemblies must be positioned so that vertical tilt, horizontal skew, longitudinal and horizontal translation do not exceed the limits specified, and dowel bars must be accurately marked, may be mechanically implanted, and have bond‑breaker lubricant applied to at least one‑half of their length for Type B coating [2]. Longitudinal joints are provided by tie bars installed as indicated, with no tie bar within 24 inches of a dowelled contraction joint and pull‑out resistance tested per the procedure in Section 501.3(j) [2]. Reinforcement must be free of rust, oil, grease or other foreign substances and placed on acceptable supports before concrete placement, with mesh reinforcement positioned as required for single‑layer pours [2]. All transverse and longitudinal joints are sawed to a depth of D/3 and a width of 1/8 inch and then cured and protected according to Sections 501.3(i) and (j) [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays, joint spacing shall follow the layout shown on the project plans, or if none are provided a department‑approved joint layout plan must be prepared before concrete placement; longitudinal joints shall be kept out of wheel paths, panels may not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness and their aspect ratio (longer dimension divided by shorter) shall not exceed 1.5 [2]. Saw cuts for transverse and longitudinal joints shall be at least D/3 within 12 inches of edges and at least D/4 elsewhere, and shall be cleaned and sealed after cutting [2]. Dowel‑bar assemblies used as load‑transfer systems must be anchored to the substrate so they do not move more than 1/4 inch in any direction, with a QC plan approved by the Representative [2]. At overlay limits transitions are required, and where existing treated base or concrete lies directly beneath the overlay a geotextile debonding layer shall be placed [2]. For bonded concrete overlays joint spacing follows the layout shown on the project plans, or if no spacing is shown a joint layout plan approved by the Department shall be prepared and joints laid out before placement; panels shall not exceed 18 times the overlay thickness and the longer‑to‑shorter panel dimension shall not exceed 1.5, and longitudinal joints should be avoided in wheel paths [2]. Joints are to be created only by sawing, using a green‑cut saw to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness and a minimum width of 1/8 inch, with cuts extended through the slab edge when possible [2]. For joint rehabilitation the required spacing is achieved by constructing a sealant reservoir that is sawn to the width and depth specified for the existing joint spacing and centered on the joint opening; a tape bond breaker must be placed on the bottom of the reservoir and may not extend more than 1/8 inch up the face [2]. If the existing joint is as wide as or wider than the required reservoir width, the joint shall be cleaned to the required depth and, when needed, joint backing material installed to that same depth; water blasting shall use a minimum capacity of 10 gallons per minute at 7,000 psi, and all vertical faces are to be media‑blasted and then air‑cleaned with compressed air at 100 psi before sealing [2]. For Type 2 rehabilitation the portion of any existing steel plate within the new reservoir must be removed, allowing no more than 1/4 inch of plate to protrude into the reservoir [2].

For concrete shoulders added during rehabilitation, joint spacing shall not exceed 20 ft, thereby limiting the distance between joints in those shoulders and controlling the interface with the existing pavement structure [1]. When a full‑width reconstruction of adjacent traffic lanes that are at least 500 feet long is performed, joint spacing for Jointed Reinforced Concrete Pavement (JRCP) must be 30 ft and for Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement (JPCP) must be 15 ft; both require perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals as specified in Publication 72M [1]. If only one lane is reconstructed for a length of 500 feet or more, the new joints should be aligned with the existing joints and an intermediate joint must be placed when the existing joint spacing exceeds 20 ft to meet current joint spacing requirements [1]. In widening or adding lanes to an existing rigid pavement, transverse joints in the added section shall be aligned with those on the original pavement; where the original transverse joint spacing is 20 ft or less, slabs are laid to the same length as the original, while where the original spacing exceeds 20 ft the widening uses JPCP and adjusts the joint spacing to a minimum of 10 ft and a maximum of 17 ft, incorporating intermediate joints so that slab lengths in the widened portion are approximately equal [1]. Full lane additions of ten feet or more shall be designed to the same thickness as the original pavement unless heavier traffic loads require design for actual traffic [1]. For bonded concrete‑on‑concrete overlays the interface is designed to be structural, requiring that the bond between overlay and existing slab be achieved so that the two act as one load‑carrying unit [1]. For unbonded overlays a fine‑graded minimum 1‑inch asphalt (or geotextile) layer separates the old concrete from the new concrete, creating a non‑bonded platform; it is not necessary to match the transverse joints in the overlay with those in the existing pavement and joint spacing for the overlay will depend on the type and thickness of concrete being constructed [1]. In roller‑compacted concrete pavements, control joint spacing is defined on the plan sheets; when control joints are required shoulder joints should match the spacing and alignment of the adjacent concrete mainline [1]. For RCC pavements less than 8 inches thick, control joints shall be spaced between 15 ft and 20 ft; for RCC pavements 8 inches or greater, joint spacing equals the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 ft unless other values are designated in Publication 72M [1]. In slip‑form concrete pavement construction, transverse joints shall be placed perpendicular across the slab and aligned with existing joints or adjoining structures whenever feasible, and no transverse joint may be located within 10 ft of another transverse joint [2]. Longitudinal joints shall run parallel to the centerline and must be positioned within 12 inches of the final pavement markings unless the construction drawings specify otherwise; longitudinal joints are avoided in wheel paths [2]. Tie bars are omitted near dowelled contraction joints as required, and load‑transfer units such as dowel bars are staked in advance with saw cuts centered over them; vertical tilt or horizontal skew of these cuts shall not exceed 0.25 inch and longitudinal translation shall not exceed 1 inch [2]. Edge slumping limits govern the interface at pavement edges, allowing no more than 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes and no more than 1/4 inch between a lane and its shoulder [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays, joint spacing follows the project plans; when no spacing is shown a joint layout plan must be prepared and approved before concrete placement, and each panel’s longest dimension shall not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness while the aspect‑ratio (longer‑to‑shorter side) shall not exceed 1.5, with slight deviations permitted when they help match joints in adjacent pavements or structures to ensure a coordinated interface across layers and features [2]. For bonded concrete overlays over asphalt‑surfaced pavement, joint spacing is laid out according to the project plans and, if not indicated, requires a pre‑approved joint layout plan; the same dimensional limits (maximum panel dimension ≤ 18 × overlay thickness and aspect ratio ≤ 1.5) apply, and the Representative may allow or require slight deviations from nominal panel dimensions in order to match joints in adjacent pavement or structures, thereby ensuring proper interface continuity [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Joint spacing design must incorporate joint placement, sealing, and protection measures that respond to moisture and climate conditions; transverse, contraction, expansion and longitudinal joints are located as specified and later sealed only after the faces are clean and dry, with sealant applied before any traffic or equipment enters the pavement (Section 501.3(n)). When rain is forecast, paving must be stopped and the sides and surface immediately protected with covering material (Section 501.3(r)), and joints are kept dry until bond‑breaker or sealing material is placed. In cold weather the pavement surface and sides must be insulated or heated so that a temperature of not less than 40°F is maintained, extending the cure period by an additional day if temperatures fall between 40°F and 35°F and declaring the concrete defective if they fall below 35°F (Section 501.3(l)2). For work performed from October 1 to April 1, early‑entry saws or another approved method are recommended to promote dominant joint development in adjacent lanes (Section 501.3(i)2). [2]

In rehabilitation projects that do not involve pavement widening, any concrete shoulder provided shall have its joint spacing limited so that it does not exceed 20 ft, establishing the sole geometric constraint for Joint Spacing in this context. For full‑width concrete reconstruction, the reinforced concrete design (RC‑21M) calls for 30‑foot joint spacing, with joints required to be perpendicular, equipped with coated dowel bars and proper seals to maintain load transfer and safety; the plain concrete design (RC‑27M) calls for 15‑foot joint spacing, also requiring perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars and appropriate seals. When only a single lane is rebuilt for 500 feet or more, the new joints should be aligned with the existing joint pattern, and if the pre‑existing spacing is greater than 20 feet an intermediate joint must be placed to satisfy the current spacing criteria. For widening or lane additions, transverse joints shall be aligned to match those on the original pavement; when the original transverse joint spacing is 20 feet or less, the widened section shall use slabs of the same length as the original, whereas when the original spacing exceeds 20 feet the design shall adjust the joint spacing to a minimum of 10 feet and a maximum of 17 feet, incorporating intermediate joints as necessary so that slab lengths in the widened portion are approximately equal. In roller‑compacted concrete pavement, joint spacing must preserve geometric continuity by matching the spacing and alignment of any adjacent concrete mainline when used on shoulders. For temporary median crossovers and roadways limited to an ADT of 2 000 and a speed limit of 55 mph or less, the plan may reference Publication 72M or apply the rule that RCC slabs thinner than 8 inches require control joints spaced 15 feet to 20 feet, while slabs 8 inches thick or greater use a spacing equal to the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet. The joint‑spacing plan for plain or reinforced cement concrete pavements must position longitudinal joints parallel to and within 12 inches of the final pavement markings and must not cross a driving lane unless the construction drawings specify otherwise, thereby addressing user guidance and accessibility. Transverse joints in this context are required to be cut across the full width, perpendicular to the centerline, and must be spaced at least 10 feet apart; that minimum separation also applies when aligning new joints with existing ones or after an interruption longer than 30 minutes. Tie bars used for load transfer may not be placed within 24 inches of a dowelled contraction joint, and one tie bar is eliminated adjacent to each slab that borders a dominant joint, especially during the October‑1 to April‑1 paving period. For unbonded concrete overlays of concrete‑surfaced pavements joints shall be spaced as indicated on the project plans and special provisions, and if no joint spacing is indicated a joint layout plan that is accepted by the Department must be developed before concrete placement. For bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavement joint spacing must follow the layout shown on the project plans and special provisions, and when a plan does not specify spacing a joint‑layout plan that is accepted by the Department must be prepared and joints laid out before concrete placement; longitudinal joints shall avoid the wheel paths to protect users, and panel geometry limits are the same as for unbonded overlays. Verification and expected performance

For concrete pavement patching the finished joint spacing is checked by measuring the actual distance between transverse joints and confirming that it equals the prescribed spacing for the pavement type—30‑foot joint spacing for JRCP as required by RC‑21M or 15‑foot joint spacing for JPCP as required by RC‑27M—and by verifying that each joint is perpendicular, incorporates coated dowel bars and has appropriate seals [1]. When a lane‑only reconstruction extends 500 ft or more the new joints must be aligned with existing joints and an intermediate joint must be placed whenever the existing spacing exceeds 20 feet so that current spacing requirements are satisfied, demonstrating constructability, expected performance, and consistency with project requirements [1]. In widening or lane‑addition projects the verification of completed joint spacing requires confirming that transverse joints in the widened or added lane are aligned with those on the existing pavement and that slab lengths conform to the applicable limits [1]. If the original transverse joint spacing is 20 feet or less, the new slabs must be the same length as the originals; when the original spacing exceeds 20 feet, the new joint spacing must be no shorter than 10 feet and no longer than 17 feet, with intermediate joints added as needed so that slab lengths in the widened section are approximately equal [1]. For slip‑form concrete pavements the finished joint layout is verified by confirming that every longitudinal joint is parallel to and within 12 inches of the final pavement markings unless the construction drawings specify otherwise, that such joints do not traverse a driving lane or conflict with those markings, that load‑transfer units have been staked in advance and dowel‑bar locations are accurately marked so each transverse joint is sawed directly over the centre of the dowels within a tolerance of ±1 inch, and that the overall spacing follows the distances shown on the construction drawings, thereby satisfying compliance, constructability, performance, and consistency with project requirements [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays the completed joint spacing is first checked by confirming that joints are placed as indicated on the project plans and special provisions; if the plans do not specify spacing a joint‑layout plan must be developed and accepted by the Department before concrete placement, the layout is examined to avoid longitudinal joints in wheel paths, to keep each panel’s longer dimension from exceeding eighteen times the overlay thickness, and to ensure that the aspect ratio (longer side divided by shorter side) does not exceed 1.5 unless a Representative permits a slight deviation, thus addressing compliance, constructability, performance, and project consistency [2]. In bonded concrete overlays of asphalt‑surfaced pavements the joint layout is verified by confirming that joints have been spaced exactly as shown on the project plans or, when no spacing is indicated, by reviewing a Department‑approved joint layout plan prepared before concrete placement; the resulting panel dimensions are then checked so that no panel exceeds eighteen times the overlay thickness and the ratio of the longer side to the shorter side does not exceed 1.5, while also confirming that longitudinal joints have been avoided in wheel paths and that any slight deviations from nominal dimensions were authorized by the Representative, thereby ensuring compliance, constructability, performance, and alignment with project requirements [2].

Joint spacing for roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements is prescribed to control cracking and to support the expected service life, which is estimated to be 20 years [1]. For RCC sections less than 8 in thick the control joints are spaced between 15 ft and 20 ft, while for thicknesses of 8 in or greater the spacing equals the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 ft; shoulders must align with the adjacent mainline joints [1]. The intended effect of these spacings is to initiate cracks at predictable locations, thereby contributing to distress resistance over the design life [1]. Ride quality for RCC pavements is not evaluated with the International Roughness Index and the guidance explicitly advises that IRI should not be used to determine ride quality or trigger payment incentives [1]. In slip‑form concrete pavement construction joint spacing is expected to control cracking and preserve ride quality by keeping joints within tight location tolerances, requiring saw cuts to be placed directly ±1 inch over the centerline of dowel baskets or mechanically implanted dowels [2]. Edge slumping is limited so that edge slump does not exceed 1/8 inch between adjacent lanes and lanes and ramps, and does not exceed 1/4 inch between lanes and shoulders [2]. Surface smoothness is measured by a straight‑edge test and the pavement surface will be accepted when the surface does not vary more than 3/16 inch using a 12‑foot straightedge [2]. Macrotexture performance is measured by confirming a minimum Mean Texture Depth of 0.040 inches in accordance with ASTM E965 [2]. Load transfer effectiveness is verified by pull‑out testing of tie bars after the concrete has attained a compressive strength of 3,500 psi, requiring either a resistance of at least 12,000 pounds or a slippage not greater than 1/32 inch and calculating resistance per cubic foot as required by Table B [2]. Serviceability for traffic opening is measured by the compressive strength of concrete cylinders, with accelerated pavements required to reach at least 1,200 psi, and pavement thickness is confirmed by core drilling or non‑destructive testing in accordance with PTM No. 606/605 [2].
