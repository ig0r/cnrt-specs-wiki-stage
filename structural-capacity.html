Structural Capacity

Part of: Pavement Condition Assessment is-subtopic-of
Structural Capacity is a topic under Pavement Condition Assessment.
structural-capacitystructural-deficienciesstructural-adequacystructural-conditionstructural-conditions
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Structural Capacity is an evaluation of the existing pavement system—including subgrade material, layer composition, and drainage condition—to determine whether it can sustain projected traffic loads without excessive deformation[1]. The assessment quantifies the effective structural capacity (D_eff for rigid/composite pavements or SN_eff for flexible pavements) using non‑destructive in‑situ testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer, Dynamic Cone Penetrometer, or Light Weight Deflectometer to measure subgrade and layer stiffness[1]. These capacity values then direct design choices—overlay type, layer thickness, base repair, and other rehabilitation actions—to ensure the rebuilt pavement meets future traffic and environmental demands while maintaining proper drainage[1].

Purpose and investigation objectives

Structural Capacity evaluates the existing pavement system—including the subgrade material, the current layer composition, and the drainage condition—to assess whether it can support anticipated traffic loads without excessive deformation. The investigation must answer what future traffic loading is expected, which distress types such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, or drainage issues are present and why they occurred, how stiff or strong the in‑situ subgrade and existing layers are based on Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD), Dynamic Cone Penetrometer (DCP), or Light Weight Deflectometer (LWD) testing, and what layer thicknesses are required to meet the structural requirement given the traffic and environmental conditions while ensuring proper drainage. In addition, the investigation focuses on quantifying the effective structural capacity of the pavement—denoted D_eff for rigid and composite pavements or SN_eff for flexible pavements—and must determine both the current effective capacity and the capacity required to accommodate future traffic (D_f or SN_f). To answer these questions the evaluation can use a condition survey or component analysis that considers pavement distress and layer properties, non‑destructive deflection testing with FWD data to assess in‑situ subgrade and pavement stiffness, or a remaining‑life method based on past traffic and present condition. [1]

Structural‑capacity assessments derived from Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing serve as a primary engineering input that guides a wide range of planning and design actions, because when non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures indicate insufficient capacity, a binder‑course thickness increase is specified and observed failures in the base trigger base‑repair actions that replace the deficient material with a layer capable of providing adequate structural capacity and proper drainage [1]. For full‑depth reclamation projects the selection among basic pulverization, calcium‑chloride amendment, or asphalt/chemical additives is guided by the expected structure‑layer coefficient values that reflect differing capacities, ensuring the reclaimed layer thickness, drainage improvements, and material design are appropriate for the anticipated traffic loading over the pavement’s design life [1]. FWD testing on existing Portland cement concrete pavements is required prior to designing an overlay; the results determine whether a bonded or unbonded overlay is appropriate, dictate the necessary overlay thickness, and identify ancillary work such as slab stabilization, subdrainage, or full‑depth patching [1]. When pavements are in good to fair condition thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays of 2.5 to 4 inches should be considered to add structural capacity while reducing maintenance cycles; for bonded JPCP overlays the recommended thickness is 4 to 8 inches, and when capacity is moderate to poor the purpose of unbonded overlays is to restore structural capacity to existing pavements that are moderately to significantly deteriorated, with surface‑preparation steps such as slab stabilization triggered by rocking or faulting and drainage issues addressed before overlay placement [1]. Pre‑rehabilitation FWD tests provide valuable information about pavement stiffness, which informs the structural requirement for the pavement based on anticipated traffic and environmental conditions; a distress survey that records the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues is specifically considered in the rehabilitation strategy to assure each is appropriately addressed [1]. Reliable estimates of future traffic loading are required before construction so planners can determine the appropriate reclaimed layer thickness, necessary drainage improvements, and material design for full‑depth reclamation, ensuring the rebuilt pavement retains adequate structural capacity over its design life [1]. Non‑destructive testing also supplies friction and riding‑quality data that engineers compare against the terminal serviceability index required for the roadway’s Maintenance Functional Classification, guaranteeing that any new, reconstructed or overlay designs shall be performed using at least the terminal serviceability value given in the applicable table [1]. The results of the structural‑capacity analysis expressed as daily 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) tell planners whether a proposed pavement can be designed with the Simple Method or requires the Rigorous Method; for new construction, if the layer thicknesses determined by the Simple Method would exceed the minimum values in Table 9.4 the ESALs must be evaluated with the Rigorous Method, and for rehabilitation projects when the calculated requirements do not exceed the existing capacity the Simple Method may be used, but if they do exceed it the Rigorous Method must be applied; an average daily traffic projection exceeding 8 000 also triggers use of the Rigorous Method [1]. The calculated slab thickness derived from structural‑capacity analysis tells planners exactly how thick a rigid pavement must be to meet durability and rideability goals while staying economical, with rounding to the nearest inch for pavements thicker than 7 inches and a quantity less than 30 000 square yards, otherwise rounding follows half‑inch increments; because designs based on saturated subgrade strength tests already provide ample reserve capacity for spring‑thaw reductions, engineers can rely on the thickness output without adding special frost provisions unless differential frost heave is expected [1]. Designers must determine the combination of design variables that will result in the most economical rigid pavement design while maintaining required durability and rideability qualities, ensuring that the recommended pavement depth falls within the minimum and maximum limits listed in Table 8.3 so structural capacity supports later maintenance or rehabilitation decisions [1]. The Design Structural Number (SN) quantifies the strength a pavement must provide; planners and designers verify that the Calculated SN is greater than the Design SN, using either the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method available in DARWin to translate the required SN into specific layer thicknesses while accounting for surface, base and subbase cost per inch, minimum and maximum lift depths, and structural coefficients [1]. Effective structural capacity provides a quantitative basis for overlay planning and design by indicating whether the existing pavement can sustain future traffic loads (Df or SNf) and which overlay type is appropriate given the current condition; when a condition survey or component analysis reveals distress or layer deficiencies those observations feed directly into the calculation of Deff for rigid/composite pavements or SNeff for flexible pavements and guide selection of an overlay that will restore sufficient capacity, while non‑destructive deflection testing using FWD data supplies in‑situ stiffness values that refine the effective capacity estimate, allowing engineers to decide whether rehabilitation or full replacement is warranted and supporting long‑term maintenance scheduling through remaining‑life predictions [1]. Laboratory slab tests of macro‑fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays demonstrate that the added residual strength (f150) of 100 to 225 psi, when combined with the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture, yields an effective flexural strength that can be entered into mechanistic‑empirical design programs; this increase permits designers to specify thinner overlays—often only 3 to 6 inches thick—while still satisfying traffic loading requirements on high‑volume routes with heavy repeated loads, and the recommended fiber content of 0.2 %–0.5 % by volume improves load‑transfer efficiency by up to 30 % and reduces crack widths and frequency, allowing maintenance planners to anticipate lower crack‑related deterioration and longer intervals between rehabilitation [1]. When total patching percentages are less than 10 % the pavement is deemed structurally sound and qualifies for low‑depth preservation treatments such as micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, or thin asphalt overlays; flexible‑pavement overlays that exceed 2 inches will not be eligible for Pavement Preservation, and rigid overlays that exceed 4.5 inches with a Superpave 9.5 mm mix are also ineligible, while patching greater than 10 % of the pavement area is generally not cost‑effective and prompts planners to consider more extensive rehabilitation or reconstruction to raise structural capacity [1]. Structural‑capacity results derived from roadway‑management system pavement history and traffic data allow planners to identify segments that are under‑designed for current loads, prioritize rehabilitation or replacement projects, select the minimum effective treatment, and apply Maintenance Functional Codes to set appropriate design criteria, thereby directly guiding design choices, maintenance scheduling, and overall roadway‑management decisions [1]. Load‑transfer unit testing supplies quantitative limits on joint movement that can be used to guide pavement planning and design; when the measured Linear Variable Differential Transformer (LVDT) differential deflection does not exceed 7.5 mils at 1 million cycles and the increase after 10 million cycles is not greater than a 2.5‑mil increment, engineers have documented evidence that the dowel system will sustain traffic loads over its intended service life, informing selection of bar type, coating thickness, and anticipated replacement intervals, and these same thresholds become performance criteria during maintenance inspections or rehabilitation planning to ensure any repaired or upgraded joint meets the established capacity standards [2].

Scope and applicability

Structural capacity considerations apply to both asphalt‑concrete and Portland‑cement‑concrete pavements when existing layers are deficient or additional support is required; for asphalt surfaces, base repair replaces areas of obvious failure with materials that provide adequate structural capacity and facilitate drainage, and a binder course may be added to increase structural capacity based on non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures, while for concrete pavements structural capacity considerations involve selecting an asphalt or unbonded‑concrete structural overlay, a crack‑and‑seat with a structural asphalt overlay, or opting for total reconstruction when warranted. When a bonded concrete overlay is used on Portland cement concrete pavement that is in good to fair condition, provided the subgrade is stable and drainage is functional, the overlay adds structural capacity; when the existing pavement is moderately to significantly deteriorated, an unbonded overlay restores structural capacity by constructing a new pavement on the existing stable platform, and these concepts may also be used for asphalt or composite pavements when a bonded JPCP overlay is specified for surface correction or rutting mitigation. Structural capacity is applied to rigid pavements whose design is based on saturated subgrade strength tests because such designs provide ample reserve structural capacity to withstand periods of reduced support during spring thaws, and it may also be considered when differential frost heave is expected, although increasing pavement thickness to boost structural strength has limited success for rigid pavements. The design structural number used to express structural capacity is applied in full‑depth flexible pavement designs and is appropriate for flexible pavements that consist of a surface layer, a base layer and a subbase layer, where the base may be either an aggregate/cement or an aggregate/lime/pozzolan material; for all layers the drainage coefficient mi shall be set to 1.0, and the resulting SN must satisfy the minimum and maximum course depths given in Tables 9.4 and 9.5, and when a flexible pavement with one of those base materials is selected, both alternatives must be included in the bid proposal. Structural capacity is evaluated for rigid or composite pavements (concrete) and flexible pavements (asphalt) using D_eff for the former and SNeff for the latter whenever an overlay design requires knowledge of the existing pavement’s ability to support traffic; the evaluation may be performed through a condition survey or component analysis that considers pavement distress and layer analysis, by non‑destructive deflection testing with Falling Weight Deflectometer data, or by a remaining‑life method based on past traffic and current condition (though the latter is not used for Department designs), however, when the existing concrete roadway exhibits severe cracking or faulting, applying a bonded PCC overlay would be inappropriate. Structural capacity is most appropriate when a fiber‑reinforced concrete overlay is placed over an existing asphalt or composite pavement, especially in sections that experience heavy repeated traffic loads, variable support conditions, or require an increased design or service life; the FRC provides additional structural capacity and helps control crack‑related distresses such as wide cracks, slab movement, misalignment, plastic shrinkage cracking and faulting, a bonded overlay can be designed when the underlying asphalt pavement is in fair to good condition, while a poor or deteriorated pavement typically calls for an unbonded overlay, both benefiting from the FRC’s higher flexural strength and load‑transfer efficiency. The assessment of a pavement’s structural capacity is limited when it relies only on surface‑distress observations because excess moisture can result in the loss of pavement material integrity and the weakening of the pavement’s structural capacity; therefore, whenever drainage problems, wet subgrade locations, or significant rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, or other distress are identified, complementary in‑situ testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer, or light weight deflectometer should be performed to quantify stiffness and strength before selecting a rehabilitation strategy. The Simple Method for estimating pavement structural requirements may be used only on collectors and local roads and provided that the pavement layer thicknesses determined using the Simple Method do not exceed the minimum layer thicknesses for the appropriate type of roadway; if those limits are exceeded for new construction, or if the required structural capacity of a rehabilitation project exceeds the existing pavement’s capacity, the daily 18‑kip ESALs must be evaluated using the Rigorous Method, and on cross routes with direct access to limited‑access freeways the Simple Method may not be used when the projected design‑year ADT is greater than 8,000, in which case the Rigorous Method must be used. Even though rigid pavements designed from saturated subgrade strength tests possess ample reserve structural capacity for spring‑thaw reductions, generally the mitigation of differential frost heave through increased structural strength design (thickness of pavement) has a limited success rate for rigid pavements; consequently, when differential frost heave is expected the designer should refer to the Frost Design provisions rather than rely solely on structural capacity. The effective structural capacity of an existing pavement can be evaluated by condition survey or component analysis, non‑destructive deflection testing, and the Remaining Life approach, but the Remaining Life method is NOT to be used for Department pavement designs because accurate historical traffic data are lacking; when that limitation applies a different evaluation method—such as a condition survey/component analysis or Falling Weight Deflectometer‑based deflection testing—must be employed. The structural capacity information derived from Falling Weight Deflectometer testing and the automated pavement condition surveys (STAMPP) can be limited because the existing data may contain imperfections and may not capture certain distresses such as premature mid‑slab cracking, aggregate‑related problems like Vanport Limestone or Alkali Silica Reaction, and ramp pavement conditions; consequently, when these specific conditions are suspected—or when STAMPP data does not identify them—additional investigation such as detailed field visual inspections or targeted tests (for example skid resistance testing by BOMO) should be employed to complement the FWD results. When Roadway Management System data are used to evaluate a pavement’s structural capacity, the analysis can be limited by outdated or incomplete information: condition surveys provide only a one‑time snapshot that does not change when maintenance or rehabilitation work is done until a later resurvey, traffic counts may be several years old, and a layer could have been missed in the pavement history if it resulted from surface treatments or other maintenance work; because these gaps reduce confidence in the capacity estimate, field visual inspection and direct testing of the existing pavement should be employed as complementary investigations whenever the RMS data are stale, lack detail on recent work, or show possible omissions in layer history. Existing information and records

A structural‑capacity review should begin by obtaining a reliable estimate of future traffic loading for the roadway and then gathering the traffic records supplied by the Bureau of Planning and Research or the Roadway Management System, which must contain the initial average daily traffic, the annual percentage growth rate, the average daily truck proportion together with its vehicle‑class breakdown, and the directional factor. The design (performance) period for which the pavement is expected to remain serviceable should also be identified. When the existing RMS classification data are absent or outdated, a 24‑hour portable or 8‑hour manual truck classification count must be obtained for the project location. A recent condition survey or component analysis that records current distress such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, edge deterioration, joint spalling and faulting, as well as drainage problems, should be collected, and the annual Automated Pavement Condition Survey information from the STAMPP program should be reviewed to capture these distresses. In‑situ testing results—including Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements of existing stiffness and Dynamic Cone Penetrometer or Light‑Weight Deflectometer data on the subgrade—must be incorporated, together with detailed material sampling information that specifies the number, locations, depths and handling of samples taken from the candidate pavement. Any existing drainage deficiencies and required layer‑thickness considerations should be identified because excess moisture can reduce structural capacity. The reviewer should also consult the Roadway Management System for pavement history, condition and roughness records, friction characteristics, divider type, lane count, urban/rural codes and the Maintenance Functional Codes that guide design criteria, recognizing that condition snapshots may be outdated until a new survey and that traffic volumes can be several years old. Finally, documented histories of routine maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation and reconstruction activities—including crack sealing, joint sealing, spall repair, patching and base repairs—must be examined to understand past performance and any prior capacity‑enhancing treatments. The existing records frequently omit forward‑looking deflection (FWD) data, which the guidelines require before any overlay design, leaving the pavement’s true structural capacity uncertain; FWD testing should be conducted on existing PCCP prior to designing an overlay. Core samples from joints are often missing, so taking cores of existing joints is needed to verify good condition. Documentation of slab movement or faulting is frequently absent, making it unclear whether slab stabilization or jacking will be required, and the adequacy of subsurface drainage is often undocumented, creating further uncertainty about support conditions. Field investigation for structural capacity must also address the absence of a reliable estimate of future traffic loading, which is required before design. In many cases a recent pavement condition survey is missing, and without a distress survey the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems that influence capacity cannot be captured. When existing data are insufficient to define subgrade strength, in‑situ stiffness information from FWD testing, complemented by DCP or LWD measurements, must be obtained. Sampling plans that lack defined numbers, locations, depths, or handling procedures, as well as any unidentified excess moisture conditions in the subgrade, must be clarified through on‑site testing and observation. Because the RMS traffic count system still lacks a fully updated FHWA 13 vehicle classification breakdown for many links, structural capacity analysis must rely on field‑collected truck classification surveys to fill those gaps; only about 30 % of the roughly 7,000 annual counts are classification counts, and when a post‑01/01/2008 count does not show a “TYPE” of “MACHINE” or “MANUAL”, the records are considered incomplete, requiring use of pre‑2008 classifications unless a new manual count recorded on Form PS‑121 is performed. The pavement condition records that depend heavily on annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (STAMPP) do not capture premature mid‑slab cracking that may stem from inadequate subbase material, nor can they detect aggregate‑related problems such as Vanport Limestone concerns or Alkali Silica Reaction; ramp sections are omitted entirely. Because these deficiencies may mask reductions in structural capacity, visual inspections and FWD testing are required to confirm the true condition and to verify whether patching greater than 10 % of the pavement area would make a preservation project uneconomical. Finally, RMS records represent only a snapshot of the segment on the survey day and remain unchanged after any maintenance or rehabilitation until a later resurvey, creating a potential mismatch between recorded and actual conditions; traffic data may be several years old, and pavement history can omit layers added as surface treatments or other maintenance work, with lapses in recording further obscuring the true structural composition. [1]

Visual inspection and condition assessment

When evaluating a pavement’s structural capacity, the reviewer should examine drainage problems, any areas of obvious failure that call for base repair to restore adequate support, faulted or sunken rigid slabs, voids beneath the existing rigid base, visible cracking—including transverse cracks—spall damage and joint deterioration, high or curled/warped slab sections, and minor rutting as evaluation criteria for both asphalt concrete and Portland cement concrete surfaces. The inspector should also look for slab movement or faulting, rocking slabs, pumping action, surface spalling, and subsurface drainage problems that must be identified and corrected because inadequate subdrainage can compromise the pavement’s ability to carry loads over its design life. A visual distress survey must document the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, and any drainage issues because these distresses indicate how traffic loads are affecting the structure and can reveal underlying damage mechanisms that could compromise future performance; any existing drainage problems should be identified and corrected before reconstruction. Visible surface distresses explicitly referenced in the serviceability framework—cracks, patches and rut depth—should be inspected as they feed into the Present Serviceability Index calculation together with overall roughness, providing primary clues about whether structural capacity is being exceeded or compromised. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, observable cracking characteristics—specifically the width of existing cracks, any widening over time, and plastic‑shrinkage cracks—should be assessed, along with slab movement or misalignment and joint faulting, because macrofibers reduce such displacement but do not replace dowel bars needed to control faulting. Rutting that may require milling and overlay, faulted or failed joints, any cracks—including transverse or longitudinal spalling—and existing asphalt patches or failed concrete patches should be inspected; joint failure is confirmed with Falling Weight Deflectometer testing. Finally, the annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys should be reviewed for cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling or weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting, and premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements—often linked to inadequate subbase material—as signals of potential loss of capacity, while aggregate‑related problems such as Vanport Limestone concerns or Alkali Silica Reaction should also be considered because they can degrade performance. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 6 (pp. 150–151), Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

A recent pavement condition survey—performed according to the criteria in Publication 336, Publication 343, or a comparable procedure such as MicroPaver™—is used to identify each distress type (e.g., rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, drainage issues), assess its severity, and record its extent and location. The resulting distress survey provides both condition data and insight into the underlying causes, and a summary report is then prepared that documents the level of distress together with the corresponding observations, ensuring that the severity of each listed distress is specifically considered in the rehabilitation strategy. Field measurements and testing

Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing is required before designing an overlay for existing Portland cement concrete pavements, and it should be conducted both prior to rehabilitation to obtain in‑situ subgrade and pavement stiffness profiles from measured surface deflections and after construction to determine the stiffness of the new pavement as well as to verify repaired joints meet design criteria. The FWD data also indicate concrete pavement joint efficiency, locations and quantities of required patching, and total patching percentages less than 10 % signal a structurally sound surface, whereas patching greater than 10 % generally indicates a project type beyond simple preservation. Extracted concrete cores from existing joints should be inspected to determine depth and integrity of the existing pavement, providing information that informs milling depths for preservation treatments. Dynamic cone penetrometer (DCP) testing and Light Weight Deflectometer (LWD) testing are useful for assessing subgrade conditions. A condition survey or component analysis examines visible distress and performs layer‑by‑layer assessment to infer remaining strength, contributing to the evaluation of structural capacity. Annual automated pavement condition surveys (videologging) generate surface‑distress data such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and faulting, which feed treatment matrices aimed at restoring structural capacity. The Single Locked Wheel Skid Tester provides friction values that indicate surface grip, an aspect of load‑bearing performance. High Speed Profilers equipped with laser sensors measure roughness to assess riding quality, supplying objective data on pavement smoothness without damaging the surface. Structural adequacy testing of concrete pavement joints per PTM No. 642 uses Linear Variable Differential Transformers (LVDTs) to record differential deflection between loaded and unloaded sides under dynamic and static loading; the measured differential deflection must not exceed 7.5 mils at 1 million cycles. The LVDT differential deflection may increase no more than 2.5 mils by 10 million cycles from the corresponding 1‑million‑cycle value. A 67.5‑V holiday detector is employed to locate coating holidays, and the joint is allowed no more than two holidays per linear foot on average. [1][2]

The structural adequacy testing required for load‑transfer units calls for using Linear Variable Differential Transformers to measure differential deflection under both dynamic and static loading on the approach and leave sides of a joint, with the deflection limited to not more than 7.5 mil at one million cycles and an increase of no more than 2.5 mil when the test is extended to ten million cycles; the same testing regime also requires detection of coating holidays using a 67.5‑V holiday detector with a limit of two holidays per linear foot on average. Sampling and laboratory investigation

In FDR projects the structural‑capacity assessment requires collecting samples from each constituent layer of the existing pavement – surface course, base or subbase material, and the supporting subgrade – because the sampling criteria listed in Section J.2.D must be applied. The number of samples and their locations are set according to the guidance in Section J.2.D.1, while the required sample size follows Section J.2.D.2; appropriate techniques are described in Section J.2.D.3 and the depth of each sample is defined by Section J.2.D.4 so that every identified layer is represented. Handling and laboratory evaluation follow the procedures in Section J.2.D.5, and samples are taken during the initial pavement condition survey and again after construction to verify the reclaimed layer’s structural capacity. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126)

To evaluate the structural capacity of fiber‑reinforced concrete (FRC) overlays, laboratory slab testing is employed to determine both flexural and ultimate load capacities as well as the load‑transfer efficiency between adjacent slabs, which indicates how effectively the overlay can sustain repeated traffic loads and maintain joint performance [1]. In addition, the residual strength test defined in ASTM C1609 is conducted to obtain the f150 value; when combined with the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture it provides an effective flexural strength for design, and typical residual strengths for overlay applications range between 100 and 225 psi [1]. These laboratory investigations also demonstrate that FRC retains significant residual strength after a large number of freeze/thaw cycles, offering insight into durability under cyclic temperature stresses [1]. For reinforced and plain cement concrete pavements, structural capacity is further verified through structural adequacy testing required by PTM No. 642, in which the load‑transfer unit is subjected to dynamic and static loading while a Linear Variable Differential Transformer records differential deflection; the test must not exceed a maximum differential deflection of 7.5 mil at one million cycles and may increase no more than 2.5 mil at ten million cycles relative to the one‑million‑cycle value [2]. Bond‑breaking performance is also assessed in accordance with AASHTO M 254, where Type A coating systems must develop sufficiently low bond strength so that a shop‑applied bond breaker is unnecessary, and Type B coatings must satisfy the pull‑out load requirements specified by the same standard [2].

Structural and functional performance

Evaluating a pavement’s structural capacity begins with determining the thickness of each structural layer, where any required additional thickness is identified through non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures. Surface friction is measured whenever an overlay does not add structure, using equipment such as a Single Locked Wheel Skid Tester to obtain friction values. Load transfer on working transverse cracks in Portland cement concrete slabs is verified by dowel bar retrofits. For existing Portland cement concrete pavement, a Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) test must be performed before an overlay is designed, providing deflection data that are used to assess structural capacity, subgrade characteristics, and concrete joint efficiency. Subgrade strength is further evaluated with dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer tests. Riding quality and roughness are captured by High Speed Profilers equipped with laser sensors, and these measurements feed into serviceability indices such as the Present Serviceability Index, which is derived from pavement roughness, cracks, patches and rut depth. Skid‑friction testing is employed to verify skid resistance of the surface. Traffic loading information, expressed as Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) data, informs the capacity analysis. A recent pavement‑condition survey documents distress types—including rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues—and provides insight into functional condition. Within the Roadway Management System, recorded pavement‑condition data together with roughness values, documented friction characteristics, compiled pavement‑history information, and traffic counts and classifications are used to analyze existing structural capacity and describe functional performance. To assess the structural capacity of a concrete pavement joint, the required measurement is the Linear Variable Differential Transformers (LVDT) differential deflection between the loaded and unloaded sides of the joint under both dynamic and static loading; this differential deflection must not exceed 7.5 mil at 1 million cycles and any increase at 10 million cycles may not be greater than 2.5 mil compared with the 1‑million‑cycle value, and the measurement is performed on both the approach and leave sides of the joint. The load‑transfer units specified in Section 705.3 are required to demonstrate through PTM No. 642 testing that the absolute LVDT differential deflection between the loaded and unloaded sides of a joint does not exceed 7.5 mils at one million loading cycles, and that the increase in differential deflection by ten million cycles is no more than 2.5 mils beyond the one‑million‑cycle value. These limits embody the design assumptions for expected traffic loading over the pavement’s intended service life; compliance indicates that the measured structural capacity remains within the applicable thresholds and should satisfy remaining service‑life needs, whereas values above these limits would suggest insufficient capacity. Data interpretation and diagnosis

Structural capacity is assessed by first conducting a pavement condition survey that records visible distress such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems; these observations are linked to underlying damage mechanisms and the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, and drainage issues should be specifically considered in the rehabilitation strategy. Field measurements obtained from in‑situ tests—including Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD), dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer—quantify the stiffness of existing pavement layers and subgrade, and FWD data is used to determine the characteristics necessary for structural design. Where a verified skid‑friction problem exists, skid‑friction testing must be submitted to BOMO. Properly collected samples are sent to the laboratory to establish material properties and appropriate stabilizer content, recognizing that proper sampling plays a vital role in successful design and construction. The measured stiffness values, laboratory‑derived material parameters, traffic loading estimates expressed as Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) figures, and anticipated environmental conditions are then combined to select a layer thickness that satisfies the required structural demand and to identify any drainage corrections needed before reconstruction. Annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys performed under the STAMPP program provide performance data on cracking, rutting, edge deterioration and other distresses; because these surveys may miss issues such as premature mid‑slab cracking or aggregate‑related reactions, field visual observations must be recorded and presented during field views to supplement the survey results. This integrated approach ensures that treatment decisions—whether routine maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction—are based on both quantitative measurements and qualitative field insights. In section 2.1 GENERAL, the observed loss of structural capacity is most often traced to poor drainage that permits surface or subsurface water to remain in the pavement system and to weak support where areas of obvious failure have been identified and must be replaced with material that can provide adequate structural capacity; material deterioration is indicated by the need for base repair and replacement of failed sections, while construction defects such as insufficient binder‑course thickness also reduce capacity because additional structural layer thickness is required based on non‑destructive testing (NDT) results. In section 4.9 REHABILITATION, the loss of structural capacity is most often linked to subsurface drainage deficiencies, because the guidelines require adequate subdrainage where drainage problems exist and advise against bonded overlays when such issues are not addressed; it also reflects weak or unstable support, as slab movement, rocking, pumping, or faulting trigger a need for slab stabilization or jacking before overlaying, and material deterioration such as surface spalling indicates that the existing pavement no longer meets the good‑to‑fair condition needed for bonded overlays. In section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES > B, inadequate drainage is again identified as a primary cause because excess moisture can result in loss of pavement material integrity and weakening of structural capacity, and wet subgrade locations should be identified and effective drainage installed before full‑depth reclamation; weak support contributes as well, with in‑situ testing (FWD, DCP, LWD) required to assess subgrade strength and sampling to verify material quality, while material deterioration is reflected by distress‑survey results that note the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, and drainage issues, all of which must be specifically considered in the rehabilitation strategy. Finally, section 13.7 PAVEMENT PROJECT CRITERIA > E notes that weak support may arise when subbase material is inadequate, as evidenced by premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements, and that material deterioration can stem from aggregate‑related problems such as Vanport Limestone concerns and alkali‑silica reaction (ASR), which affect pavement integrity even though they are not captured by STAMPP surveys; when the functional pavement life has been exceeded, aging becomes a factor that necessitates reconstruction to restore capacity. Findings, confidence, and next actions

Structural capacity can be evaluated using non‑destructive testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer, light weight deflectometer, or other acceptable design procedures; these methods provide stiffness and load‑carrying information that guide decisions on additional structural layer thickness, rehabilitation alternatives, and overlay design. [1] For full‑depth reclamation projects the structure layer coefficient varies with the type of stabilization material, ranging from values typical of subbase material to those representative of stabilized materials. [1] In rehabilitation of Portland cement concrete pavements (PCCP), FWD testing prior to overlay design confirms existing capacity; bonded overlays add structural capacity and eliminate surface distress on pavements that are in good to fair condition, while unbonded overlays restore capacity for pavements that are moderately to significantly deteriorated. [1] Thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays of 2.5 to 4 inches or standard bonded overlays of 4 to 8 inches are prescribed for asphalt or composite bases, and the amount of capacity added depends on overlay thickness. [1] Incorporating macrofibers into concrete overlays provides additional structural capacity by markedly raising flexural and ultimate load capacity, with residual strength typically ranging between 100 and 225 psi; however, slump may be reduced by up to 4 inches depending on fiber type and content, requiring trial mixes and careful specification. [1] Rigid pavements designed on the basis of saturated subgrade strength tests have ample reserve structural capacity to withstand reduced support during spring thaws, although increasing thickness offers limited mitigation of differential frost heave for rigid pavements. [1] Rehabilitation treatments such as extensive base repair, patching, or overlays improve or increase structural capacity, whereas reconstruction is reserved for sections whose functional life has been exceeded; patching greater than 10 % of the pavement area is generally not cost‑effective for preservation projects. [1] Limitations remain because specific numeric thresholds or performance limits for acceptable capacity are not provided, creating uncertainty about exact design criteria and confidence in predicted pavement life; future traffic loading estimates may be uncertain, distress surveys give only a snapshot of damage mechanisms, test results can vary with location and sampling quality, and excess moisture can weaken structural capacity. [1] Condition data from the Roadway Management System or automated surveys represent a single point in time, may miss premature cracking, aggregate‑related problems, or ramp pavements, and traffic counts may be several years old; consequently field inspection and testing remain necessary. [1] The effectiveness of overlays is limited by subgrade condition and drainage—unstable support, poor drainage, or slab movement must be corrected before placement, otherwise performance is uncertain. [1] The investigation demonstrates that structural adequacy of load‑transfer units in reinforced and plain cement concrete pavements can be confirmed when dowel bars satisfy the LVDT differential deflection limits prescribed for testing; the maximum differential deflection must not exceed 7.5 mils at 1 million cycles and the increase at 10 million cycles must not be greater than 2.5 mil relative to the 1‑million‑cycle value. [2] These criteria apply specifically to the dowel‑bar types, coatings, and cyclic loading conditions outlined in the specification, so conclusions are limited to those configurations and do not address other aspects of pavement structural capacity or behavior beyond the tested cycle range, leaving uncertainty for alternative loads, longer service lives, or different pavement components. [2]

After a structural‑capacity assessment indicates deficiency, the project team should verify the required layer thickness using non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures and evaluate drainage to improve removal of surface and subsurface water; if areas of obvious failure are identified, base repair shall be performed by replacing those areas with material that will provide adequate structural capacity. For asphalt overlays a binder course may be added when the non‑destructive testing indicates insufficient capacity, whereas for rigid pavements corrective actions such as slab jacking, dowel‑bar retrofit, or full‑depth reclamation should be considered based on the anticipated structure layer coefficient values associated with the chosen stabilization material. Falling Weight Deflectometer testing shall be conducted on existing Portland cement concrete pavement prior to any overlay design and also used to verify joint repair and full‑depth patching; when a bonded overlay is selected for pavements in good to fair condition, joint cores must be taken to verify condition, while an unbonded overlay requires a fine‑graded one‑inch minimum asphalt separation layer (or a non‑woven geotextile) and may involve cracking or rubblizing the existing concrete into specified fragment sizes before placement. If slab movement or faulting is present, slab stabilization or jacking shall be undertaken, and adequate subdrainage must be provided where subsurface drainage problems exist. Additional in‑situ testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements both before rehabilitation to gauge existing stiffness and after construction to confirm the new pavement’s stiffness should be performed, complemented by dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer tests to evaluate subgrade strength; representative material samples must then be collected according to the sampling criteria to support laboratory design of the stabilization method, and the required layer thickness shall be checked against traffic‑level and material‑composition requirements. Any identified drainage deficiencies shall be corrected before placing reclaimed pavement so that excess moisture does not degrade the restored capacity. When non‑destructive testing reveals that structural capacity is below what is needed, additional monitoring of friction and ride quality should be performed and the results compared to the terminal serviceability index for the road’s maintenance functional classification; design checks must verify that any new, reconstructed or overlay design uses at least the terminal serviceability value specified for that classification, and if capacity remains insufficient the appropriate corrective action—such as resurfacing, reconstruction, geometric upgrades, or surface treatments aimed at preserving durability—should be planned. If Simple Method layer‑thickness limits are exceeded for new construction or the required structural capacity of a rehabilitation project is greater than the existing pavement’s capacity, the next step shall be to run a rigorous 18‑kip ESAL calculation; similarly, when the projected design‑year ADT on a cross route exceeds 8 000, the Rigorous Method must be applied, and if traffic classification data are missing or appear outdated a truck classification survey (24‑hour portable or 8‑hour manual) shall be performed before completing the ESAL analysis. After determining structural capacity for an overlay that will use macro‑fibers, the engineer should verify the macro‑fiber contribution by performing ASTM C1609 residual strength testing to obtain f150 (typically 100–225 psi) and run trial fiber‑reinforced concrete batches to confirm that the mix meets required fresh properties such as slump loss up to 4 in. (100 mm) and any needed air‑content adjustments; cores of the existing pavement must be taken before design to ensure stripping is not prevalent when a bonded overlay is considered, and during service crack widths and joint load transfer efficiency should be monitored because fiber‑reinforced concrete can reduce crack growth and maintain LTE. Coring of the existing pavement is required to confirm existing layer thickness because it determines the required milling depth; if rutting or material problems are identified, milling and overlay depths of one‑and‑half inches or two inches may be exceeded to remove the deficient portion, and joint repair and full‑depth patching verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing shall remain below a ten‑percent patching limit unless cost effectiveness is demonstrated. Preservation options that respect these limits include micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses applied early in the maintenance cycle, cold in‑place recycling of the base to a maximum depth of four inches with a one‑and‑half‑inch (maximum) asphalt overlay, and ultra‑thin bonded concrete overlays limited to two inches as non‑structural wearing courses; any overlay on flexible pavements that exceeds two inches will not be eligible for consideration as Pavement Preservation. After using RMS pavement history and traffic data to evaluate a segment’s structural capacity, the program should be followed by on‑site visual inspection and physical testing of the existing pavement because the RMS snapshot may not reflect recent maintenance or missing layers; these field activities are essential to verify the actual condition before proceeding with design decisions. Following a structural‑capacity assessment, the pavement design shall require structural adequacy testing in accordance with PTM No. 642 to confirm that the Linear Variable Differential Transformer differential deflection does not exceed 7.5 mils at one million cycles and that the increase at ten million cycles is no greater than 2.5 mils. Routine monitoring shall verify that coated dowel bars have no more than two holidays per linear foot as detected with a 67.5‑V holiday detector; any coating damage observed must be repaired so that the total repaired area does not exceed two percent of the bar surface per foot and patching material never covers more than five percent of the bar surface.
