Structural Capacity

Part of: Pavement Condition Assessment is-subtopic-of
Structural Capacity is a topic under Pavement Condition Assessment.
structural-capacitystructural-deficienciesstructural-adequacystructural-conditionstructural-conditions
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Structural Capacity is the quantitative measure of a pavement’s ability to sustain present and future traffic loads without excessive deformation, expressed as D_eff for rigid or composite pavements and SN_eff for flexible pavements [1]. The assessment combines a distress‑focused condition survey (rutting, cracking, drainage issues, etc.) with non‑destructive in‑situ testing—principally Falling Weight Deflectometer, Dynamic Cone Penetrometer, or Light‑Weight Deflectometer—to evaluate subgrade and layer stiffness and to calculate the effective structural number required for the projected traffic and environmental conditions [1]. These results direct design actions such as binder‑course thickness increases, base repairs, bonded or unbonded overlays, or full‑depth reclamation, and are checked against performance limits (e.g., loaded slab corner deflection ≥0.02 in with joint efficiency ≥65 % for stabilization, or LVDT differential deflection ≤7.5 mil at 1 million cycles) to confirm that the pavement will meet its intended service life [2].

Purpose and investigation objectives

Structural Capacity evaluates the existing pavement system—including the subgrade material, the current layer composition, and the drainage condition—to assess whether it can support anticipated traffic loads without excessive deformation. The investigation must answer what future traffic loading is expected, which distress types such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, or drainage issues are present and why they occurred, how stiff or strong the in‑situ subgrade and existing layers are based on Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD), Dynamic Cone Penetrometer (DCP), or Light Weight Deflectometer (LWD) testing, and what layer thicknesses are required to meet the structural requirement given the traffic and environmental conditions while ensuring proper drainage. In addition, the investigation focuses on quantifying the effective structural capacity of the pavement—denoted D_eff for rigid and composite pavements or SN_eff for flexible pavements—and must determine both the current effective capacity and the capacity required to accommodate future traffic (D_f or SN_f). To answer these questions the evaluation can use a condition survey or component analysis that considers pavement distress and layer properties, non‑destructive deflection testing with FWD data to assess in‑situ subgrade and pavement stiffness, or a remaining‑life method based on past traffic and present condition. [1]

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), the structural‑capacity assessment of an existing pavement serves as a primary input for planning and design actions; when non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures indicate insufficient capacity, a binder‑course thickness increase is specified, and observed failures in the base trigger base‑repair actions that replace the deficient material with a layer capable of providing adequate structural capacity and proper drainage [1]. For full‑depth reclamation projects, the Pavement Policy Manual states that selection among basic pulverization, calcium‑chloride amendment, or asphalt/chemical additives is guided by the expected structure‑layer coefficient values that reflect differing capacities [1]. The manual further requires Falling Weight Deflectometer testing on existing Portland cement concrete pavements prior to designing an overlay, with the results determining whether a bonded or unbonded overlay is appropriate, dictating the necessary overlay thickness, and identifying ancillary work such as slab stabilization, subdrainage, or full‑depth patching [1]. For pavements in good to fair condition thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays of 2.5 to 4 inches should be considered to add structural capacity while reducing maintenance cycles [1]; for bonded JPCP overlays the recommended thickness is 4 to 8 inches [1]; when the capacity is moderate to poor the purpose of unbonded overlays is to restore structural capacity to existing pavements that are moderately to significantly deteriorated, and surface‑preparation steps such as slab stabilization are triggered when rocking or faulting is present, with drainage issues addressed before overlay placement [1]. The Pavement Policy Manual emphasizes that a reliable estimate of future traffic loading on the road should be obtained before construction so planners can determine the appropriate reclaimed layer thickness, necessary drainage improvements, and material design for full‑depth reclamation, thereby ensuring the rebuilt pavement retains adequate structural capacity over its design life [1]. Non‑destructive testing results, specifically Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements of structural capacity, are treated as an engineering input when choosing rehabilitation strategies; because the same testing package also provides friction and riding‑quality data, engineers can compare those values against the terminal serviceability index required for the roadway’s Maintenance Functional Classification and ensure that any new, reconstructed or overlay designs shall be performed using at least the terminal serviceability value given in this table [1]. The manual notes that structural‑capacity findings help planners decide whether resurfacing, reconstruction, or a surface treatment is warranted and guide designers to size the pavement structure so it will sustain anticipated traffic loads throughout its intended service life [1]. Results expressed as daily 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) tell planners whether a proposed pavement can be designed with the Simple Method or requires the Rigorous Method; for new construction, if the layer thicknesses determined by the Simple Method would exceed the minimum values in Table 9.4 the ESALs must be evaluated with the Rigorous Method to establish actual structural requirements, and for rehabilitation when the calculated requirements do not exceed the existing capacity the Simple Method may be used, but if they do exceed it the Rigorous Method must be applied; additionally, on cross routes that interchange with limited‑access freeways an average daily traffic projection exceeding 8 000 triggers use of the Rigorous Method for ESAL calculation [1]. The calculated slab thickness derived from structural‑capacity analysis tells planners exactly how thick a rigid pavement must be to meet durability and rideability goals while staying economical; pavements thicker than 7 inches and a quantity less than 30 000 square yards have the result rounded to the nearest inch, otherwise rounding follows half‑inch increments, and because designs based on saturated subgrade strength tests already provide ample reserve capacity for spring‑thaw reductions engineers can rely on the thickness output without adding special frost provisions unless differential frost heave is expected; the designer must determine the combination of design variables that will result in the most economical rigid pavement design while maintaining the required durability and rideability qualities, and the recommended pavement depth must also fall within the minimum and maximum limits listed in Table 8.3, ensuring that structural capacity supports later maintenance or rehabilitation decisions [1]. The Pavement Policy Manual further explains that the calculated Design Structural Number quantifies the strength a pavement must provide, so planners and designers verify that the proposed structure will be structurally adequate by ensuring the Calculated SN is greater than the Design SN; using either the Specified Thickness Design method or the Optimized Thickness Design method available in DARWin, engineers translate the required SN into specific layer thicknesses while accounting for surface, base and subbase cost (per inch), minimum and maximum lift depths, and structural coefficients, thereby informing material selection, cost‑effective layering, and compliance with design limits [1]. Evaluating the effective structural capacity provides a quantitative basis for overlay planning and design by indicating whether the existing pavement can sustain future traffic loads (Df or SNf) and which overlay type is appropriate given the current condition; when a condition survey or component analysis reveals distress or layer deficiencies those observations feed directly into the calculation of Deff for rigid/composite pavements or SNeff for flexible pavements and guide selection of an overlay that will restore sufficient capacity, while non‑destructive deflection testing using Falling Weight Deflectometer data supplies in‑situ stiffness values that refine the effective capacity estimate, allowing engineers to decide whether rehabilitation or full replacement is warranted, and the remaining life method can be used to predict service life and support long‑term maintenance scheduling [1]. Laboratory slab tests of macro‑fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays demonstrate that the added residual strength (f150) of 100 to 225 psi, when combined with the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture, yields an effective flexural strength (feff) that can be entered into mechanistic‑empirical design programs; this increase in flexural and ultimate load capacity permits designers to specify thinner overlays—often only 3 to 6 inches thick—while still satisfying traffic loading requirements on high‑volume routes with heavy repeated loads [1]. The recommended fiber content of 0.2 %–0.5 % by volume, despite a possible slump loss of up to 4 in, improves load‑transfer efficiency by up to 30 % and reduces crack widths and frequency, allowing maintenance planners to anticipate lower crack‑related deterioration and longer intervals between rehabilitation [1]. When total patching percentages are less than 10 % the pavement is deemed structurally sound and qualifies for low‑depth preservation treatments such as micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, or thin asphalt overlays; flexible‑pavement overlays that exceed 2 inches will not be eligible for Pavement Preservation, and rigid overlays that exceed 4½ inches with a Superpave 9.5 mm mix are also ineligible [1]; if the calculated patching required exceeds the threshold of greater than 10 % of the pavement area preservation is generally not cost‑effective and planners are prompted to consider more extensive rehabilitation or reconstruction to raise structural capacity, linking measured capacities to treatment matrices that enable prioritization of projects—routine maintenance for minor distress, preservation when capacity remains adequate, and rehabilitation involving base repair and/or overlays when additional structural capacity is needed [1]. Finally, structural‑capacity results derived from roadway management system pavement history and traffic data allow planners to identify segments that are under‑designed for current loads, prioritize rehabilitation or replacement projects, select the minimum effective treatment, and apply Maintenance Functional Codes to set appropriate design criteria, thereby directly guiding design choices, maintenance scheduling, and overall roadway‑management decisions [1]. According to the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 Specifications, structural‑capacity testing required by PTM No. 642 limits the LVDT differential deflection to not more than 7.5 mil at 1 million load cycles and restricts the increase to no greater than a 2.5‑mil rise at 10 million cycles, directly informing pavement design decisions by confirming that selected dowel bars will transfer loads without excessive joint movement [2]. Because these deflection limits are tied to specific coating requirements—providing a fusion‑bonded epoxy coating as a Type B coating with a minimum non‑abraded thickness of 8 mil and allowing no more than 2 holidays per linear foot as detected with a 67.5 V holiday detector—the results guide maintenance and rehabilitation actions, including repair of damaged coatings or replacement of bars that exceed the thresholds, and they serve as quantitative checkpoints for choosing dowel‑bar specifications, scheduling inspections, and justifying design modifications throughout the pavement’s service life [2]. Deflection testing performed according to ASTM D4695 provides measured loaded slab corner deflection values and joint efficiency percentages that serve as quantitative criteria for maintenance planning; when the measured deflection is at least 0.02 inch and the joint efficiency meets or exceeds 65 % the pavement may be slated for slab stabilization, while the same deflection with a joint efficiency less than 65 % triggers patching and stabilization actions [2]. These thresholds must be evaluated under temperature conditions above 40 °F and below 70 °F, and test data must be submitted fifteen working days before work begins to obtain approval, thereby enabling engineers to schedule interventions only where structural capacity is insufficient and to allocate resources for rehabilitation in accordance with the defined load‑bearing performance criteria [2].

Scope and applicability

Structural capacity considerations apply to both asphalt‑concrete and Portland‑cement‑concrete pavements when existing layers are deficient or additional support is required; for asphalt surfaces, base repair replaces areas of obvious failure with materials that provide adequate structural capacity and facilitate drainage, and a binder course may be added to increase structural capacity based on non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures, while for concrete pavements structural capacity considerations involve selecting an asphalt or unbonded‑concrete structural overlay, a crack‑and‑seat with a structural asphalt overlay, or opting for total reconstruction when warranted. When a bonded concrete overlay is used on Portland cement concrete pavement that is in good to fair condition, provided the subgrade is stable and drainage is functional, the overlay adds structural capacity; when the existing pavement is moderately to significantly deteriorated, an unbonded overlay restores structural capacity by constructing a new pavement on the existing stable platform, and these concepts may also be used for asphalt or composite pavements when a bonded JPCP overlay is specified for surface correction or rutting mitigation. Structural capacity is applied to rigid pavements whose design is based on saturated subgrade strength tests because such designs provide ample reserve structural capacity to withstand periods of reduced support during spring thaws, and it may also be considered when differential frost heave is expected, although increasing pavement thickness to boost structural strength has limited success for rigid pavements. The design structural number used to express structural capacity is applied in full‑depth flexible pavement designs and is appropriate for flexible pavements that consist of a surface layer, a base layer and a subbase layer, where the base may be either an aggregate/cement or an aggregate/lime/pozzolan material; for all layers the drainage coefficient mi shall be set to 1.0, and the resulting SN must satisfy the minimum and maximum course depths given in Tables 9.4 and 9.5, and when a flexible pavement with one of those base materials is selected, both alternatives must be included in the bid proposal. Structural capacity is evaluated for rigid or composite pavements (concrete) and flexible pavements (asphalt) using D_eff for the former and SNeff for the latter whenever an overlay design requires knowledge of the existing pavement’s ability to support traffic; the evaluation may be performed through a condition survey or component analysis that considers pavement distress and layer analysis, by non‑destructive deflection testing with Falling Weight Deflectometer data, or by a remaining‑life method based on past traffic and current condition (though the latter is not used for Department designs), however, when the existing concrete roadway exhibits severe cracking or faulting, applying a bonded PCC overlay would be inappropriate. Structural capacity is most appropriate when a fiber‑reinforced concrete overlay is placed over an existing asphalt or composite pavement, especially in sections that experience heavy repeated traffic loads, variable support conditions, or require an increased design or service life; the FRC provides additional structural capacity and helps control crack‑related distresses such as wide cracks, slab movement, misalignment, plastic shrinkage cracking and faulting, a bonded overlay can be designed when the underlying asphalt pavement is in fair to good condition, while a poor or deteriorated pavement typically calls for an unbonded overlay, both benefiting from the FRC’s higher flexural strength and load‑transfer efficiency. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 8 (p. 164), Ch. 9 (pp. 172–173), Ch. 10 (pp. 177–188)

The assessment of a pavement’s structural capacity is limited when it relies only on surface‑distress observations because excess moisture can cause loss of material integrity and weakening of the structure; consequently, whenever drainage problems, wet subgrade locations, significant rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, or other distress are identified, complementary in‑situ testing such as a Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer, or light weight deflectometer should be performed to quantify stiffness and strength before selecting a rehabilitation strategy. The Simple Method for estimating pavement structural requirements may be used only on collectors and local roads and only when the pavement layer thicknesses determined using the Simple Method do not exceed the minimum layer thicknesses for the appropriate type of roadway; if those limits are exceeded for new construction, or if the required structural capacity of a rehabilitation project exceeds the existing pavement’s capacity, the daily 18‑kip ESALs must be evaluated using the Rigorous Method, and on cross routes with direct access to limited‑access freeways the Simple Method may not be used when the projected design‑year ADT is greater than 8,000, in which case the Rigorous Method must be used. Even though rigid pavements designed from saturated subgrade strength tests possess ample reserve structural capacity for spring‑thaw reductions, generally the mitigation of differential frost heave through increased structural thickness has a limited success rate for rigid pavements; consequently, when differential frost heave is expected the designer should refer to the Frost Design provisions rather than rely solely on structural capacity. The effective structural capacity of an existing pavement can be evaluated by condition survey or component analysis, non‑destructive deflection testing, and the Remaining Life approach, but the Remaining Life method is NOT to be used for Department pavement designs because accurate historical traffic data are lacking; when that limitation applies a different evaluation method—such as a condition survey/component analysis or Falling Weight Deflectometer‑based deflection testing—must be employed. Structural capacity information derived from Falling Weight Deflectometer testing and the automated pavement condition surveys (STAMPP) can be limited because existing data may contain imperfections and may not capture certain distresses such as premature mid‑slab cracking, aggregate‑related problems like Vanport Limestone or Alkali Silica Reaction, and ramp pavement conditions; consequently, when these specific conditions are suspected—or when STAMPP data does not identify them—additional investigation such as detailed field visual inspections or targeted tests (for example skid resistance testing by BOMO) should be employed to complement the FWD results. When Roadway Management System data are used to evaluate a pavement’s structural capacity, the analysis can be limited by outdated or incomplete information: condition surveys provide only a one‑time snapshot that does not change when maintenance or rehabilitation work is done until a later resurvey, traffic counts may be several years old, and a layer could have been missed in the pavement history if it resulted from surface treatments or other maintenance work; because these gaps reduce confidence in the capacity estimate, field visual inspection and direct testing of the existing pavement should be employed as complementary investigations whenever the RMS data are stale, lack detail on recent work, or show possible omissions in layer history. Deflection testing, which is used to gauge a pavement’s structural capacity, cannot be relied upon when ambient conditions fall outside the prescribed range; testing must not be performed if air temperature is below 40 °F or exceeds 70 °F, nor during spring‑thaw or when the subgrade is frozen, and whenever these temperature or ground‑freeze limits are violated the specification requires a shift to visual verification of pumping quality or joint performance as an alternative (or complementary) investigation before deciding on slab stabilization. Additionally, the method only triggers stabilization actions for joints or cracks that exhibit a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more combined with a joint efficiency at or above 65%, indicating that structural‑capacity decisions are constrained by both environmental and measured‑deflection thresholds. Existing information and records

A structural‑capacity review should begin by obtaining a reliable estimate of future traffic loading for the roadway and then gathering the traffic records supplied by the Bureau of Planning and Research or the Roadway Management System, which must contain the initial average daily traffic, the annual percentage growth rate, the average daily truck proportion together with its vehicle‑class breakdown, and the directional factor. The design (performance) period for which the pavement is expected to remain serviceable should also be identified. When the existing RMS classification data are absent or outdated, a 24‑hour portable or 8‑hour manual truck classification count must be obtained for the project location. A recent condition survey or component analysis that records current distress such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, edge deterioration, joint spalling and faulting, as well as drainage problems, should be collected, and the annual Automated Pavement Condition Survey information from the STAMPP program should be reviewed to capture these distresses. In‑situ testing results—including Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements of existing stiffness and Dynamic Cone Penetrometer or Light‑Weight Deflectometer data on the subgrade—must be incorporated, together with detailed material sampling information that specifies the number, locations, depths and handling of samples taken from the candidate pavement. Any existing drainage deficiencies and required layer‑thickness considerations should be identified because excess moisture can reduce structural capacity. The reviewer should also consult the Roadway Management System for pavement history, condition and roughness records, friction characteristics, divider type, lane count, urban/rural codes and the Maintenance Functional Codes that guide design criteria, recognizing that condition snapshots may be outdated until a new survey and that traffic volumes can be several years old. Finally, documented histories of routine maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation and reconstruction activities—including crack sealing, joint sealing, spall repair, patching and base repairs—must be examined to understand past performance and any prior capacity‑enhancing treatments. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126), Ch. 7 (pp. 154–157), Ch. 10 (pp. 177–179), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221), Ch. 14 (p. 224)

The existing records frequently omit forward‑looking deflection (FWD) data, which the guidelines require before any overlay design, leaving the pavement’s true structural capacity uncertain; FWD testing should be conducted on existing PCCP prior to designing an overlay. Core samples from joints are often missing, so taking cores of existing joints is needed to verify good condition. Documentation of slab movement or faulting is frequently absent, making it unclear whether slab stabilization or jacking will be required, and the adequacy of subsurface drainage is often undocumented, creating further uncertainty about support conditions. Field investigation for structural capacity must also address the absence of a reliable estimate of future traffic loading, which is required before design. In many cases a recent pavement condition survey is missing, and without a distress survey the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems that influence capacity cannot be captured. When existing data are insufficient to define subgrade strength, in‑situ stiffness information from FWD testing, complemented by DCP or LWD measurements, must be obtained. Sampling plans that lack defined numbers, locations, depths, or handling procedures, as well as any unidentified excess moisture conditions in the subgrade, must be clarified through on‑site testing and observation. Because the RMS traffic count system still lacks a fully updated FHWA 13 vehicle classification breakdown for many links, structural capacity analysis must rely on field‑collected truck classification surveys to fill those gaps; only about 30 % of the roughly 7,000 annual counts are classification counts, and when a post‑01/01/2008 count does not show a “TYPE” of “MACHINE” or “MANUAL”, the records are considered incomplete, requiring use of pre‑2008 classifications unless a new manual count recorded on Form PS‑121 is performed. The pavement condition records that depend heavily on annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (STAMPP) do not capture premature mid‑slab cracking that may stem from inadequate subbase material, nor can they detect aggregate‑related problems such as Vanport Limestone concerns or Alkali Silica Reaction; ramp sections are omitted entirely. Because these deficiencies may mask reductions in structural capacity, visual inspections and FWD testing are required to confirm the true condition and to verify whether patching greater than 10 % of the pavement area would make a preservation project uneconomical. Finally, RMS records represent only a snapshot of the segment on the survey day and remain unchanged after any maintenance or rehabilitation until a later resurvey, creating a potential mismatch between recorded and actual conditions; traffic data may be several years old, and pavement history can omit layers added as surface treatments or other maintenance work, with lapses in recording further obscuring the true structural composition. [1]

Visual inspection and condition assessment

When evaluating a pavement’s structural capacity, the reviewer should examine drainage problems, any areas of obvious failure that call for base repair to restore adequate support, faulted or sunken rigid slabs, voids beneath the existing rigid base, visible cracking—including transverse cracks—spall damage and joint deterioration, high or curled/warped slab sections, and minor rutting as evaluation criteria for both asphalt concrete and Portland cement concrete surfaces. The inspector should also look for slab movement or faulting, rocking slabs, pumping action, surface spalling, and subsurface drainage problems that must be identified and corrected because inadequate subdrainage can compromise the pavement’s ability to carry loads over its design life. A visual distress survey must document the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, and any drainage issues because these distresses indicate how traffic loads are affecting the structure and can reveal underlying damage mechanisms that could compromise future performance; any existing drainage problems should be identified and corrected before reconstruction. Visible surface distresses explicitly referenced in the serviceability framework—cracks, patches and rut depth—should be inspected as they feed into the Present Serviceability Index calculation together with overall roughness, providing primary clues about whether structural capacity is being exceeded or compromised. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, observable cracking characteristics—specifically the width of existing cracks, any widening over time, and plastic‑shrinkage cracks—should be assessed, along with slab movement or misalignment and joint faulting, because macrofibers reduce such displacement but do not replace dowel bars needed to control faulting. Rutting that may require milling and overlay, faulted or failed joints, any cracks—including transverse or longitudinal spalling—and existing asphalt patches or failed concrete patches should be inspected; joint failure is confirmed with Falling Weight Deflectometer testing. The annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys should be reviewed for cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling or weathering, joint spalling and joint faulting, and premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements—often linked to inadequate subbase material—and aggregate‑related problems such as Vanport Limestone concerns or Alkali Silica Reaction should also be considered because they can degrade performance. When deflection testing cannot be done because air temperature is outside the 40 °F–70 °F range, a visual check for pumping signs or poor joint performance should be made before deciding on slab stabilization. Structural capacity reviews also must note any radial cracks spreading outward from grout injection holes as an indication of poor quality, and any transverse cracks that appear between adjacent injection holes because such cracks trigger full‑slab replacement. During cement grout injection the pavement must not lift more than 0.05 inch, and joints or cracks showing a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more with joint efficiency below 65 % are candidates for stabilization. A recent pavement condition survey—performed according to the criteria in Publication 336, Publication 343, or a comparable procedure such as MicroPaver™—is used to identify each distress type, including rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, and drainage issues, assess its severity, and record both extent and location [1]. Upon completion of the distress survey, a summary report is prepared that documents the level of distress together with the corresponding observations, and the severity of each listed distress is specifically considered in the rehabilitation strategy [1]. Structural Capacity assessments require a field quality‑control plan that includes deflection testing of every joint and crack according to ASTM D4695 [2]. For each observed distress the type (joint or crack) is recorded along with its location, the loaded slab corner deflection is measured, and joint efficiency is calculated [2]. A distress is classified as needing stabilization when the measured deflection is 0.02 inch or more and the joint efficiency is at least 65 % if approved by the representative [2]. When the same deflection threshold is met but the joint efficiency is less than 65 %, the distress is classified for patching and stabilization, again subject to representative approval [2]. Any upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch will not be allowed [2]. All measurements, classifications, and locations are documented in test data submitted to the Representative fifteen working days before work begins, with the overall QC plan provided ten working days prior [2].

Field measurements and testing

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), evaluating the structural capacity of a pavement relies primarily on Falling Weight Deflectometer testing, which provides nondestructive deflection measurements that reflect how the pavement will respond to traffic loads and generate in‑situ subgrade and pavement stiffness profiles from measured surface deflections; the test is required before designing an overlay for existing Portland cement concrete pavements and should be conducted both prior to rehabilitation to obtain valuable information about the stiffness of the existing materials and after construction to determine the stiffness of the new pavement, while also verifying repaired joints meet design criteria and confirming that total patching percentages are less than 10 % for a structurally sound surface [1]. When calculated patching exceeds 10 % of the pavement area it signals a project type beyond simple preservation [1]. The manual further recommends extracting concrete cores from existing joints, which should be inspected to determine depth and integrity of the existing pavement and to verify a good condition, thereby informing milling depths for preservation treatments [1]. Subgrade assessment uses a dynamic cone penetrometer or a light weight deflectometer, both of which are useful for testing the subgrade [1]. A condition survey or component analysis that examines visible distress and performs layer‑by‑layer assessment to infer remaining strength is also described, as is an automated pavement condition survey (videologging) performed annually that generates surface‑distress data such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling and faulting, feeding treatment matrices aimed at restoring structural capacity [1]. Non‑destructive testing equipment currently used includes a Single Locked Wheel Skid Tester to obtain friction values indicating surface grip and High Speed Profilers equipped with laser sensors that assess riding quality by measuring roughness; together these measurements provide objective data on load‑bearing performance, surface traction and ride smoothness without damaging the pavement [1]. In evaluating the structural capacity of cement concrete pavements, the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 specifies PTM No. 642 structural adequacy testing for load‑transfer units; this test uses Linear Variable Differential Transformers to record the absolute difference in deflection between the loaded and unloaded sides of a joint under both dynamic and static loading, and the procedure requires that the differential deflection not exceed 7.5 mils at 1 million cycles, with any increase measured at 10 million cycles be no greater than 2.5 mil relative to the 1‑million‑cycle value [2]. A 67.5 V holiday detector is also employed as a nondestructive check of dowel‑bar coating integrity, with the number of holidays limited to no more than 2 per linear foot [2]. For slab‑stabilization projects, deflection testing performed in accordance with ASTM D4695 serves as the primary nondestructive evaluation of structural capacity; the test measures loaded slab corner deflection at each joint or crack under traffic loading, and a loaded corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more triggers stabilization actions provided that the calculated joint efficiency—defined as the unloaded corner deflection divided by the loaded corner deflection times 100—is at least 65 %, while joint efficiencies below 65 % indicate that patching is required [2]. During grout injection, vertical‑movement testing equipment capable of detecting slab lift to within 0.001 inch monitors upward movement, and any uplift greater than 0.05 inch will not be allowed because it indicates excessive deformation of the structure; all deflection and vertical‑movement measurements must be made when air temperature is between 40°F and 70°F and the subgrade is unfrozen [2]. In addition, flowability measurements of the grout mix are taken twice each shift to ensure proper material properties [2].

Sampling and laboratory investigation

In FDR projects the structural‑capacity assessment requires collecting samples from each constituent layer of the existing pavement – surface course, base or subbase material, and the supporting subgrade – because the sampling criteria listed in Section J.2.D must be applied. The number of samples and their locations are set according to the guidance in Section J.2.D.1, while the required sample size follows Section J.2.D.2; appropriate techniques are described in Section J.2.D.3 and the depth of each sample is defined by Section J.2.D.4 so that every identified layer is represented. Handling and laboratory evaluation follow the procedures in Section J.2.D.5, and samples are taken during the initial pavement condition survey and again after construction to verify the reclaimed layer’s structural capacity. To assess the structural capacity of fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, laboratory slab tests are run to measure flexural and ultimate load capacity as well as the load transfer efficiency (LTE) between slabs, thereby indicating how well the overlay can support repeated traffic loads and maintain joint performance. In addition, a residual strength test defined in ASTM C1609 is performed to obtain the f150 value, which together with the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture yields an effective flexural strength for design; typical residual strengths fall between 100 and 225 psi. These tests also reveal that fiber‑reinforced concrete retains significant residual strength after many freeze/thaw cycles, providing insight into durability under cyclic temperature stresses [1]. Structural capacity testing for load‑transfer units requires structural adequacy testing per PTM No. 642, which records Linear Variable Differential Transformer (LVDT) differential deflection of the joint under both static and dynamic loading; the test must show a maximum differential deflection of not more than 7.5 mils at 1 million cycles and an increase of no greater than 2.5 mils at 10 million cycles compared with the 1‑million‑cycle value, thereby evaluating long‑term deformation resistance [2]. Coated dowel bars are subject to ASTM A513 and AASHTO T 253 Section 5 to verify compliance with material and dimensional requirements, while bond‑breaking performance is assessed by AASHTO M 254 pull‑out load tests that determine whether the coating develops low or high bond strength with concrete (Type A or Type B); these laboratory procedures collectively examine deflection behavior, coating integrity, and bond characteristics that influence the pavement’s ability to sustain traffic loads without excessive movement or failure [2].

Structural and functional performance

Evaluating a pavement’s structural capacity and functional condition begins with determining the thickness of each structural layer, using non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures to identify any required additional thickness. Surface friction is measured with a Single Locked Wheel Skid Tester whenever an overlay does not add structure, and load transfer on working transverse cracks in rigid pavements is verified through dowel bar retrofits. For existing Portland cement concrete pavement, a Falling Weight Deflectometer test must be performed before an overlay is designed to provide deflection data for assessing structural capacity, subgrade characteristics, and joint efficiency. Prior to rehabilitation, in‑situ stiffness testing with the Falling Weight Deflectometer supplies deflection information for both existing and new layers, while subgrade strength is further evaluated using dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer tests; accurate determination of current layer thicknesses and verification of adequate drainage are also required. The agency’s non‑destructive testing equipment includes a Single Locked Wheel Skid Tester for friction values, High Speed Profilers equipped with laser sensors for riding quality, and the Falling Weight Deflectometer for structural capacity assessments. Serviceability indices such as the Present Serviceability Index are derived from measurements of pavement roughness, cracks, patches and rut depth, and automated pavement condition surveys record distress types including cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling, joint spalling and faulting; traffic loading information from Equivalent Single Axle Load data informs the capacity analysis. Evaluation of structural capacity for load‑transfer units in reinforced and plain cement concrete pavements relies on measuring the differential vertical movement between the loaded and unloaded sides of a joint with Linear Variable Differential Transformers, requiring that this LVDT differential deflection not exceed 7.5 mils at 1 million cycles and that any increase at 10 million cycles be no greater than an additional 2.5 mil. For slab stabilization work, a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more triggers stabilization, joint efficiency is calculated as (Unloaded Slab Corner / Loaded Slab Corner Deflection) × 100 and joints having a joint efficiency of 65% or more, if approved by the representative, are considered for treatment; equipment capable of detecting lift to within 0.001 inch must be used, and any upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 inch is prohibited. Data interpretation and diagnosis

Visual observations are first captured through a pavement condition survey that records visible distress such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems; the severity of these distresses should be specifically considered in the rehabilitation strategy. When air temperature is outside the 40 °F–70 °F range required for deflection testing, visual observations are used to verify pumping quality or joint condition before any slab stabilization is specified. In‑situ tests such as Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD), dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer quantify the stiffness of existing pavement layers and subgrade, and FWD data is used to determine the characteristics necessary for structural design. ASTM D4695 deflection testing provides loaded slab corner deflection values and joint efficiency percentages; joints or cracks that exhibit a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more and a joint efficiency at 65% or higher are candidates for stabilization, while those below the efficiency threshold are treated differently. Properly collected samples are sent to the laboratory to establish material properties and appropriate stabilizer content, recognizing that proper sampling plays a vital role in successful design and construction. Laboratory‑related actions such as mixing grout include flowability checks performed at least two times each shift, providing additional data on material quality. The measured stiffness values, laboratory‑derived material parameters, traffic loading estimates expressed as Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) figures, and anticipated environmental conditions are then combined to select a layer thickness that satisfies the required structural demand and to identify any drainage corrections needed before reconstruction. Annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys performed under the STAMPP program provide performance data on cracking, rutting, edge deterioration and other distresses; because these surveys may miss issues such as premature mid‑slab cracking or aggregate‑related reactions, field visual observations must be recorded and presented during field views to supplement the survey results. Performance data from retesting after grouting—specifically whether movement exceeds 0.02 inch after a second grout injection—are used to decide acceptance or the need for full‑depth replacement. This integrated approach ensures that treatment decisions—whether routine maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation or reconstruction—are based on both quantitative measurements and qualitative field insights. Findings, confidence, and next actions

Structural capacity can be evaluated using non‑destructive testing (NDT) such as Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer, light weight deflectometer, or other acceptable design procedures, which provide stiffness and load‑carrying information that guide decisions on additional structural layer thickness, rehabilitation alternatives, and overlay design. For full‑depth reclamation projects the structure layer coefficient varies with the type of stabilization material, ranging from values typical of subbase material to those representative of stabilized materials. In rehabilitation of Portland cement concrete pavements (PCCP), FWD testing prior to overlay design confirms existing capacity; bonded overlays add structural capacity and eliminate surface distress on pavements that are in good to fair condition, while unbonded overlays restore capacity for pavements that are moderately to significantly deteriorated. Thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays of 2.5 to 4 inches or standard bonded overlays of 4 to 8 inches are prescribed for asphalt or composite bases, and the amount of capacity added depends on overlay thickness. Incorporating macrofibers into concrete overlays provides additional structural capacity by markedly raising flexural and ultimate load capacity, with residual strength typically ranging between 100 and 225 psi, which can justify thinner slabs in high‑traffic or variable‑support situations; however, slump may be reduced by up to 4 inches depending on fiber type and content. Rigid pavements designed on the basis of saturated subgrade strength tests have ample reserve structural capacity to withstand reduced support during spring thaws, although increasing thickness offers limited mitigation of differential frost heave. Rehabilitation treatments such as extensive base repair, patching, or overlays improve or increase structural capacity, whereas reconstruction is reserved for sections whose functional life has been exceeded. Limitations and uncertainties remain because the source does not provide specific numeric thresholds or performance limits for acceptable capacity, leaving uncertainty about exact design criteria and confidence in predicted pavement life. Future traffic loading estimates may be uncertain, distress surveys give only a snapshot of damage mechanisms, test results can vary with location and sampling quality, and excess moisture can weaken structural capacity. Condition data from the Roadway Management System or automated surveys represent a single point in time, may miss premature cracking, aggregate‑related problems, or ramp pavements, and traffic counts may be several years old; consequently field inspection and testing remain necessary. The effectiveness of overlays is limited by subgrade condition and drainage—unstable support, poor drainage, or slab movement must be corrected before placement, otherwise performance is uncertain. The magnitude of macrofiber benefits depends on fiber type, content, and resulting residual strength, requiring trial mixes and careful specification. Patching greater than 10 % of the pavement area is generally not cost‑effective for preservation projects, constraining decisions based solely on FWD results. [1]

After a structural‑capacity assessment indicates deficiency, the project team should verify required layer thickness with non‑destructive testing or other acceptable design procedures and evaluate drainage to improve removal of surface and subsurface water; if obvious failure areas are found, base repair by replacing those areas with material that will provide adequate structural capacity is required. For asphalt overlays a binder course may be added when NDT indicates insufficient capacity, whereas for rigid pavements corrective actions such as slab‑jacking, dowel‑bar retrofit, or full‑depth reclamation should be considered based on the anticipated structure layer coefficient values associated with the chosen stabilization material. Falling Weight Deflectometer testing shall be conducted on existing Portland cement concrete pavement prior to any overlay design and also used to verify joint repair and full‑depth patching; if a bonded overlay is selected for pavements in good to fair condition, joint cores must be taken to verify condition. An unbonded overlay requires a fine‑graded minimum 1‑inch asphalt separation layer (or a non‑woven geotextile) and may involve cracking/breaking and seating the existing concrete into 12‑to‑24‑inch pieces or rubblizing it to 12‑inch (Type 1) or 8‑inch (Type 2) fragments before placing the overlay. When slab movement or faulting is present, slab stabilization or jacking shall be undertaken and adequate subdrainage provided where subsurface drainage problems exist. Additional in‑situ testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements both before rehabilitation to gauge existing stiffness and after construction to confirm the new pavement’s stiffness should be performed, complemented by dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer tests to evaluate subgrade strength; representative material samples must then be collected according to the sampling criteria to support laboratory design of the stabilization method. If non‑destructive testing reveals that structural capacity is below what is needed, additional monitoring of friction and ride quality should be performed and results compared to the terminal serviceability index for the road’s maintenance functional classification; design checks must verify that any new, reconstructed or overlay design uses at least the terminal serviceability value specified for that classification. When the Simple Method layer‑thickness limits are exceeded for new construction or the required structural capacity of a rehabilitation project is greater than the existing pavement’s capacity, the next step is to run a rigorous 18‑kip ESAL calculation; similarly, when projected design‑year ADT on a cross route exceeds 8,000, the Rigorous Method must be applied, and if traffic classification data are missing or appear outdated a truck classification survey (24‑hour portable or 8‑hour manual) shall be performed before completing the ESAL analysis. After determining structural capacity for an overlay that will use macro‑fibers, the engineer should verify the macro‑fiber contribution by performing ASTM C1609 residual strength testing to obtain f150 (typically 100–225 psi) and run trial FRC batches to confirm that the mix meets required fresh properties such as slump loss up to 4 in. (100 mm) and any needed air‑content adjustments; cores of the existing pavement must be taken before design to ensure stripping is not prevalent when a bonded overlay is considered, and the Residual Strength Estimator spreadsheet should be used to compute the effective flexural strength for input into mechanistic‑empirical design programs. During service crack widths and joint load transfer efficiency should be monitored because fiber‑reinforced concrete can reduce crack growth and maintain LTE. Coring of the existing pavement is required to confirm existing layer thickness because it determines the required milling depth; if rutting or material problems are identified, milling and overlay depths of 1½ inches or 2 inches may be exceeded to remove the deficient portion, and joint repair and full‑depth patching verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing should remain below a 10 % patching limit unless cost effectiveness is demonstrated. Preservation options that respect these limits include micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses applied early in the maintenance cycle, cold in‑place recycling of the base to a maximum depth of 4 inches with a 1½‑inch (maximum) asphalt overlay, and ultra‑thin bonded concrete overlays limited to 2 inches as non‑structural wearing courses; any overlay on flexible pavements that exceeds 2 inches will not be eligible for consideration as Pavement Preservation. After using RMS pavement history and traffic data to evaluate a segment’s structural capacity, the program should be followed by on‑site visual inspection and physical testing of the existing pavement because the RMS snapshot may not reflect recent maintenance or missing layers; these field activities are essential to verify the actual condition before proceeding with design decisions. When a structural capacity assessment identifies potential joint movement, the pavement design team shall require testing per PTM No. 642 to measure Linear Variable Differential Transformer (LVDT) differential deflection on both the approach and leave sides of each dowel‑bar load‑transfer unit, confirming that the deflection does not exceed 7.5 mils at one million loading cycles and that the increase after ten million cycles is no greater than 2.5 mil relative to the one‑million‑cycle value; any units exceeding these limits must be repaired or replaced. Coating integrity shall be verified by confirming a minimum non‑abraded epoxy thickness of 8 mils and by scanning for holidays with a 67.5‑V detector, allowing no more than two holidays per linear foot; any detected deficiencies shall be corrected before paving according to the prescribed repair procedures. When excessive deflection or low joint efficiency is identified, the contractor must conduct field deflection testing in accordance with ASTM D4695 only when air temperature is between 40 °F and 70 °F; joints or cracks that exhibit a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more together with a joint efficiency of 65 % or higher are to be stabilized. During grout injection vertical movement shall be monitored and pumping stopped if upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is observed, after which the affected joints should be retested and re‑grouted if deflection remains at least 0.02 inch; any pavement that has risen above the 0.05‑inch allowable tolerance must be ground to correct grade. If transverse cracks develop between adjacent grout injection holes, the entire slab shall be replaced at no additional cost to the Department as specified in Section 516. [1][2]
