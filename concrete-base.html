Concrete Base

Part of: Base Course is-subtopic-of
Concrete Base is a topic under Base Course.
concrete-baseconcrete-base-courseplain-cement-concrete-base-courserigid-basepcc-base-course
Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

The Concrete Base is a rigid foundation layer that provides the load‑bearing capacity required for brick pavements and for asphalt wearing courses on low‑volume roadways, with structural analysis employing a coefficient for a rigid base.[1] It is typically placed as roller‑compacted concrete (RCC), a very dry, low‑slump mix of cement, aggregate and water designed to achieve compressive strengths between 4,000 psi and 10,000 psi and installed in thicknesses from 4 inches to 18 inches on a prepared subgrade and compacted with rollers to obtain the required density.[1][2] Because forms, reinforcement and dowel bars are not required, the layer relies on proper joint spacing—15–20 feet for slabs under 8 inches thick or thickness‑times‑2.5 feet for thicker sections—and strict surface‑tolerance checks that remove any high points over ¼ inch or depressions over ⅜ inch after curing.[1][2]

Purpose and design objectives

The concrete base is intended to provide the rigid foundation that supplies the load‑bearing capacity required for brick pavements and any later overlay, and when a brick pavement rests on such a base the structural analysis shall employ the coefficient for a rigid base. For low‑volume roadways with an average daily traffic of ≤ 2000 vehicles and speed limits of ≤ 55 mph, the roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) base is used as a base course beneath an asphalt wearing course or surface treatment to carry traffic loads, relying on its high compressive strength—potentially ranging from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi—and its ability to be placed over various prepared subgrade and subbase conditions without the need for forms, reinforcement, or dowel bars. The specification further requires that the RCC base be constructed with a low‑slump mix and compacted by rollers of appropriate size to achieve the required thickness and density, thereby satisfying the performance need for a uniformly smooth surface. To meet the surface‑tolerance requirement, any high points exceeding 1/4 inch must be removed by grinding or cutting, and depressions greater than 3/8 inch must be corrected by replacing at least a 10‑foot length of the affected lane section. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (p. 62), Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 206–219)

The Concrete Base, when placed as Roller Compacted Concrete, is required to deliver a structural capacity that is comparable to conventional concrete [1]. This structural capacity is achieved by selecting a slab thickness in the range of 4 inches to 18 inches and by attaining a compressive strength that may be from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi [1]. Functionally, the layer shall be capable of supporting roadways with an average daily traffic not exceeding 2,000 vehicles and speed limits up to 55 mph, while providing an estimated design life of 20 years under the anticipated traffic and environmental conditions [1]. For any exposed RCC surface on routes where the posted speed limit is 35 mph or greater, the surface must be textured by diamond grinding in order to satisfy safety and ride‑quality requirements [1]. The underlying subgrade and subbase shall be prepared so as to support proper compaction of the RCC layer, thereby contributing to both structural integrity and serviceability [1]. After the concrete has hardened, the surface of the plain cement concrete base course shall be retested with straightedges in accordance with Section 501.3(k)3, and any high points that exceed ¼ inch must be removed by grinding or cutting tools [2]. Sections containing depressions greater than ⅜ inch shall be replaced when directed in writing, with replacement requiring removal of the full lane width of the depression for a length of at least 10 feet [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 206)

Scope and applicability

Concrete Base is suitable for brick pavement projects where the historic or existing roadway consists of bricks laid on a rigid concrete foundation; in those situations the base material is treated as a concrete base and the structural analysis of any overlay shall employ the Brick with Rigid Base coefficient, while the brick layer itself receives its own coefficient applied only to its depth. Concrete Base is also appropriate when Roller Compacted Concrete is placed beneath an asphalt wearing course or surface treatment on roadways whose average daily traffic does not exceed 2,000 vehicles and whose speed limit does not exceed 55 mph; it may further serve as the base for temporary median crossovers, widening areas, or as a shoulder adjacent to reinforced or plain cement‑concrete pavements. Roller compacted concrete base is appropriate when a prepared subgrade and subbase need a stiff, low‑slump concrete layer that will support an asphalt wearing course or other surface treatment. The RCC mix used for the concrete base is a very dry concrete of cement, aggregate and water with a low water‑cement ratio as low as 0.30 and can be designed from 4 inches up to 18 inches thick, providing compressive strengths between 4,000 psi and 10,000 psi. Construction of the roller compacted concrete base course employs conventional or high‑density asphalt paving equipment together with rollers of various sizes to achieve the required thickness and density. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (p. 62), Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 219)

In brick pavements that are supported by stone or other aggregate rather than a rigid concrete base, the design must treat the system as having a flexible base and apply the structural coefficient for “Brick with Flexible Base” instead of the concrete‑base method, making that alternative approach preferable. Additionally, when the community wishes to preserve the historic appearance of the brick pavement, an assessment of its historical and cultural significance may be required before selecting a design that replaces the existing base. For roller‑compacted concrete used as a concrete base, the Department limits its use to roadways whose average daily traffic does not exceed 2,000 vehicles and whose posted speed limit is 55 mph or less; when either of those thresholds is exceeded another pavement design should be considered. RCC pavements are also discouraged wherever the International Roughness Index will be measured for ride‑quality payment incentives because the typical surface texture is rougher than conventional concrete or asphalt. Moreover, if an exposed RCC surface will serve a road with a speed limit of 35 mph or greater, the pavement surface must be textured by diamond grinding, which may also favor an alternative treatment. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (p. 62), Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99)

Design methods and criteria

In the structural analysis of a concrete base that supports a brick pavement, the manual requires the use of the “Brick with Rigid Base” structural coefficient taken from Table 9.3 and applied only to the depth of the brick layer, while a separate structural coefficient is assigned exclusively to the depth of the concrete‑base material; if the underlying layer were stone or another flexible material the analysis would instead employ the “Brick with Flexible Base” coefficient [1]. For roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) bases the design follows the PennDOT RCC design procedures set out in Chapter 8 of the manual, and thickness selection is performed by applying the current PennDOT concrete pavement design procedures described in Chapter 8, Section 8.15 [1]. The mix design for these RCC bases limits the water‑to‑cement ratio to as low as 0.30 but typically 0.40, and a compressive strength that may range from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi is used as an input to the structural design calculations [1]. Control joint spacing for RCC pavements less than 8 inches thick shall be provided at intervals of 15 feet to 20 feet, whereas for RCC pavements 8 inches or greater the spacing equals the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet [1]. After a plain cement concrete base course has hardened, its surface must be retested with straightedges in accordance with Section 501.3(k)3, and any high points that exceed ¼ inch are to be removed using grinding or cutting tools as directed [2]. Sections of the base that contain depressions greater than ⅜ inch must be removed and replaced when instructed in writing, and the removal shall extend across the full lane width for a length of at least 10 feet where such a depression occurs [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (p. 62), Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 206)

When a brick pavement rests on a concrete base, the design must treat that base as a rigid support and the structural analysis shall be performed using the structural coefficient designated for “Brick with Rigid Base”. The concrete base is assigned its own separate structural coefficient that applies only to the depth of the base material. When roller‑compacted concrete is used as a concrete base beneath an asphalt wearing course or surface treatment, it may be employed only where the roadway’s average daily traffic does not exceed 2000 vehicles and the speed limit is no greater than 55 mph. The design thickness shall be selected between 4 inches and 18 inches in accordance with current PennDOT concrete pavement procedures. For roller‑compacted concrete bases thinner than 8 inches, control joints shall be spaced at 15 feet to 20 feet. For bases that are 8 inches or thicker, the joint spacing shall equal the thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet. Because the RCC base will be covered by an asphalt layer, the base surface does not need diamond grinding before the wearing course is placed. After the concrete has hardened, the surface of the base course shall be retested using straightedges. Any high points in excess of 1/4 inch shall be removed by grinding or cutting tools as directed. Depressions greater than 3/8 inch shall be removed and replaced when directed in writing, with replacement extending across the full lane width for a length of at least 10 feet. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (p. 62), Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 206)

Structural section and dimensions

The concrete base may be designed with a thickness ranging from 4 inches to 18 inches [1]. This required thickness can be achieved by placing the mix in either a single lift or multiple lifts, with the width and depth of each lift governed by the dimensions of the paver used for placement [1]. For concrete bases less than 8 inches thick, control joints shall be spaced between 15 feet and 20 feet; when the base is 8 inches or greater, joint spacing shall equal the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet [1]. No dimensional requirements are provided for overlay, subbase, treated‑soil, edge‑support, widening, or reinforcement in this section of the manual [1]. In the Pennsylvania construction specifications, forms are not required for base‑course widening that does not exceed six feet in width [2]. The same subsection does not specify values for pavement thickness, slab lift height, overlay depth, or subbase depth [2]. Reinforcement dimensions are not prescribed because the reinforcement sections have been deleted from the specification [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 205–206)

For a concrete base constructed with roller compacted concrete, slab or lift thicknesses are selected using the current PennDOT concrete pavement design procedures and may be placed in single or multiple lifts, with allowable design thickness ranging from 4 inches to 18 inches; joint spacing is then governed by that thickness—RCC pavements less than 8 inches thick require control joints spaced 15 feet to 20 feet, while those 8 inches or greater use a spacing equal to the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet, and shoulders must have control joints that match the spacing and alignment of the adjacent mainline concrete—no additional guidance on layer configurations, structural layer coefficients, cross‑slopes, transitions, or tie‑ins is provided in this section. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99)

Materials and mixture design

For a concrete base constructed as roller‑compacted concrete, the mix must include cement, aggregate and water, and it may incorporate limited admixtures and may or may not have air entrainment. The design should employ a dense aggregate gradation whose maximum aggregate size is less than 1 inch. A lower water‑to‑cement ratio, as low as 0.30 but typically 0.40, produces a no‑slump mixture that resembles damp gravel. Such RCC mixtures are expected to achieve compressive strengths ranging from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi, and the design thickness for base applications may be anywhere from 4 inches to 18 inches. For the plain cement concrete base course, a white liquid membrane forming curing compound shall not be allowed, whereas a black liquid membrane forming curing compound may be used only when the base course is not accessible to traffic before the surface course is placed and must be discontinued if it becomes unsatisfactory. The roller‑compacted concrete base must be placed on a prepared subgrade and subbase using a stiffer low‑slump concrete mix, and placement shall be carried out with conventional or high‑density asphalt paving equipment together with rollers of various sizes selected to obtain the required thickness and density. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 205–219)

For a concrete base constructed as Roller Compacted Concrete, the mix is proportioned with cement, aggregate and water at a low water‑cement ratio (as low as 0.30, but typically 0.40) to produce a no‑slump, dry mixture that can be placed with a paver and compacted by rollers, facilitating constructability; the mix may use a dense aggregate gradation with a maximum aggregate size less than 1 inch, and it may or may not include air entrainment while admixture use is limited, which supports durability; structural capacity is achieved by targeting a compressive strength potential of 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi and selecting a pavement thickness between 4 inches and 18 inches in accordance with PennDOT concrete design procedures for the expected traffic loads, thereby meeting performance requirements. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99)

Structural details and interfaces

In roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) bases the Department’s guidelines specify that forms, steel reinforcing, and dowel bars are not required, and control joints may be omitted except where the Department requires them in shoulders to align with adjacent mainline concrete pavement; when joints are shown on the plans their spacing must follow the prescribed limits. For RCC pavements thinner than 8 inches the recommended control‑joint spacing is between 15 feet and 20 feet, while for thicknesses of 8 inches or greater the joint spacing is calculated as the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet [1]. In plain cement‑concrete base courses, forms are not required for widening projects that are six feet wide or less, provided that an acceptable method is used to contain the concrete during placement [2]. Where longitudinal joints are introduced for construction convenience in such base courses, a tied joint as illustrated on the standard cement‑concrete pavement drawings shall be formed and full‑width placements do not require sawing of those joints [2]. Transverse joints for these base courses are governed solely by Section 501.3(i)1, with no additional detail supplied in this specification section [2]. The reinforcement handling and placement sections have been removed from the concrete‑base specifications, resulting in no explicit reinforcement requirements for the concrete base [2]. No load‑transfer systems such as dowel bars are mandated for either RCC or plain cement‑concrete bases under the cited provisions [1][2]. Likewise, the documents do not prescribe any paving interlayers, membranes, edge treatments beyond the form‑free widening allowance, notches, transitions, or other connection details for the concrete base.

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 205–206)

When a brick pavement is placed on a concrete base the structural analysis shall be performed using the structural coefficient designated for “Brick with Rigid Base,” while the concrete base itself receives its own separate coefficient that applies only to the depth of the base material, and the appropriate coefficient—rigid for a concrete base or flexible for a stone or aggregate base—is selected as specified in Table 9.3. [1] Joint and form provisions are used to treat the interfaces between the concrete base and adjacent pavement layers or structures; transverse joints shall follow Section 501.3(i)1, and longitudinal joints that are indicated or formed for construction convenience must be a tied joint as shown on the Standard Drawings for cement‑concrete pavement joints, with sawing not required when the work is full width. [2] For base‑course widening of six feet or less, forms are not required provided acceptable containment methods are used. [2] The finished surface of the concrete base shall receive a rake or broom finish, and curing may employ a Liquid Membrane Forming Curing Compound in black only when the base course will not be accessible to traffic before the surface course is placed; a white compound shall not be allowed. [2] In roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) bases the control joints shall be aligned with the spacing and alignment of the adjacent concrete mainline, with joint spacing set at fifteen feet to twenty feet for RCC thicknesses less than eight inches and at a distance equal to the thickness multiplied by 2.5 ft for RCC eight inches or greater. [1] If an RCC pavement will have an exposed surface and the posted speed limit is thirty‑five miles per hour or higher, the surface must be textured by diamond grinding; no grinding is required when the RCC serves only as a base beneath an asphalt wearing course prior to placement of the overlay. [1] The RCC base course is installed directly on a prepared subgrade and subbase, creating the lower interface for the pavement system and serving as the foundation beneath an asphalt wearing course or surface treatment; the design calls for a stiffer low‑slump concrete mix applied with conventional or high‑density asphalt paving equipment and compacted with rollers of various sizes until the required thickness and density are achieved. [2] The subgrade and subbase shall be treated as separate construction items to support compaction of the RCC pavement. [1] After the concrete has hardened, the surface of the base course is retested with straightedges as specified in Section 501.3(k)3; any high points exceeding one‑quarter inch are removed by grinding or cutting tools, and sections containing depressions greater than three‑eighths inch shall be removed and replaced when directed in writing, with the full width of the lane removed for a length of at least ten feet wherever such a depression occurs. [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (p. 62), Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 205–219)

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Concrete base courses must be limited to roadways where the average daily traffic does not exceed 2,000 vehicles and the posted speed limit does not exceed 55 mph when roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) is used as the base layer [1]. If the RCC surface will remain exposed and the speed limit is 35 mph or greater, the pavement surface must be textured by diamond grinding; however, when the RCC serves only as a base beneath an asphalt wearing course or other surface treatment, diamond grinding is not required [1]. For RCC bases thinner than 8 inches, control joints shall be spaced between 15 and 20 feet, whereas for thicknesses of 8 inches or more the joint spacing shall equal the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet [1]. When a plain cement concrete base course is widened no more than 6 feet, forms may be omitted provided that acceptable containment methods are employed; longitudinal joints used for construction convenience shall be tied joints as shown on standard drawings and full‑width sections do not require sawing [2]. The surface finish of the concrete base must be limited to a rake or broom texture [2]. Curing may employ the black liquid‑membrane forming compound only if the base course will not be accessible to traffic before the overlying surface course is placed, and the compound shall be discontinued should it become unsatisfactory [2]. After the concrete has hardened, the surface must be re‑tested with straightedges in accordance with Section 501.3(k)3; any high points that exceed 1/4 inch shall be removed by grinding or cutting tools [2]. Depressions that are more than 3/8 inch deep must be replaced when directed in writing, the replacement covering the full lane width for a length of at least 10 feet [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 205–206)

Verification and expected performance

The hardened concrete base is inspected by retesting its surface with straightedges. High points that exceed 1/4 inch are removed by grinding or cutting tools, and depressions greater than 3/8 inch are removed and replaced when directed in writing; the replacement extends across the full lane width for a length of at least 10 feet. [2]

[2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 206)

Under Section 4.11 the Roller Compacted Concrete base is expected to provide a design life estimated to be 20 years, with longevity dependent on construction materials, environmental conditions, and traffic volumes. Its compressive strength has the potential to be from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi, giving structural behavior comparable to conventional concrete and thus good resistance to cracking and other distress. Because RCC typically has a rougher surface than conventional Portland Cement Concrete or asphalt pavements, the ride‑quality result will vary with the equipment used during placement, and projects that require International Roughness Index (IRI) measurements for ride‑quality assessment and payment incentives are not suitable for RCC. Performance is evaluated by estimating design life from material and traffic inputs and by measuring surface roughness through IRI testing when such measurement is required. [1]
