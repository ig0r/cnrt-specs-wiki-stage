Pavement Base Drain

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Pavement Base Drain is a topic under Pavement Design.
pavement-base-drainspavement-base-draincontinuous-pavement-base-draincontinuous-pavement-base-drainsbase-drains
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-29

A pavement base drain is a continuous subsurface conduit placed beneath the pavement edge or low side of superelevated sections to intercept and convey both free and capillary water from the base and sub‑base layers, thereby protecting subgrade support and preventing moisture‑induced distress such as frost‑heave, cracking and rutting[1][2]. It functions by providing a gravity‑driven flow path through a prefabricated drain whose inert plastic core is wrapped in Class 1 geotextile; the core must deliver at least fifteen gallons per minute per foot of width (ASTM D4716) with a minimum open‑core area of six percent on the pavement face and allow unobstructed inflow across no less than half of the geotextile surface[1][2]. Installed in a trench extending at least two inches into the subgrade and backfilled with compacted fine‑aggregate Type A lifts, all fittings are sealed to prevent soil intrusion, ensuring continuous drainage that preserves structural integrity and extends pavement service life[1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Pavement base drains are incorporated to address the fundamental problem of excess moisture accumulating in the base and sub‑base layers, which undermines subgrade support and leads to distress such as frost‑heave, cracking, rutting and surface roughness, thereby shortening pavement life. By providing a continuous subsurface conduit that removes infiltrating water, soil and other foreign material before they can fill the base, the drain preserves structural integrity and prevents pressure buildup within the pavement structure. The removal of water from beneath the surface increases pavement life by maintaining subgrade support. In addition, proper drainage contributes to more consistent ride quality and reduces variability in distress development. To achieve the required flow capacity, the drain must be capable of moving at least fifteen gallons per minute for each foot of pavement width, a rate defined by ASTM D4716. This capacity is attained through a minimum open‑core area of six percent on the side face that contacts the pavement. A geotextile envelope surrounds the conduit and must permit unobstructed inflow across at least half of its area, ensuring adequate filtration while allowing water to enter the drain. Installation practices call for all fittings and connections to be sealed so that soil or other foreign material cannot intrude into the drainage core or outlet piping, thereby preserving continuity of flow. Where the drain intersects zones where flowable backfill is placed, solid outlet pipes and separation membranes are required to keep the drainage path free of contaminating material. The combined effect of these measures is a subsurface drainage system that safeguards the pavement structure from moisture‑induced deterioration and delivers durable, uniformly performing pavements. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 187–189), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 518–522)

A pavement base drain must remove both free and capillary water from the base and subbase, lower intersecting water tables, intercept seepage from cuts, prevent surface ponding that could infiltrate the structure, and provide positive gravity drainage within one day of trenching at a rate of at least fifteen gallons per minute per foot of width. The system must protect its outlets from crushing or plugging so that reliable flow is maintained throughout its service life. Structurally it is required to be installed with appropriate headwalls in mowable areas and sized and spaced according to the design manual for edge, low‑side superelevation, and median locations where subsurface water problems exist. Installation occurs after the adjacent roadway pavement has been constructed, with a trench that does not disturb the subbase; Class 1 geotextile surrounds the drain, which is backfilled with fine‑aggregate Type A compacted in nine‑inch hydraulic lifts, while the pipe rests on at least two inches of satisfactorily tamped embankment material, is no more than two inches thick, has discharge openings spaced not more than fifteen feet apart, uses joints and connections per the manufacturer’s recommendations, limits bends to forty‑five degrees, and employs forms that resist lateral pressure with a factor of safety of at least 1.3, backfilled in four‑inch lifts and thoroughly compacted. The design must keep the subgrade supported by eliminating moisture that would cause loss of support, aggregate deterioration, freeze‑thaw damage, pumping, and frost heave, thereby extending pavement life. Material durability is ensured by an inert plastic core meeting a tensile strength of at least one‑thousand two hundred pounds per square inch, a crush strength of forty pounds per square inch, water absorption not exceeding zero point zero one percent after twenty‑four hours, resistance to fungal growth and chemicals, and protection from ultraviolet exposure through concrete endwalls or suitable coverings. Safety is provided by barricading trenches, never leaving them open during precipitation or overnight, limiting any exposed trench depth to two inches, prohibiting contact between backfill and aluminized materials, preventing pipe floating until the lift hardens, and embedding a red subsurface drain outlet marker at least eighteen inches below the surface. Serviceability is achieved through smooth, non‑restricting transitions, clear outlets, protection of the drainage core from damage, proper alignment during placement, and uniform performance that prevents rutting, cracking, surface roughness, loss of ride quality, and other distress over the design life. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 187–189), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 518–526)

Scope and applicability

Pavement Base Drain is appropriate for any pavement—whether concrete or asphalt—where subsurface water presents a problem, including both new construction and rehabilitation projects on Interstate and non‑Interstate routes. The design is especially called for in sections cut into hillsides, at cut‑to‑fill transitions, in wet subgrade areas, sag zones, or wherever existing drainage is deficient, as identified by maintenance staff. It is intended for standard roadway sections that require edge drainage and is not used under curbs or other special locations. The approach assumes a highly permeable granular base or subbase that can convey water rapidly by gravity, and the trench for the drain must extend at least two inches into the subgrade without disturbing the existing pavement structure or subbase. The trench is lined with Class 1 geotextile, and prefabricated drainage cores are placed vertically within it. Backfill material shall be fine‑aggregate Type A compacted in lifts not exceeding nine inches, providing a positive gravity‑flow sub‑surface drain while preserving pavement integrity. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 521–522)

When the level of permeability required for a pavement base drain would undermine the stability needed for construction equipment, designers may choose an alternative that reduces drainability in favor of a firmer work platform [1]. Budgetary pressures that make the full suite of drainage measures prohibitively expensive also prompt selection of a less permeable solution to lower initial costs while still providing acceptable performance [1]. In addition, when the pavement must accommodate special locations such as under curbs, the usual practice of installing the base drain after the adjacent roadway is built cannot be applied because it would require trenching beneath existing pavement and could disturb the subbase; in those cases the drain is placed before paving or an alternative under‑drain system is used [2]. The specification therefore restricts post‑pavement installation to situations where it will not interfere with existing structures, making any condition that would necessitate work beneath curbs or similar features a trigger for a different design approach [2].

Design inputs and requirements

The design of pavement base drains is governed by state highway publications that prescribe sizing methods and outlet spacing, requiring adherence to the calculations set out in Publication 13M, Design Manual Part 2, Chapter 10, Section 10.3.D [1]. Protection of all outlets located within mowable areas must be provided by headwalls as detailed in Publication 72M, Roadway Construction Standards (RC‑30M), to prevent crushing or plugging of the drainage system [1]. For Interstate facilities continuous edge drains are mandated along the outer pavement edge and on the low side of superelevated sections, with additional median‑side drains where subsurface water problems have been identified; comparable continuous base drain placement is required on all non‑Interstate projects that exhibit subsurface water issues [1]. Lateral underdrains are to be installed at cut‑to‑fill transitions and subgrade drains incorporated wherever evidence of water damage, sagging areas, or other indications of moisture intrusion exist [1]. In addition to these highway publications, the design and construction must conform to the project’s Standard Drawings and associated specification sections that control every aspect of the work [2]. All base‑drain assemblies are required to use Class 1 geotextile, with any fabric splices overlapping a minimum of six inches, as stipulated in Section 212.3(b) [2]. The trench layout follows the dimensions shown on the Standard Drawings; excavation is performed without disturbing the existing subbase and must extend at least two inches into the subgrade so that the bottom remains uniformly firm and smooth to permit gravity flow [2]. After excavation, pipe is placed on a two‑inch layer of properly tamped embankment material before backfilling [2]. The backfill is placed with fine‑aggregate Type A in lifts not exceeding nine inches when hydraulically compacted; each lift is sprayed with one gallon of water per linear foot and thoroughly tamped, or alternatively four‑inch lifts of satisfactory material may be used provided they are fully tamped in accordance with Section 601.3(f) [2]. The backfill material must satisfy the criteria listed in Table A for flowable fill, and where a drain outlet would intersect such fill a solid pipe is required; preformed drains may be employed only if their thickness does not exceed two inches and outlets are spaced no more than fifteen feet apart unless directed otherwise by the Representative [2]. Prefabricated drain units are governed by Section 610.2‑9, requiring an inert plastic core that meets ASTM D638 tensile strength of at least 1,200 psi, ASTM D570 water absorption not greater than 0.01 % after twenty‑four hours, and a minimum crush strength of 40 psi as defined by GRI‑GC4 [2]. The core must also demonstrate fungus resistance in accordance with ASTM G21 and provide a flow capacity of no less than fifteen gallons per minute per foot of width verified by ASTM D4716, with an open area of at least six percent on the pavement face and three percent on the shoulder [2]. The plastic core is wrapped in a non‑woven needle‑punched geotextile that conforms to Table A, and each unit must be approved through certified test data or laboratory testing before being listed in Bulletin 15 [2]. Outlet pipe connections are prescribed by Section 615.3 and must follow the manufacturer’s approved drawings, avoid flow restriction or soil intrusion, and be limited to a maximum 45‑degree bend using appropriate couplings; plastic pipe ends are protected from sunlight with endwalls and red subsurface drain markers are embedded at least eighteen inches deep on the traffic‑facing side of each opening [2]. Installation cannot commence until a written method statement is submitted to the Representative at least two weeks in advance, open trenches may not be left exposed during precipitation or overnight, any open depth is limited to two inches, and barricading must comply with 67 PA Code Chapter 203 while prefabricated drains are stored protectively on site [2]. Outlet pipe installation is required to occur concurrently with base‑drain placement so that positive drainage is achieved within twenty‑four hours of completion [2].

Design methods and criteria

Pavement‑base drain design follows the sizing and outlet‑spacing procedure prescribed in Publication 13M, Design Manual Part 2, which calls for highly permeable base and subbase materials identified by gradation as an indicator of permeability, maximizes subbase slope to enhance gravity drainage, and selects pipe spacing to achieve rapid water removal; lateral underdrains are recommended at wet subgrade areas and cut‑to‑fill transitions, and outlet protection is required per Publication 72M [1]. Construction begins after the adjacent roadway pavement has been placed, with the contractor submitting a detailed sequence of operations and formwork design capable of resisting the lateral fluid pressure of unhardened flowable backfill at a minimum factor of safety of 1.3 [2]. A trench is excavated to standard drawing widths, extending at least two inches into the subgrade while avoiding disturbance of the existing subbase; the bottom must be uniformly firm, smooth and graded to assure positive gravity flow [2]. On the prepared base a Class 1 non‑woven needle‑punched geotextile is laid, followed by a prefabricated drain whose plastic core satisfies ASTM performance criteria, including a minimum tensile strength of 1,200 psi, water absorption below 0.01 %, crush strength of at least 40 psi, and a flow capacity not less than 15 gpm per foot of width as verified by ASTM D4716 [2]. The drain must provide an open‑core area of at least 6 % on the pavement face (3 % on the shoulder) and guarantee that no less than 50 % of the geotextile surface on the pavement side remains unobstructed to water [2]. The pipe is positioned on a two‑inch layer of satisfactorily tamped embankment material, after which the trench is backfilled with fine‑aggregate Type A in lifts not exceeding nine inches (or four‑inch lifts where tighter compaction control is required); each lift is hydraulically compacted and sprayed with one gallon of water per linear foot, fully tamped, and trenches deeper than two inches are never left open overnight [2]. Connections employ manufacturer‑approved fittings, splice joints with a minimum six‑inch geotextile overlap, and bends limited to 45 degrees or less, while outlet piping is installed to provide positive drainage within twenty‑four hours of trenching, spaced no farther than fifteen feet apart, and protected from flowable backfill by solid pipe where the fill would intersect [2]. A pre‑formed drain not thicker than two inches is placed between wall and fill with required outlets, concrete endwalls may be constructed where needed, plastic pipe ends are shielded from sunlight, and each outlet is marked with a red marker embedded at least eighteen inches deep [2]. All material certifications, test data, and dimensional details are documented in accordance with the referenced ASTM and AASHTO standards before approval [2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 187–189), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 518–526)

Pavement base drains are required to provide reliable subsurface drainage by employing highly permeable base and subbase materials that allow rapid gravity‑driven water removal. [1][2] The design must incorporate sufficient slope in the sub‑base layers and maintain trench bottoms that are uniformly firm and smooth to ensure positive gravity flow toward each outlet. [1][2] Drainage pipe sizing, outlet spacing, and the maximum allowable distance between outlets—no more than fifteen feet through retaining walls—are prescribed by the sizing procedures in Publication 13M and must be observed to promote rapid flow. [1][2] Outlets are required to be protected from crushing or plugging, typically with appropriately designed headwalls or protective endwalls when not tied into an existing system, to preserve functionality under traffic loads. [1][2] Lateral underdrains are strongly encouraged in locations of wet subgrade and at cut‑to‑fill transitions, while continuous edge drains should be placed along the pavement’s outer edge, low side of superelevations, and median where water problems have been identified. [1] Installation must be performed in a trench whose width follows Standard Drawings and whose depth extends at least two inches into the subgrade; trenches may not remain open with more than two inches of exposure overnight. [2] Class 1 geotextile must completely wrap an inert plastic core not exceeding two inches in thickness, with the longitudinal seam positioned on the shoulder side and bonded to prevent loading from reducing flow capacity. [2] The perforated pipe is placed with its holes facing downward and rests on a minimum two‑inch layer of satisfactorily tamped embankment material; fittings and splices must follow manufacturer‑approved details and be limited to deflection angles of forty‑five degrees or less. [2] Backfill is placed in compacted lifts—up to nine inches when using Type A fine aggregate, each lift sprayed with one gallon of water per linear foot—or four‑inch lifts of satisfactory material where specified, and each layer must be thoroughly tamped to meet the compaction requirements applicable beneath pavement or shoulder. [2] The prefabricated drain core must satisfy minimum performance thresholds of 1,200 psi tensile strength (ASTM D638), water absorption not greater than 0.01 % after twenty‑four hours (ASTM D570), crush strength of at least 40 psi (GRI‑GC4), and a flow capacity of no less than fifteen gallons per minute per foot of width (ASTM D4716). [2] Open core area requirements call for at least six percent on the pavement side face and three percent on the shoulder side, with unobstructed inflow through at least fifty percent of the geotextile area on the pavement side and twelve percent on the shoulder side. [2] Forms that retain flowable backfill must be designed to resist the full lateral pressure of the unhardened material with a factor of safety of not less than 1.3. [2] All subsurface drain outlets must be marked with red markers embedded at least eighteen inches below the surface to ensure future locateability. [2] Each shipment of the drain assembly is required to be certified with lot number, manufacturer details, product dimensions, and geotextile designation before inclusion in Bulletin 15, providing traceability and reliability of the system. [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 187–189), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 518–526)

Structural section and dimensions

The pavement base drain must be installed in a trench whose plan width follows the dimensions shown on the Standard Drawings. Vertically, the trench is cut deep enough that its bottom extends at least two inches below the existing subgrade. The pipe is then placed on a uniformly tamped embankment layer that is two inches thick. Backfill material is introduced in compacted lifts; while the specification permits hydraulic compaction of lifts up to nine inches high, the typical practice for the immediate bedding around the pipe uses four‑inch lifts that are each thoroughly compacted. Each lift is water‑sprayed before placement. The geotextile wrapper surrounding the drain must overlap adjacent sections by at least six inches. Where the outlet emerges beneath the pavement or shoulder, compaction shall follow the requirements of Section 601.3(f), although no explicit thickness is prescribed. A red subsurface drain outlet marker is required to be positioned a minimum of eighteen inches below the finished surface. No additional dimensions for pavement, base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab, shoulder, edge‑support or reinforcement are stipulated in this specification. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 521–526)

Thickness of the base drain is controlled by excavating a trench that extends at least two inches below the subgrade and then backfilling in hydraulically compacted lifts no greater than nine inches thick, each sprayed with water to achieve proper moisture. The layer configuration follows the sequence of placing the prefabricated drain vertically, installing the first lift of fine‑aggregate Type A backfill immediately to maintain contact with the geotextile, and continuing with successive 9‑inch lifts while preserving a minimum six‑inch overlap of the geotextile at each joint. Joint spacing and any splice connections are set according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, using fittings that limit deflection to forty‑five degrees or less and ensuring that splices do not restrict flow; transitions between sections must be smooth and non‑restricting, and tie‑ins to existing drainage structures are made with satisfactory connections that prevent soil intrusion. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 521–522)

Materials and mixture design

The specification requires that the base and subbase material be a highly permeable granular aggregate whose gradation satisfies PennDOT’s permeability indicator, with the aggregate type, size range, shape and any gradation limits identified to create voids large enough to prevent capillary rise. [1] In addition, a Class 1 geotextile of virgin‑resin, non‑woven, needle‑punched fabric must be provided as defined in the applicable table. [2] Where a prefabricated drainage unit is used, an inert plastic core is required to meet mechanical and durability criteria, including a minimum tensile strength of 1,200 psi (ASTM D638), water absorption not greater than 0.01 % after 24 h (ASTM D570), resistance to fungal growth, a crush strength of at least 40 psi, and the capacity to convey fifteen gallons per minute for each foot of width. [2] The core’s open‑core geometry must provide at least six percent open area on the pavement side face and three percent on the shoulder side face. [2] A perforated pipe shall be installed with its perforations facing downward and the smooth circular side of the semicircular pipe positioned at the bottom, and all fittings are limited to deflection angles of forty‑five degrees or less; outlet connections must incorporate galvanized wire‑mesh shields as shown on the standard drawings. [2] Backfill material is specified in two forms: when fine aggregate is used, Type A sand meeting Section 703.1 criteria shall be placed in hydraulically compacted lifts not exceeding nine inches, each lift being sprayed with one gallon of water per linear foot. [2] When a flowable backfill is required, the mix must conform to the type and performance limits shown in Table A, including prescribed slump, air‑content, yield and compressive‑strength values verified by the applicable AASHTO and ASTM test methods (e.g., AASHTO T 119 for slump, T 152 or T 196 for air content, T 121 and ASTM C136 for yield). [2] The material temperature at placement may not be below fifty degrees Fahrenheit, and the in‑place backfill must be maintained at that minimum temperature for at least twenty‑four hours; flowable backfill lifts are limited to four feet unless a special approval is obtained, and dike mixes shall be placed with reduced water to achieve a minimum three‑inch slump. [2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 58–59); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 187–189), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 518–522)

Materials for a pavement base drain are chosen to provide high permeability so that gravity‑driven water can be removed rapidly, which protects structural support and durability. [1] The guides specify that the aggregate gradation must create sufficient void space, using particle‑size distribution as the field indicator of permeability. [1] At the same time, a minimum proportion of coarse particles is retained to furnish a stable platform for construction equipment, allowing a modest reduction in permeability when constructability demands it. [1] Large interparticle voids also prevent capillary rise by eliminating small pores that could wick water upward, thereby enhancing long‑term performance. [1] Subbase slopes are maximized and drain outlet spacing is minimized within cost constraints to further promote rapid drainage. [1] When a prefabricated base‑drain unit is used, the core material must satisfy minimum structural and durability criteria, including a tensile strength of at least 1,200 psi (ASTM D638) and a crush strength of no less than 40 psi (GRI‑GC4). [2] Water absorption of the core is limited to 0.01 % after 24 hours, it must resist fungal attack, and it must be capable of conveying at least 15 gpm per foot of width under hydraulic testing (ASTM D4716). [2] The open‑core geometry is constrained to a minimum of 6 % on the pavement side face and 3 % on the shoulder side, while at least 50 % of the geotextile area on the pavement side and 12 % on the shoulder side must remain unobstructed to preserve flow capacity. [2] A Class 1 geotextile is placed between the drain core and surrounding soil to provide separation, prevent intrusion, and satisfy durability requirements. [2] The trench is backfilled with fine‑aggregate Type A sand (or equivalent) that supplies both support and drainage, placed in hydraulically compacted lifts not exceeding nine inches in thickness. [2] Each lift is sprayed with water at a rate of one gallon per linear foot to achieve the required density and moisture condition. [2] When flowable backfill is specified, its mix must conform to the type listed in Table A and meet the prescribed slump, air‑content, yield, and compressive‑strength limits, with placement temperature at or above 50 °F and ambient or surface temperatures not below 40 °F. [2] After each lift, the backfill must be maintained at a minimum of 50 °F for at least twenty‑four hours before the next lift is placed, ensuring proper curing and strength development. [2] Flowable backfill lifts are limited to four feet in height unless an exception is granted, and any formwork used must be designed for a lateral fluid‑pressure factor of safety of 1.3. [2] Drainage provisions include solid outlet pipes or a preformed drain no thicker than two inches positioned between the retaining wall and the fill, with outlets spaced at most fifteen feet apart to guarantee adequate discharge. [2] A Class 4 Type A geotextile membrane separates the base drain from any flowable material, protecting the core from chemical or mechanical damage. [2] Constructability is supported by using standard splice fittings and seams for the prefabricated units, and by enforcing daily slump and yield testing together with compressive‑strength cylinders prepared according to PTM procedures; any backfill that fails strength criteria must be removed or may incur liquidated damages. [2] By coordinating selection of aggregate gradation, geotextile class, core material properties, fine‑aggregate type, lift thickness, moisture application, temperature control, and quality‑control testing, the pavement base drain satisfies structural load‑bearing capacity, durability against water and soil intrusion, ease of installation, and long‑term performance. [1][2]

Structural details and interfaces

Pavement base drains must be installed continuously along the outer edge of the pavement, on the low side of any superelevated sections, and on median sides where subsurface water is problematic, as well as at cut‑to‑fill transitions and other critical locations identified by maintenance staff. The drain system is assembled from prefabricated core sections that are joined with splice connectors placed within the drainage core so that flow capacity is not restricted. Where additional mechanical linkage is required, connecting bands or split couplings may be used to secure joints between pipe segments. All fittings and connections, whether to an under‑drain or other drainage structure, are limited to a deflection angle of 45 degrees or less, achieved either with a single bend or a series of such bends. Each joint is overlapped by at least six inches of Class 1 geotextile extending from one section to the next to maintain continuity and keep soil out of the core. The entire prefabricated drain is fully encased in a non‑woven, needle‑punched geotextile that is wrapped around the core with its longitudinal seam positioned on the shoulder side face near the midpoint, and any adhesive employed for the seam must be a non‑water‑soluble type. The geotextile is firmly attached to protrusions on the pavement‑side face of the core and spot‑bonded on the shoulder side, or alternatively verified by flow testing under load, ensuring that loading does not deform the fabric or reduce capacity. Standard splice fittings and other connections are specified to resist both construction handling and permanent loads, providing the necessary reinforcement for the drain structure. At outlet locations solid pipe sections are installed, kept clear of any flowable backfill, and protected by concrete endwalls or, when plastic pipe is used, by satisfactory endwalls and backfill material that shields the ends from sunlight; galvanized wire‑mesh shields are also required on outlet pipes. A pre‑formed drainage layer no thicker than two inches is placed between the retaining wall and the backfill, with outlet openings spaced at intervals not exceeding fifteen feet. Formwork surrounding the drain must be designed to resist lateral fluid pressure equal to the total weight of unhardened backfill with a minimum safety factor of 1.3. No additional joints, reinforcement, or load‑transfer devices are prescribed specifically for the base drain beyond those described for its connections and encasement. [1][2]

The pavement base‑drain system is configured so that each structural layer is tied to the next through highly permeable base and subbase materials that permit rapid gravity drainage, and the subbase is given the maximum practical slope to increase flow rate [1]. Continuous edge drains are installed along the outer pavement edge, on the low side of any superelevation, and in median sections where subsurface water problems exist, while lateral underdrains are placed at cut‑to‑fill transitions and other identified wet zones [1]. Drain outlets are protected from crushing or plugging by using headwalls in mowable areas and, when the outlet is not tied to an existing system, concrete endwalls are provided; spacing and layout of the outlet pipe follow the criteria set out in Publication 13M to assure adequate removal of surface runoff and subgrade water [1][2]. At each drain location a uniformly firm, smooth trench bottom is prepared, and the drainage pipe is laid on at least two inches of well‑tamped embankment material before backfilling begins [2]. Connections to existing underdrains or new drainage structures are made with approved couplings whose bends do not exceed forty‑five degrees, and splice fittings are selected so that they impose no hydraulic restriction [2]. All joints are wrapped with a minimum six‑inch overlap of geotextile that is wound completely around the inert core, with the longitudinal seam positioned on the shoulder side near the midpoint to prevent deformation under load [2]. The backfill surrounding the drain consists of fine‑aggregate Type A sand (excluding limestone sand) placed in four‑inch lifts of satisfactory material, each lift being thoroughly tamped; additional compaction is applied beneath the pavement or shoulder as required [2]. The drain core must provide at least six percent open area on the traffic‑facing side and three percent on the shoulder side to allow unobstructed inflow across half of the geotextile facing the pavement [2]. Outlet pipes are installed concurrently with the base drain, ensuring smooth, non‑restricting transitions; when an outlet must intersect a zone where flowable backfill will be placed, a solid pipe is used to prevent blockage [2]. Foundation drains adjacent to retaining walls are protected by a Class 4 Type A geotextile membrane and a pre‑formed drain no thicker than two inches placed between the wall and the fill, with outlets spaced at intervals not greater than fifteen feet through the wall [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Pavement base drains are required to remove both surface runoff and subsurface water so that moisture does not degrade subgrade support, cause frost heave, or promote cracking.[1] To achieve this, the design calls for highly permeable base and subbase materials that allow gravity‑drainable water to be removed rapidly, together with sufficient subbase slope and closely spaced lateral underdrains and outlets that keep water tables below the subbase level.[1] Drain inlets are protected from crushing or plugging, and additional lateral drains are placed at cut‑to‑fill transitions where moisture accumulation is likely, thereby lowering water tables, intercepting seepage from cuts, and mitigating climate‑related problems such as freeze‑thaw damage.[1] During construction all drainage components are stored on elevated platforms shielded from weather and ultraviolet radiation, and trenches are never left open during rain or overnight to prevent ingress of soil and water.[2] Pipe ends are sealed before backfilling, and a Class 1 geotextile lines each trench to filter fine material while preserving drainage capacity.[2] The backfill consists of fine‑aggregate Type A sand placed in hydraulically compacted lifts that are sprayed with water to achieve the required moisture content for proper compaction.[2] The prefabricated drain unit incorporates an inert plastic core that is chemically resistant and absorbs no more than 0.01 % water after 24 hours (ASTM D570), and this core is wrapped in a non‑woven needle‑punched geotextile that maintains at least half of the pavement‑side area open, providing a minimum of 6 % open area on the pavement face and 3 % on the shoulder.[2] This configuration delivers a flow capacity of at least 15 gpm per foot of width (ASTM D4716) and retains that capacity under permanent loading.[2] Positive gravity drainage is established by installing the outlet pipe and fittings concurrently with trenching, achieving continuous flow within 24 hours of starting each section.[2] Outlets are sited away from zones where flowable backfill will be placed; when crossing such zones a solid pipe is required to prevent blockage.[2] The surrounding finished surface is protected against frost, erosion and other damage by approved covers such as soil, aggregate, concrete or pavement.[2] Placement of flowable backfill is limited to ambient temperatures above 50 °F (with the surface not below 40 °F) and must be maintained at least 50 °F for a full day to avoid moisture‑related problems.[2]

The pavement base drain shall be installed continuously along the outer edge of the roadway and on the low side of any superelevated sections, with additional drains provided in medians where subsurface water problems exist, and lateral underdrains required at cut‑to‑fill transitions and other wet subgrade locations. The trench that receives the drain must follow the Standard Drawings for width and extend a minimum of two inches into the subgrade while being excavated so as not to disturb the existing pavement structure or subbase. For safety, trenches may not be left open during periods of precipitation or overnight and must be barricaded in accordance with applicable code requirements. All connections are limited to fittings that do not exceed a 45‑degree deflection, and any exposed plastic pipe ends are required to be shielded from sunlight by endwalls and appropriate backfill. Backfilling is performed in short lifts with continuous coverage of the specified geotextile; joints and outlets are sealed to keep soil out, the pipe is laid on a two‑inch tamped embankment followed by four‑inch compacted lifts. The subbase beneath the drain shall be sloped as steeply as practical to promote gravity flow, and the drain material must be highly permeable. Prefabricated drains consist of an inert plastic core encased in a non‑woven needle‑punched geotextile, with the core required to provide at least six percent open area on its pavement‑side face and three percent on the shoulder side. The surrounding geotextile must allow unobstructed inflow across no less than fifty percent of its pavement‑side area and twelve percent of its shoulder side. Each foot of drain width is required to convey a minimum flow of fifteen gallons per minute as measured by ASTM D4716, and the core material must meet strength criteria that include a tensile strength of 1,200 psi, water absorption below 0.01 % after twenty‑four hours, resistance to fungal attack, and a crush strength of forty pounds per square inch. Formwork retaining the drain shall be designed to resist the lateral pressure of unhardened flowable backfill with a factor of safety of at least 1.3. Drain outlets must be located away from zones where flowable backfill is placed; when intersection cannot be avoided, solid pipe outlets are required. Where a pre‑formed drain is installed between a wall and fill, its thickness may not exceed two inches and outlet openings shall be spaced no farther than fifteen feet through the wall unless directed otherwise. All outlet structures situated in mowable areas must be protected from crushing or plugging by using appropriate headwalls to ensure safe operation and accessibility for maintenance. Protection of the drain from intrusion or contamination is achieved with a Class 4, Type A geotextile membrane where specified. For user visibility and accessibility each subsurface drain outlet is marked on the traffic‑facing side with a red marker embedded at least eighteen inches below the surface. [1][2]

Verification and expected performance

The completed pavement base drain is verified through a sequence of documented and on‑site checks that together confirm compliance, constructability, performance, and alignment with the project requirements. First, the installation method must be submitted in writing at least two weeks before work begins and receive the Representative’s approval. Each drainage component is visually inspected for damage before placement, and every roll must bear a certification tag showing lot number, manufacturer information and dimensions. The core material is then compared to product specifications: it must achieve a minimum tensile strength of 1,200 psi (ASTM D638), water absorption under 0.01 % after 24 hours, crush strength of at least 40 psi, and a flow capacity not less than 15 gpm per foot of width (ASTM D4716). Open‑core percentages are measured to ensure at least 6 % on the pavement side face and 3 % on the shoulder side face. The geotextile must cover at least 50 % of the pavement face and 12 % of the shoulder face, be fully wrapped around the core with a seam along the length on the shoulder side near the midpoint, and be securely bonded to the core.

During construction the Inspector‑in‑Charge examines the pipe and all connections before backfilling, confirming that trench width and depth follow the standard drawings, that the geotextile remains intact, and that splice fittings are installed so they do not restrict flow or allow soil intrusion. All fittings and connections must be approved for handling and permanent loading. Backfill is placed in 9‑inch lifts of fine aggregate Type A, each lift hydraulically compacted and water‑sprayed as specified; backfilling proceeds only after the pipe inspection confirms compliance. Outlet piping is installed according to Section 615.3 within the required time frame.

Compliance is demonstrated by reviewing manufacturer test data or LTS testing records, inspecting certification tags, measuring open‑core areas, verifying geotextile coverage and seam placement, and confirming that trench dimensions, lift thickness, compaction, and connection details match the project specifications. These coordinated steps ensure that the pavement base drain meets all contractual and performance criteria. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 518–522)

The expected performance of a pavement base drain is to prolong the pavement’s service life by keeping the subgrade dry, which preserves support and limits moisture‑induced deterioration; it also reduces moisture‑related distresses such as frost heave, rutting, surface roughness and cracking, resulting in more uniform behavior and improved ride quality. These outcomes are verified by monitoring the frequency and severity of those specific distress types, recording maintenance interventions, and comparing actual service life to design expectations.
