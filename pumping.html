Pumping

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Pumping is a topic under Distress.
pumpingpumped-under-the-slab
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Pumping is the action that creates small voids beneath a concrete slab, typically developing near transverse joints, cracks, and especially at outside slab corners; these voids allow water and fine material to be ejected from under the pavement, resulting in loss of support [1]. The distress manifests as transverse‑joint faulting, fines deposited near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a joint or crack, corner breaks, and overall slab settlement, and it is observed in Portland‑cement concrete pavement slabs (PCCP) that may rest on an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) over a 2A subbase [1]. It originates from the combined effect of water infiltration, subgrade consolidation and settlement, which generate the voids and force water and fines upward through joints and cracks [1].

Description and identification

Pumping is the action that creates small voids beneath a concrete slab, typically developing near transverse joints, cracks, and especially at outside slab corners; these voids allow water and fine material to be ejected from under the pavement, leading to loss of support. The resulting distress appears as transverse joint faulting, fines deposited near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, corner breaks, and overall slab settlement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

In Section 4.5 Slab Stabilization of the Pavement Policy Manual, pumping is recognized by a loss of slab support that manifests as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, corner breaks, and overall slab settlement—conditions that typically appear near transverse joints, cracks, and outside slab corners where voids develop. These visual signs are interpreted as indicating underlying voids beneath the concrete rather than merely surface cracking, which allows pumping to be distinguished from other pavement distresses. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require a visual verification of pumping when deflection testing cannot be performed because air temperature is outside the 40 °F‑70 °F range; this verification consists of identifying water or fine material ejection from joints or cracks and differentiating it from poor joint performance. Pumping is further confirmed by observing any vertical movement during grout injection, and the operation must be stopped as soon as such movement is noted. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Where it occurs

In Section 4.5 of the Pavement Policy Manual pumping is described as a distress that occurs in Portland‑cement concrete pavement slabs, developing when voids form beneath the slab most often at transverse joints, cracks and outside slab corners, and can be observed as fines accumulating near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder and as small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at those locations. Section 4.9 of the same manual further identifies pumping as a distress that can develop in the slabs of existing Portland‑cement concrete pavements, requiring any slab exhibiting rocking, pumping, or faulting to be stabilized before an overlay is placed; this discussion remains limited to PCCP and its concrete overlays without reference to asphalt, composite, or other pavement structures. Section 8.6 treats pumping in the context of rigid‑pavement design, applying a loss‑of‑support factor to the effective k‑value to correct for potential erosion or pumping of the subbase material beneath those structures, and specifies that all Interstate and Expressway rigid pavements shall have a base consisting of an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) over a 2A subbase, indicating that pumping can occur under concrete slabs that use either an ATPB or a CTPB drainage layer. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications in Section 679.3 describe pumping as being observed on concrete slab pavements, specifically at joints and cracks that are subject to vertical movement during slab‑stabilization work, with the distress identified by visual verification or monitoring for any vertical movement while grout is placed, and require that pumping operations be stopped when such movement is noted. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–98), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

In the Pavement Policy Manual, Section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION describes pumping as voids that develop beneath the concrete slab primarily adjacent to transverse joints and cracks, with a pronounced concentration at the outside corners of the slab【1】. The manual notes that fines may be observed near those joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder and that small depressions (blow holes) can appear in the shoulder at a joint or crack【1】. Section 4.9 REHABILITATION further characterizes pumping as a distress that occurs within the concrete slabs themselves, indicating that the phenomenon can be associated with the slab body rather than being limited to joints, cracks, corners, edges, wheel paths, layer interfaces, or the base/subgrade【1】. In Section 8.6 MEAN EFFECTIVE K‑VALUE the manual treats pumping as a loss‑of‑support condition that occurs in the subbase (and thus the combined base‑subbase layer), applying a loss‑of‑support factor to correct the effective k‑value for potential erosion or pumping of the subbase material【1】. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification, Section 679.3 CONSTRUCTION, identifies pumping at pavement discontinuities—specifically joints and cracks—and requires visual verification of pumping when joint performance is poor【2】. Stabilization is directed at any joint or crack that exhibits excessive deflection, defined as a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more【2】. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–98), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Causes and mechanisms

In the slab‑stabilization context pumping is attributed to the combined effect of pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement that can generate small voids beneath a concrete slab, particularly adjacent to transverse joints and cracks. The presence of water infiltrating the pavement aggravates this mechanism, so joint and crack sealing must be performed to limit water entry and mitigate pumping. In overlay design active pumping is interpreted as an indicator of poor subgrade condition, meaning inadequate support beneath the pavement is considered the primary cause that will require treatment before applying an interlayer. Neither section provides a table that classifies distresses by load‑related, climate‑related or material‑related causes; consequently no row‑by‑row classification for pumping is available. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

In Section 4.5 Slab Stabilization, pumping develops when the combined action of water infiltration, subgrade consolidation and settlement creates small voids beneath the concrete slab, most of which form adjacent to transverse joints or cracks—particularly at outside slab corners; the loss of support produces excessive slab deflections and stresses that appear as transverse‑joint faulting, fines near joints, blow‑hole depressions, corner breaks, slab settlement and diagonal cracking, eventually leading to complete breakup of the slab. Continued water infiltration and inadequate drainage accelerate this mechanism by increasing the voids, so joint and crack sealing together with good subsurface drains are required as part of the prescribed stabilization. Within Section 5.12 Overlays, pumping originates when water infiltrates the pavement’s structural section, causing loss of subgrade support and forcing water and fine material to be expelled through cracks or joints; it tends to develop further when a poor subgrade condition exists, because active pumping usually signals that underlying support is deficient. The process is intensified by continued water intrusion and freeze‑thaw cycles, which expand and contract the trapped moisture and thereby accelerate the ejection of materials. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

Measurement, survey and extent

In the slab‑stabilization specification pumping is evaluated by measuring the vertical deflection of the pavement at each joint or crack. The required quantity is the loaded slab corner deflection, which is recorded during a deflection test performed in accordance with ASTM D4695; any joint that shows a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more must be stabilized. During grout injection a separate vertical‑movement testing device, capable of detecting lift to within 0.001 inch, is used to monitor the pavement edge or adjacent corners and the operation is stopped as soon as any upward movement is observed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Effects on performance

When pumping is observed it signals that water and fine material are being forced up through cracks, which the source notes as an indication of poor subgrade condition that must be treated before any interlayer can be placed. The resulting water intrusion is identified as a major cause of loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation and freeze‑thaw deterioration; these effects collectively reduce structural capacity, degrade ride quality, create safety hazards and shorten the pavement’s durability and service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

Within slab-type pavements, pumping may create small voids beneath the concrete slab—most often near transverse joints, cracks and the outside corners of the slab—which removes support and induces excessive deflection and stress [1]. If untreated, this loss of support first manifests as transverse joint faulting, often accompanied by fines or blow‑holes; it then advances to corner breaks, diagonal cracking, and ultimately the complete breakup of the slab (section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION) [1]. In overlay applications, active pumping signals that water and fine material are being forced out from beneath the pavement, usually indicating a poor subgrade condition that must be treated before an interlayer can be placed (section 5.12 OVERLAYS) [1]. The resulting water intrusion into the structural section becomes a major cause of loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation and freeze‑thaw deterioration; each of these further degrades the pavement and can lead to premature failure [1]. During slab stabilization, continued pumping can cause vertical movement of the pavement; when such movement is observed the work must be stopped because upward lift greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed and any panel raised in excess of the 0.05‑inch allowable tolerance must be ground back to grade (section 679.3 CONSTRUCTION) [2]. If pumping proceeds unchecked, radial cracks may spread outward from the grout injection holes, indicating poor quality, and transverse cracks can develop between adjacent holes, which requires replacement of the entire slab [2]. Thus untreated pumping progresses from vertical displacement to cracking and ultimately to full‑depth slab failure [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Repairs and treatments

In the slab‑stabilization portion of the guide (section 4.5), pumping is defined as the ejection of water and fines that creates voids beneath a concrete slab, producing loss of support such as transverse joint faulting, blow‑holes or settlement. The treatment called slab stabilization addresses this distress by filling those subgrade voids, thereby restoring support and reducing future pumping. Slab stabilization may employ one of three grout materials—cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane—each selected according to site conditions to fill the voids beneath the slab. When no preliminary testing has been performed, the source requires that at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints be stabilized, and it also mandates joint and crack sealing together with the stabilization to limit water ingress that contributes to pumping. In the rehabilitation portion (section 4.9), any concrete slab that is rocking, pumping, or faulted must first be stabilized—or jacked—before a concrete overlay can be placed, making slab stabilization a prerequisite surface‑preparation step for overlay projects. Section 679—Slab Stabilization prescribes cement grout stabilization as the repair that directly mitigates pumping by immobilizing water‑filled voids beneath joints or cracks. Cement grout stabilization is required for any joint or crack whose loaded slab corner deflection reaches at least 0.02 inch and whose joint efficiency is either 65 % or more (full stabilization) or less than 65 % (patch‑and‑stabilize), provided the Representative approves. The procedure also mandates that pumping be stopped immediately whenever any vertical movement of the pavement is observed, and it limits allowable upward movement during grout injection to not exceed 0.05 inch. These actions are outlined in Section 679.3(a)4 for pumping operations and Section 679.3(b)2.c for cement grout injection. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–98); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

In the Pavement Policy Manual, Section 4.5 Slab Stabilization states that a treatment is applicable when pumping has produced voids that cause loss of slab support, which is indicated by transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, corner breaks, or slab settlement; if no preliminary testing has been performed, at least 25 % of the transverse joints and all patch joints shall be stabilized, and the treatments that may be used are cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane [1]. The same manual, in Section 4.9 Rehabilitation, requires that when existing concrete pavement exhibits pumping—identified as slabs that are rocking, pumping, or faulted—the slabs should be stabilized or jacked before any overlay is placed, thereby making stabilization the applicable treatment for any occurrence of pumping [1]. Section 5.12 Overlays of the manual further indicates that the presence of active pumping signals a poor subgrade condition that must be treated prior to placing an asphalt paving interlayer, and consequently an interlayer such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grid, or composite reinforcing grid is not applicable in the presence of active pumping [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specification, Section 679‑3 Construction, limits cement‑grout stabilization to joints or cracks that show a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more; when the measured joint efficiency is at least 65 % the joint may be stabilized, whereas if the joint efficiency is less than 65 % the joint must be patched and then stabilized, subject to representative approval [2]. The same specification also requires that pumping operations be stopped as soon as any vertical movement is noted, and it prohibits upward pavement movement greater than 0.05 inch during grout injection [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Prevention in design and construction

Slab stabilization is prescribed as a design measure that fills voids at the slab‑subbase interface; when voids are filled sufficiently full support is restored and the potential for future pumping, faulting, and slab cracking is reduced. The source states that it shall be applied when voids are identified and that for estimating purposes at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized if no preliminary testing has been performed; joint and crack sealing must be performed in conjunction with slab stabilization to limit water ingress, and subsurface drains are required to be kept in good condition as an additional preventive condition (section 4.5). Adequate subdrainage is required wherever subsurface drainage problems exist; providing such drainage removes the water that drives the ejection of fine material from beneath slabs (section 4.9 B‑4). When slab movement or faulting is observed, the source calls for slab stabilization or jacking before any overlay, and explicitly requires that slabs that are rocking, pumping, or faulted be stabilized prior to overlay placement (sections 4.9 B‑3 and 4.9 C‑1). For pavements with severe moisture or poor subgrade conditions—situations that often generate pumping—the rubblizing option is recommended as a means of addressing the underlying water problem before an overlay (section 4.9 F). If active pumping is present, it usually indicates a poor subgrade condition that will require drainage treatment before the use of any asphalt paving interlayer; consequently drainage work must be performed prior to installation of the interlayer (section 5.12 F). Thin‑asphalt overlays are permitted only where surface and subsurface drainage are already good or will be upgraded to good as part of the project, thereby directly addressing water that could cause pumping (section 5.12 B, non‑Interstate condition). A loss‑of‑support factor is applied in the effective‑k calculation whenever the effective k‑value is computed; this factor corrects the k‑value for potential erosion or pumping of the subbase material and follows the LS factors listed in Table 8.1 for Pennsylvania pavements (section 8.6). All Interstate and Expressway rigid pavement designs that specify a drainage layer shall employ an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base over a 2A subbase, and the use of Open Graded Subbase is prohibited; this requirement replaces the prohibited material with a stable permeable layer that reduces the likelihood of pumping (section 8.6). When selecting base elastic moduli for seasonal conditions, the ratio of subbase modulus to roadbed soil resilient modulus must not exceed four in order to avoid artificial conditions that could exacerbate pumping (section 8.6). [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162)

Section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION of the Pavement Policy Manual requires that voids at the slab‑subbase interface be filled with a grout material such as cement, hot‑asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane so that full support is restored and deflections that would otherwise promote future pumping, faulting and cracking are reduced; this practice applies whenever loss‑of‑support signs—such as transverse joint faulting, fines near joints, small depressions, corner breaks or slab settlement—are observed [1]. The same section further states that at least 25 % of the transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized if no preliminary testing has been performed, and that joint and crack sealing must be performed in conjunction with slab stabilization while subsurface drains are kept in good condition [1]. Section 4.9 REHABILITATION prescribes providing adequate subdrainage where subsurface drainage problems exist so that water which drives the ejection of fine materials is removed; this requirement applies whenever drainage deficiencies are identified in the existing pavement, and it also requires that slabs exhibiting rocking, pumping or faulting be stabilized before an overlay is placed, thereby mandating slab‑stabilization (or jacking) when any of those distress conditions are present [1]. In Section 8.6 MEAN EFFECTIVE K‑VALUE a loss‑of‑support factor shall be applied to the effective k‑value whenever the subbase is evaluated, because the factor corrects the modulus for potential erosion or pumping of the subbase material; this adjustment applies during the calculation of the effective k‑value for each subbase type and depth [1]. The same section prohibits the use of Open Graded Subbase on any concrete pavement project and mandates an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base as the drainage layer, thereby preventing pumping by providing a more stable, less erodible base; this requirement applies to all concrete pavement projects [1]. Drainage layers may be specified only where a positive outlet drainage can be achieved, otherwise a dense base with longitudinal edge drains should be used, which addresses pumping by ensuring water is removed rather than accumulating under the slab [1]. Section 679.3 of the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications requires that grout injection be halted as soon as any vertical movement of the pavement is observed, thereby preventing further displacement that could contribute to pumping [2]. The same specification limits upward lift measured during cement‑grout filling to no more than 0.05 inch and restricts pump pressure to not exceed 200 psi unless specifically directed, controls that could otherwise drive water out of joints [2]. Finally, traffic may not be allowed onto the treated area for at least twelve hours after grouting, following the field‑QC plan and manufacturer guidance, to avoid loading the slab before it has stabilized [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–98), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Specification limits and acceptance

In Section 679‑3 of the Slab Stabilization specifications, pumping must be stopped as soon as any vertical movement is observed (Section 679.3(a)4), and during cement grout injection the upward movement of the pavement may not exceed 0.05 inch (Section 679.3(b)2.c). Vertical movement is monitored with lift‑measurement equipment that can detect displacement to within 0.001 inch as required in Section 679.3(b)1.d. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

When any vertical movement is observed during grout injection, the specification requires an immediate stop of pumping; the contractor performing the pumping must halt operations and the Representative may order the contractor to cease pumping at that location, with no additional time delay specified. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)
