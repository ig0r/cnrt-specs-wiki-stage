Pumping

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Pumping is a topic under Distress.
pumpingpumped-under-the-slab
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-27

Pumping is the action that creates small voids beneath a Portland cement concrete slab, most often adjacent to transverse joints, cracks and the outside corners of the slab, allowing water and fine material to be expelled upward [1]. The resulting loss of support manifests visually as transverse‑joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder, corner breaks and overall slab settlement [1]. It is driven by water infiltration combined with subgrade consolidation and settlement, which generate the voids that produce excessive slab deflection, stress and, if untreated, progressive cracking and eventual breakup of the slab [1]. When temperature conditions preclude deflection testing, the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require that pumping be confirmed visually by observing the ejection of water or fine material from joints or cracks [2].

Description and identification

Pumping is the action that creates small voids beneath a concrete slab, typically developing near transverse joints, cracks, and especially at outside slab corners; these voids allow water and fine material to be ejected from under the pavement, leading to loss of support. The resulting distress appears as transverse joint faulting, fines deposited near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, corner breaks, and overall slab settlement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

In Section 4.5 Slab Stabilization of the Pavement Policy Manual, pumping is recognized by a loss of slab support that manifests as transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, corner breaks, and overall slab settlement, especially adjacent to transverse joints, cracks, and outside slab corners where voids develop [1]. These observable conditions are interpreted as indications of underlying voids beneath the concrete rather than merely surface cracking, thereby allowing pumping to be distinguished from other pavement distresses [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications prescribe that when deflection testing cannot be performed because air temperature is below 40 °F or exceeds 70 °F, pumping must first be identified by a visual verification in which the inspector looks for the ejection of water or fine material, a sign that differentiates pumping from ordinary joint deterioration [2]. During the grouting operation the crew is required to stop pumping as soon as any vertical movement is observed, which separates true pumping from other distresses such as radial cracks that may appear around injection holes [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Where it occurs

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, pumping is described as a distress that occurs in Portland cement concrete pavement slabs, developing when voids form beneath the slab most often at transverse joints, cracks and outside slab corners, and it can be observed as fines accumulating near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder and as small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at those locations. The manual further limits the phenomenon to concrete slabs and their adjoining shoulders, stating that it is not associated with asphalt, composite, overlays, or other pavement types. In the rehabilitation section of the same manual, pumping is identified as a distress that can develop in existing Portland‑cement concrete pavements, and any slab exhibiting rocking, pumping, or faulting must be stabilized before an overlay is placed, again restricting the concern to PCCP and its concrete overlays. The effective‑k discussion of the manual treats pumping as a loss‑of‑support factor applied to rigid pavement designs, indicating that erosion or pumping of the subbase material beneath concrete slabs may be corrected for, and it notes that all Interstate and Expressway rigid pavements are required to have a base consisting of an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base over a 2A subbase, implying that pumping can occur under concrete slabs using either an ATPB or a CTPB drainage layer. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications describe pumping as a distress that appears in Portland cement concrete slabs when water and fine material are forced out through joints, transverse cracks or radial cracks during slab‑stabilization work. The specification directs that pumping be stopped whenever any vertical movement is noted while grout is being placed, and it applies to concrete pavements whose loaded slab corner deflection reaches 0.02 inch or more, or where joint efficiency meets the defined threshold of 65%* or more if approved by the representative. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–98), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

In Section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION of the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10), pumping is described as voids that form beneath the concrete slab primarily adjacent to transverse joints and cracks, with a pronounced concentration at the outside corners of the slab; fines may be observed near those joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder and small depressions (blow holes) can appear in the shoulder at a joint or crack. The same manual, in Section 4.9 REHABILITATION, identifies pumping as a distress that occurs within the concrete slabs themselves, noting that slabs that are rocking, pumping, and/or faulted should be stabilized prior to overlaying. In Section 8.6 MEAN EFFECTIVE K‑VALUE of the Pavement Policy Manual, pumping is treated as a loss‑of‑support condition that occurs in the subbase (and thus the combined base‑subbase layer), and a loss‑of‑support factor is applied to correct the effective k‑value for potential erosion or pumping of the subbase material. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2020 SPECIFICATIONS, in Section 679.3 CONSTRUCTION, directs that pumping be identified during visual inspection of pavement joints and cracks and considers the distress to occur at the joints and cracks (including loaded slab corners) rather than elsewhere in the pavement structure. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–98), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Causes and mechanisms

In the slab‑stabilization context pumping is attributed to the combined effect of pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement that can generate small voids beneath a concrete slab, particularly adjacent to transverse joints and cracks. The presence of water infiltrating the pavement aggravates this mechanism, so joint and crack sealing must be performed to limit water entry and mitigate pumping. In overlay design active pumping is interpreted as an indicator of poor subgrade condition, meaning inadequate support beneath the pavement is considered the primary cause that will require treatment before applying an interlayer. Neither section provides a table that classifies distresses by load‑related, climate‑related or material‑related causes; consequently no row‑by‑row classification for pumping is available. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

In Section 4.5 Slab Stabilization, pumping develops when the combined action of water infiltration, subgrade consolidation and settlement creates small voids beneath the concrete slab, most of which form adjacent to transverse joints or cracks—particularly at outside slab corners; the loss of support produces excessive slab deflections and stresses that appear as transverse‑joint faulting, fines near joints, blow‑hole depressions, corner breaks, slab settlement and diagonal cracking, eventually leading to complete breakup of the slab. Continued water infiltration and inadequate drainage accelerate this mechanism by increasing the voids, so joint and crack sealing together with good subsurface drains are required as part of the prescribed stabilization. Within Section 5.12 Overlays, pumping originates when water infiltrates the pavement’s structural section, causing loss of subgrade support and forcing water and fine material to be expelled through cracks or joints; it tends to develop further when a poor subgrade condition exists, because active pumping usually signals that underlying support is deficient. The process is intensified by continued water intrusion and freeze‑thaw cycles, which expand and contract the trapped moisture and thereby accelerate the ejection of materials. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

Measurement, survey and extent

Pumping is assessed by measuring the loaded slab corner deflection at each joint or crack using a deflection test performed according to ASTM D 4695; the test records the vertical movement in inches and, together with an unloaded measurement, yields a joint‑efficiency percentage. When air temperature falls outside the 40°F–70°F range for testing, pumping is verified visually rather than quantitatively. Stabilization actions are triggered when the measured loaded slab corner deflection reaches 0.02 inch or more (with joint efficiency criteria also considered). [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Effects on performance

When pumping is observed it signals that water and fine material are being forced up through cracks, which the source notes as an indication of poor subgrade condition that must be treated before any interlayer can be placed. The resulting water intrusion is identified as a major cause of loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation and freeze‑thaw deterioration; these effects collectively reduce structural capacity, degrade ride quality, create safety hazards and shorten the pavement’s durability and service life. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

Within slab-type pavements, pumping creates small voids beneath the concrete slab—most often near transverse joints, cracks and the outside corners of the slab—which removes support and induces excessive deflection and stress. If untreated, this loss of support first manifests as transverse joint faulting, often accompanied by fines or blow‑holes; it then advances to corner breaks, diagonal cracking, and ultimately the complete breakup of the slab (section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION). In overlay applications, active pumping signals that water and fine material are being forced out from beneath the pavement, usually indicating a poor subgrade condition that must be treated before an interlayer can be placed (section 5.12 OVERLAYS). The resulting water intrusion into the structural section becomes a major cause of loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation and freeze‑thaw deterioration; each of these further degrades the pavement and can lead to premature failure. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

Repairs and treatments

Pumping, defined as the ejection of water and fines that creates voids beneath a concrete slab, is addressed primarily through slab‑stabilization treatments. In the slab‑stabilization portion of the Pavement Policy Manual (section 4.5), slab stabilization treats pumping by filling the subgrade voids, thereby restoring support and reducing future pumping; the method may employ cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane selected according to site conditions [1]. When no preliminary testing has been performed, the manual requires that at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints be stabilized, and it also mandates joint and crack sealing together with the stabilization to limit water ingress that contributes to pumping [1]. The rehabilitation portion (section 4.9) further states that any concrete slab that is rocking, pumping, or faulted must first be stabilized—or jacked—before a concrete overlay can be placed, making stabilization a prerequisite surface‑preparation step for overlay projects [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications apply slab stabilization under Section 679.3 to joints or cracks that exhibit a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more; when the joint efficiency is at least 65 percent the treatment is to stabilize the joint, and when the joint efficiency is less than 65 percent the treatment is to patch and stabilize, both only after Representative approval [2]. The specifications also require that pumping be stopped as soon as any vertical movement is observed, and the Representative may direct cessation of pumping if continued material placement is judged not economically feasible or likely to cause vertical movement [2]. After grouting, the drill holes must be patched full depth with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete patching material to seal locations that could otherwise permit further water ejection [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–98); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

In Section 4.5 Slab Stabilization of the Pavement Policy Manual, a treatment is considered applicable when pumping has created voids that cause loss of slab support, which is indicated by transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, corner breaks, or slab settlement; under those conditions cement grout, hot‑asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane may be used, and if no preliminary testing has been performed at least 25 % of the transverse joints and all patch joints shall be stabilized [1]. The same manual’s Section 4.9 Rehabilitation states that when existing concrete pavement exhibits pumping—identified as slabs that are rocking, pumping, or faulted—the slabs should be stabilized or jacked before any overlay is placed, making slab stabilization the applicable treatment for any occurrence of pumping [1]. In Section 5.12 Overlays of the Pavement Policy Manual, the presence of active pumping is taken as an indication of a poor subgrade condition that must be treated before any asphalt paving interlayer can be placed, and consequently an interlayer such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grid, or composite reinforcing grid is not applicable in the presence of active pumping [1]. Under Section 679.3 CONSTRUCTION of the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification 408/2020, slab‑stabilization treatment is applicable when a pumped joint or crack shows a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more; if the measured joint efficiency is at least 65 % the pavement may be stabilized, whereas if the joint efficiency is less than 65 % the pavement must be patch‑and‑stabilized, provided the Representative approves [2]. The same specification allows that when air temperature limits prevent deflection testing, a visual verification of pumping or poor joint performance may be used before specifying slab stabilization [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Prevention in design and construction

The Pavement Policy Manual prescribes slab stabilization as a design measure that fills voids at the slab‑subbase interface; when voids are filled sufficiently full support is restored and the potential for future pumping, faulting, and slab cracking is reduced (section 4.5) [1]. The same manual states that slab stabilization shall be applied when voids are identified, and for estimating purposes at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized if no preliminary testing has been performed (section 4.5) [1]. Joint and crack sealing must be performed in conjunction with slab stabilization to limit water ingress, which contributes to pumping (section 4.5) [1]. Adequate subdrainage is required wherever subsurface drainage problems exist; providing such drainage removes the water that drives the ejection of fine material from beneath slabs (section 4.9 B‑4) [1]. When slab movement or faulting is observed, the manual calls for slab stabilization or jacking before any overlay, and explicitly requires that slabs that are rocking, pumping, or faulted be stabilized prior to overlay placement (sections 4.9 B‑3 and 4.9 C‑1) [1]. For pavements with severe moisture or poor subgrade conditions—situations that often generate pumping—the manual recommends the rubblizing option as a means of addressing the underlying water problem before an overlay (section 4.9 F) [1]. If active pumping is present, the manual states that it usually indicates a poor subgrade condition that will require drainage treatment before the use of any asphalt paving interlayer; consequently drainage work must be performed prior to installation of the interlayer (section 5.12 F) [1]. Thin‑asphalt overlays are permitted only where surface and subsurface drainage are already good or will be upgraded to good as part of the project, thereby directly addressing water that could cause pumping (section 5.12 B, non‑Interstate condition) [1]. A loss‑of‑support factor is applied in the effective‑k calculation whenever the effective k‑value is computed; this factor corrects the k‑value for potential erosion or pumping of the subbase material (section 8.6) [1]. All Interstate and Expressway rigid pavement designs that specify a drainage layer shall employ an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base over a 2A subbase, and the use of Open Graded Subbase is prohibited; this requirement replaces the prohibited material with a stable permeable layer that reduces the likelihood of pumping (section 8.6) [1]. When selecting base elastic moduli for seasonal conditions, the manual requires that the ratio of subbase modulus to roadbed soil resilient modulus must not exceed four in order to avoid artificial conditions that could exacerbate pumping (section 8.6) [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require slab stabilization of joints and cracks that exhibit a loaded slab corner deflection of at least 0.02 inch; when the joint efficiency is 65 percent or higher this stabilization is applied to prevent or reduce pumping by reinforcing the pavement where water and fine material could be expelled (section 679.3(a)2) [2]. For joints or cracks with the same deflection but a joint efficiency below 65 percent, the specifications mandate that they be patched and stabilized for the same purpose of preventing or reducing pumping (section 679.3(a)2) [2]. The specifications also state that pumping shall be stopped whenever any vertical movement is observed, thereby halting further ejection of water from the pavement (section 679.3(a)4) [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

In the Pavement Policy Manual, slab‑stabilization is required to fill voids at the slab‑subbase interface with a grout material such as cement, hot‑asphalt undersealing, or high‑density polyurethane so that full support is restored and deflections that would otherwise promote pumping, faulting and cracking are reduced; this practice applies whenever loss‑of‑support signs—such as transverse joint faulting, fines near joints, small depressions, corner breaks or slab settlement—are observed [1]. The same manual further requires that at least 25 % of the transverse joints and all patch joints be stabilized when no preliminary testing has been performed, and it mandates joint and crack sealing in conjunction with slab‑stabilization while subsurface drains are kept in good condition [1]. Under the rehabilitation section, adequate subdrainage must be provided wherever subsurface drainage problems are identified so that water which drives the ejection of fine materials is removed, and any slabs exhibiting rocking, pumping or faulting must be stabilized before an overlay is placed [1]. The manual also directs that a loss‑of‑support factor be applied to the effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k) during calculation of the effective k‑value for each subbase type and depth in order to correct the modulus for potential erosion or pumping of the subbase material [1]. It prohibits the use of Open Graded Subbase on any concrete pavement project and mandates an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base as the drainage layer, a requirement that applies to all concrete pavement projects and prevents pumping by providing a more stable, less erodible base [1]. Moreover, drainage layers may be specified only where a positive outlet drainage can be achieved; otherwise a dense base with longitudinal edge drains should be used, thereby ensuring water is removed rather than accumulated under the slab [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require a detailed field‑quality‑control plan that identifies hole spacing, depth, drilling method and injection sequence before any slab‑stabilization work begins, because controlling the injection geometry addresses pumping—the ejection of water and fines—by ensuring proper grout placement [2]. Deflection testing is permitted only when air temperature is between 40 °F and 70 °F and the subgrade is not frozen; joints or cracks whose loaded slab‑corner deflection reaches 0.02 inches must be stabilized if their joint efficiency is at least 65 % (or patched if below that threshold), a practice that directly prevents pumping by correcting joints that are moving enough to expel material [2]. During grout injection the procedure instructs stopping pumping as soon as any vertical movement is observed, and allows the Representative to order a cease‑pumping if continued placement is not economically feasible, thereby halting further ejection of water and fines [2]. After grouting, all drill holes must be removed and patched with a rapid‑set, non‑shrink concrete material flush with the pavement surface, which prevents future pumping through unfilled holes [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–98), Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

Specification limits and acceptance

In Section 679—SLAB STABILIZATION > 679.3 CONSTRUCTION the specification limits pumping by requiring the contractor to stop pumping whenever any vertical movement is noted, and it further allows the Representative to order cessation of pumping at a location if continued placement would cause vertical movement or is not economically feasible; these limits apply to slab‑stabilization work under the Pumping Operations clause. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)

When the pumping limit is reached, Section 679.3(a)4 requires that pumping be stopped as soon as any vertical movement is observed; the Representative may also order the Contractor to cease pumping at that location if further placement is not economically feasible. The action is performed by the Contractor under direction of the Representative, and no specific time frame beyond the immediate cessation is stipulated in the specification. No distress‑limit table for Pumping appears in this excerpt. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 588–589)
