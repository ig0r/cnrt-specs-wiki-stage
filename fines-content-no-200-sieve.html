Fines Content (No. 200 Sieve)

Part of: Gradation is-subtopic-of
Fines Content (No. 200 Sieve) is a topic under Gradation.

Created 2026-10-30 · Updated 2026-10-30

Fines Content (No. 200 Sieve) is the percentage of aggregate that passes a No. 200 (75 µm) sieve, representing the silt‑ and clay‑sized fraction in subgrade soils or asphalt mixtures [1][2]. This fine fraction strongly influences moisture sensitivity, compaction effort, California Bearing Ratio values and frost‑heave potential, so it is incorporated into the AASHTO Group Index and serves as a key quality‑control parameter in Superpave mixture design, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, flowable backfill and pervious asphalt specifications [1][2]. The measured percent passing the 200 sieve is reported as a Percent Within Limits (PWL200) or payment factor (PF200); values below prescribed tolerances trigger defect status, corrective actions, and reduced contractor pay, thereby linking fines content directly to acceptance criteria and pavement performance [1][2].

Material purpose and applicability

Fines Content (No. 200 Sieve) is expressed as the percent of aggregate that passes a No. 200 sieve, representing silt‑ and clay‑sized particles in a subgrade soil [1]. In the general quality measure (Percent Within Limits), it appears as one of the measured parameters and the standard deviation component of Percent Within Limits influences the graduated pay factors for asphalt content, percent passing the No. 200 sieve, percent passing the primary control sieve, and pavement in‑place density [1]. The fines fraction strongly influences soil behavior: soils with high fines are usually more moisture sensitive than soils with less fines and can be more difficult to compact; increasing fines content typically results in decreasing CBR values and a higher susceptibility to frost heave [1]. Because the percent passing #200 is also used in calculating the AASHTO Group Index, it helps assess whether the subgrade meets the suitability criteria for pavement design [1]. Fines Content (No. 200 Sieve) is defined as the percentage of aggregate that passes a No. 200 (75 µm) sieve when the gradation of an asphalt or aggregate mixture is tested [2]. In Superpave mixture design (Section 413), this percent passing the 75 μm sieve is a key quality‑control parameter expressed as PWL200 and used to calculate a pay factor (PF200); if the payment factor for asphalt content, percent passing the 75 μm sieve, and percent passing the 2.36 mm (No. 8) sieve are all 85%, the pavement is considered defective, and when PWL200 is less than 50 the lot is defective unless the average percent passing the 75 µm sieve stays within ± 2.0% of the Joint Mix Formula [2]. The same section requires that fines content be evaluated together with asphalt content to determine whether a mix lot meets the acceptance tolerances in Table A, and any mix that does not meet those percent‑passing tolerances must be corrected or halted because proper fines content is required for an acceptable aggregate distribution [2]. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses (Section 489), the percent passing the 75 µm sieve is listed as one of the acceptance values; deviation from the tolerances set in Table E renders the material defective and it must be corrected before production can continue, thereby ensuring that the aggregate gradation meets the required specifications [2]. In stone matrix asphalt mixtures (Section 419), fines content is treated as a payment‑factor metric (P_A); measuring this proportion verifies that the mix gradation falls within specified limits, and if the percent passing falls outside those limits the lot may be required to be removed and replaced, although the District Executive may permit the lot to remain when only one characteristic is below 64 % [2]. For flowable backfill (Section 220) the specification permits at most 10 % of the coarse aggregate to pass a No. 200 sieve, as required by Section 703.2, defining the acceptable gradation of the aggregate component within the backfill mix [2]. In extensible reinforcement for mechanically stabilized earth walls (Section 221), fines content (minus No. 200‑sieve material) is the proportion of particles that pass a No. 200 sieve in the backfill; it must be measured by a wash test in accordance with PTM No. 100 to verify that the backfill meets the required gradation and quality criteria, ensuring proper drainage and stability of the pavement structure [2]. For pervious asphalt pavement (Section 420), the percentage of material passing the No. 200 sieve in the composite mixture must not exceed a 4 % maximum; exceeding this limit makes the pavement defective because excess fines can compromise the intended performance of the pervious course [2]. More generally, the aggregate specifications (Section 703) identify “Material Finer than the 75 µm (No. 200) Sieve” as the fines content, determined by washing according to PTM No. 100 and Table D; limiting fine particles helps ensure proper binder adhesion and overall pavement performance [2].

Composition and characteristics

The fines content defined by the percent of material passing the #200 sieve represents the proportion of silt‑ and clay‑sized particles in a soil. Because these fine particles increase moisture sensitivity, soils with higher fines are generally more difficult to compact, tend to show lower CBR values, and exhibit a greater susceptibility to frost heave. In the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, fines content for MSE wall backfill is defined as the material passing the No. 200 sieve and must be quantified by a wash test according to PTM No. 100. That fine fraction must be part of a backfill that meets overall quality requirements, including a maximum plasticity index of 3, a pH between 5.0 and 9.0, an angle of internal friction not less than 34° for the portion finer than the No. 8 sieve, and it must be free of organic matter, clay lumps, friable particles, coal, coke, metallurgical slag or cinders. In the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course, fines content is expressed as the proportion of aggregate that passes a No. 200 (75 µm) sieve, expressed as a percent by mass of the total aggregate in the plant‑mixed asphalt concrete and used as one of four acceptance values to verify mixture compliance with the Job Mix Formula; if the measured percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve falls outside the tolerances set in Table E, the material is deemed defective. For flowable backfill, fines content is expressed as the proportion of material that passes a No. 200 sieve and the specification allows at most ten percent of the coarse‑aggregate portion to be finer than that sieve, governing the composition of the coarse‑aggregate mix. In the general aggregate specifications, fines content is identified as the portion of aggregate that passes the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve and its amount is determined by washing loss according to PTM No. 100 and Table D; for aggregates processed through a mechanical dryer for asphalt concrete this test may be omitted, but the material still must be clean and free of fines that would adversely affect coating of the aggregate with asphalt material, and the requirement does not apply to AASHTO No. 10 aggregates. For pervious asphalt pavement systems, fines content is defined by the proportion of material that passes a No. 200 sieve, limited to a maximum of 4% of the total mixture, and at least 95% of the aggregate particles must be coated with binder; exceeding either limit renders the pavement defective under the Defective Work clause. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 186–190), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 310–344), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–655)

Variations that increase the percent passing the #200 sieve raise the fines content, which makes the soil more moisture‑sensitive, harder to compact, and more prone to frost heave; at the same time higher fines content tends to lower CBR values and raises the optimum moisture content needed for maximum dry density, thereby influencing the calculated Group Index. Conversely, soils with less material passing the #200 sieve exhibit lower moisture sensitivity, are easier to compact, achieve higher CBR values, and have lower optimum moisture contents. Key material properties

The fines content, expressed as the percent of material passing the #200 sieve, is identified as a key physical parameter because it strongly influences moisture sensitivity, compaction effort, and load‑bearing capacity of subgrade soils. As the fine fraction increases, soils become more difficult to compact, exhibit lower CBR values, and are more prone to frost heave, reflecting mechanical degradation under wet or freezing conditions. Chemical characteristics captured by the Atterberg limits—liquid limit, plastic limit, and plasticity index—further control compressibility and elasticity, with higher LL and PI indicating greater potential loss of strength when moisture rises. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications state that the performance of fines content is governed by a number of physical, mechanical, chemical and thermal properties that are specified for different pavement‑engineering applications. In the Superpave mixture design the percent passing the 75 mm (No. 200) sieve is measured on loose‑mix acceptance samples and must fall within the single‑sample or multiple‑sample tolerances set out in Table A together with asphalt content; the mixtures also must be delivered and placed at the laying temperatures specified in Table A, and material delivered outside those temperature limits must not be incorporated. For an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course the percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve must fall within the tolerances defined in the Job Mix Formula, and any deviation beyond those limits renders the material defective because the fines interact with the binder to achieve the required mixture characteristics. In a pervious asphalt pavement system the composite mixture must not exceed a 4 % maximum passing the No. 200 sieve, and the mix is deemed unacceptable when the percent of coated aggregate particles is less than 95 %, both limits being directly related to durability and infiltration performance. For extensible reinforcement in MSE walls the fines content (minus No. 200 sieve material) must be determined by a wash test according to PTM No. 100, the backfill must meet a maximum plasticity index of 3, a pH between 5.0 and 9.0, an angle of internal friction not less than 34° for the fraction finer than the No. 8 sieve, and it also must be free of clay lumps, friable particles, coal and coke. In flowable backfill coarse aggregate may contain no more than 10 % material finer than the No. 200 sieve; fine aggregate and bottom ash are each limited to a maximum loss of 20 % in the Soundness Test, fly ash must not exceed a loss on ignition of 16 %, and when the cured backfill is expected to contact metal it must achieve a resistivity greater than 2000 ohm‑centimeters, maintain a pH between 5.0 and 9.5, keep chlorides below 200 ppm, sulfates below 1000 ppm and sulfides below 200 ppm. For aggregates used in asphalt concrete the proportion of material finer than the No. 200 sieve is determined by washing according to PTM No. 100 and Table D; the aggregate must be clean and free of fines that would adversely affect coating with asphalt, and this test may be omitted only when a mechanical dryer is employed. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 186–190), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 270–344), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–655)

The fines content is defined as the proportion of material that passes a No. 200 (75 µm) sieve. In the Pavement Policy Manual, Section 2.1 GENERAL, fines content is characterized by this percent and incorporated into the Percent Within Limits (PWL) quality metric, which captures both the mean and the standard deviation of test results; the variability component of PWL then influences the graduated pay factors applied to asphalt content, the percent passing the No. 200 sieve, other control sieves, and in‑place density. In Section 6.2 SUBGRADE SOILS of the same manual, the percent passing the #200 sieve is determined by sieving a representative soil sample and recording that percentage; this measured value is also incorporated into the AASHTO Group Index calculation together with the Atterberg limits to classify the subgrade material. Soils with higher fines content are usually more moisture sensitive than soils with less fines, can be more difficult to compact, typically result in decreasing CBR values, and have a higher susceptibility to frost heave. In the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, Section 413.4 of the Superpave mixture design quantifies fines content by sieving a representative aggregate sample through a 75 µm (No. 200) sieve and recording the percent passing; this percentage is used to calculate a Percent Within Limits value (PWL200), which must be at least 50 for the lot to be acceptable. If PWL200 is less than 50, the lot is considered defective unless the average percent passing the 75 µm sieve lies within ± 2.0 % of the Joint Mix Formula. In Section 413.3(h), a loose mixture sample from each sublot is sent to Laboratory Testing Services where PTM No. 757 or PTM No. 702 Modified Method D, together with calibration factors, are applied to determine the percent passing the 75 mm (No. 200) sieve; the obtained value is compared with the single‑sample (n=1) or multiple‑sample (n≥3) tolerances set out in Table A, and if it falls outside those limits for two consecutive lots production must be stopped and corrected. In Section 221.2, fines content for backfill used in mechanically stabilized earth walls is quantified by performing a wash test in accordance with PTM No. 100, and this determination is required in addition to the procedures outlined in PTM No. 616. In Section 489.2, the fines content of plant‑mixed asphalt concrete is quantified by conducting a sieve analysis and recording the percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve; the result is included in the daily quality‑control test package that is sent to the Inspector‑In‑Charge within one working day, and the reported percentage must fall within the tolerance limits defined in Table E of the Job Mix Formula, otherwise the material is classified as defective and rejected. In Section 419.4, fines content appears in the payment formula as the Payment Factor Percentage (P_A); during mixture acceptance testing the percent passing the 75 µm sieve is measured, and if the value is less than 64 % the lot may be classified as defective and subject to a remove‑and‑replace provision, with retesting performed on cores taken 24 inches from the original sampling locations and the newly determined percentage used to recalculate the payment factor. Behavior and mechanisms

Fines content, expressed as the percent passing the #200 sieve, strongly influences subgrade behavior. Soils that contain a high proportion of fines are usually more moisture sensitive and harder to compact, which reduces their load‑bearing capacity as reflected by lower CBR values, and they become more prone to frost heave when temperatures drop. Consequently, increasing fines content tends to diminish the soil’s stiffness and durability under service conditions. [1]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143)

The percent passing the #200 sieve (fines content) influences subgrade soil behavior primarily through physical and mechanical interactions of silt‑ and clay‑sized particles. Physically, a higher fines content increases moisture sensitivity and makes the soil harder to compact because fine particles fill voids and alter gradation. Mechanically, increasing fines leads to lower CBR values and greater susceptibility to frost heave as the fine fraction reduces stiffness and strength. Chemically, the fines affect the Atterberg limits—liquid limit and plasticity index—which in turn affect compressibility and elasticity, so soils with higher liquid and plasticity indices—often linked to finer particles—exhibit lower CBR values. In SECTION 221 of the Pennsylvania specifications, the fines content (minus No. 200 sieve material) for MSE wall backfill is controlled physically by the particle‑size distribution obtained through a wash test in accordance with PTM No. 100. The same specification limits the chemical characteristics of that backfill to a maximum plasticity index of 3 as determined according to AASHTO T 89 and T 90 and to a pH that must lie between 5.0 and 9.0 as determined according to AASHTO T 289, thereby ensuring low clay activity and neutral‑to‑slightly alkaline conditions. Mechanically the shear behavior of the backfill is governed by an angle of internal friction that must be not less than 34° as determined according to AASHTO T 236, providing the strength required for stability. In SECTION 703, the quantity of fines passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve is governed primarily by the physical size distribution of the aggregate, which is quantified by washing the material and measuring the loss as prescribed in PTM No. 100 and Table D. When the aggregate has been processed through a mechanical dryer for asphalt concrete this wash test may be omitted, but the specification still requires the aggregate to be clean and free of fines that could adversely affect the mechanical coating of the particles by asphalt binder. Durability and deterioration

In subgrade soils, the percent of material passing the #200 sieve (fines content) can have a significant effect on soil behavior [1]. Soils with high fines content are usually more moisture sensitive than soils with less fines and can be more difficult to compact [1]. As the fines fraction increases, California Bearing Ratio values typically decrease, indicating reduced load‑bearing capacity, and the soil exhibits a higher susceptibility to frost heave, which can compromise both strength and stability of the pavement foundation [1]. In Superpave plant‑mixed asphalt courses, the primary durability concern associated with the fines fraction measured on the No. 200 sieve is pattern segregation, whereby an uneven distribution of fine and coarse aggregates creates zones that may be classified as defective pavement if the mixture fails the percent‑passing‑No. 200‑sieve tolerance [2]. The LTS test each lot for the percent passing the 75 mm (No. 200) sieve and production shall be stopped when this value is out of tolerance for two consecutive lots or a total of three lots, indicating that improper fines content directly compromises pavement uniformity and long‑term performance [2]. For extensible reinforcement in mechanically stabilized earth (MSE) walls the fines content (minus No. 200 sieve material) must be determined by wash test according to PTM No. 100, and the backfill is required to be free of organic matter and other deleterious substances such as clay lumps, friable particles, coal, coke, metallurgical slag, or cinders [2]. The backfill material shall have a maximum plasticity index of 3, a pH between 5.0 and 9.0, and an angle of internal friction not less than 34 degrees, specifications intended to limit durability concerns associated with excessive fines [2]. In coarse‑aggregate specifications excess fines defined as material passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve are monitored because they can interfere with the proper coating of coarse aggregate by asphalt binder, potentially compromising pavement durability; loss of such fine material shall be determined by washing according to PTM No. 100 and Table D unless the aggregate has been processed through a mechanical dryer for use in asphalt concrete or is an AASHTO No. 10 aggregate, in which cases the test is not required [2]. For pervious asphalt pavement systems the composite mixture is deemed defective when the proportion of particles passing the No. 200 sieve exceeds the 4% maximum specified, because excessive fines can impair water transmission and thus threaten durability; this condition often coincides with infiltration rates falling below the required minimum of 200 inches per hour, which also classifies the course as defective [2].

Material interactions and compatibility

The percent passing the #200 sieve, referred to as fines content, strongly influences subgrade behavior by making soils more moisture‑sensitive and harder to compact when the fines are high. As the fines increase, the soil typically shows decreasing CBR values and a higher susceptibility to frost heave, while the optimum moisture content normally increases with increasing fines content of the soil. Consequently, higher fines content can lower achievable dry densities and affect the overall stiffness of the subgrade used beneath pavement layers. In Superpave mixture design, fines content is monitored by measuring the percent passing the 75 mm (No. 200) sieve, and this measurement serves as a primary quality‑control check during mixture acceptance; if the percent passing the No. 200 sieve is not within the single‑sample (n=1) or multiple‑sample (n≥3) tolerances in Table A for two consecutive lots, production must be stopped and corrective actions taken. Failure of this test can also contribute to pattern‑segregation defects that require removal and replacement of the affected pavement. In aggregate specifications, fines are defined as material passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve and are evaluated by washing according to PTM No. 100 and Table D; for aggregates processed through a mechanical dryer for asphalt concrete this test may be omitted, but the aggregate is required to be clean and free of fines because excess fines can interfere with the coating of the aggregate by the asphalt binder. Controlling the amount of material finer than the No. 200 sieve therefore helps ensure proper binder‑aggregate adhesion in asphalt mixes. In pervious asphalt pavement systems, the proportion of material passing the No. 200 sieve is limited to a maximum of 4 % of the composite mixture, and if more than 4 % passes, the pavement is classified as defective because the percentage of coated‑aggregate particles drops below 95 %, indicating insufficient binder coating and reduced performance. Mixing, production and placement temperatures must stay within the limits set out in Table A; material delivered below the minimum temperature is prohibited because it can become segregated or cold and thus alter the percent passing the No. 200 sieve. After spreading, any mixture that is segregated, below the minimum temperature, or has an asphalt‑content deficiency or excess must not be added to the mat, ensuring the fines content remains within the specified tolerance. Selection, specification, and improvement

The specification of fines content, expressed as the percent passing the No. 200 sieve, is governed by the Percent Within Limits (PWL) quality measure, which captures both mean and standard deviation and therefore directly influences the graduated pay factors applied to asphalt content, percent passing the No. 200 sieve, the primary control sieve, and in‑place density [1]. Accordingly, districts must consider the condition of the underlying base when determining the method for density acceptance, and contractors are expected to achieve consistent field densities near the middle of the target range—approximately 92.0 % to 98.5 % for Standard and RPS construction of wearing courses—to maximize payment under the PWL system [1]. Designers also evaluate the amount of material passing the #200 sieve because higher fines content makes the subgrade more moisture‑sensitive, harder to compact, tends to lower CBR values, and increases susceptibility to frost heave; the percent passing #200 is a component of the Group Index calculation together with liquid limit and plasticity index [1]. In Superpave plant‑mixed asphalt courses the percent passing the No. 200 sieve is controlled as a PWL parameter and must be at least 50 % within limits to avoid a defective lot, although when the average percent passing lies within ± 2.0 % of the Joint Mix Formula the lot may remain acceptable even if the PWL200 falls below 50 [2]. Payment‑factor calculations follow the specified equations: for PWL ≥ 90 the factor is PF = 100 + 0.4(PWL‑90), and for 50 ≤ PWL < 90—or when PWL200 < 50 but the average is within ± 2.0 % of the JMF—the factor is PF = 70 + 0.75(PWL‑50) [2]. A pavement is deemed defective if the payment factors for asphalt content, percent passing the No. 200 sieve and percent passing the No. 8 sieve are all 85 %, or if PWL200 < 50 and the average exceeds ± 2.0 % of the JMF [2]. Acceptance testing requires mixture‑acceptance samples to meet the single‑sample (n=1) or multiple‑sample (n≥3) tolerances listed in Table A, and if the percent passing the No. 200 sieve falls outside those limits for two consecutive lots—or three lots in total—production must be halted until a corrective plan is approved [2]. For ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses the fines content must fall within the Joint Mix Formula tolerances shown in Table E; any deviation renders the material defective and triggers corrective action [2]. In stone matrix asphalt mixtures the percent passing the No. 200 sieve is tied to a payment‑factor percentage, and a lot that does not meet the established limits must be removed and replaced unless only one characteristic of the lot falls below 64 %, in which case the District Executive may permit the contractor to leave the defective lot in place [2]. The selection of fines content for backfill used in mechanically stabilized earth walls is governed by a wash test according to PTM No. 100 (supplementing PTM No. 616) to determine material passing the No. 200 sieve, and the backfill must satisfy Type C coarse‑aggregate quality or better while being free of clay lumps, friable particles, coal, coke, metallurgical slag or cinders; additionally the fines fraction must have a maximum plasticity index of 3, a pH between 5.0 and 9.0, and an angle of internal friction not less than 34 degrees measured on the portion finer than the No. 8 sieve [2]. In flowable backfill any material that passes the No. 200 sieve may not exceed ten percent of the coarse‑aggregate portion, a requirement that ensures the aggregate gradation remains within the acceptable range for the mix design and mandates sourcing from Bulletin 14 [2]. General aggregate specifications require determination of material finer than the No. 200 sieve by washing according to PTM No. 100 and Table D; this test is not required for aggregates processed through a mechanical dryer for asphalt concrete or for AASHTO No. 10 aggregates, but in all cases the aggregate must be clean and free of fines that would adversely affect coating with asphalt material [2]. For pervious asphalt pavement the percent passing the No. 200 sieve for the composite mixture is limited to a maximum of 4 %; exceeding this threshold makes the course defective and obligates removal or replacement [2].

Performance evaluation and implications

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), evaluation of #200‑sieve fines content for subgrade soils is based on a suite of laboratory tests that include AASHTO M 145 soil classification, gradation expressed as percent passing the #200 sieve, Atterberg limits, maximum dry density and optimum moisture content, and calculation of the AASHTO Group Index; these results are used to corroborate California Bearing Ratio (CBR) test values, which must be verified against the companion laboratory data. The same manual notes that field‑derived falling‑weight deflectometer measurements may be performed beforehand to locate sampling points for the material that will later be tested in the laboratory. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications (Publication 408/2026) require laboratory determination of the percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve on aggregate samples taken from the in‑place pavement, typically box or core specimens, and express the result as a Percent Within Limits for the 200‑sieve (PWL200); this value is used to calculate a Pay Factor for percent passing the 75 µm sieve (PF200), which contributes to the Overall Lot Pay Factor. Under those specifications a lot is considered defective when PWL200 is less than 50 unless the average percent passing the 75 µm sieve is within ±2.0 % of the Joint Mix Formula, and retesting may be performed on samples collected 24 inches from the original locations if the initial result fails. The specifications also state that laboratory evidence for fines content can be obtained from LTS testing of loose mixture acceptance samples measured according to PTM No. 757, PTM No. 702 (Modified Method D) and PTM No. 739. Field evidence in the same section includes cores taken from the placed pavement—typically three 6‑inch diameter cores from suspected pattern‑segregated areas and three from non‑segregated zones—and tested at the plant for gradation, including the percent passing the 75 µm sieve, to determine whether the pavement is defective. Section 221 of the Pennsylvania specifications evaluates fines content for MSE wall backfill by a laboratory wash test according to PTM No. 100 in addition to PTM No. 616; no field or long‑term performance evidence is prescribed. Section 489 requires laboratory sieve‑analysis to determine the percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve as part of routine quality‑control testing, with the value required to fall within tolerances set out in Table E of the Joint Mix Formula; field evidence is supplied by certification from the material producer and QA samples taken by QA Teams from the BOPD, and if either laboratory or field results deviate from those tolerances the material is deemed defective. Section 419 evaluates fines content through laboratory mixture‑acceptance testing of the percent passing the 75 µm sieve performed by the LTS lab; any failing result triggers a field retest using 6‑inch pavement cores taken at specified offset locations, and an acceptance limit of less than 64 % passing may be applied to determine defectiveness. Section 703 specifies that fines content, defined as material passing the 75 µm sieve, is evaluated in the laboratory by determining loss on washing according to PTM No. 100 and Table D; this test is omitted for aggregates processed through a mechanical dryer for asphalt concrete use or for AASHTO No. 10 aggregates. Section 420 provides that the only criterion for assessing fines content of the composite pervious asphalt mixture is a sieve‑analysis check: the percentage of material passing the No. 200 sieve must not exceed 4 %; this laboratory result determines whether the mix is considered defective, and no additional field or long‑term performance tests are cited. Neither the Pavement Policy Manual nor the Pennsylvania specifications describe any long‑term performance monitoring specific to fines content. [1][2]

In the Pavement Policy Manual, fines content expressed as percent passing No.200 sieve is incorporated into the Percent Within Limits (PWL) quality metric; its standard deviation influences graduated pay factors applied to asphalt content, fines content, other control sieves and in‑place density [1]. Because PWL creates these pay differentials, contractors must achieve consistent field densities near the middle of the target range—approximately 92.0 % to 98.5 % for Standard and RPS wearing courses—to obtain full payment, while pavements that fall toward the bottom of the density range may receive reduced pay factors [1]. In subgrade design, the percentage of material passing the #200 sieve directly influences soil behavior; soils with higher fines become more moisture‑sensitive, are more difficult to compact, typically show decreasing CBR values and greater susceptibility to frost heave, thereby reducing long‑term durability [1]. The increased fines content also raises the optimum moisture content needed for maximum dry density, demanding tighter control during construction to achieve proper compaction [1]. Within Superpave mixture design, the percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve is used as a quality metric called PWL200, and when PWL200 falls below 50 the lot can be declared defective unless the average value is within ±2.0 % of the Joint Mix Formula [2]. For PWL200 values between 50 and 90 a reduced pay factor is calculated by PF200 = 70 + 0.75(PWL200‑50), while for PWL200 at or above 90 the pay factor rises according to PF200 = 100 + 0.4(PWL200‑90) [2]. The fines content therefore carries a ten‑percent weight in the Overall Lot Pay Factor that determines final payment and incentives, and controlling fines content directly influences acceptance, economic reward, and indirectly supports pavement performance and durability by ensuring the mixture meets the specified gradation limits [2]. During construction, fines content is monitored by testing the percent passing the 75 µm sieve on each mixture‑acceptance sample; the result must fall within the single‑sample or multiple‑sample tolerances in Table A, and if it falls outside those limits for two consecutive lots or a total of three lots production must be stopped until corrective action is approved [2]. Because pattern segregation is deemed defective when the deviation from the Joint Mix Formula for percent passing the No. 200 sieve exceeds 20 %, excessive fines can cause non‑uniform aggregate distribution, flushing and reduced macrotexture, which in turn compromise pavement durability and may require removal and replacement of the affected lane [2]. For the ultra‑thin bonded wearing course mixture, the percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve is a defined acceptance value; any deviation from the Joint Mix Formula tolerances classifies the material as defective and requires immediate corrective action, and production may be suspended if compliance cannot be achieved within 150 tons, thereby protecting pavement performance and durability by ensuring proper mix composition during construction [2]. In stone matrix asphalt, the percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve is entered as a separate payment‑factor percentage (P_A) in the lot payment formula; when the fine content is within limits less than 64 % the lot is considered defective, and although the District Executive may allow the contractor to leave that lot in place, the Department will pay only 50 % of the contract unit price [2]. A failure on this criterion triggers mandatory retesting—requiring new cores taken 24 inches from the original sample locations and using the same test methods—adding extra quality‑control steps during construction that help ensure the final pavement gradation meets requirements and supports long‑term performance [2]. The flowable backfill specification limits the proportion of particles passing the No. 200 sieve to no more than 10 % of the coarse‑aggregate portion; keeping fines below this threshold helps achieve the required compaction and load‑bearing capacity, which supports pavement durability and reduces the risk of premature settlement during construction and service [2]. Exceeding the 10 % limit would trigger a revised mix design and could compromise the intended performance [2]. For extensible reinforcement in MSE walls, fines content (minus No. 200 sieve material) is required to be measured by wash test; designers must verify that the backfill does not exceed a plasticity index of 3, has a pH between 5.0 and 9.0, and possesses an angle of internal friction of at least 34°, because meeting these thresholds ensures the material remains free of deleterious substances, retains adequate shear strength, and drains properly, which supports the structural stability of the wall and thus contributes to durable pavement performance [2]. In pervious asphalt pavement systems, the amount of material passing the No. 200 sieve is limited to a maximum of 4 %; when this fines content exceeds that limit the composite mixture is classified as defective and must be removed and replaced, directly impacting the intended performance and durability of the pervious course and triggering corrective actions during construction or later maintenance to restore proper infiltration and surface quality [2]. The amount of material passing the No. 200 sieve is controlled because fines that remain on coarse aggregate can hinder proper asphalt coating, leading to inconsistent performance and reduced durability; determination of loss by washing according to PTM No. 100 and Table D is required, although this test is not required for aggregate processed through a mechanical dryer for use in asphalt concrete, the aggregate must still be clean and free of fines that would adversely affect the coating of the aggregate with asphalt material [2].
