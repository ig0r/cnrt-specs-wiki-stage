Service Life

Part of: Pavement Design is-subtopic-of
Service Life is a topic under Pavement Design.
service-lifepavement-liferemaining-service-life
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

Service Life is the projected period over which a pavement is expected to retain acceptable structural and functional performance under anticipated traffic loads and environmental conditions, and it serves as the primary basis for selecting pavement type, thickness, and associated maintenance schedules in life‑cycle cost analyses (e.g., fifty‑year horizons for long‑life concrete or asphalt and a twenty‑year design life for Superpave courses) [1]. By integrating factors such as traffic‑generated ESALs, drainage quality (overall coefficient C_d), and the requirement for high‑performance dowel bars in patches expected to remain in service beyond twenty years, Service Life translates engineering assumptions into concrete design parameters and prescribed treatment cycles that aim to prevent premature deterioration, reduce user delay, and extend the pavement’s functional period [1]. Success is verified through condition‑specific outcomes—such as reduced moisture‑related distress, maintained surface friction, and adherence to scheduled maintenance actions—that demonstrate the pavement has performed adequately throughout its intended service duration [1].

Purpose and decision context

For long‑life concrete pavements—whether new construction, reconstruction (including work on fractured concrete) or unbonded concrete overlay—Service Life is used to support the decision to select that pavement type and its associated maintenance schedule when the analysis period is fifty years in a life‑cycle cost analysis. In concrete pavement patching, Service Life supports the decision of whether to specify high‑performance dowel bars; designers should consider those bars when a patched or repaired section is expected to remain in service for more than 20 years before its programmed replacement. For Superpave paving courses, Service Life (a 20‑year design life) is used to compute the design ESALs that drive the volumetric asphalt mixture design; engineers apply the 20‑year design life ESALs to place the expected loading rate into one of five predefined ESAL ranges, which determines the appropriate PG‑binder and mix design parameters, even if the project’s anticipated service life is shorter than 20 years. In roller‑compacted concrete design for local and collector roads with an ADT ≤ 2000, Service Life is used to decide the required structural thickness; designers adopt a 20‑year design life with a 1 % traffic growth rate and then calculate the minimum pavement depth that will satisfy the specified terminal serviceability and reliability targets. Under the state‑funded pavement preservation guidelines, Service Life is used to decide whether a proposed maintenance treatment should be classified as pavement preservation, requiring confirmation that the treatment restores the existing system’s function and extends its service life rather than increasing capacity or strength. Service Life is needed to ensure that pavements continue to perform adequately over their intended period despite traffic loads and environmental exposure, supporting the goal of extending pavement life, improving structural or functional capacity where appropriate, and avoiding premature deterioration that would require costly rehabilitation or reconstruction. Excessive water within or beneath pavement layers can cause loss of subgrade support, freeze‑thaw damage and other distress; therefore subsurface drainage is required because it increases pavement life and more uniform performance will result from proper drainage. For long‑life asphalt new construction or reconstruction (including construction on fractured concrete pavement) a 50 Year Pavement Life analysis period is established so the pavement must continue to function adequately under traffic and environmental loads, and for asphalt new construction or reconstruction the same 50 Year horizon is used with maintenance schedules designed to manage user‑delay impacts and life‑cycle cost. For long‑life concrete new construction, reconstruction and unbonded concrete overlay a 50 Year Pavement Life analysis period creates the need for periodic clean‑and‑seal at 10‑year intervals (50 % of longitudinal joints including shoulders and 50 % of transverse joints), joint treatments at 20‑year intervals, concrete patching and diamond grinding at year 30 (2 % of pavement area patched, diamond grinding of 100 % of pavement width and 25 % of shoulders) and further sealing at year 40 (50 % of longitudinal joints including shoulders and 50 % of transverse joints), all intended to keep performance adequate and limit traffic‑user delay. For concrete pavement rehabilitation with an asphalt overlay a 30 Year Pavement Life analysis period is used to meet the performance goal of keeping traffic‑user delay under control, with maintenance actions scheduled at years 10, 15, 20 and 25 for milling, sealing, crack repair and overlay classified as Maintenance and Protection of Traffic User Delay. In concrete pavement patching Service Life addresses the need that patches retain adequate long‑term performance so repaired sections remain in service; patches must be properly designed and constructed, and high‑performance dowel bars should be considered when the patched pavement will be in service for more than 20 years before programmed replacement. For concrete joints Service Life addresses the need to keep a concrete pavement performing adequately over its intended period by preventing joint and crack deterioration; resealing should occur when distress first appears because more extensive repairs will be necessary the longer distressed joints are left unattended, and joint rehabilitation, dowel‑bar retrofits and cross‑stitching are prescribed as cost‑effective methods to preserve or extend the pavement’s life when overlay is not planned. Partial‑depth repair addresses the need to prolong useful life after damage by restoring rideability; the repair depth may not exceed half the slab thickness and an appropriate cement mix must be selected based on time before traffic reopening, temperature, cost and repair size, with measures such as polystyrene boards or expansion‑joint inserts required to prevent compression failures caused by thermal movement. Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (LLCP) addresses the need for pavements that remain functional over an extended period despite traffic loads and environmental exposure; this is met by using high‑quality materials, high‑performance dowel bars, full‑depth replacement and strict control of every construction step from subgrade preparation through curing and sawing, and LLCP shall not be used for thin (less than 8 in) or bonded concrete applications. The friction requirement for highway asphalt wearing courses addresses the need to keep surface friction adequate for safe driving throughout its intended period; the surface must provide sufficient texture to prevent water‑pressure buildup at the tire/pavement interface at the posted speed limit and retain this capability under traffic and environmental conditions over the life of the surface. Superpave paving courses address the need to predict how long a pavement will perform acceptably by establishing a 20‑year design life that drives ESAL calculations; these 20‑year design‑life ESAL values are required for all Superpave courses even when the anticipated service life is shorter because they establish the expected loading rate and guide binder grade selection. In rigid pavement design the need to guarantee satisfactory performance throughout the intended period incorporates drainage quality via an overall drainage coefficient C_d; for pavements on unbound aggregate only C_d = 1.0, for new construction or reconstruction that includes a treated permeable base, dense asphalt base and a positive underdrain system C_d = 1.1, and for rehabilitation projects where past performance shows substandard drainage and no significant improvements are planned C_d = 1.0. Tied concrete shoulders address the need to keep pavements performing as long as possible by mitigating edge stresses and limiting deflection; tied shoulders reduce differential heaving, settlement and horizontal separation, thereby delaying the first maintenance cycle, reducing overall maintenance requirements and improving safety. For roller‑compacted concrete on local and collector roads with ADT ≤ 2000 the need to guarantee functionality for the intended period is met by adopting a 20‑year design life with a 1.0 % traffic growth rate and constraining slab cracking to no more than 15 % at the end of that period while using subgrade resilient modulus, terminal serviceability and reliability criteria. The Remaining Life evaluation method addresses the need to predict how long an existing pavement will continue to perform adequately so designers can select suitable overlay types and determine required structural capacity; it combines past traffic data with current condition, but it is excluded from Department designs because reliable historical traffic records are lacking. Forecasting and prescribed treatment cycles address the need to guarantee performance for a defined period, enabling agencies to forecast condition and plan maintenance before deterioration compromises service; by quantifying how long a pavement can perform under anticipated loads, managers can choose cost‑effective treatments that deliver the desired service level over the analysis horizon, supporting the goal of maximizing pavement life while minimizing total lifecycle cost. Treatment cycles selected according to surface type, functional class and traffic characteristics such as ADT < 1000 and truck percentage < 10 % for the Recycling Network extend pavement life, reduce premature deterioration and provide a methodology for identifying roads that are “out of cycle”. In bonded concrete overlay design Service Life addresses the need to keep pavements performing adequately over time by preventing premature failure from rutting, shoving, slippage or thermal cracking; this is achieved by adding structural capacity through a Portland cement concrete layer placed on milled asphalt that meets minimum thickness requirements—at least 3 inches of remaining asphalt, concrete thickness no less than 2.5 inches for thin BCOA and 4 inches or more for conventional BCOA—and is applied where traffic demand reaches at least 2,500 ESALs per day, with the performance goal of extending service life without resorting to thick asphalt overlays or full reconstruction using the BCOA‑ME methodology for overlays up to 6.5 inches. Preservation criteria address the need to keep existing pavements performing adequately over the intended period without increasing structural depth; preservation projects are prohibited from adding structural depth solely to extend life and require travel lanes be preserved for at least 10,000 contiguous feet while maintaining safety features. Overall, Service Life addresses the problem of premature pavement deterioration that forces costly and disruptive rehabilitation or reconstruction, meeting the need to preserve investment, keep traffic flowing safely, maintain mobility, reduce congestion and user delays, and deliver smoother, longer‑lasting pavements that meet motorist expectations. Scope and applicability

Service Life is applied to long‑life asphalt new‑construction or reconstruction projects—including work that rebuilds fractured concrete pavement—when the analysis period is defined as a 50 Year Pavement Life; in those cases life‑cycle cost analysis uses a fifty‑year horizon and schedules maintenance actions such as clean‑and‑seal at five years, crack seal of 25 % of longitudinal joints with 500 lineal feet per mile seal coat or micro‑surface shoulders, and other prescribed treatments throughout the period. The same 50 Year Pavement Life analysis period is used for long‑life concrete new‑construction, reconstruction (including work on fractured concrete pavement) and unbonded concrete overlay treatments. New composite asphalt concrete over Portland cement concrete (AC/PCC) construction or reconstruction—including work on fractured concrete pavement—also follows a 50 Year Pavement Life with maintenance actions scheduled at ten, twenty, thirty, thirty‑five and forty‑five years; specific actions include clean‑and‑seal at ten years on 50 % of longitudinal joints, clean‑and‑seal on 50 % of transverse joints, crack seal of 615 lineal feet per mile, and at twenty years mill asphalt wearing course and shoulders, asphalt overlay mainline and shoulders, saw and seal all transverse joints. Concrete pavement patching is appropriate when the repaired section is expected to remain in service for more than 20 years before a programmed replacement; the use of high‑performance dowel bars should be considered and patches must be properly designed and constructed to achieve adequate long‑term performance. Partial‑depth repair may be performed on pavements as part of a CPR project or as maintenance, and it is suitable for transverse or longitudinal joints, cracks and interior slab spalls when the depth of the repair does not exceed half the slab thickness; the material used on any given project will depend on the time available before opening to traffic, expected ambient temperature, cost, size, depth and location. Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (LLCP) must be new construction or full‑depth replacement that allows control of every aspect of the paving operation from subgrade preparation through concrete placement, jointing, finishing, curing and sawing; it is limited to applications where pavement thickness is at least 8 inches and LLCP is not appropriate for thin (less than 8 inches) or bonded concrete pavement applications. Design ESALs are calculated using a 20‑year design life and must be specified in the item numbers and descriptions for Superpave paving courses; the 20‑year design life ESALs are to be used for all projects including Pavement Preservation projects, even if the project’s service life is intended or expected to be less than 20 years. Service Life is also taken into account for new construction or reconstruction that employ a treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate together with an under‑drain system and positive drainage, in which case the overall drainage coefficient C_d is set to 1.1; rigid pavements built directly on unbound aggregate only use C_d = 1.0, and rehabilitation projects where existing pavement shows substandard drainage performance and no major drainage improvements are planned also use C_d = 1.0. For roller‑compacted concrete design on local and collector roads with an average daily traffic of ≤ 2000 vehicles, Service Life is incorporated by assuming a 20‑year design life and a 1.0 % traffic growth rate; the subgrade resilient modulus is calculated as MRSG = 1500 × CBR, terminal serviceability is taken as 2.5 with reliability 90 % for collector roads and 2.0 with reliability 85 % for local roads. Finally, Service Life is appropriate for pavement preservation projects that consist of preventive maintenance, minor non‑structural rehabilitation or routine maintenance because these actions restore function and extend the pavement’s service life; the concept therefore applies to assets already in service during the planning and design phases of preservation programs where the objective is to maintain performance and prolong lifespan. Service Life predictions should not be used for Pavement Preservation projects because their purpose is to maintain existing conditions without adding structural capacity, and the Pavement Preservation Guidelines are to be applied instead. Likewise, on PennDOT Oversight projects that involve less than 30,000 square yards of mainline pavement surface work, the type‑determination procedure that normally includes a Service Life assessment is not required, allowing engineers to rely on documented engineering judgment and existing conditions. When a concrete pavement defect requires repair deeper than half the slab thickness, relying on a partial‑depth patch to extend the pavement’s service life is not appropriate; a full‑depth concrete patch should be used, and corner spalls that extend beyond mid‑slab depth require full‑depth corner repairs as defined in the PCCP guidelines. The Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (LLCP) approach, which is intended to provide an extended service life, is not appropriate for slabs thinner than eight inches or for bonded concrete pavement applications; in those cases a conventional concrete pavement or restricted‑performance specifications should be used instead. When accurate historical traffic data are unavailable—as with Department pavement designs—the Remaining Life method that estimates remaining service life from past traffic records is not appropriate; designers should evaluate effective structural capacity through a Condition Survey/Component Analysis or Non‑Destructive Deflection Testing. Preservation projects are also unsuitable when the objective is to increase structural depth to extend service life, because the guidelines prohibit adding structural depth; reconstruction or rehabilitation treatments that allow deeper overlays must be used instead, and preservation cannot provide new travel lanes or capacity beyond limited operational improvements such as weaving or auxiliary lanes up to 3,000 feet where adjacent travel lanes are being preserved for at least 10,000 contiguous feet (excluding bridges). Finally, when a pavement does not meet the condition of being in good condition with significant remaining service life—the criterion for preventive maintenance—the program shifts to rehabilitation or reconstruction: minor rehabilitation provides non‑structural enhancements, major rehabilitation adds structural capacity that both extends service life and improves load‑carrying capability, and full reconstruction replaces the entire structure when the pavement has failed or become functionally obsolete. Information needs

In the drainage assessment (section 2.3 DRAINAGE) the analysis must incorporate condition data such as presence of free or capillary water, intersecting water tables and seepage from cuts; site information on base and subbase permeability, subbase slope, and design and spacing of drains; cost considerations compare the one‑time investment in a proper drainage system with higher maintenance expenses and reduced service life that result from compromise; environmental factors focus on moisture levels and freeze‑thaw effects that accelerate deterioration; stakeholder input is obtained by contacting the local maintenance representative to identify problem drainage areas. For pavement type selection (section 3.1 PAVEMENT TYPE SELECTION FACTORS) Service Life assessment may be influenced by cost data derived from an engineering economic analysis or life‑cycle cost analysis, performance information from similar pavements in the area, and municipal or local government preferences as stakeholder input; site and traffic conditions such as seasonal construction constraints, protection of traffic during construction, need for reduced future maintenance in highly congested locations, grades, curvature and unusual loadings are also considered. When evaluating a Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (section 4.12 LONG‑LIFE CONCRETE PAVEMENT) the analysis requires detailed site information including an on‑site mix plant that needs approximately 4 acres within the project limits and a slip‑form paver that must have a minimum clearance of 3 feet on each side; cost data are needed because any water/cement ratio incentive is calculated at 4 percent of the estimated total LLCP SY paving item cost unless district experience suggests a lower amount; condition data covering subgrade preparation through concrete and joint placement to finishing, curing and sawing must be captured. In providing friction for asphalt wearing courses (section 5.8 PROVIDING FRICTION IN ASPHALT WEARING COURSES) Service Life assessment should incorporate the anticipated initial ADT for new facilities or the current ADT for resurfacing, determine the SRL designation based on that traffic, consider the polishing resistance of the coarse aggregate, and account for the seasonal environmental effect that causes friction values to follow an annual sine‑like cycle with lows in late summer and fall whose amplitude depends on aggregate characteristics and traffic volume. For Superpave paving courses (section 5.9 SUPERPAVE PAVING COURSES) Service Life is based on climatic and traffic condition data, with the traffic load expressed as equivalent single‑axle loads calculated over a 20‑year design life; the resulting 20‑year design life ESALs establish the expected loading rate and drive selection of an appropriate PG‑binder grade, and designers are instructed to limit the number of individual mix designs to no more than three per typical project while major projects should not exceed five. When estimating the service life of an overlay (section 5.12 OVERLAYS) the analysis must incorporate condition data that the existing pavement surface is asphalt, its structural soundness and that less than 2 percent of the pavement requires patching; site information on both surface and subsurface drainage being good or upgraded to good for the project is required; traffic data are expressed through ADT limits referenced in Tables 10.1/10.2; environmental factors include climate, moisture intrusion, freeze‑thaw cycles and overall water‑damage potential; cost considerations compare interlayer material prices against projected life‑cycle savings. In the overall drainage coefficient assessment for rigid pavements (section 8.10 OVERALL DRAINAGE COEFFICIENT, C_d) Service Life requires drainage condition data because the coefficient captures how drainage quality influences pavement life; the required input includes whether the pavement is on unbound aggregate only (C_d = 1.0), or if a new construction or reconstruction uses a treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system and positive drainage (C_d = 1.1); rehabilitation projects where past performance shows substandard drainage and no major improvements are planned use the same C_d = 1.0. For roller‑compacted concrete design on local and collector roads with an ADT ≤ 2000 (section 8.16 ROLLER COMPACTED CONCRETE DESIGN…) Service Life estimation requires data on construction materials, environmental conditions and traffic volumes; the analysis assumes a 20‑year design life with a 1.0 percent Traffic Growth Rate and a 6‑inch unbound granular subbase layer, permits up to 15 percent of slabs to crack at the end of that period, uses one‑way Average Daily Truck Traffic together with the subgrade resilient modulus calculated as 1,500 × CBR, and applies a Terminal Serviceability of 2.5 and a Reliability of 90 percent for collector roads and a Terminal Serviceability of 2.0 and a Reliability of 85 percent for local roads. More generally, evaluating a pavement’s Service Life must incorporate condition data such as observed distress and any layer‑by‑layer component analysis, site information derived from in‑situ stiffness measurements of the subgrade using Falling Weight Deflectometer data, and traffic data that includes historical volumes combined with the current condition to estimate remaining life. For bonded concrete overlays (section 10.8 BONDED PCC / THIN BONDED PCC OVERLAY OF AC PAVEMENT) Service Life evaluation must consider that the existing asphalt is in a good‑to‑fair structural state and, after milling, retains at least a three‑inch thickness; site information on whether the treatment is for an intersection, steep grade or ramp guides the choice of thin versus conventional BCOA, with thin overlays limited to a concrete thickness no less than 2.5 inches and no more than 4 inches while conventional overlays require four inches or more; traffic data are required because the overlay is recommended where daily traffic reaches at least 2,500 ESALs. Pavement management systems rely on regularly collected highway condition data entered into a computer database together with construction, traffic, inventory and maintenance information, and cost‑benefit analysis and budgeting are then used to evaluate performance over the projected service period. In pavement treatment cycles (section 11.2 PAVEMENT TREATMENT CYCLES) Service Life assessment for a roadway segment depends on condition data such as the RMS‑derived surface type code and Functional Class Code that define its treatment network, traffic data are incorporated through thresholds that identify a Recycling Network segment as one having an ADT less than 1,000 and a Truck Percentage less than 10 percent, site information is captured in the RMS network identifiers, and stakeholder input may be reflected when districts manually edit the treatment network indicators based on facility usage or other factors. A complete Pavement History recorded in the Roadway Management System together with initial condition measurements such as rideability obtained from IRI testing after the pavement reopens to traffic is required for Service Life assessment because without these data it is difficult to analyze deterioration rates, evaluate construction effectiveness, or predict how long the pavement will perform adequately. Alternatives

Within the life‑cycle cost analysis framework for a 50‑year pavement life, the feasible options to be compared for asphalt new construction or reconstruction (section 3.4 B) consist of periodic clean and seal combined with crack sealing of 25 % of longitudinal joints and application of seal coat or micro‑surface shoulders at years 5, 10, 20, 30, 40 and 45; full‑depth patching paired with milling, asphalt inlay (1.5 in or 2.0 in) and seal coat or micro‑surface shoulders at years 15 and 25 covering 2 % and 4 % of the pavement area respectively; a more extensive intervention at year 35 that adds a scratch course, an asphalt overlay applied at 60 lb per square yard with 1.5 in or 2.0 in thickness and Type 7 paved shoulders; and finally a partial‑depth asphalt surface patching of 2 % of the pavement area at year 45. For long‑life concrete new construction, reconstruction or unbonded concrete overlay (section 3.4 C) the feasible alternatives to be compared include clean‑and‑seal of 50 % of longitudinal joints including shoulders and 50 % of transverse joints at years 10, 20 and 40, and at year 30 the strategy expands to incorporate concrete patching on 2 % of the pavement area, diamond grinding across 100 % of the pavement width and on 25 % of the shoulders, together with clean‑and‑seal of all longitudinal and transverse joints. For a bonded concrete overlay with a 30‑year pavement life (section 3.4 F) the feasible service‑life options that can be compared include periodic clean‑and‑seal targeting 25 % of longitudinal joints including shoulders and 25 % of transverse joints at years 5, 10 and 15, and again at year 25 covering 25 % of sawed and sealed joints; concrete patching on 5 % of the pavement area at year 10 and on 8 % at year 20; diamond grinding applied to 50 % of the pavement surface at year 10; an asphalt overlay of 4 in or 4.5 in combined with Saw‑and‑Seal of all transverse joints at year 20; and crack sealing of 500 lineal feet per mile at year 25. The feasible alternatives that should be compared when evaluating the service life of an existing Portland cement concrete pavement include joint rehabilitation (type 1 for saw‑cut joints or type 2 for metal‑plate joints), cleaning and sealing of longitudinal, transverse, and pavement/shoulder joints, crack sealing (full‑depth or partial‑depth as specified), dowel bar retrofits to restore load transfer, and cross‑stitching to provide near‑100 % load transfer across longitudinal cracks or joints. The feasible options to compare for a partial‑depth repair consist of PennDOT’s two repair classifications—Type 1 spot repairs covering lengths from 15 inches to 6 feet and Type 2 extended‑length repairs exceeding 6 feet—and the material choices that depend on time before opening to traffic, ambient temperature, cost, size, depth and location: Class AA Cement Concrete Modified for regular curing schedules, accelerated repair material when a faster cure is required, and rapid‑set concrete when quantity makes delivery of other materials economically infeasible. For long‑life concrete pavement (section 4.12) districts should compare LLCP with conventional concrete alternatives such as plain cement concrete and Restricted Performance Specifications, taking into account the requirement for high‑performance dowel bars, a minimum thickness greater than eight inches, full‑depth replacement capability, and any water‑to‑cement ratio or ride‑quality incentives; they must also decide whether to fund the work under Section 501, 506 or 530. For existing pavements (section 5.12) the feasible alternatives that should be compared include rigid concrete overlays—either unbonded or bonded—and flexible asphalt overlays, as each addresses structural deficiencies differently; thin‑asphalt preservation options such as a 6.3 mm Superpave Thin Asphalt Overlay, which is noted to have a longer service life, and a 9.5 mm Fine Grade Superpave Wearing Course that may be used when the required thickness is less than the typical 1 ½ inches; and the use of an asphalt paving interlayer, with system types described as geotextile paving fabric, fiberglass mesh grids, or composite reinforcing grids, each intended to mitigate reflective cracking and extend pavement life. In evaluating service life for rigid pavements (section 8.10) the drainage coefficient provides a practical alternative to compare: a baseline value of C_d = 1.0 applied to pavements built on unbound aggregate only; a higher value of C_d = 1.1 used for new or reconstructed sections that incorporate a treated permeable base (or dense asphalt base) over unbound aggregate together with an under‑drain system and positive drainage; and a return to C_d = 1.0 for rehabilitation projects where existing drainage is judged substandard and no major improvements are planned. When considering shoulders for jointed plain concrete pavement (section 8.15) the feasible alternatives that must be compared for service‑life performance are full‑depth plain cement concrete shoulders equipped with dowelled transverse joints for urban Interstates/Expressways; tied concrete shoulders using undowelled transverse shoulder joints on rural Interstates/Expressways where no work‑zone or incident‑management use is anticipated; full‑depth PCC shoulders with dowelled joints on rural Interstates/Expressways when a high probability exists of using the shoulder for traffic control in work zones or incidents; and a matching‑material approach that requires all layers from subgrade to surface to be identical when full‑depth concrete shoulders are selected, with selection constrained by anticipated incident‑management activity, corridor traffic volume where two‑way ADT is less than 10 000, and the requirement that urban projects always employ tied concrete shoulders. Under the bonded concrete overlay design (section 10.8) the feasible options to compare for service‑life performance are a thin bonded PCC overlay (Thin BCOA) with a nominal concrete thickness between 2.5 inches and 4 inches and a conventional bonded PCC overlay (Conventional BCOA) whose concrete thickness is 4 inches or more; both alternatives aim to add structural capacity and can extend pavement life, the thin option often being used as an alternative to traditional milling‑and‑asphalt overlay and the conventional option selected when larger improvements in structural capacity or longer service life are required. Finally, from a Service Life perspective (section 11.2) the feasible options to compare are the maintenance cycles and the reconstruction cycles defined in the Pavement Management plan; because maintaining roadways according to these cycles will extend pavement life and increase the time before major rehabilitation or reconstruction is necessary, both sets of cycles should be examined when choosing a project type. Decision criteria

In evaluating pavement service life the controlling criteria are tied to the functional context of each element. For drainage (section 2.3) decisions that affect service life should be governed primarily by performance‑related durability, and cost considerations also control the decision because compromised subsurface drainage predicts poorer ride quality, higher maintenance and reduced service life. In pavement type selection (section 3.1) the decision on service life should be driven primarily by economic factors, including the results of an Engineering Economic analysis or Life‑Cycle Cost Analysis that compare construction and future maintenance costs, and by performance information from similar pavements in the area; construction considerations such as seasonal constraints, protecting traffic during construction, and project duration influence schedule and durability, while grades, curvature and unusual loadings are additional technical criteria that can affect the expected service life. For jointed concrete pavements (section 4.3) cost‑effectiveness is the primary driver for service‑life decisions, with joint rehabilitation recommended when it provides the most cost‑effective remedy for early distress, and joint performance issues must be corrected to preserve or extend the pavement’s service life. High Friction Surface Treatment (section 5.6) is governed primarily by safety and durability; a safety evaluation must be completed to determine if an HFST is appropriate, mechanized application is preferred because it yields a more consistent resin‑aggregate distribution, and the treatment is expected to meet an anticipated service life of six to eight years. Providing friction in asphalt wearing courses (section 5.8) requires that the decision about service life be governed primarily by safety and performance considerations; the pavement surface must retain an adequate level of friction throughout its life to ensure safe driving conditions, and durability of those friction properties is a key performance factor. For shoulders on jointed plain concrete pavement (section 8.15) the desired service life should be achieved chiefly through durability and safety because tied concrete shoulders increase stiffness, reduce edge stresses and therefore prolong service life while also raising safety relative to other shoulder types; cost or schedule are not presented as controlling factors, although where the two‑way ADT is less than 10 000 widened slabs with asphalt or RCC shoulders may be considered. In bonded PCC or thin bonded PCC overlays of asphalt pavement (section 10.8) the decision on service life should be driven primarily by performance criteria—specifically the need to add structural capacity, address existing rutting, shoving or slippage, and accommodate traffic volumes of at least 2 500 ESALs per day—and by durability considerations that aim to extend pavement life without resorting to thick asphalt overlays or full reconstruction; cost, schedule, sustainability, accessibility and broader public impact are not cited as controlling factors. The pavement‑management framework (section 11.1) states that the decision about service life should be driven primarily by performance measured through objective system‑condition indicators and by total cost effectiveness over the analysis period, ensuring that treatments provide the desired service levels at the least overall expense while meeting required condition targets. In preservation projects (section 12.2) actions that affect service life are governed first and foremost by safety; projects must not degrade existing safety features such as guide‑rail height, and any operational improvement is permissible only when it can enhance safety, with cost playing a decisive role because safety‑related improvements must be justified at a reasonable cost, while durability alone does not drive the decision. Under the 100 % state‑funded pavement preservation guidelines (section 12.7) decisions about service life should be driven by the need to preserve or improve performance while using cost‑effective treatments that also enhance safety and meet motorist expectations; consequently performance, safety and cost together constitute the primary criteria governing service‑life choices. Analysis methods and tools

The Service Life framework uses an engineering‑economic evaluation known as Life‑Cycle Cost Analysis (LCCA) to compare construction costs with future maintenance expenditures over the designated analysis period. For asphalt new construction or reconstruction that is intended to achieve a fifty‑year service life, the LCCA schedule specifies a clean‑and‑seal treatment at five years applied to twenty‑five percent of longitudinal joints, crack sealing at fifteen years, and full‑depth patching at fifteen years affecting two percent of the pavement area. A separate asphalt maintenance schedule evaluated over a fifty‑year analysis period calls for clean‑and‑seal and crack sealing at ten years on twenty percent of longitudinal joints (615 lineal feet per mile), full‑depth patching at twenty years on one percent of the pavement area, another clean‑and‑seal at thirty years, combined full‑depth patching and SMA overlay at thirty‑five years, and a final clean‑and‑seal at forty‑five years. Long‑life concrete pavements evaluated with LCCA over a fifty‑year analysis period require clean‑and‑seal of fifty percent of longitudinal joints including shoulders at ten years and again at twenty years, concrete patching of two percent of the pavement area together with full‑width diamond grinding at thirty years, and an additional clean‑and‑seal at forty years. For bonded concrete overlays, a thirty‑year LCCA framework schedules maintenance actions at five, ten, fifteen, twenty and twenty‑five years, each with defined percentages of longitudinal joints (including shoulders) to be clean‑and‑sealed or concrete patching extents as prescribed for cost‑based comparison. Concrete pavement rehabilitation combined with an asphalt overlay follows a thirty‑year LCCA that incorporates a mill wearing‑course asphalt inlay at ten years (1.5 inches or 2.0 inches saw & seal), clean‑and‑seal operations at fifteen and twenty‑five years covering twenty‑five percent of sawed and sealed joints, concrete patching with overlay at twenty years affecting two percent of the pavement area, a scratch course, and an asphalt overlay applied at sixty pounds per square yard. The Long‑Life Concrete Pavement (LLCP) guidelines augment LCCA with a decision‑tree tool to assist districts in selecting items of work, and they require evaluation of an Overall Lot Pay Factor and concrete pavement ride quality before incentive payments are made.

In the rigid‑pavement service‑life context, condition is also expressed through a drainage coefficient C_d; a value of 1.0 corresponds to a fair rating for drainage quality as observed in the AASHO Road Test. Service Life evaluation further relies on three basic methods: a Condition Survey or Component Analysis that considers pavement distress and layer analysis; Non‑Destructive Deflection Testing that uses Falling Weight Deflectometer data to assess in‑situ subgrade and pavement stiffness; and a Remaining Life approach that combines historic traffic data with the current condition to estimate remaining service life, although this latter method is not recommended for Department designs because of insufficient historical traffic records. In addition, pavement management under Service Life employs life‑cycle analysis for treatments and a risk‑based asset management plan that uses objective measures of system condition and performance together with an analysis program to determine the most cost‑effective strategies. Service‑life predictions are built on a series of explicit assumptions that introduce corresponding uncertainties. For pavement type selection, it is assumed that reference pavements exhibit a good correlation between conditions and service requirements of the design under study; uncertainty arises when performance records come from pavements that were subjected to much lighter loadings or constructed with materials or design features no longer common, which leads to the recommendation that reference pavements be re‑analyzed periodically. In the case of concrete partial‑depth repairs, it is assumed that the repair depth does not exceed half the slab thickness and that representative cores are taken to confirm this limit, and it is further presumed that the repair is placed correctly with a durable patch material; uncertainty stems from the need to match the chosen concrete mix to the time available before opening to traffic, expected ambient temperature, cost, size, depth and location of each repair, and from expansion‑contraction cycles during cool nights and hot days that can cause compression or point‑loading failures if joint spacing is inadequate. The Superpave procedure assumes that equivalent single‑axle loads are computed for a 20‑year design life and that those 20‑year ESAL values are applied to every project even when the anticipated service life is shorter, which fixes the loading rate used in mixture design; uncertainty is introduced because actual traffic growth or climate conditions may differ from this assumption, and for fiber‑modified courses the potential benefit of delaying crack initiation depends heavily on how uniformly aramid fibers are distributed. Overlay designs assume that the existing surface meets soundness criteria—specifically that the pavement is structurally sound and requires less than 2 % patching—and that both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded; they also require a minimum of 1½ inches of remaining asphalt layer when milling to preserve bond integrity. Uncertainty for overlays includes possible debonding of the existing overlay, variations in climate and traffic conditions, moisture intrusion, ADT limits, and compliance with thickness and weather constraints set out in the design tables. For rigid pavements, service‑life calculations assume that drainage quality can be represented by a single overall drainage coefficient C_d; C_d is taken as 1.0 when the pavement rests on unbound aggregate only or when rehabilitation projects are expected to retain substandard drainage with no improvements planned, and it is increased to 1.1 for new or reconstructed sections that incorporate a treated permeable or dense‑asphalt base over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system providing positive drainage. Uncertainty therefore depends on how accurately the selected C_d reflects actual field drainage conditions and whether planned drainage improvements are fully realized. Roller‑compacted concrete designs assume a 20‑year design horizon, a 1.0 % annual traffic growth rate, a 6‑inch unbound granular subbase, and standard PennDOT inputs; they also permit up to 15 % of slabs to crack by the end of that period and rely on terminal serviceability values of 2.5 for collector roads and 2.0 for local roads together with reliability targets of 90 % and 85 % respectively. Uncertainty in these predictions is inherently sensitive to the quality of construction materials, prevailing environmental conditions, and actual traffic volumes. Finally, the Remaining Life approach assumes that past traffic volumes are known and that the current condition captured by distress or layer analysis, or by non‑destructive deflection testing, correctly reflects the pavement’s structural capacity; uncertainty arises because the method depends on accurate historical traffic data—often lacking for Department designs—and on the quality of the distress survey, component analysis, or Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements used to characterize existing stiffness, making the service‑life result less reliable when those inputs are uncertain or unavailable. Timing and implementation

In the Long‑Life Asphalt new construction or reconstruction strategy that assumes a fifty‑year analysis period, a clean‑and‑seal operation together with sealing of about twenty percent of longitudinal joints and treatment of six hundred fifteen lineal feet per mile is required at year ten; the same clean‑and‑seal recurs at years thirty and forty‑five, while full‑depth patching of roughly one percent of the pavement area combined with milling and placement of an SMA wearing course 1.5 in or 2.0 in thick plus tack coat on the overlay and shoulders is prescribed at year twenty; a second full‑depth patching and SMA overlay occurs at year thirty‑five, and the analysis terminates at year fifty. For the Asphalt New Construction or Reconstruction (including construction on fractured concrete pavement) scenario with a fifty‑year pavement life, the first trigger occurs at five years when a clean‑and‑seal plus crack sealing of twenty‑five percent of longitudinal joints and seal coat of five hundred lineal feet per mile on shoulders is performed; this same set of tasks repeats at ten, twenty, thirty, forty and forty‑five years. At fifteen years a full‑depth patch covering two percent of the pavement area together with a mill wearing course, an asphalt inlay of 1.5 in or 2.0 in, and seal coat or micro‑surface shoulders is required; similar full‑depth patching with higher percentages is specified at twenty‑five, thirty‑five and forty‑five years, accompanied by additional treatments such as scratch course, asphalt overlay, guide‑rail adjustment and partial‑depth surface patching. For the Long‑Life Concrete new construction, reconstruction or unbonded concrete overlay strategy that assumes a fifty‑year pavement life, clean‑and‑seal of fifty percent of longitudinal joints including shoulders is triggered at years ten and twenty, and again at year forty; at thirty years concrete patching of two percent of the pavement area, diamond grinding of one hundred percent of the pavement width plus twenty‑five percent of the shoulders, and clean‑and‑seal of all longitudinal and transverse joints are required. In the composite Asphalt Concrete over Portland Cement Concrete (AC/PCC) case with a fifty‑year analysis period, the first trigger at ten years is a clean‑and‑seal of fifty percent of longitudinal joints and fifty percent of transverse joints together with crack seal of six hundred fifteen lineal feet per mile and maintenance and protection of traffic user delay; the same clean‑and‑seal pattern recurs at thirty and forty‑five years, while at twenty years milling of an asphalt wearing course and shoulders plus asphalt overlay, mainline and shoulder saw and seal, and treatment of all transverse joints are required; a full‑depth patching of one percent of the pavement area together with mill asphalt wearing course and shoulders, asphalt overlay, mainline and shoulder saw and seal, and treatment of all transverse joints is prescribed at thirty‑five years, and the analysis concludes at fifty years. For a bonded concrete overlay evaluated over a thirty‑year service life, clean‑and‑seal of twenty‑five percent of longitudinal joints including shoulders and twenty‑five percent of transverse joints is triggered at five years; at ten years concrete patching of five percent of the pavement area, diamond grinding of fifty percent of the pavement area, and repeat of the twenty‑five percent clean‑and‑seal for both joint directions are required; a repeat of the five‑year clean‑and‑seal occurs at fifteen years; at twenty years concrete patching of eight percent of the pavement area, clean‑and‑seal of all longitudinal joints including shoulders and all transverse joints, a scratch course, an asphalt overlay applied at sixty pounds per square yard with four‑inch or 4.5‑inch saw and seal of all transverse joints, and adjustments to guide rail and drainage structures are prescribed; at twenty‑five years clean‑and‑seal of twenty‑five percent of sawed and sealed joints together with crack sealing of five hundred lineal feet per mile and seal coat or micro surface treatment of shoulders is scheduled. In the concrete pavement rehabilitation and asphalt overlay alternative with a thirty‑year analysis period, a mill wearing course asphalt inlay of 1.5 inches or 2.0 inches with saw & seal and sealing of all transverse joints triggers at ten years; at fifteen years a clean & seal covering twenty‑five percent of sawed & sealed joints together with crack seal of five hundred lineal feet per mile is required; at twenty years concrete patching of two percent of the pavement area, a scratch course, an asphalt overlay of 1.5 inches or 2.0 inches with saw & seal and sealing of all transverse joints for Type 7 paved shoulders, plus any necessary guide‑rail and drainage adjustments, must be performed; at twenty‑five years another clean & seal involving twenty‑five percent of longitudinal and transverse joints and crack seal of five hundred lineal feet per mile is scheduled. In concrete pavement partial‑depth repair (section 4.4), action is triggered when the repair depth needed exceeds half the slab thickness, at which point full‑depth concrete pavement patches must be used; likewise, if the length of spalling or cracking surpasses six feet the work shifts from a Type 1 spot repair to a Type 2 extended‑length repair. When minor transverse‑joint spalling is identified as being caused by poor joint performance, slab stabilization and dowel‑bar retrofit should be considered, and corner spalls that extend beyond mid‑slab depth require full‑depth corner repairs. During periods of large temperature swings partial‑depth patches placed when slabs are contracted must provide adequate space to avoid compression failure. For Superpave paving courses (section 5.9), action for Service Life is triggered when the design ESALs are calculated using a twenty‑year design life and the resulting value falls into one of five defined ESAL ranges; if the estimated ESAL range is greater than ten million, the district should specify a polymer modified PG 64E‑22 binder for wearing courses to avoid rutting, whereas if the range is 0.3 to < 3.0 million, the district may lower the specified range to < 0.3 million when cracking is an issue and other conditions apply; when mix design quantities are less than 250 tons or less than 1 000 square yards at 1½ inches, the ESAL range may be changed up or down by one range, but any larger changes require PDAU permission. In pavement design analysis (section 6.1), reconstruction is triggered when an existing pavement has either failed or become functionally obsolete, and for preservation treatments a project must be reviewed by the District PME/PM if more than fifteen months elapse after the Pavement Type Selection approval date. Roller‑compacted concrete design for local and collector roads (section 8.16) triggers action when the RCC pavement reaches its designed twenty‑year horizon; at that point the design permits up to fifteen percent of slabs to be cracked and requires that terminal serviceability not fall below 2.5 for collector roads (reliability target 90 %) or below 2.0 for local roads (reliability target 85 %). A bonded PCC or thin bonded PCC overlay (section 10.8) is triggered when an existing asphalt pavement in good‑to‑fair condition shows rutting, shoving or slippage and anticipated traffic reaches at least 2 500 ESALs per day; the treatment also requires that after milling a minimum recommended asphalt material thickness of three inches remain. For thin bonded overlays the concrete must be no less than 2.5 inches and no more than 4 inches thick, while conventional overlays require a concrete thickness of four inches or more, and any design with an overlay thickness less than or equal to 6.5 inches must follow the BCOA‑ME methodology. Pavement treatment cycles (section 11.2) trigger maintenance when a pavement segment is identified as “out of cycle,” meaning it has not received the prescribed treatment within the defined maintenance timeframe; a change in RMS surface‑type classification or Functional Class Code updates the network indicator, prompting re‑evaluation of the appropriate treatment, and the same cycle deadlines apply to routes in the Recycling Network (ADT < 1000 and Truck Percentage < 10 %); failure to treat these segments within their cycles signals that corrective action is needed. State‑funded pavement preservation guidelines (section 12.7) trigger action when a pavement’s condition moves out of the “good” range or shows signs of serious damage, prompting preservation or maintenance treatments; preventive maintenance should be applied while pavements are still in good condition and have significant remaining service life, and routine maintenance consists of work that is planned and performed on a routine basis to maintain and preserve the condition of the highway system or to respond to specific conditions and events that restore an adequate level of service. For the asphalt new‑construction or reconstruction alternative (50‑year pavement life) the sequence of maintenance actions includes clean and seal together with crack sealing of 25 % of longitudinal joints and application of seal coat or micro‑surface to 500 lineal feet per mile at years 5, 10, 20, 30, 40 and 45; full‑depth patching of 2 % of the pavement area plus a mill wearing course and an asphalt inlay of 1.5 inches or 2.0 inches at year 15; a larger 4 % full‑depth patch with milling at year 25; at year 35 a scratch course, 60 pounds per square yard asphalt overlay of 1.5 inches or 2.0 inches plus Type 7 paved shoulders and possible guide‑rail adjustments; and finally at year 45 a partial‑depth asphalt surface patch covering 2 % of the area. These entries define both material quantities and timing required to sustain the intended service life. For the new composite asphalt concrete over Portland cement concrete alternative (50‑year pavement life) maintenance resources are scheduled as follows: at year 10 clean and seal of 50 % of longitudinal joints and 50 % of transverse joints together with crack sealing of 615 lineal feet per mile; at year 20 a mill of the asphalt wearing course and shoulders together with an asphalt overlay on mainline and shoulders followed by saw‑and‑seal of all transverse joints; at year 30 the same clean‑and‑seal and crack‑seal actions as in year 10 are repeated; at year 35 full‑depth patching of 1 % of the pavement area is performed together with a mill of the asphalt wearing course and shoulders, an overlay on mainline and shoulders and saw‑and‑seal of all transverse joints; and at year 45 clean‑and‑seal of 50 % of longitudinal and transverse joints and crack sealing of 615 lineal feet per mile are again applied, with the analysis concluding at year 50. For the bonded concrete overlay alternative (30‑year service life) the schedule requires cleaning and sealing on 25 % of longitudinal joints including shoulders and on 25 % of transverse joints at year 5; at year 10 concrete patching of 5 % of the surface, diamond grinding of 50 % of the area, followed by cleaning and sealing of 25 % of both longitudinal and transverse joints; at year 15 another clean‑and‑seal cycle repeats the same joint percentages; at year 20 concrete patching on 8 % of the area, cleaning and sealing of all longitudinal and transverse joints, a scratch course, an asphalt overlay applied at 60 lb/yd² together with a 4‑inch (or 4.5‑inch) saw‑and‑seal treatment and any needed guide‑rail or drainage adjustments; and finally at year 25 clean‑and‑seal on 25 % of sawed and sealed joints and crack sealing of about 500 ft per mile. The resources implied include concrete patching mix, grinding equipment, sealant, asphalt overlay material, saw‑and‑seal equipment and guide‑rail adjustment capability. For the concrete pavement rehabilitation with an asphalt overlay alternative (30‑year service life) maintenance actions are scheduled at year 10 a mill wearing course and asphalt inlay of 1.5 inches or 2.0 inches together with saw & seal of all transverse joints and seal coat or micro surface on shoulders; at year 15 clean & seal treats 25 % of sawed & sealed joints, applies crack seal to 500 lineal feet per mile and adds seal coat or micro surface on shoulders; at year 20 concrete patching covering 2 % of the pavement area is performed along with a scratch course of 60 lb/yd², an asphalt overlay of 1.5 inches or 2.0 inches plus saw & seal of all transverse joints and adjustment of guide rail and drainage structures if necessary; and at year 25 another clean & seal treats 25 % of longitudinal and transverse joints, applies crack seal to 500 lineal feet per mile and provides seal coat or micro surface on shoulders. Implementation of a long‑life concrete pavement requires high‑performance dowel bars, a central mix plant and a slip‑form paver; roughly 4 acres of site must be allocated for the on‑site plant and at least 3 feet of clearance on each side of the paver must be ensured. The work is carried out as full‑depth replacement with control from subgrade preparation through concrete placement, joint finishing, curing and sawing. The selected option also demands systematic data acquisition, storage and analysis resources. Regular collection of highway condition data must be supported, a computer database must sort and store the condition information together with construction, traffic, inventory and maintenance records, and an analysis program must operate on an ongoing basis to identify the most cost‑effective maintenance and preservation strategies; these activities are performed continuously with data gathered at regular intervals to feed periodic analyses over the analysis period. Finally, International Roughness Index testing resources consist of the Bureau of Maintenance and Operations Asset Management Division Pavement Testing and Asset Management Section staff together with a videologging contractor who collect and analyze IRI data. The schedule requires that pavement history for Department‑force projects be entered within four weeks of reopening to traffic and for contractor projects within six weeks; thereafter the Pavement Testing and Asset Management team runs weekly searches and must complete IRI testing on the new surface within three months of the data entry. Outcomes and follow-up

Success for Service Life is defined by a series of condition‑specific outcomes that are confirmed through the measurements prescribed in each applicable section. Within the drainage provisions of section 2.3, success is realized when drainage problems are handled effectively, which is demonstrated by the absence or reduction of moisture‑related distresses such as transverse cracks in concrete, rutting that reflects a softened subgrade, surface roughness that degrades ride quality, and by fewer maintenance interventions during the pavement’s lifespan. In the concrete pavement patching context of section 4.2, success is realized when a repaired concrete pavement continues to function for more than 20 years before any programmed replacement is required; this outcome is measured by selecting high‑performance dowel bars for patches expected to meet that duration and by confirming that the patch meets all prescribed design and material specifications so that the structure can remain in service without premature failure. For partial‑depth repairs addressed in section 4.4, success means that the repair actually extends the pavement’s usable period and remains functional for the remaining life of the slab when installed with a durable patch material, respects the depth limit of no greater than half the slab thickness, and avoids compression or point‑loading failures; these outcomes are measured by confirming that the repair depth complies with the half‑thickness rule, that the appropriate joint filler—rigid polystyrene board for longitudinal joints or premolded expansion joint material for transverse joints—is installed to keep the patch from contacting adjacent slabs, and that adequate space is provided between the patch and neighboring slabs during contracted conditions so that no compression failure occurs. Under the Long‑Life Concrete Pavement program of section 4.12, success is achieved when the pavement delivers a longer service life than conventional concrete pavement by using high‑quality materials, high‑performance dowel bars and full‑depth construction controlled from subgrade preparation through curing; this success is measured by meeting the Lot acceptance criteria set out in Publication 408 Sections 506 and 530 and by completing the required Evaluation of Concrete Pavement Ride Quality as described in Section 507. For high‑friction surface treatments covered in section 5.6, success is achieved when the treatment remains effective for its anticipated service life of 6 to 8 years, which is demonstrated by maintaining exceptional skid‑resistance properties indicated by Skid Numbers of 70 and above; periodic friction testing that records these skid numbers provides the measurement needed to verify that the target has been met. In the provision of friction in asphalt wearing courses described in section 5.8, success is achieved when the asphalt wearing course maintains an adequate level of friction and sufficient surface texture for the entire design period, thereby preventing water pressure buildup at the tire‑pavement interface under the posted speed limit; this condition shall be verified by periodic friction testing using the AASHTO T 242 test method and by confirming that the coarse aggregate employed meets the Skid Resistance Level designation appropriate to the anticipated average daily traffic as specified in the design documents. For shoulders on jointed plain concrete pavement in section 8.15, success is achieved when tied concrete shoulders provide greater stiffness that lowers deflection magnitudes, thereby extending the pavement’s functional period; this is measured by a delayed first maintenance cycle relative to conventional concrete pavements and by tracking reductions in differential heaving, settlement, horizontal separation, and overall maintenance frequency through periodic inspections that record deflection levels, occurrences of heaving or settlement, and timing of any maintenance actions. In the roller‑compacted concrete designs for local and collector roads with an ADT ≤ 2 000 addressed in section 8.16, success is achieved when the pavement performs for the intended 20‑year design life while keeping slab cracking at or below 15 % and meeting the prescribed terminal serviceability and reliability targets—2.5 with 90 % reliability for collector roads and 2.0 with 85 % reliability for local roads—under a 1.0 % annual traffic growth assumption and the specified 6‑inch granular subbase; these outcomes are measured by tracking the percentage of cracked slabs at the end of the design period and verifying that the serviceability index and reliability criteria are satisfied for each road class. Success is also defined through broader asset‑management criteria outlined in sections 11.1, 11.2 and 11.4: the pavement must continue to meet the required service level throughout its intended design period, as demonstrated by the Pavement Asset Management System’s documented performance measures that remain within established thresholds; this is measured through regular collection of highway condition data, storage of that data together with traffic, construction, inventory and maintenance information in a database, and application of an analysis program that produces life‑cycle analyses, statistical reports and forecasts to verify that the pavement’s condition stays within the defined performance criteria. Additionally, each pavement segment must receive its prescribed treatments within the MECE‑defined cycles, because following those cycles extends pavement life and delays major rehabilitation or reconstruction; success is measured by using RMS data to confirm that treatment dates fall inside the cycle timeframes and by quantifying the reduction in the backlog of “out of cycle” roadways. The initial ride quality of new pavement surfaces must be captured promptly after construction so that future performance can be tracked; districts are required to enter Pavement History data for force projects within 4 weeks of reopening and for contractor projects within 6 weeks, and Pavement Testing and Asset Management staff must complete IRI testing on those new surfaces within 3 months of the entry, with success measured by adherence to these data‑entry and testing windows and by the availability of up‑to‑date IRI measurements for all newly treated pavements. Finally, preservation treatments should actually restore the function of the existing pavement system and extend its service life, which should be reflected in smoother, longer‑lasting pavements that deliver improved safety and mobility while reducing congestion and user delays; these outcomes are measured by tracking pavement performance indicators such as condition ratings, delay statistics, and safety/mobility improvements to confirm that the intended extensions of service life have been achieved. Service‑life results are best monitored by keeping systematic records of how existing pavements have performed under local traffic and environmental conditions; those performance records become the primary data for predicting future behavior, and they must be compiled as supporting documentation that accompanies Form D‑4332 when a pavement type is selected (section 3.1 PAVEMENT TYPE SELECTION FACTORS). The agency should periodically re‑analyze reference pavements to verify that past service‑life observations remain applicable to new designs, and any discrepancies guide adjustments in the selection criteria for subsequent projects (section 3.1). Results that reflect the expected service life are monitored by regularly collecting highway condition data, storing it in a computer database together with construction, traffic and maintenance information, and then using an analysis program to track treatment performance through life‑cycle analysis; the outcomes are documented as objective performance measures, statistical analyses and formal reports that record how parameters affecting performance have changed over time (section 11.1 INTRODUCTION). These documented results are fed back into the pavement management system to refine cost‑effective maintenance and rehabilitation strategies, adjust resource allocation and improve future decision making about treatments (section 11.1). Service‑life results should also be tracked through systematic International Roughness Index measurements, with IRI collected each year for the entire National Highway System and on a two‑year cycle for non‑NHS routes; any newly resurfaced or reconstructed segment is re‑tested within three months after its Pavement History record is entered (section 11.4 INTERNATIONAL ROUGHNESS INDEX TESTING FOR NEW PAVEMENT SURFACES). Pavement History data should be entered promptly—within four weeks for Department force projects and within six weeks for contractor projects—to link IRI values to the exact construction date and treatment, and weekly programs that search Pavement History data then trigger testing, providing timely condition information that can be analyzed for trends, anomalies or deterioration rates, thereby supporting assessments of construction effectiveness and enabling predictions of future pavement service life (section 11.4).
