Roller-Compacted Concrete

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Roller-Compacted Concrete is a topic under Rigid Pavement.

Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-29

Roller‑compacted concrete is a very dry, stiff concrete mix placed without forms, reinforcement, or dowel bars to provide a high‑strength, durable pavement on low‑volume, lower‑speed roadways where conventional cast‑in‑place concrete or asphalt would be uneconomical[1][2]. It is laid as a uniformly thick slab—typically 4 to 18 inches—using a low water‑cement ratio (≈0.30–0.40) that achieves compressive strengths of at least 3,000 psi after 72 hours and often 4,000–10,000 psi, with immediate compaction behind the paver to reach 85–90 % of maximum wet density for each lift and a final density exceeding 96 %[1][2]. The resulting joint‑free (or sparsely jointed) layer can serve as either the final wearing surface or as a base beneath an asphalt overlay, delivering a dense, high‑strength pavement capable of withstanding repetitive heavy loads for an assumed 20‑year service life on routes carrying ≤2,000 vehicles per day and posted speeds of 55 mph or less[1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Roller‑compacted concrete is specified where a high‑strength, durable pavement is required but conventional cast‑in‑place concrete or asphalt would be uneconomical.[1] It addresses the need for a surface that can resist repetitive heavy loads on low‑volume routes, typically those with an average daily traffic of 2 000 vehicles or fewer and speed limits of 55 mph or less.[1] The design also accommodates right‑hand shoulders on widened concrete slabs, providing a joint‑free shoulder of appropriate width that helps limit slab deflection on low‑volume rural interstates where an incident‑management lane is not needed.[1] Across the guides, the primary structural demand is for a uniformly thick, highly dense base capable of reliably supporting traffic loads beneath an asphalt wearing surface while delivering a durable, joint‑free concrete layer.[1][2] To meet this demand the mix is placed without forms, reinforcement, or dowel bars, using a low water‑cement ratio that yields compressive strengths of several thousand psi; the guides require a minimum compressive strength of no less than 3 000 psi after at least 72 hours of curing.[1][2] Rapid, continuous placement is achieved by placing the stiff, low‑slump concrete within a temperature window of 50 °F to 90 °F and compacting it immediately behind the paver.[2] The required compaction levels are at least 85 % of the maximum wet density for lifts under six inches, or 90 % for thicker lifts, with a final rolling that raises density above 96 % of the maximum wet density for all thicknesses.[2] Subgrade and granular subbase support are used to further control cracking and eliminate the need for dowelled joints or supported edges, providing the stiffness needed for load transfer.[1][2] The design life is typically set at 20 years with modest traffic growth, targeting terminal serviceability values between 2.0 and 2.5 and a reliability of 85–90 %, while permitting up to 15 % slab cracking at the end of design life.[1] Together these requirements satisfy the performance need for a durable, high‑strength pavement that can be placed quickly, maintain structural integrity under repetitive heavy loads on low‑volume roads, and serve either as a final wearing course or as a base beneath asphalt.[1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–167); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 214), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 370–374)

RCC pavement is intended for low‑volume roadways, typically carrying no more than 2,000 vehicles per day and posted for speeds up to 55 mph, providing the functional role of a smooth, continuous riding surface. The structural design achieves required capacity by selecting a compressive strength between about 4,000 psi and 10,000 psi and by placing a slab whose thickness may range from four to eighteen inches, with minimum depth reduced by one inch when a supported edge is present. In addition, the placed material must reach more than 96 % of the maximum wet density after final rolling and develop at least 3,000 psi compressive strength, ensuring continuity of cross‑section and meeting strict density and strength targets. Durability is addressed through mix composition that employs a low water‑cement ratio and a dense aggregate gradation, while placement temperature is limited to the plastic range of 50 °F to 90 °F and work is prohibited when air temperature falls below 40 °F or during hard rain. After compaction the surface must be kept continuously moist for a minimum of 72 hours, supporting the anticipated service life of roughly twenty years even with a 1 % annual traffic growth rate. Safety requirements call for a textured finish produced by diamond grinding whenever the exposed pavement surface serves speeds of 35 mph or greater, and surface irregularities are limited to within one‑quarter inch to maintain ride quality. Control joints are spaced in proportion to slab thickness to limit cracking, and the design tolerates up to fifteen percent of slabs developing cracks at the end of service while still achieving the prescribed terminal serviceability index of 2.5 for collector roads and 2.0 for local roads. Reliability targets of ninety percent for collector routes and eighty‑five percent for local routes further define the acceptable level of serviceability throughout the design period. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

Roller‑compacted concrete is appropriate as a concrete pavement on low‑traffic roadways where the average daily traffic does not exceed 2,000 vehicles and the speed limit is 55 mph or less, and it can also be used as a concrete shoulder placed directly adjacent to reinforced or plain cement‑concrete pavements when tied shoulders are not required; in both cases it may serve as a base course beneath an asphalt wearing surface or other surface treatment provided the subgrade and subbase have been properly prepared. The guides further recognize RCC for temporary pavement functions such as median crossovers or widening areas employed during construction, either as the final wearing layer or as the underlying base, and they note that a widened concrete slab limited to a 14‑foot width can be paired with an RCC right shoulder on low‑volume rural Interstates and Expressways carrying two‑way traffic volumes under 10,000 vehicles per day where the need for an incident‑management lane is minimal, using the load‑transfer coefficient specified for widened lanes with asphalt or RCC shoulders. The material itself is a very dry concrete mix with a water‑cement ratio typically between 0.30 and 0.40, described as a stiff, low‑slump mix that can be placed with conventional or high‑density asphalt paving equipment such as an asphalt paver and then compacted with rollers to achieve the required thickness and density; this process requires no forms, reinforcing, or dowel bars, and control joints are provided only where needed. Design follows the same structural principles as jointed plain concrete pavement but assumes a 20‑year service life, a 1 % annual traffic growth rate, and incorporates a six‑inch unbound granular subbase, with slab thickness derived from the subgrade resilient modulus using one‑way average daily truck traffic; for collector roads the target terminal serviceability index is 2.5 with 90 % reliability, while for local roads it is 2.0 with 85 % reliability. All of these applications are governed by project plans and specifications such as Section 502.3 for RCC shoulders, ensuring that the prepared subgrade and subbase meet the required conditions before placement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–167); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 214), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 564–565)

Roller‑compacted concrete is best suited to low‑volume, lower‑speed facilities. When the average daily traffic rises above roughly two thousand vehicles per day or when the posted speed limit exceeds fifty‑five miles per hour, the rougher surface texture of RCC and its limited compatibility with International Roughness Index (IRI) based ride‑quality assessments make conventional concrete or asphalt pavements a more appropriate choice. Likewise, RCC shoulders are only justified on low‑volume rural interstates or expressways where the two‑way average daily traffic stays below ten thousand vehicles and there is little demand for an incident‑management lane; if traffic volumes exceed that level, a dedicated incident lane is required, or the slab width needed to accommodate the shoulder would have to be greater than fourteen feet, a conventional design with twelve‑foot lanes and a tied concrete shoulder should be used instead. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 8 (p. 165)

Design inputs and requirements

The design of an RCC pavement begins with site characterization that identifies the facility as a shoulder, temporary crossing, or a local/collector road. Such facilities must carry an average daily traffic not exceeding 2,000 vehicles and have a posted speed limit of 55 mph or less; when the surface will be exposed to traffic at speeds of 35 mph or higher, the slab is required to receive a diamond‑ground finish. Traffic inputs focus on low‑to‑moderate volumes and include the one‑way average daily truck traffic (ADTT) together with an assumed annual growth rate of 1 percent. Material specifications call for a very dry concrete mix consisting of cement, a dense aggregate gradation whose maximum size is less than 1 inch, water at a water‑cement ratio around 0.40, optional air entrainment and limited use of admixtures. The mix is designed to achieve a compressive strength between 4,000 and 10,000 psi, and the resulting slab thickness typically ranges from 4 to 18 inches. Environmental considerations prescribe a design service life of about 20 years; the actual service life is influenced by the selected construction materials, climate conditions, and traffic volumes, and surface texturing may be required when speed limits exceed 35 mph. Geotechnical inputs require a prepared subgrade and a 6‑inch unbound granular subbase capable of supporting RCC compaction, with the subgrade resilient modulus calculated as 1,500 times the CBR value. Existing‑condition data must confirm whether the pavement will be placed on new, rehabilitated or existing surfaces and whether a supported edge is present, since these factors affect the minimum slab depth required to meet terminal serviceability of 2.5 and a reliability target of 90 %. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 8 (pp. 166–167)

The design and construction of roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavement are governed primarily by PennDOT Publication 408, especially Sections 323, 502, and 658 together with the joint‑spacing rules in Publication 72M RC‑27M, and they also require compliance with AASHTO M 241 for continuous mixing at the plant. [1][2] RCC pavement may be used only on roadways or shoulders where the average daily traffic does not exceed 2,000 vehicles and the posted speed limit is 55 mph or less. [1] When an exposed surface is required on a roadway with a speed limit of 35 mph or higher, the slab must be diamond‑ground before it is opened to traffic. [1] For widened concrete slabs that serve as shoulders, the slab width may not exceed fourteen feet and such configurations are limited to low‑volume rural Interstates or Expressways with a combined two‑way ADT of less than ten thousand vehicles. [1] The design assumes a twenty‑year service life, a 1 % annual traffic growth rate, and a six‑inch unbound granular subbase, and it limits end‑of‑design‑life cracking to fifteen percent of the slab area. [1] Terminal serviceability targets are set at a value of 2.5 with ninety‑percent reliability for collector roads and 2.0 with eighty‑five percent reliability for local roads. [1] Slab thickness may range from four inches to eighteen inches, with each lift required to be no thinner than four inches and no thicker than nine inches; when a supported edge is provided the minimum slab thickness may be reduced by one inch, but no alternative method for lowering depth is permitted. [1][2] The compressive strength of the RCC mix must fall between 4,000 and 10,000 psi, and after moist curing for at least seventy‑two hours the concrete shall have attained a minimum strength of three thousand psi. [1][2] Control joints are spaced at fifteen to twenty feet for sections thinner than eight inches, or at a distance equal to the slab thickness multiplied by 2.5 ft for thicker sections, and joint treatment follows the provisions of Section 502.3. [1][2] A quality‑control plan must include a minimum fifty‑foot test section and the mix design must be produced at a plant that meets the requirements of AASHTO M 241. [2] Placement is temperature‑controlled; work must cease when ambient air temperature falls to forty degrees Fahrenheit, and the plastic concrete mixture must be maintained between fifty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit, with cooling required if it exceeds ninety degrees. [2] Initial compaction before rolling must achieve at least eighty‑five to ninety percent of the maximum wet density, and final rolling must increase density to more than ninety‑six percent of the maximum wet density. [2] Equipment specifications call for pavers capable of delivering the required initial compaction and primary rollers with a minimum weight of eight tons. [2] The pavement must be placed on a prepared subgrade and subbase using a low‑slump concrete mix, and it must satisfy the project‑specified thickness, density, and surface texture. [2] RCC shoulders are to be constructed exactly as shown on the project plans and in accordance with Section 502.3, which governs material selection, joint treatment, and overall performance for the shoulder element. [2] For designs that incorporate widened lanes with asphalt or RCC shoulders, the load‑transfer coefficient (J) prescribed in Table 8.2 must be used. [1] Local and collector roads with ADT of two thousand or fewer are required to use the American Concrete Pavement Association’s StreetPave12 software employing standard PennDOT values for Portland cement concrete. Design methods and criteria

Design of RCC pavement uses the same structural analysis as conventional concrete but incorporates specific procedures and empirical relationships [1]. Thickness is obtained by applying PennDOT’s concrete‑pavement design methodology (Chapter 8, Section 8.15) which evaluates traffic loads against a target compressive strength ranging from 4,000 to 10,000 psi [1]. The mix design calls for a very dry concrete with a water‑to‑cement ratio as low as 0.30 and typically about 0.40, a dense aggregate gradation limited to particles smaller than one inch, and only limited use of admixtures or air entrainment [1]. Production must be from a plant that meets AASHTO M 241 specifications, ensuring the low‑slump mixture behaves like damp gravel [2][1]. Placement is performed in lifts of at least four inches using pavers equipped with compaction devices that achieve a minimum of eighty‑five percent of the maximum wet density for thin lifts and ninety percent for thicker lifts before roller compaction, while final rolling must reach greater than ninety‑six percent of the maximum wet density [2]. In‑place field density is measured by ASTM C1040 Test Method A‑Direct Transmission and expressed as a percentage using X = (ρw/ρt) × 100, where ρw is the recorded wet density and ρt is the computed unit weight from the mix design; at least five tests are required behind the paver and five after final compaction for each five hundred cubic yards placed [2]. A minimum fifty‑foot test section is constructed before full production to verify mix consistency, equipment performance, roller selection and curing procedures [2]. After achieving the required densities, the surface is kept moist for a minimum of seventy‑two hours using water curing, polyethylene sheeting or curing compounds before opening to traffic [2]. Control joints are placed according to thickness: pavements thinner than eight inches receive joints every fifteen to twenty feet, while thicker sections have joint spacing equal to 2.5 times the pavement thickness in feet [1]. When an RCC shoulder is incorporated into a widened concrete slab, the slab width is limited to fourteen feet and longitudinal striping is provided at twelve‑foot intervals for low‑volume rural interstates with average daily traffic below ten thousand vehicles [1]. The interaction between slab and shoulder is governed by the load‑transfer coefficient J taken from Table 8.2 for “widened lane with asphalt or RCC shoulders,” which serves as the primary empirical relationship within the conventional rigid‑pavement framework [1]. The thickness of the RCC layer can also be generated with the ACPA StreetPave12 software, which models the subgrade resilient modulus as MRSG = 1,500 × CBR and assumes a six‑inch unbound granular subbase [1]. Designs are based on a twenty‑year service life, a one percent annual traffic growth rate, and different terminal serviceability and reliability targets: collector roads use a terminal serviceability of 2.5 and a reliability of 90 %, while local roads employ a serviceability of 2.0 and an 85 % reliability [1]. Both classifications permit up to fifteen percent slab cracking, and when a supported edge is present the computed thickness may be reduced by one inch [1].

RCC pavement is limited to roadways with average daily traffic not exceeding 2,000 vehicles and a posted speed limit of 55 mph or less. When the surface will be exposed and the speed limit is 35 mph or higher, the concrete must be textured by diamond grinding before service entry. The structural thickness of the RCC layer may vary from 4 inches to 18 inches, with lift thicknesses required to be at least 4 inches and no more than 9 inches per single lift; sections thicker than a single lift must be placed in multiple lifts within 60 minutes to avoid cold joints. Placement temperature of the mix shall be maintained between 50 °F and 90 °F, and work is halted if ambient air falls below 40 °F or if the mixture exceeds 90 °F. For lifts under 6 inches, compaction must achieve at least 85 % of the maximum wet density directly behind the paver, while lifts of 6 inches or greater require at least 90 %; after final rolling a field density greater than 96 % of the maximum wet density is required. Density testing must be performed within 30 minutes of completing the rolling operation. The pavement shall be cured continuously for a minimum of 72 hours while kept moist until it reaches a compressive strength of 3,000 psi. A 6‑inch unbound granular subbase is required, and the subgrade resilient modulus is calculated as MRSG = 1500 × CBR following standard PennDOT values for PCC pavement. The analysis assumes one‑way average daily truck traffic. Control joints are spaced between 15 and 20 feet for sections thinner than 8 inches, whereas for thicker sections the spacing is taken as 2.5 times the pavement thickness expressed in feet. When a supported edge is provided, the required slab thickness may be reduced by one inch. For designs that incorporate an RCC shoulder on a widened concrete slab, the total slab width shall not exceed 14 feet, with a 12‑foot strip designated for traffic and the remainder serving as an effective shoulder when paired with a Type 1 asphalt or RCC shoulder; the appropriate load‑transfer coefficient J must be taken from the table for widened lanes with such shoulders. This widened‑slab configuration is limited to low‑volume rural Interstates and Expressways where the two‑way ADT is under 10,000 vehicles and the need for an incident management lane is minimal. All RCC designs for local and collector roads are based on a 20‑year design life with an assumed 1 % annual traffic growth rate. The designs tolerate up to 15 % of slabs cracking at the end of service life. For collector roads a terminal serviceability index of 2.5 is required together with a reliability target of 90 %; for local roads the terminal serviceability index is 2.0 with an 85 % reliability requirement. [1][2]

Structural section and dimensions

Roller‑compacted concrete pavement is placed as a slab whose thickness may vary from four to eighteen inches. The placement must be executed in lifts that are at least four inches thick, and any single lift is limited to a thickness between four and nine inches; greater overall depths are achieved by building up multiple equal lifts. When the slab thickness is less than eight inches, control joints are spaced fifteen to twenty feet apart, whereas for slabs eight inches or thicker the joint spacing equals 2.5 times the slab thickness expressed in feet. The design calls for a six‑inch unbound granular subbase beneath the concrete, while the subgrade and any additional base layers are simply required to be prepared according to project specifications without prescribed dimensions. No specific dimensional requirements are provided for treated‑soil, additional base material, or reinforcement such as steel bars or dowel bars. If an edge is supported by a curb‑and‑gutter, widened lane, or similar feature, the required slab depth may be reduced by one inch relative to the minimum depth for an unsupported edge. The slab may be widened but must not exceed fourteen feet in total width; when a right‑hand RCC shoulder is provided the slab is striped at twelve feet to define the travel lane. For a tied concrete shoulder the outside lane may be thirteen feet wide and also striped at twelve feet, with the remaining portion of the slab serving as an effective shoulder in conjunction with a Type 1 asphalt or RCC shoulder. No forms, dedicated edge support, or additional reinforcement are required for the RCC pavement. Prior to full construction a test section at least fifty feet long, sized according to the planned thickness and width, is constructed to verify mix consistency, equipment performance, compaction, curing procedures, and strength development. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–167); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 370–374)

Thickness selection uses standard concrete‑pavement design procedures that allow RCC sections from four to eighteen inches depending on traffic loads and subgrade conditions, with the target depth achieved in a single lift when it falls between four and nine inches or divided into equal lifts for greater depths. The PennDOT methodology is complemented by ACPA StreetPave tables which embed a twenty‑year design life, a one percent annual traffic growth rate, and assume a six‑inch unbound granular subbase beneath the RCC slab; the minimum thickness derived from these tables reflects the subgrade resilient modulus (1 500 times CBR), the terminal serviceability index for the road class, and a reliability level of ninety or eighty‑five percent. When a supported edge such as curb‑and‑gutter is provided, one inch may be deducted from the tabulated minimum, otherwise the full thickness indicated by the tables is retained. Accordingly, the layer configuration consists of the RCC slab placed over the six‑inch granular subbase, with the slab constructed in either a single lift (if total thickness ≤ 9 in) or in multiple equal lifts to satisfy density requirements for thicker sections. Control joint spacing is primarily governed by slab thickness: slabs thinner than eight inches receive joints spaced between fifteen and twenty feet, while slabs eight inches thick or greater are spaced at a distance in feet equal to two point five times the slab thickness. In addition, placement timing influences joint condition; adjacent lanes placed within sixty minutes form fresh joints, whereas intervals exceeding sixty minutes create cold joints that must be treated accordingly. Control joints are saw‑cut to a depth of one quarter of the RCC slab thickness as shown on the plans. Shoulder joints are required to align with the mainline concrete joints to maintain continuity across the pavement width. Specific cross‑slope values, transition treatments, and tie‑in details are not prescribed within the RCC design guidance itself; these items are addressed by the broader project specifications that govern alignment and drainage for the roadway. [1][2]

Materials and mixture design

Roller‑compacted concrete is required to be a very dry, stiff mix that uses the same three basic constituents as conventional concrete—cement, aggregate and water—while maintaining a distinctly lower water‑to‑cement ratio, typically about 0.40 but permitted down to 0.30 [1][2]. The approved mix design must state the exact percentages of cement, aggregates, water and any admissible admixtures, and all proportions must stay within the tolerances established for the design [2]. Aggregate shall be densely graded with a maximum nominal size less than one inch, and air entrainment may be provided but is not mandatory [1]. Only limited types of chemical admixtures are allowed, and their quantities are to be included in the mix proportions as defined by the design [1][2]. The resulting mixture exhibits a no‑slump, damp‑gravel consistency that is suitable for placement with rollers and must achieve compressive strengths ranging from roughly four thousand to ten thousand psi [1]. Placement temperature of the RCC must be maintained between fifty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit, and the material supplied to the paver must be free of frost, lumps or any crusted hardened pieces; moisture content may be adjusted as needed for the selected paving equipment [2].

To meet structural, durability, constructability and performance goals, an RCC mix is based on cement, aggregate and water but is proportioned for a low water‑to‑cement ratio—often between 0.30 and 0.40—to achieve a damp‑gravel consistency with essentially no slump and compressive strengths in the 4 000–10 000 psi range. The aggregate should be densely graded, limited to sizes under one inch, and air entrainment or admixtures are used only as needed. Design thickness is selected from four to eighteen inches using standard concrete pavement procedures, while the subgrade and subbase must be prepared to support compaction; exposed surfaces on higher‑speed sections require post‑placement diamond grinding for texture. Structural details and interfaces

Roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavements are placed without the use of traditional formwork, steel reinforcement, or dowel bars, relying instead on the mass and compaction of the mix for structural integrity [1][2]. The only structural joints normally incorporated are control joints, which are required along shoulders to align with the adjacent conventional concrete roadway and may be provided elsewhere according to design spacing criteria [1][2]. Joint spacing is governed by slab thickness: for sections thinner than eight inches, control joints are typically spaced at intervals of fifteen to twenty feet, whereas for pavements eight inches thick or greater the spacing equals the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet [1]. In addition to control joints, RCC construction must accommodate vertical and horizontal joints that are classified as fresh or cold depending on the interval between successive lane or lift placements [2]. A fresh joint occurs when an adjacent lane or lift is placed within sixty minutes of the preceding one, allowing a continuous bond without additional preparation [2]. When the time gap exceeds sixty minutes, a cold joint is created and must be prepared by cutting the full depth of the pavement at least six inches from the exposed edge, cleaning the surface, moistening it, or applying a cement slurry for horizontal joints before the next lane is placed [2]. Control joints are saw‑cut to a depth equal to one‑quarter of the compacted RCC thickness and must be made no later than approximately eighteen hours after placement [2]. If any joints are specified to be sealed, they are treated in accordance with the applicable sealing requirements outlined in the specifications [2]. No additional reinforcement or load‑transfer devices such as dowel bars are prescribed beyond these joint treatments [1][2].

Design of RCC interfaces follows coordinated criteria covering joints, surface treatment, subgrade preparation, edge support, and load transfer with adjoining structures [1][2]. When RCC abuts another concrete pavement, shoulder, or adjacent structure, control joints are required to align with the neighboring concrete joints, and their spacing is a function of slab thickness—15‑20 ft for slabs thinner than eight inches and 2.5 times the slab thickness in feet for slabs eight inches thick or greater [1]. The interface between the RCC base course and the prepared subgrade or granular subbase is achieved by placing a low‑slump, stiffer concrete mix directly on the surface and compacting it with rollers to attain the specified thickness and density, thereby creating a sound bond that can support an overlying asphalt wearing course or other surface treatment [2][1]. The subgrade and the six‑inch unbound granular subbase must be prepared to sustain the compaction effort required for the dry mix, ensuring adequate support for the RCC slab [1]. Joint integrity between successive lifts or adjacent lanes depends on timing; any vertical or horizontal joint formed within sixty minutes of the preceding placement is treated as a fresh joint and must be kept moist to preserve bond, whereas joints formed after more than sixty minutes become cold joints that require full‑depth cutting at least six inches from the exposed edge, cleaning, and immediate moisture application or cement slurry [2]. Adjacent paving lanes therefore need to be placed within the same sixty‑minute window; otherwise their vertical joint is classified as a cold joint and must be prepared accordingly [2]. Control joints are saw‑cut after rolling to one‑quarter depth of the compacted RCC pavement, with cutting required no later than eighteen hours from placement to manage cracking [2]. If the RCC surface will remain exposed and the posted speed limit is thirty‑five miles per hour or higher, the top must be textured by diamond grinding; this requirement is waived when the RCC serves only as a base beneath an asphalt wearing course [1]. When a supported edge such as a curb and gutter or widened lane is provided, the slab thickness may be reduced by one inch from the standard tables; if no supported edge exists, the minimum slab depth from those tables is retained [1]. For configurations that widen a concrete lane to incorporate an RCC right‑hand shoulder, the widened slab shall not exceed fourteen feet in width and is striped at twelve feet, with the interface between the concrete slab and the adjacent RCC or asphalt shoulder addressed by applying the appropriate load‑transfer coefficient for “widened lane with asphalt or RCC shoulders” as given in the design tables [1]. These provisions are primarily intended for low‑volume rural interstates and expressways where average daily traffic does not exceed ten thousand vehicles, and all other design inputs follow standard Portland cement concrete values without alternative slab‑depth reduction methods [1].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

RCC placement must be scheduled so that ambient temperatures stay within defined limits; work stops when the air temperature away from artificial heat falls to 40 °F or rises above 85 °F unless the mix temperature is kept between 50 °F and 90 °F by cooling aggregates or water, and placement ceases if the mixture exceeds 90 °F. Evaporation rates greater than 0.20 lb/ft²·h are prohibited unless mitigated with windbreaks or fog‑mist systems that atomize fine droplets without creating standing water. The subgrade/subbase must be clean, free of ponded water and uniformly moistened before placement, and the RCC surface must be kept continuously moist for at least 72 hours using water trucks with mist nozzles, curing compounds, or polyethylene sheeting to prevent premature moisture loss. These controls ensure that drainage, moisture, and climatic conditions do not compromise compaction, density, or long‑term performance of the RCC pavement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 370–374)

RCC pavement is limited to low‑volume routes, defined as roadways carrying an average daily traffic of two thousand vehicles or fewer and having a posted speed limit not exceeding fifty‑five miles per hour. When the surface will remain exposed and the design speed is thirty‑five miles per hour or higher, the concrete must be textured by diamond grinding before it is opened to traffic to provide adequate skid resistance. The pavement thickness governs control joint spacing; for sections thinner than eight inches joints are placed at intervals of fifteen to twenty feet, while for thicknesses of eight inches or more the spacing equals the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet. All joints must be treated as fresh if adjacent lanes or successive lifts are placed within sixty minutes; otherwise a cold‑joint preparation that includes cleaning, moistening and optional cement slurry is required. Lifts shall not be thinner than four inches, and each lift must achieve at least 85 % of the maximum wet density for lifts under six inches or 90 % for lifts six inches or greater before roller finishing, with a final compaction target exceeding 96 % for all thicknesses. The finished surface may not deviate by more than one‑quarter inch from a straightedge, ensuring geometric uniformity and ride quality. Because RCC typically exhibits a rougher texture, it should not be selected where the International Roughness Index will be used to evaluate ride quality or determine payment incentives. When RCC is employed as a shoulder on widened concrete slabs, the slab width shall not exceed fourteen feet and the travel lane must be striped at twelve feet, leaving the remaining width for an effective RCC or asphalt shoulder. For a tied concrete shoulder, an outside lane up to thirteen feet wide may be striped at twelve feet to limit slab deflection, while the passing lane retains conventional twelve‑foot lanes with a tied concrete shoulder. These configurations are intended only for low‑volume rural Interstates and expressways with two‑way average daily traffic below ten thousand vehicles where an incident‑management lane is unlikely to be required. Verification and expected performance

The contractor must first submit a paving‑operation quality‑control plan for Representative review before any RCC work begins, and then construct a minimum 50‑foot test section to verify that the plant mix, equipment, lift thickness, compaction sequence and curing procedures meet the design intent. During production the pavement is evaluated by wet‑density measurements taken within thirty minutes of rolling, with at least 85 % (90 % for lifts ≥6 in.) of maximum wet density behind the paver and greater than 96 % after final compaction, using a minimum of five tests per 500 cubic yards. After rolling, the surface is kept continuously moist by water‑curing, curing compounds or sheeting for at least seventy‑two hours until a compressive strength of three thousand psi is achieved, ensuring performance and consistency with project requirements. [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 370–374)

Roller‑compacted concrete pavement is projected to achieve a design service life of about twenty years. Its high compressive strength, typically in the range of four to ten thousand psi, provides substantial resistance to cracking and other forms of distress when subjected to the relatively low traffic volumes for which RCC is approved. The mix yields a dry, dense surface that is generally rougher than conventional Portland‑cement concrete or asphalt, so ride quality can be variable and depends on the placement equipment; consequently the International Roughness Index should not be used as a performance metric or payment trigger for sections built with RCC. Performance monitoring relies on estimating the design life from material properties, environmental conditions and traffic levels, while surface roughness is observed visually or measured by IRI only when explicitly required. The design also permits up to fifteen percent of the concrete slabs to be cracked at the end of the twenty‑year period. Ride‑quality and distress resistance are further quantified through a terminal serviceability index, targeted at 2.5 for collector roads and 2.0 for local roads, with associated reliability levels of ninety percent and eighty‑five percent respectively.
