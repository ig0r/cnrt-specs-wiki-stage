Roller-Compacted Concrete

Part of: Rigid Pavement is-subtopic-of
Roller-Compacted Concrete is a topic under Rigid Pavement.

Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-30

Roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) is a very dry, low‑slump concrete mix—typically water‑cement ratio 0.30–0.40 with densely graded aggregate under one inch—that is placed without forms, steel reinforcement or dowel bars using conventional or high‑density asphalt paving equipment and compacted with rollers to achieve at least 85 %–90 % of maximum wet density and a final compaction exceeding 96 % of that density[1]. It is specified for low‑volume (≤ 2,000 vehicles per day) roadways or shoulders with speed limits of 55 mph or less where a high‑strength, durable rigid pavement is needed but conventional cast‑in‑place concrete or asphalt would be uneconomical[1][2]. The mix is designed for compressive strengths between 4,000 and 10,000 psi (minimum 3,000 psi after 72 h of moist curing) and slab thicknesses from four to eighteen inches, producing a rough texture comparable to hot‑mix asphalt that may serve as the final wearing course or as a base beneath an asphalt layer[1][2]. By maintaining placement temperatures between 50 °F and 90 °F, keeping the subgrade moist, and curing the pavement continuously for at least seventy‑two hours, RCC develops the density and strength needed to resist repetitive heavy loads over a design life of about twenty years with acceptable serviceability and limited cracking[1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Roller‑compacted concrete is specified where a high‑strength, durable rigid pavement is required but conventional cast‑in‑place concrete or asphalt would be uneconomical, addressing the need for a surface that can resist repetitive heavy loads on low‑volume routes. The targeted traffic conditions are typically average daily traffic of 2 000 vehicles or fewer and speed limits of 55 mph or less, which aligns with national experience of RCC use on roadways with lower speeds but heavy repetitive loading. Because the method does not require forms, steel reinforcement, or dowel bars, it enables rapid placement of a stiff, low‑slump concrete layer without the time and material costs associated with conventional concrete construction. The mix is produced with a low water‑cement ratio to achieve compressive strengths of several thousand psi—approximately 3 000 psi after curing—and provides a rough texture comparable to hot‑mix asphalt, making it suitable either as the final wearing course or as a base beneath an asphalt layer. To meet these performance goals the placement must occur within a temperature window of 50 °F to 90 °F and be compacted with rollers to at least 95 % of maximum dry density, ensuring uniform thickness and density. After placement the pavement is kept moist for no less than 72 hours so that the intended compressive strength develops, which contributes to crack resistance and high performance under adverse weather conditions. The design incorporates a prepared subgrade and granular subbase to further control cracking and eliminate the need for dowelled joints or supported edges, supporting the requirement for a stable foundation. For low‑volume rural interstates where an incident‑management lane is not needed, RCC can also serve as a right‑hand shoulder, providing an effective shoulder width that limits slab deflection. The design life target is 20 years with modest traffic growth (approximately 1 % per year), aiming for terminal serviceability values between 2.0 and 2.5 and reliability of 85–90 %, while permitting up to 15 % slab cracking at the end of design life. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–167); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 219), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 378–381)

RCC pavement is intended for low‑volume roadways carrying no more than about 2,000 vehicles per day and posted for speeds up to 55 mph. The design must provide a structural capacity by selecting a concrete compressive strength typically between 4,000 and 10,000 psi and by providing a slab thickness that can range from four to eighteen inches, with the minimum depth allowed to be reduced by one inch when a supported edge is present. To achieve the required density during construction, lifts thinner than six inches must be compacted to at least 85 % of the maximum wet density, while lifts six inches thick or greater must reach no less than 90 % of that density, and after the final rolling pass every section of pavement must exceed 96 % of the maximum wet density. The mix must be placed when its temperature is between 50 °F and 90 °F, and the underlying subgrade and any sub‑base must be clean, uniformly moist and free of frost or standing water to ensure proper bonding and load transfer. A low water‑cement ratio together with a dense aggregate gradation is required so that the hardened concrete attains long‑term durability, supporting an anticipated service life of roughly twenty years even under a 1 % annual traffic growth rate. After placement the concrete must be kept continuously moist for at least seventy‑two hours to develop a compressive strength of not less than three thousand pounds per square inch, which contributes to both durability and the serviceability needed for long‑term traffic loading. When the surface is exposed and the posted speed limit equals or exceeds 35 mph, the slab must be textured by diamond grinding in order to meet safety and ride‑quality expectations. Control joints are spaced proportionally to slab thickness to limit cracking, and the design tolerates up to fifteen percent of slabs developing cracks at the end of service while still achieving the prescribed terminal serviceability index—2.5 for collector roads and 2.0 for local roads. Reliability targets of ninety percent for collector classifications and eighty‑five percent for local classifications are incorporated into the design to ensure that functional performance, structural adequacy, durability, safety and acceptable serviceability are maintained throughout the design period. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

Roller‑compacted concrete is considered appropriate for a range of concrete pavement applications where traffic demands are modest, specifically on roadways whose average daily traffic does not exceed 2,000 vehicles and where the posted speed limit is 55 mph or less [1]. The guides also recognize its suitability as a concrete shoulder placed directly adjacent to reinforced or plain cement‑concrete pavements when tied shoulders are unnecessary, and as a base course beneath an asphalt wearing surface or other surface treatment, provided the subgrade and subbase have been properly prepared [1][2]. In addition, RCC can be employed for temporary pavement functions such as median crossovers or construction widening areas, serving either as the final wearing layer or as the underlying base [1]. For low‑volume rural Interstates and Expressways that carry two‑way traffic volumes under 10,000 vehicles per day and where an incident‑management lane is not required, a widened concrete slab limited to a 14‑foot width may be paired with a right shoulder constructed of RCC, using the load‑transfer coefficient specified for widened lanes with asphalt or RCC shoulders [1]. The material itself is a very dry mix characterized by a water‑cement ratio typically between 0.30 and 0.40, producing a stiff, low‑slump concrete that can be placed with conventional or high‑density asphalt paving equipment and compacted with rollers to achieve the required thickness and density; it requires no forms, reinforcing, or dowel bars, and control joints are provided only where needed [1][2]. Design of RCC pavements follows the same structural principles as jointed plain concrete pavement but assumes a 20‑year service life, a 1 % annual traffic growth rate, and incorporates a six‑inch unbound granular subbase; slab thickness is derived from the subgrade resilient modulus using one‑way average daily truck traffic [1]. Serviceability targets differ by road classification, with collector roads aiming for a terminal serviceability of 2.5 at 90 % reliability and local roads targeting a value of 2.0 at 85 % reliability [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–167); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 219)

Roller‑compacted concrete is generally limited to low‑volume, lower‑speed facilities; when average daily traffic exceeds roughly two thousand vehicles per day or the posted speed limit is greater than fifty‑five miles per hour, the rougher surface texture and its limited compatibility with International Roughness Index based ride‑quality assessments make conventional concrete or asphalt pavements a more appropriate choice. RCC shoulders are only justified on low‑volume rural interstates or expressways where two‑way average daily traffic stays below ten thousand vehicles and there is little demand for an incident‑management lane; if traffic volumes exceed that level, a dedicated incident lane is required or the slab width needed to accommodate the shoulder would have to be greater than fourteen feet, at which point a conventional design with twelve‑foot lanes and a tied concrete shoulder should be used. Environmental conditions also restrict RCC placement; when ambient air temperature falls to forty degrees Fahrenheit or lower, the mix cannot retain sufficient heat for proper compaction and curing, prompting selection of an alternative pavement type. Likewise, if the plastic mix temperature rises above ninety degrees Fahrenheit after mixing, the material may set too quickly and lose workability, making other designs preferable. High evaporation rates exceeding 0.20 pounds per square foot per hour can cause premature drying of the surface, so unless mitigation measures are employed a different pavement approach should be used. Placement during hard rain that could damage the surface is prohibited for RCC, leading designers to choose another pavement system under such weather conditions. Finally, a frozen subgrade, base or subbase prevents adequate compaction of RCC, so when ground conditions are frozen a conventional pavement design is recommended. [1][2]

Design inputs and requirements

The design of an RCC pavement begins with site characterization that identifies the facility as a shoulder, temporary crossing, or a local/collector road. Such facilities must carry an average daily traffic not exceeding 2,000 vehicles and have a posted speed limit of 55 mph or less; when the surface will be exposed to traffic at speeds of 35 mph or higher, the slab is required to receive a diamond‑ground finish. Traffic inputs focus on low‑to‑moderate volumes and include the one‑way average daily truck traffic (ADTT) together with an assumed annual growth rate of 1 percent. Material specifications call for a very dry concrete mix consisting of cement, a dense aggregate gradation whose maximum size is less than 1 inch, water at a water‑cement ratio around 0.40, optional air entrainment and limited use of admixtures. The mix is designed to achieve a compressive strength between 4,000 and 10,000 psi, and the resulting slab thickness typically ranges from 4 to 18 inches. Environmental considerations prescribe a design service life of about 20 years; the actual service life is influenced by the selected construction materials, climate conditions, and traffic volumes, and surface texturing may be required when speed limits exceed 35 mph. Geotechnical inputs require a prepared subgrade and a 6‑inch unbound granular subbase capable of supporting RCC compaction, with the subgrade resilient modulus calculated as 1,500 times the CBR value. Existing‑condition data must confirm whether the pavement will be placed on new, rehabilitated or existing surfaces and whether a supported edge is present, since these factors affect the minimum slab depth required to meet terminal serviceability of 2.5 and a reliability target of 90 %. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 8 (pp. 166–167)

The design and construction of Roller‑compacted Concrete (RCC) pavement are governed primarily by PennDOT Publication 408, including Sections 323, 502, and 658, together with the joint‑spacing rules in Publication 72M RC‑27M, while project execution must follow a quality‑control plan as specified in Section 704.1(d)1.a of the contract documents. Applicability is limited to roadways or shoulders where the average daily traffic does not exceed 2,000 vehicles and the posted speed limit is 55 mph or less, with RCC shoulders that are part of widened concrete slabs restricted to a maximum width of fourteen feet and intended for low‑volume rural Interstates or Expressways having a combined two‑way ADT under ten thousand vehicles. When an exposed surface is required on a roadway with a speed limit of 35 mph or higher, the pavement must be diamond‑ground before it is opened to traffic, and design calculations are performed using the American Concrete Pavement Association’s StreetPave12 software employing standard PennDOT values for Portland cement concrete. Slab thickness may range from four to eighteen inches; lift thicknesses must be at least four inches, single lifts may not exceed nine inches, and multiple lifts must be placed within sixty minutes of each other, with a supported edge permitting a reduction of one inch in the minimum slab depth. Compressive strength requirements call for a design strength between 4,000 and 10,000 psi, while curing moisture must be maintained for at least seventy‑two hours after rolling until the concrete reaches three thousand psi compressive strength. Control joints are spaced fifteen to twenty feet apart for sections thinner than eight inches, or at a distance equal to the slab thickness multiplied by 2.5 ft for thicker sections, and joint‑spacing rules from Publication 72M RC‑27M apply. Compaction criteria require pavers and rollers to achieve at least eighty‑five percent of maximum wet density behind the paver (ninety percent for lifts six inches or greater) and a final compaction exceeding ninety‑six percent of that density, with equipment approved to meet AASHTO M 241 standards. Weather constraints dictate cessation of placement when ambient temperature falls below forty degrees Fahrenheit, when the plastic mixture exceeds ninety degrees Fahrenheit, or when evaporation rates surpass 0.20 lb/ft²·hr unless mitigation measures are employed; mix temperature must be maintained between fifty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit during placement. The design assumes a twenty‑year service life with a one percent annual traffic growth rate, a six‑inch unbound granular subbase, terminal serviceability targets of 2.5 (90 % reliability) for collector roads and 2.0 (85 % reliability) for local roads, and limits slab cracking at the end of design life to fifteen percent, while load‑transfer coefficients for widened lanes with asphalt or RCC shoulders are taken from Table 8.2. [1][2]

Design methods and criteria

RCC pavement design employs the same structural analysis framework used for conventional concrete while integrating several specialized procedures and empirical relationships specific to the material. [1] The required slab thickness is determined by applying a state‑level concrete‑pavement design methodology that evaluates traffic loads against a target compressive strength typically ranging from 4,000 to 10,000 psi. [1] Mix design specifications call for a very dry concrete with a water‑to‑cement ratio as low as 0.30 and commonly around 0.40, combined with a dense aggregate gradation limited to particles smaller than one inch and only limited use of admixtures or air entrainment. [1] Production of the mix must follow an approved design such as AASHTO M 241 for continuous plants and is verified on‑site through a field‑operation quality‑control plan that includes a minimum 50‑ft test section to confirm consistency, equipment performance, roller selection, and curing practices. [2] Placement is carried out in lifts ranging from four to nine inches, with successive lifts scheduled no more than sixty minutes apart to prevent the formation of cold joints. [2] Compaction is monitored using ASTM C1040 direct‑transmission density testing, where the compaction percentage X is calculated as (ρw/ρt)×100, and a minimum of 85 % of maximum wet density is required for lifts thinner than six inches while lifts six inches or thicker must achieve at least 90 % behind the paver. [2] After final rolling, overall compaction must exceed 96 % of the maximum wet density. [2] Curing is achieved by maintaining surface moisture for a minimum of seventy‑two hours through water misting, polyethylene sheeting, or curing compound application. [2] Control joints are spaced according to slab thickness: sections thinner than eight inches receive joints every fifteen to twenty feet, whereas thicker slabs use joint spacing equal to 2.5 times the pavement thickness expressed in feet. [1] When an RCC shoulder is incorporated into a widened concrete slab, the slab width is limited to fourteen feet and longitudinal striping is provided at twelve‑foot intervals for low‑volume rural interstates with average daily traffic below ten thousand vehicles. [1] The interaction between slab and shoulder is governed by a load‑transfer coefficient J taken from an empirical table for “widened lane with asphalt or RCC shoulders,” serving as the primary empirical relationship within the rigid‑pavement framework. [1] Thickness of the RCC layer can also be generated using the ACPA StreetPave12 software, which models subgrade resilient modulus as MRSG = 1,500 × CBR and assumes a six‑inch unbound granular subbase. [1] Design life is typically set at twenty years with an assumed 1 % annual traffic growth rate; terminal serviceability and reliability targets differ by road classification, with collector roads using a terminal serviceability of 2.5 and a reliability of 90 %, while local roads employ a serviceability of 2.0 and an 85 % reliability. [1] Both classifications permit up to fifteen percent slab cracking, and when a supported edge is present the computed thickness may be reduced by one inch. [1]

RCC pavement is limited to roadways whose average daily traffic does not exceed 2,000 vehicles and where the posted speed limit is no greater than 55 mph [1]. When the surface will be exposed and the speed limit is 35 mph or higher, the concrete must be textured by diamond grinding before service entry [1]. The structural thickness of the RCC layer may vary from 4 inches to 18 inches, with control‑joint spacing determined by thickness—sections thinner than 8 inches require joints spaced between 15 and 20 feet, while thicker sections use a spacing equal to 2.5 times the pavement thickness expressed in feet [1]. For widened concrete slabs the total width may not exceed 14 feet, allocating a 12‑foot strip for traffic and using the remaining width as an effective shoulder when paired with a Type 1 asphalt or RCC shoulder; this configuration is confined to low‑volume rural Interstates and Expressways with two‑way ADT under 10 000 vehicles and relies on the appropriate load‑transfer coefficient J from the designated table [1]. All designs for local and collector roads assume a 20‑year design life, a 1 % annual traffic growth rate, a one‑way average daily truck traffic assumption, and require a 6‑inch unbound granular subbase; the subgrade resilient modulus is calculated as MRSG = 1500 × CBR following standard values [1]. The design tolerates up to 15 % of slabs cracking at the end of service life, with collector roads requiring a terminal serviceability index of 2.5 and a reliability target of 90 %, while local roads use a terminal serviceability of 2.0 and an 85 % reliability requirement; provision of a supported edge permits reduction of slab thickness by one inch [1]. Placement of RCC pavement is permitted only when ambient temperature lies between 40 °F and 90 °F, and work must be halted if air temperature falls to 40 °F or if the concrete mix temperature exceeds 90 °F; evaporation rates greater than 0.20 lb/ft²·hr are prohibited unless mitigation measures are applied [2]. The pavement is constructed on a prepared subgrade and sub‑base using a low‑slump concrete mix, with conventional or high‑density asphalt paving equipment and rollers of various sizes employed to achieve the specified thickness, density, and surface texture [2]. Each lift must be at least 4 inches thick and shall not exceed 9 inches in a single pass; compaction after each lift must reach at least 85 % of the maximum wet density, rising to 90 % for lifts six inches or thicker, and after the final rolling operation the compacted material must achieve more than 96 % of the maximum wet density [2]. Field density testing in accordance with ASTM C1040 is required within thirty minutes of rolling, and curing must be continued for a minimum of seventy‑two hours during which time the pavement must develop a compressive strength of at least three thousand psi [2].

Structural section and dimensions

Roller‑compacted concrete pavement is placed as a slab whose thickness may vary from four to eighteen inches. When the slab is less than eight inches thick, control joints are spaced fifteen to twenty feet apart; for slabs eight inches or thicker, joint spacing equals 2.5 times the slab thickness expressed in feet. The design calls for a six‑inch unbound granular subbase beneath the concrete. If an edge is supported—by a curb‑and‑gutter, widened lane, or similar feature—the required slab depth can be reduced by one inch compared with the minimum depth specified for an unsupported edge. No forms, steel reinforcement, dowel bars, or dedicated edge support are required, and no specific dimensional requirements are given for base, treated‑soil, shoulders, or other reinforcement. The concrete slab may be widened but must not exceed fourteen feet in total width; when a right‑hand RCC shoulder is used the slab is striped at twelve feet to define the travel lane. Where a tied concrete shoulder is employed, the outside lane may be thirteen feet wide and also striped at twelve feet, with the remaining portion of the slab serving as an effective shoulder whose width is determined by the accompanying Type 1 asphalt or RCC shoulder. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–167)

The thickness of a roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) pavement is first established using the standard PennDOT concrete‑pavement design procedures, which permit sections ranging from four to eighteen inches depending on traffic loads and subgrade conditions. That target depth may be placed in a single lift or divided into multiple lifts to achieve the required overall thickness. In parallel, the ACPA StreetPave12 tables are often consulted; these tables embed a twenty‑year design life, a one percent annual traffic growth rate, and assume a six‑inch unbound granular subbase beneath the RCC. The minimum depth derived from the tables reflects the resilient modulus of the subgrade (computed as 1,500 times the CBR value), the terminal serviceability index for the road class, and a reliability level of ninety percent or eighty‑five percent. When a supported edge such as a curb‑and‑gutter is provided, one inch may be deducted from the tabulated minimum depth, otherwise the full thickness indicated by the tables is retained.

The layer configuration therefore consists of the RCC slab over a six‑inch granular subbase, with optional edge support that can reduce the required slab thickness. Control joint spacing follows a simple relationship to slab thickness: for slabs thinner than eight inches, joints are placed at intervals between fifteen and twenty feet; for slabs eight inches thick or greater, the spacing in feet equals two point five times the slab thickness. Shoulder joints are required to line up with the adjacent mainline concrete joints to maintain continuity across the pavement width.

Specific cross‑slope values, transition treatments, and tie‑in details are not prescribed within the RCC design guidance itself; these items are addressed by the broader project specifications that govern alignment and drainage for the roadway. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 8 (pp. 166–167)

Materials and mixture design

Roller‑compacted concrete is required to be a very dry, low‑slump mix that incorporates the three conventional constituents—cement, aggregate and water—while employing a markedly lower water‑to‑cement ratio, typically about 0.40 but permitted to be as low as 0.30, which produces a damp‑gravel consistency with essentially no slump. The aggregate must be densely graded with a maximum nominal size of less than one inch, and air entrainment may be used at the designer’s discretion; admixture use is limited to those necessary for workability or durability. All proportions of cement, aggregate and water are to be set out in an approved mix design that defines the exact quantities and must satisfy the project‑specified tolerances. The mixture temperature at placement shall be maintained between 50 °F and 90 °F, and the moisture content may be adjusted as needed for the selected paving equipment, provided the material contains no frost, lumps or crusted hardened pieces. Placement is performed with conventional or high‑density asphalt paving equipment and compacted with rollers to achieve the required thickness and density over a prepared subgrade and subbase. The resulting RCC pavement is expected to develop compressive strengths ranging from approximately 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (p. 219), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 377–381)

Roller‑Compacted concrete mixes are based on the same three basic ingredients—cement, aggregate, and water—as conventional readymixed concrete, but they are proportioned for a low water‑to‑cement ratio, typically between 0.30 and 0.40, to produce a damp‑gravel consistency with essentially no slump and compressive strengths ranging from about 4,000 to 10,000 psi. The aggregate is densely graded and limited to sizes under one inch; air entrainment or chemical admixtures are incorporated only when the design calls for them. Design thicknesses are selected between four and eighteen inches using standard concrete pavement procedures, and the subgrade and subbase must be prepared to provide adequate support for compaction. On higher‑speed sections the exposed surface is often finished with post‑placement diamond grinding to achieve the required texture for performance. The contractor first prepares an approved RCC mix design that precisely defines material proportions, and the mixing plant must produce the mixture within the specified tolerances while keeping the plastic temperature between fifty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit. Before full‑scale paving a minimum 50‑foot test section is placed with the project’s intended equipment to confirm that the plant can consistently meet the design, that placement and compaction achieve the required density and surface condition, and that the resulting compressive strength satisfies structural and durability criteria. This verification also provides an opportunity to fine‑tune roller combinations, field‑density acceptance testing procedures, and curing practices so that constructability and long‑term performance objectives are met. Structural details and interfaces

Roller‑compacted concrete pavements are placed without traditional formwork, steel reinforcement, or dowel bars, so no conventional load‑transfer devices are required [1]. The only structural joints normally provided are control joints, which must be aligned on shoulders with the adjacent conventional concrete and may be spaced according to thickness‑based rules [1]. For sections thinner than eight inches the recommended spacing is fifteen to twenty feet, while for pavements eight inches thick or greater the spacing equals the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet [1]. Control joints are saw‑cut to a depth of one‑quarter of the compacted RCC thickness and must be cut no later than eighteen hours after placement; where specified they are sealed in accordance with the applicable sealing provisions [2]. In addition to saw cuts, an edge shoe mounted on the paver can be used at lane edges to produce a compacted edge that is inclined between fifteen and thirty degrees, providing an alternative means of forming the peripheral joint [2]. When adjacent lanes or lifts are placed within sixty minutes a fresh vertical or horizontal joint forms; these joints are kept moist to preserve bond [2]. Any joint created after more than sixty minutes is treated as a cold joint and requires full‑depth cutting, cleaning of the faces, and immediate moistening before the next lift is placed, with the cut extending at least six inches from the exposed edge into sound compacted material [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 378–381)

Design of RCC interfaces is governed by coordinated criteria covering joints, surface texture, subgrade preparation, edge support and load transfer with adjoining structures. Joints between lifts or lanes are classified as fresh when the next placement occurs within sixty minutes; otherwise they are treated as cold joints that require full‑depth cutting, removal of loose material, moistening of the exposed face and, where specified, application of cementitious grout before the subsequent lift. For slabs thinner than eight inches control joints are spaced at fifteen to twenty feet, while for slabs eight inches thick or greater the spacing equals 2.5 times the slab thickness expressed in feet. Control joints are saw‑cut to a depth of one‑quarter of the pavement thickness no later than eighteen hours after placement, and any required sealing follows the provisions of the applicable joint‑sealing section. If the RCC surface will remain exposed and the posted speed limit is thirty‑five miles per hour or higher, the top must be textured by diamond grinding; this requirement is waived when the RCC serves only as a base beneath an asphalt wearing course. A six‑inch unbound granular subbase is placed beneath the concrete slab and both subgrade and subbase must be prepared to sustain the compaction effort required for the dry mix. When a supported edge such as a curb and gutter or a widened lane is provided, one inch may be deducted from the slab thickness indicated in the standard tables; if no supported edge exists the minimum slab depth from those tables is retained. For a concrete lane widened to incorporate an RCC right‑hand shoulder, the combined slab width shall not exceed fourteen feet and is striped at twelve feet, with the load‑transfer coefficient for “widened lane with asphalt or RCC shoulders” applied as given in the design tables. This configuration is intended primarily for low‑volume rural interstates and expressways where average daily traffic does not exceed ten thousand vehicles. All other design inputs follow standard Portland cement concrete values and no alternative methods for reducing slab depths are permitted. [1][2]

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

RCC placement must be coordinated with weather to protect drainage and moisture conditions: hard rain is prohibited while light rain or mist may be allowed only if the surface remains undamaged; placement stops when ambient temperature drops below 40 °F or when mixture temperatures exceed 90 °F, with cooling measures required once air reaches 85 °F. Evaporation rates greater than 0.20 lb/ft²·h must be mitigated using windbreaks or fog‑mist systems, and the subgrade/subbase must be free of standing water, frost, or frozen material and uniformly moistened before paving to prevent runoff erosion from curing water. After rolling, the RCC surface is kept continuously moist for at least 72 hours by water trucks with fine mist nozzles, fogging, sheet covering, or curing compounds to maintain proper moisture while curing. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 378–381)

Roller‑compacted concrete pavement is restricted to low‑volume routes and must satisfy a series of accessibility, safety, geometric and user requirements. The roadway may carry an average daily traffic of two thousand vehicles or fewer and the posted speed limit cannot exceed fifty‑five miles per hour. When the surface will remain exposed and the speed limit is thirty‑five miles per hour or higher, the concrete must be textured by diamond grinding before it is opened to traffic. Control joints are required to control cracking; for sections thinner than eight inches they are spaced at intervals of fifteen to twenty feet, while for thicknesses of eight inches or more the spacing equals the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet. Because RCC typically has a rougher texture, it should not be selected where ride quality will be evaluated with the International Roughness Index, as this could affect safety assessments and payment incentives.

When RCC is used as a shoulder on widened concrete slabs, the slab width may not exceed fourteen feet and the travel lane must be striped at twelve feet, leaving the remaining width to function as an effective RCC or asphalt shoulder. This configuration is intended only for low‑volume rural Interstates and expressways with two‑way average daily traffic below ten thousand vehicles where an incident‑management lane is unlikely to be required. If a tied concrete shoulder is provided, an outside lane up to thirteen feet wide may be striped at twelve feet to limit slab deflection, while the passing lane retains conventional twelve‑foot lanes with a tied concrete shoulder. Verification and expected performance

The completed RCC pavement is verified by first submitting a paving operation QC Plan for Representative approval, then placing a minimum 50‑foot test section using the proposed equipment to confirm mix consistency, plant performance and roller selection. Throughout placement field density tests are taken in accordance with ASTM C1040 within thirty minutes of rolling; at least five measurements behind the paver and five after final compaction must show a wet‑density compaction exceeding 96% of the maximum for all lifts. The same QC process includes confirming that approved mixing, hauling and paving equipment meet tolerances, that joints are prepared as fresh or cold according to specifications, and that curing is maintained for at least seventy‑two hours until the pavement reaches a compressive strength of three thousand psi. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 378–381)

Roller‑compacted concrete pavement is designed for a service life of about twenty years, with performance monitoring based on material properties, environmental conditions and traffic levels. The high compressive strength of RCC, typically ranging from four to ten thousand psi, contributes to resistance against cracking and other distress under the relatively low traffic volumes for which it is approved, and a minimum compressive strength of 3,000 psi must be attained after at least 72 hours of curing as verified by standard cylinder testing. The specification requires that the mix achieve at least 85 %–90 % of maximum wet density immediately behind the paver, depending on lift thickness, and that a final compaction exceed 96 % of the maximum wet density; these densities are confirmed with ASTM C1040 field‑density tests performed within thirty minutes of rolling. Field‑density testing follows the direct‑transmission method of ASTM C1040, while compressive strength is measured on cured cylinders after the prescribed curing period. Because the RCC surface is generally drier and rougher than conventional Portland‑cement concrete or asphalt, ride quality can be variable and depends on placement equipment; consequently the International Roughness Index is not employed as a performance metric or payment trigger for RCC sections, although visual observation may be used when explicitly required. Serviceability is further expressed through a terminal serviceability index targeted at 2.5 for collector roads and 2.0 for local roads, with reliability levels of ninety percent and eighty‑five percent respectively, and the design permits up to fifteen percent of slabs to be cracked by the end of the twenty‑year period.
