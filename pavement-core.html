Pavement Core

Part of: Pavement Condition Assessment is-subtopic-of
Pavement Core is a topic under Pavement Condition Assessment.
pavement-corescorescore-sampling
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-28

Pavement‑core investigation is a field technique that extracts full‑depth cylindrical samples—typically six inches in diameter—from both visibly distressed joints and randomly selected locations to obtain point‑specific data on every structural layer of an asphalt or concrete pavement, including the surface courses, base, subbase and underlying soils. The cores are measured for thickness, bulk specific gravity, density, air‑void content, binder content, aggregate gradation, macrotexture and mechanical properties such as compressive strength, providing a quantitative basis to compare against contractual limits for thickness tolerance, density (≥91–92 % of theoretical maximum), strength (≥3,500 psi) and other material criteria. By entering the measured layer depths and material coefficients into structural analysis tools such as the Component Method, engineers calculate an effective Structural Number that reveals whether the existing pavement satisfies design requirements or exhibits deficiencies that dictate overlay type, rehabilitation strategy, payment adjustments, or replacement. [1][2]

Purpose and investigation objectives

The pavement‑core investigation is used to evaluate the condition of both the structural layers of the in‑place pavement and the underlying soil support, including subbase or subgrade soils as well as asphalt concrete, hot‑mix asphalt, roller‑compacted concrete, Portland‑cement slabs, pervious concrete, and other surface courses [1][2]. By extracting cylindrical cores—typically six inches in diameter for new construction and appropriate diameters for existing traffic lanes—from locations adjacent to visibly distressed joints and from randomly selected points, engineers can obtain direct measurements of layer thicknesses, material densities, air‑content, strength, binder content, aggregate gradation, macrotexture, and the presence of stripping or other deterioration [1][2]. The measured thickness of each course is compared with the design thickness (D), and the average core depth must exceed D minus one‑half inch while no individual sublot core may be less than this limit; a single core short by more than one‑quarter inch or three consecutive cores short by more than one‑eighth inch constitutes defective work [2]. For flexible pavements, bulk specific gravity and bulk density are required to be at least 92 % of the theoretical maximum, binder content and the percentage passing a No. 200 sieve must fall within contractual limits, and aggregate gradation is verified against the mix design; macrotexture depth greater than 0.024 in between segregated and sound zones indicates unacceptable segregation, while an average texture depth of 0.006 in or less signals flushing [2]. For rigid pavements, hardened density, entrained‑air content (3.5–8.5 %), void content, and 28‑day compressive strength (minimum 3,300 psi for conventional concrete or 3,500 psi for roller‑compacted concrete) are determined, with at least 55 % of samples required to meet these limits [2]. In existing asphalt overlays, the cores reveal whether stripping has occurred in the lower layers; confirmation of a fair‑to‑good condition supports a bonded overlay, whereas evidence of extensive stripping or poor overall condition leads to an unbonded design decision [1]. When subbase or subgrade soils are found to be deficient, they may be excluded from the structural evaluation for overlay design, reflecting their impact on the effective structural number used in methods such as DARWin [1]. The investigation therefore seeks to answer two primary questions: first, does the as‑built or existing pavement satisfy all specified thickness, material composition, density, strength, air‑content, and surface‑quality criteria; second, if deficiencies are present, what is their magnitude, spatial distribution, and severity so that appropriate design choices (e.g., bonded vs unbonded overlay), corrective actions, payment adjustments, or replacement decisions can be defined [2][1]. By entering the layer depths and corresponding structural coefficients into a Component Method calculation tool, engineers obtain an effective structural number that reflects the condition of the evaluated layers and informs subsequent design recommendations [1].

Pavement‑core sampling supplies the quantitative evidence that underlies every subsequent engineering decision, allowing planners and designers to confirm whether an existing structure meets its intended performance criteria or requires intervention [1][2]. By extracting cylindrical sections from both distressed joints and random locations, engineers obtain measurements of subbase or subgrade conditions, concrete compressive strength, air content, percent patching, slab thickness, in‑place density, void content, asphalt content, and fine‑aggregate gradations, all of which are compared against explicit thresholds to assess structural adequacy [1][2]. When the percentage of concrete requiring patching exceeds ten percent—excluding areas already in good condition—a cost‑effectiveness analysis is initiated to weigh patching against alternative rehabilitation treatments [1]; similarly, any core that shows a compressive strength below 3,500 psi triggers an immediate investigation of the concrete operations to determine the cause of low strength [2]. The measured layer thicknesses and material coefficients are entered into Component or Non‑Destructive Testing methods to calculate the effective structural number (SNeff), which is then compared with the structural number required for projected traffic volumes; this comparison dictates the additional overlay structural number and thus the appropriate overlay thickness, while a deficient subbase may be omitted from the evaluation, directly influencing the scope of rehabilitation [1]. For asphalt pavements, core results reveal lower‑layer stripping or other distresses; if the underlying surface is in fair to good condition a bonded concrete overlay can be designed, whereas a deteriorated surface mandates an unbonded design and may affect decisions regarding macrofibers [1]; the same cores also verify that rutting severity is less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth is under 1 inch with average crack width not exceeding ½ inch, and that the remaining asphalt layer exceeds the planned milling depth by at least 1½ inches, thereby confirming eligibility for Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) treatments and guiding rejuvenator dosage [1]. The guides require that individual core depths be within ±¼ inch of the design thickness, that an average lot thickness exceed the design thickness minus 0.5 inch, and that a uniform deficiency greater than ⅛ inch across three consecutive cores signal a defect; air‑void content must lie between 3.5 % and 8.5 %, density must be above the lower specification limit of 91 % (or exceed 92 % of theoretical maximum with wet‑density limits of 96–98 %), and percent‑within‑limits for strength or air content must not fall below 55 % (50 % where specified) [2]. Sieve‑gradation deviations greater than 20 % from the joint mix formula, as well as asphalt‑content and gradation pay factors below 85–90 %, also render a lot defective, prompting corrective actions such as mixture redesign, additional compaction, targeted grinding or sealing, or removal and replacement of entire panels when thickness, strength, or density fall short of prescribed limits [2]. When core results satisfy all criteria, the pavement is accepted as built, confirming that mix design, compaction practices, and subbase preparation were executed as intended; this acceptance supports planning for future overlays or precast slab installations and enables the application of appropriate Overall Lot Pay Factors and payment‑factor percentages for density, asphalt content, and gradation [2]. Conversely, any deficiency initiates a defined pathway: additional coring at 100‑foot intervals until depth is within tolerance, written evaluations of out‑of‑range air content, modification of mixture design or placement practices, and, where necessary, removal and replacement of defective sections, with the severity of the defect determining payment adjustments ranging from reduced OLPF (as low as 70 %) to limited compensation (5–50 % of contract price) or no payment at all [2]. By providing a baseline of spatial variability through systematic coring—every lane for short repairs and every 100 feet on longer stretches—the data guide maintenance scheduling, prioritize rehabilitation actions, allocate resources efficiently, and inform long‑term performance monitoring; they also empower stop‑paving orders when cores are not taken within one day of placement or when density fails consecutive lot requirements, thereby ensuring construction quality aligns with design intent [2]. In special‑study projects such as Superpave binder evaluations, cores taken after construction and during a three‑to‑five‑year monitoring period supply direct measurements of binder performance across climate, traffic, and structural conditions, allowing districts to validate viable polymer‑modified binders, PG‑binder grades, and blending techniques that shape future binder selection, layer thickness design, and long‑term maintenance strategies [1]. Thus, pavement cores integrate condition assessment, structural analysis, material verification, and threshold testing into a single evidence base that drives planning, design, maintenance scheduling, and rehabilitation decisions across the pavement network.

Scope and applicability

Pavement coring is applied to both flexible asphalt structures and rigid concrete pavements whenever verification of thickness, density, material properties, or the existence of specific distresses is required [1][2]. For flexible systems the technique is appropriate for standard dense‑graded asphalt, Superpave mixes, stone‑matrix‑asphalt (SMA) wearing courses, thin‑lift 6.3 mm wear layers, Asphaltic Rubberized Base Course (ARBC), generic base‑course lifts, and specialty surfaces such as open‑graded friction courses, because these materials must satisfy density acceptance criteria and meet or exceed the minimum compacted depths prescribed for the project [2][1]. In concrete pavements—including plain and reinforced cement‑concrete slabs, concrete overlays, ramps, pervious‑concrete sections, and precast‑slab repairs—cores are taken to confirm that slab thickness is within half an inch of design values and that compressive strength reaches at least a 55 % percent‑within‑limits (or exceeds 3,500 psi) while plastic air content remains between 3.5 % and 8.5 % [2][1]. Cores are also used for asphalt‑concrete overlays to obtain accurate layer thicknesses and material properties needed by the Component Method or Non‑Destructive Testing Method, especially when subbase conditions are uncertain, such as a poor‑condition subbase on Interstates or a completely missing subbase on non‑Interstates, or when traffic loads exceed five million 18‑kip ESALs [1][2]. When a concrete overlay is to be bonded to an existing asphalt or composite surface, coring verifies that stripping is not present in the lower asphalt layers; the findings then dictate whether a bonded design (fair‑to‑good underlying condition) or an unbonded design (poor, deteriorated condition) is appropriate [1][2]. For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled treatments cores are justified only when distress is confined to the surface—rutting less than 1½ in., cracks shallower than 1 in. with widths no greater than 0.5 in., medium‑rated raveling, and minor corrugation or shoving within those depth limits—and the existing pavement is structurally sound, well‑drained, free of load‑related base problems, and has an asphalt thickness of at least 1½ in. beyond any planned milling depth [1][2]. Distresses that trigger coring include load‑related cracking, climate‑induced damage, pattern segregation, flushing, fatigue cracking, surface irregularities greater than one‑quarter inch, suspected base failure, rutting, raveling, corrugation, shoving, stripping, rain‑induced damage, improper joint construction or curing, honeycombing, depressions, high points, edge slumping, and any other defect that exceeds specification limits [1][2]. Site conditions governing the need for cores encompass placement on a stable, uniform base for flexible pavements, compacted depths meeting prescribed minima, well‑drained subgrades, cure periods of at least seven days for pervious concrete with ambient temperatures above 55 °F, and any situation where performance surveys detect fatigue cracking or other indications of unacceptable base condition, prompting additional coring to evaluate the problem [2][1].

Pavement cores give the most direct point‑specific information on layer thicknesses and material properties but they represent only a single location, so they cannot capture the full spatial extent of subbase or subgrade distress across an entire slab [1][2]. Because each core is a localized measurement, it also fails to reveal surface defects such as rain damage, improper joint construction, curing problems, cracking, honeycombing, depressions, high points or edge slumping [2]. The practice is further limited by the number of cores that may be taken; typically only one 6‑inch core per sublot is permitted unless a written exception is granted [2]. Cores must be extracted as soon as possible and no later than the day following placement, and any deviation from this timing triggers a stop‑paving requirement and necessitates alternative investigative actions [2]. In addition, cores may only be obtained from compacted layers placed on stable, uniform bases at or beyond the minimum specified depth; shallow placements, unstable bases, small‑area features such as intersections or gorges, and locations within one foot of construction obstructions are excluded, requiring density verification by approved non‑movement or optimum‑rolling patterns using nuclear or electrical impedance gauges [2]. When cores cannot be obtained because of logistical, budgetary, or site constraints, designers must rely on historic pavement data from a Roadway Management System and apply the Component Method or a Non‑Destructive Testing Method to estimate an effective structural number for overlay design [1]. The reliability of core results is also constrained by quality thresholds; if the percent‑within‑limits (PWL) for bulk density or asphalt content falls below fifty percent, the lot is classified as defective, and similarly a PWL for compressive strength or air content under fifty‑five percent renders the core insufficient [2]. An overall lot payment factor under ninety percent or individual density or asphalt‑content pay factors under eighty percent indicate that the core data cannot support payment, prompting the contractor to request retesting using alternative laboratory procedures such as PTM 757 or PTM 702 or to employ other in‑place testing methods [2]. Cores whose surfaces become coated with paraffin wax during density testing cannot be retested for density, and when the required specimen mass is not achieved the laboratory must waive that requirement, which reduces confidence in the calculated Gmm [2]. Poor repeatability of bulk‑density tests also mandates discarding the original values and drilling replacement cores at least twenty‑four inches from the original location, further limiting the efficiency of coring programs [2]. Thickness verification is limited to a tolerance of one‑half inch below the design thickness; if the average core depth is less than this limit or if the measured depth deviates more than a quarter‑inch from the specified depth, additional coring will not be funded, leaving gaps in the thickness record [2]. Whenever any of these quantitative limits are reached—such as defective PWL values, low pay factors, prohibited core locations, excessive thickness deficiency, or contamination that compromises the sample—a complementary investigation must be employed. In such cases broader non‑destructive investigations—including deflection testing, faulting measurements, ground‑penetrating radar, visual inspection, ride‑quality assessments, and in‑place nuclear or electrical impedance density measurements—are used to locate distressed joints, assess overall structural performance, and verify material properties when core data are insufficient or unavailable [1][2].

Existing information and records

In preparing a pavement‑core investigation the reviewer must first gather all documentation that characterizes the existing section, including detailed pre‑construction manual distress surveys and post‑construction manual distress surveys together with any core samples taken during the three‑to‑five‑year period after construction. The file of previous cores and the recorded short‑term (construction) and long‑term (five‑to‑ten‑year) performance data for polymer‑modified binders under the region’s climate, traffic regimes and project types should also be examined. Recent automated performance survey reports that list identified distresses such as flushing, potholes and rut depth measured with a twelve‑foot straight edge must be reviewed, noting whether any segment exceeds the threshold of thirty percent of the area having two or more low‑severity distresses, which would trigger remediation. Condition surveys that quantify surface distress—rutting severity, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth and crack width, and raveling or weathering severity—must be evaluated using the established thresholds of less than one‑and‑a‑half inches rut depth, cracking depth under one inch with average crack widths not exceeding a medium rating of half an inch, and raveling/weathering limited to a medium rating. Reports describing the condition of the base, sub‑base and subgrade layers as well as any prior sampling or laboratory test results should be gathered. Prior core‑drilling records and laboratory test results for those cores—including void content determined per PTM No. 715 and AASHTO T‑209, asphalt percentage per PTM No. 702 or other approved methods, and gradation per PTM No. 739—must be collected. The construction and maintenance history for the segment, typically stored in the Roadway Management System, provides essential context and should include documentation of emergency repairs, routine snow removal, crack sealing or other work that could influence core condition. Traffic information such as average daily traffic counts and the average initial truck factors used for ESAL calculations (as listed in Table 7.1) must be incorporated to relate existing structural capacity to anticipated loading, and traffic classification counts should be used to verify actual ESALs against design estimates. [1][2]

The existing project files frequently omit the quantitative surface‑distress measurements needed to calculate the exact percent of area requiring patching, which prevents verification of whether the ten‑percent threshold that triggers a cost‑effectiveness analysis is exceeded. Records also lack documented concrete properties such as compressive strength, air content, frost susceptibility and friction performance, leaving uncertainty about durability. Information on subbase or subgrade thickness, material quality, drainage capability, or even the presence of a subbase is often missing, creating inconsistency when structural coefficients are calculated for overlay designs. For asphalt sections that use non‑standard polymer‑modified binders (e.g., PG 64E‑22), baseline core results and long‑term distress observations are rarely provided, so the expected performance under Pennsylvania traffic and climate conditions remains unclear. The absence of control sections and post‑construction distress surveys further widens the gap in evaluating these experimental binders. When cores are unavailable, reliance on Roadway Management System pavement history is required, yet those data sets are frequently incomplete or contradictory, compromising the reliability of existing‑pavement inputs. Design documents list target temperatures, lot sizes, densities, asphalt contents, gradations and nominal thicknesses but do not confirm field density, bulk specific gravity, true asphalt percentage, macrotexture condition, or the presence of defects such as pattern segregation or flushing. Payment‑factor calculations that reveal a lot payment factor below ninety, an asphalt‑content factor under eighty, or a density factor under eighty signal possible inconsistencies that must be verified through on‑site coring. Thickness measurements are often limited to design values; any measured depth exceeding a quarter‑inch deviation from plan or showing a uniform deficiency greater than one‑eighth inch over three consecutive locations requires additional core extraction to map the shortfall and confirm compliance with the minimum allowable thickness. Macrotexture data are missing, yet differences in average texture depth greater than 0.024 in between segregated and non‑segregated zones indicate unacceptable segregation, while an average texture of 0.006 in or less denotes flushing that must be documented through core testing. Reinforced concrete records frequently do not demonstrate that the average density reaches at least ninety‑eight percent of maximum wet density with no individual test below ninety‑six percent, nor do they confirm early‑age (3,000 psi) and 28‑day (3,500 psi) compressive strengths or a strength‑pay factor exceeding fifty‑five percent. When laboratory results are non‑repeatable or quality‑control charts, companion sample data, and trend analyses are absent, the uncertainty can only be resolved by obtaining definitive bulk‑density, asphalt‑content, gradation, void content and strength values from cores. Occasional reports of 28‑day compressive strengths below the required 3,500 psi lack explanation, and limited air‑content and strength testing on selected cores raises doubts about representativeness, necessitating core extraction at each full‑depth repair segment. Box samples that flag a lot as defective for excessive asphalt content or poor sieve‑passing results often lack clear sample locations, trend analyses and complete quality‑control data, creating further uncertainty that must be addressed through precisely located 6‑inch cores. Visual inspection and condition assessment

The core assessment is intended to confirm that only surface‑level distresses are present and that no load‑related distress or base problems are evident [1]. Rutting severity is examined with a target of less than 1 ½ inches, and the depth is verified using a twelve‑foot straight edge [1][2]. Transverse and miscellaneous cracking is checked for a maximum depth of one inch and an average crack width not exceeding the medium rating of half an inch, while medium‑severity cracks that influence six inches on either side of the fracture are also recorded as indicators of fatigue or inadequate support [1][2]. Edge slumping is acceptable only up to one‑eighth inch between adjacent lanes; any greater displacement is considered defective, and geometric irregularities such as depressions, high points, or faulting exceeding a quarter‑inch—or depressions larger than three‑sixteenths inch between lanes—are likewise classified as defects [2]. Edge spalling or longitudinal edge spall that cannot be satisfactorily repaired must be documented, as must any spalling observed between lanes [2]. Surface damage attributable to freeze–thaw cycling, rain infiltration, improper joint construction, inadequate curing, or an unsatisfactory final finish is required to be identified, and honeycomb voids or other honeycombing are specifically noted [2]. Raveling or weathering may be present only to a medium rating characterized by a rough, pitted surface with loose particles [1]. Minor corrugation, shoving, slippage, flushing, and minor bleeding are tolerated provided their average depth remains below one‑½ inch and low friction numbers are observed [1][2]. Potholes, evidence of pumping or settlement, and broken aggregate indicating insufficient compaction are also examined [2]. Finally, the core is inspected for compliance with thickness tolerances, ensuring that underlying layers are not more than a half inch short of design specifications or density requirements [2].

Pavement cores are employed to classify observed distresses by their primary cause—load‑related versus climate or materials‑related, including drainage issues—and also by the specific distress types defined in Publication 336 [1][2]. The classification proceeds by assigning each distress a type according to the rating procedures of Publication 336 while simultaneously noting its causal category as described in the other guide [1][2]. Severity is assigned using the same rating system; low‑severity items are recorded as such, medium‑severity cracks are treated as affecting a six‑inch width on each side for their full length, and when the percent patching required exceeds ten percent an analysis of cost‑effectiveness must be performed [1][2]. Extent is quantified through the extent‑estimation methods of Publication 336, with core results incorporated so that any depth or surface irregularity beyond defined tolerances expands the extent to include all sublots represented by adjacent deficient cores [2][1]. Surface profile measurements are taken with a twelve‑foot straightedge and any irregularity greater than three‑sixteenths of an inch is flagged as defective, while core drilling uses six‑inch diameter cylinders at prescribed intervals; depth deficiencies greater than one‑quarter inch are recorded as defective work, and a deficiency exceeding one‑eighth inch in three or more consecutive cores also constitutes defect [2]. The visible surface distress must be measured precisely and tabulated, and cores taken near deteriorated joints and at random locations reveal the depth and spread of deterioration beneath the slab [1][2]. All findings—including type, severity rating, quantified extent derived from both surface and core data, and the precise location referenced to the segment level of the Department’s Location Reference System or State Route segment—are entered into a systematic report [2][1]. The exact position of each core is documented within this reference system so that the spatial distribution of distresses can be mapped for later analysis [2][1]. Results are compiled and reported within fourteen days of the survey, and if a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses covering at least thirty percent of the segment area, remedial action such as microsurfacing is required [2]. The combined documentation also supports the cost‑effectiveness evaluation triggered when patching percentages exceed ten percent, guiding selection of alternative treatment approaches [1].

Field measurements and testing

When a full‑depth cylindrical core is removed—commonly six inches in diameter and taken as soon as possible after placement, often within the same day—the extracted sample serves as the basis for a series of direct measurements and laboratory tests that characterize layer thicknesses, material densities, and mechanical properties [2]. The core’s compacted thickness is measured with the calibrated apparatus prescribed in PTM 614 or PTM 737; any individual measurement that falls short of the design depth by more than one‑quarter inch triggers additional coring, while a shortfall exceeding one‑half inch declares the slab defective, and a pattern of deficiencies greater than ¼ in at one location or more than ⅛ in over three consecutive points also signals non‑conformance [2]. When coring is impractical or used as a complement, nondestructive thickness surveys such as ground‑penetrating radar or impact‑echo are performed in accordance with PTM 605 to estimate pavement depth without extracting material [2][1]. Prior to coring, field density can be estimated nondestructively with an approved nuclear gauge (PTM 402) or an electrical impedance gauge (PTM 403), providing a rapid indication of the compacted mat’s percent compaction that later forms the basis for the density payment factor (PF_D) [2]. After extraction, the core is cleaned and refilled for bulk‑specific‑gravity (Gmb) and bulk‑density determination following PTM 715, with PTM 716 applied when necessary; a loose‑mix sample taken at the same location is tested to AASHTO T209 to obtain the theoretical maximum specific gravity (Gmm), and the ratio Gmb/Gmm expressed as a percentage indicates percent compaction [2]. For concrete pavements, the core supplies specimens for entrained‑air analysis under PTM 623 and for 28‑day compressive‑strength testing per PTM 606 (or PTM 604); a minimum strength of 3,500 psi is required and a quality index (Percent‑Within‑Limits) of at least 55 percent must be achieved [2]. For asphaltic pavements the core material is used to determine binder content with PTM 757 or PTM 702, to verify that the proportion of binder meets contract limits, and to perform aggregate gradation by sieving, reporting the percent passing the No. 200 (75‑µm) sieve as well as the primary control sieve for calculation of PF200 and PFPCS payment factors [2]. Volumetric testing of the core provides air‑void values that are compared with the design N_design(V_a), and the mixture’s maximum specific gravity is used to confirm that compaction and binder proportions satisfy the mix design [2]. Surface condition that may reflect underlying quality issues is assessed nondestructively with a macrotexture test according to PTM 751; an average texture depth difference greater than 0.024 in between segregated and non‑segregated zones indicates unacceptable segregation, while an overall average texture depth of 0.006 in or less identifies flushing and renders the pavement defective [2]. Visual inspection of each core records surface defects such as rain damage, improper joint construction, curing flaws, honeycombing, cracking, depressions or high points, all of which signal potential durability problems [2]. Deflection testing is performed on the slab to reveal its structural stiffness, and faulting measurements quantify joint movement, together providing insight into the pavement’s load‑bearing capacity [1]. Ground‑penetrating radar surveys detect anomalies in the subbase or subgrade, complementing thickness estimates and helping locate voids or delamination [1][2]. Surface friction tests assess slip resistance, while roughness measurements gauge ride quality; the MIRA system integrates these observations into an overall condition rating for both the existing surface and subsurface layers [1]. Specialized concrete inspection tools such as MIT‑Scan 2 verify dowel bar alignment and MIT‑Scan T2 confirm concrete thickness, and a tensile bond tester evaluates the bond strength of any repair material applied to the core [1]. The direct measurements obtained from the extracted core—layer thicknesses, material types, bulk specific gravity, air content, compressive strength, binder content, gradation, and macrotexture—are entered into the Component Method together with appropriate structural coefficients to compute an effective Structural Number that indicates the remaining load‑bearing capacity of the pavement [1]. When coring is not feasible or as a complementary approach, nondestructive testing methods incorporated in software such as DARWin can estimate the same effective Structural Number; the program offers three calculation routes—the Component Method, the Remaining Life Method, and the Non‑Destructive Testing Method—and for asphalt concrete overlay designs either the Component Method or the NDT route is recommended [1]. Sampling frequency guidelines typically require at least three cores from suspect (e.g., pattern‑segregated) areas and three from normal sections, with additional cores per sublot, per tonnage placed, or per repair length to ensure representative coverage; every fifth core may be submitted as an information sample for entrained‑air review and every tenth core for depth verification or concrete performance testing [2]. Optional nondestructive evaluation methods such as additional ground‑penetrating radar or impact‑echo surveys may be employed after written approval from the District Executive, providing further insight without further damaging the pavement [2].

Pavement‑core testing must be performed with a full‑depth coring drill that can extract cylinders at least six inches in diameter for conventional asphalt or concrete overlays; a four‑inch bit is prescribed when pervious concrete is involved. The drill is operated under the direct supervision of the Inspector or Representative, who selects locations according to the random‑selection procedures of PTM 1 (and PTM 729 where applicable). A calibrated measuring device that conforms to PTM 614 and a depth‑measuring instrument meeting PTM 737 are required for all cores. After extraction each core is rinsed with water and backfilled within twenty‑four hours using a material that matches the original mix design – SMA, Superpave, JMF, or a non‑shrink grout – and compacted to seal the hole.

The pavement lane is divided into lots not exceeding three thousand square yards; one core is taken from each lot, which also satisfies the rule of one core per two thousand linear feet of lane or one core for every five hundred tons of material placed. Retest cores are positioned twenty‑four inches downstream (or upstream if necessary) from the original sample.

All cores are tested for bulk specific gravity, density (lb/ft³), asphalt content (PTM 757 or 702), and density using PTM 715/716; the same methods apply to any retests requested within three weeks of result release. Coring must occur on a dry surface with ambient air or surface temperature above 35 °F (55 °F for pervious concrete) and traffic control is required throughout the operation. Sampling and laboratory investigation

Pavement core sampling is intended to verify the thickness, material type, and performance characteristics of every structural layer that influences pavement behavior, which includes the rigid concrete slab or overlay, each asphalt‑concrete lift (binder and wearing courses), any additional compacted asphalt mixtures, asphalt‑reinforced base courses, pervious‑concrete sections, longitudinal joint zones, as well as the underlying subbase and subgrade soils when existing conditions are being assessed [1][2]. The sampling plan is organized into lots that represent a contiguous area of uniform depth not exceeding 5,600 square yards; each lot is subdivided into four equal sublots of about 1,400 square yards for concrete slabs and asphalt lifts, while rigid‑pavement overlays are limited to no more than 3,000 square yards per traffic lane, with ramps treated as separate lots [2]. Within each sublot a random coring location is selected in accordance with the applicable procedural test method (PTM No. 1), avoiding transitional zones, and the inspector may direct additional locations when visible distress or suspected deficiencies are observed; cores are also taken near deteriorated joints to capture conditions beneath those features [1][2]. A six‑inch diameter core is the standard size, and it must be drilled through the full thickness of the layer under investigation—through the entire concrete slab or overlay, through all asphalt lifts down to the existing pavement or base, or through the complete base‑course material—and for subbase or subgrade evaluation the core is extended until that soil layer is reached [1][2]. Core length is determined by the relevant test method, with PTM No. 614 governing concrete cores and PTM No. 737 governing base‑course cores; the measured depth of each core may not fall more than 0.5 inch below the design thickness (D), and for reinforced cement concrete a tighter tolerance of ¼ inch is required, prompting additional closely spaced cores if this limit is exceeded [2]. The number of cores collected is driven by both the extent of observed distress and prescribed quantitative criteria; when visible distress is extensive the sampling set is enlarged, while the standard practice also calls for one depth‑check core for approximately every 2,000 feet of lane length and supplemental random cores at a rate of one six‑inch core per 1,000 tons of material placed (or per 500 tons when yield calculations suggest possible insufficient depth) [1][2]. For projects involving compacted lifts, the maximum of one core per sublot is typical unless the representative authorizes additional cores, and for rigid‑pavement overlays smaller than 3,000 square yards two cores are required; pervious‑concrete areas receive three four‑inch cores for each 5,000 square feet, while longitudinal joint zones are sampled with six‑inch cores in each 2,500‑foot sublot, yielding five cores per lot and optional companion cores for quality‑control density testing [2]. When a performance survey identifies fatigue cracking or other distress that may indicate an unacceptable base condition, a series of additional six‑inch cores is taken within the affected area at locations directed by the department, with depth recorded and laboratory testing performed according to PTM No. 715 and AASHTO T‑209 [2]. For existing pavement thickness verification during repairs, at least one core per lane is obtained at each full‑depth repair location when the repair length is less than 100 feet, and for longer repairs cores are spaced at minimum 100‑foot intervals, with further cores possible as directed by the representative to confirm underlying conditions before design of precast slabs [1]. All coring operations are performed in the presence of the inspector or representative, and cores must be handled without compression, bending, or distortion before being sealed, transported, and submitted for laboratory analysis [2][1].

Pavement cores are first measured in the field to verify that their thickness is within design tolerances, typically no more than a quarter‑inch short for rigid concrete and not exceeding 0.5 in short for individual cores, with pervious concrete allowed a range of –3/8 in to +3/4 in [2]. The retrieved core is then split into two molded cylinders that are cured and tested at three, seven and twenty‑eight days in accordance with AASHTO T‑24 (or ASTM C‑1435) and PTM No. 604/606 to obtain compressive‑strength values, the lot being accepted only when the three‑day or seven‑day strength reaches at least 3,000 psi and the twenty‑eight‑day strength is 3,500 psi or greater, with a minimum of 3,000 psi required before traffic may be opened [2]. Simultaneously an entrained‑air test performed by AASHTO T‑152/T‑196 or PTM No. 623 must produce a result between roughly 3.5 % and 8.5 %, or a percent‑within‑limits of at least 55 %, to confirm adequate plastic air for freeze‑thaw durability [2]. A fresh‑concrete verification sample taken from the same sublot is analyzed for water content by AASHTO T‑318 so that the calculated water‑to‑cementitious ratio can be checked within ±0.03 of plant records, a control that limits shrinkage cracking and honeycombing [2]. Density evaluation of the cured cylinders follows PTM No. 715 (or PTM No. 716 when necessary) to determine bulk specific gravity (Gmb) and bulk density, which are compared with the theoretical maximum specific gravity (Gmm) obtained from AASHTO T‑209/Bulletin 27; compaction is deemed acceptable when percent compaction exceeds 92 % of Gmm for asphalt or at least 91 % for concrete, and contractor daily Gmm values must lie within ±0.030 of the laboratory result [2]. For asphalt pavements the cores are further analyzed for binder content using PTM No. 757 or PTM No. 702 (Modified Method D) and for fine‑aggregate gradation by determining the percent passing a 75‑µm (No. 200) sieve and a 2.36‑mm (No. 8) sieve, each result required to be within ±2 % of the joint mix formula, typically expressed as at least 85 % passing for both sieves, with larger deviations indicating segregation, flushing, shoving, cracking or aggregate breakage [2]. Surface macrotexture depth is recorded in the field according to PTM No. 751 and linked to the core data to assist in evaluating ride quality and potential rutting susceptibility [2]. For pervious concrete cores oven‑dry density (ASTM C‑1754) and percent void content are measured, each required to be within ±5 % of the test‑section average, while adjacent sections undergo infiltration testing per ASTM C‑1701 to verify that permeability remains above the required minimum, thereby detecting clogging or loss of void structure [2]. Visual inspection of the split core also identifies durability problems such as aggregate degradation or frost damage, providing direct evidence of distress mechanisms that may not be captured by quantitative tests alone [1]. In addition to concrete quality indicators, the cores supply material for evaluating subbase or subgrade soil conditions and for determining the depth of deterioration beneath the surface, information that guides decisions on rehabilitation scope and method [1]. Collectively these laboratory examinations assess structural capacity, dimensional compliance, mix proportioning, compaction quality, air‑void integrity, binder dosage, aggregate gradation, as well as durability mechanisms including freeze‑thaw damage, shrinkage cracking, honeycombing, rutting and loss of permeability [2][1].

Structural and functional performance

To evaluate a pavement’s structural capacity and functional condition, cores are taken both adjacent to visibly deteriorated joints and at random locations so that layer thicknesses, subbase or subgrade soil conditions, and any hidden distress can be quantified. The extraction follows the core‑length requirements of PTM No. 614 and is performed in the presence of a representative or inspector who measures each full‑depth core according to PTM No. 737. Individual core depths are averaged for each lot; the average may not be more than one‑half inch below the design thickness, and no single core may be deficient by more than a quarter‑inch unless additional coring is performed to correct the shortfall. The remaining asphalt thickness obtained from the core must exceed the milling depth by at least one‑and‑a‑half inches. Rutting severity measured on the core surface should be less than one‑and‑a‑half inches, while transverse and miscellaneous cracks are recorded with depths under one inch and average widths not exceeding a half‑inch. Corrugation, shoving or slippage are also assessed, with an average depth below one‑and‑a‑half inches. Friction values derived from the core surface are checked against low‑friction limits, and roughness or raveling ratings are noted as part of the functional assessment. Non‑destructive deflection testing and faulting measurements are conducted on the pavement to identify joints that may require replacement and to provide an additional indication of structural capacity. Ride‑quality evaluations are performed to capture overall surface smoothness and user experience. In‑place density of the extracted material must average at least 98 % of the maximum wet density with no individual test falling below 96 %, while hardened (oven‑dry) density is required to be within ±5 lb/ft³ of the target and not to exceed 130 lb/ft³. Void content is quantified, targeting a void structure of approximately 20 % with an allowable variation of plus or minus five percentage points. Concrete compressive strength is determined from two cylinders per sublot tested at 28 days; the average must reach a minimum of 3,500 psi and early‑age checks at three and seven days require at least 3,000 psi, with a percent‑within‑limits (PWL) of 55 % or greater. Air content is measured either as entrained‑air percentage, which must lie between 3.5 % and 8.5 %, or as plastic‑air content, and the PWL for air content also must meet the 55 % threshold. Infiltration testing on the core requires an average water‑absorption rate of at least 200 inches per hour. Visual inspection of the core surface records defects such as cracking, honeycombing, rain damage, improper joint construction, curing problems, edge slumping, depressions or high points that cannot be satisfactorily repaired, completing the set of measurements needed to judge both structural capacity and functional condition. The pavement cores extracted for evaluation reveal distress levels that remain within the limits established by the respective design guides, including rutting depths under 1½ in., transverse and miscellaneous cracking depths below 1 in., crack widths not exceeding a medium rating of 0.5 in., and raveling severity classified as medium, while cores taken from fatigue‑cracking zones also meet material quality criteria such as density greater than 92 % of theoretical maximum, air voids between roughly five and 8.5 %, and asphalt content and gradation within mix‑design tolerances [1][2]. Because the high‑intermediate‑rate (HIR) asphalt mixtures are specified to perform under any average daily traffic level, these measured distress parameters align with the anticipated traffic loading and the design assumptions that underpin the selection of the HIR treatment, just as the fatigue‑cracking cores are judged consistent with original loading assumptions when cumulative ESALs have not exceeded 100 % of the projected twenty‑year allocation [1][2]. The core data provide layer thicknesses and material coefficients that are used to compute an Effective Existing Structural Number, which is then compared against a Future Traffic Structural Number derived from average initial truck factors and the applicable design thresholds; similarly, the fatigue‑cracking cores are evaluated against a minimum engineered thickness requirement, with the average core depth permitted to be no more than one‑half inch below the specified design thickness [1][2]. Where the Effective Existing Structural Number falls short of the Future Traffic target, the methodology calls for additional overlay thickness so that the Overlay Design Structural Number is achieved, ensuring the pavement can accommodate projected ESALs and meet its intended remaining service life, while cores that fail to meet the half‑inch depth reduction or other mechanical thresholds such as a compressive‑strength quality index below 55 % trigger remedial action under warranty provisions [1][2]. A specific threshold excludes sub‑base layers from structural evaluation when 18‑kip ESALs exceed five million unless their condition has been verified, and the fatigue‑cracking assessment likewise requires that observed ESALs remain within the design envelope to deem the pavement adequate for future loading [1][2]. Overall, the measured conditions satisfy the prescribed thresholds across both guides, confirming that the existing structure can be rehabilitated with the HIR surface or appropriate overlay and indicating that the remaining service‑life requirements can be met provided any necessary thickness adjustments are implemented to achieve the required structural number [1][2].

Data interpretation and diagnosis

The engineer initiates a systematic visual survey of the pavement surface, quantifying each type of distress—rut depth, crack depth and width—and comparing them against defined limits of less than 1½ in. rut severity, less than 1.0 in. crack depth, and an average crack width not exceeding ½ in. [1]. Simultaneously, any visual cue such as pattern segregation, flushing, fatigue cracking, rain damage, improper joint work, curing errors, honey‑combing or excessive surface irregularities prompts targeted field measurements to verify the severity of the observed condition [2]. For pattern segregation the macrotexture depth is measured and a value greater than 0.024 in. signals a problem, while flushing is flagged when texture depth is 0.006 in. or less [2]. A straightedge inspection that reveals surface irregularities exceeding three‑sixteenths of an inch, a depth shortfall beyond one‑quarter inch, or a one‑eighth‑inch deficit in three adjacent cores also flags the segment for further testing [2]. Field measurements then include non‑destructive techniques such as deflection testing, faulting measurement, ground‑penetrating radar scans, friction and roughness assessments, together with the specific texture, slope and depth checks described above [1][2]. The safety‑edge slope is recorded, and if it does not meet the prescribed slope requirement paving must be stopped pending corrective action [2]. Cores are extracted both adjacent to visibly deteriorated joints and at random locations to capture subbase, base and subgrade variability, with a minimum of three 6‑inch diameter cores taken from affected zones and an equal number from unaffected zones no later than one day after placement [1][2]. The average core depth is required to remain within half an inch of the design thickness and no individual core may fall below that limit; any core deeper than this threshold in three consecutive locations also constitutes a defect [2]. Each extracted core undergoes a visual inspection for packaging integrity and aggregate breakage, and compromised security tape mandates replacement cores and a restart of the integration process [2]. Laboratory analysis determines compressive strength, air content, material composition and, when required, the quantity of rejuvenator needed, while bulk specific gravity and bulk density are measured to calculate percent theoretical maximum density, which must exceed 92 % of the contractor‑provided value [1][2]. Additional laboratory tests evaluate void content, asphalt percentage, gradation, and, where applicable, a compressive‑strength quality index expressed as Percent Within Limits, with a minimum acceptable PWL of 55 % for both strength and plastic air content [2]. All laboratory results are cross‑referenced with the visual‑field observations, construction and maintenance history, and traffic volume records to determine whether distress is confined to the surface or reflects deeper structural deficiencies [1]; they are also checked against Joint Materials Formula tolerances, allowing no more than a 20 % deviation in any two sieve percentages and requiring compliance with density, asphalt content and No. 200 sieve limits [2]. The integrated dataset—comprising quantified visual distress, field measurement outputs, core laboratory properties, and performance histories—is assembled to provide a single interpretation of pavement condition [1]; performance thresholds such as minimum thickness, ≥92 % theoretical maximum density, PWL ≥ 55 %, macrotexture limits and irregularity limits supply the final verdict on acceptability [2]. If the calculated percent of area requiring patching exceeds 10 % (excluding existing sound concrete patches), a cost‑effectiveness analysis is triggered to compare simple patching with more extensive rehabilitation alternatives, while any parameter that surpasses its specified limit classifies the segment as defective and initiates immediate corrective actions such as stopping paving or conducting cause analysis [1][2]. This holistic integration of visual observations, field measurements, laboratory results and performance data ensures that both observed symptoms and measured material properties drive repair‑strategy selection, aligning design decisions with the actual condition of the pavement [1][2].

The cores often reveal that overloading is a primary factor when traffic counts during the warranty period exceed the design ESALs, indicating loads beyond the pavement’s intended capacity.[1][2] Inadequate drainage emerges from climate‑related distress and from rain or low temperature exposure before final curing, which can cause surface ponding, fine‑material accumulation in the subgrade, and failure to meet the required infiltration rate of at least 200 in/hr.[1][2] Weak support is indicated when fatigue cracking or other distress suggests an unacceptable base, prompting coring to verify subgrade quality, and it may be compounded by a subgrade that has eroded or become saturated with fine materials.[1][2] Material deterioration is signaled by compressive‑strength quality indices or air‑content performance falling below the 55 percent threshold, as well as by aging‑related loss of structural capacity noted in climate‑material distress.[1][2] Construction defects are identified when core thickness falls short of design by more than one‑quarter inch at any location, or by more than one‑eighth inch across three adjacent cores, and especially when deficits reach half an inch or greater that cannot be corrected, reflecting failure to achieve the specified compacted depth.[2] Additional workmanship issues such as inadequate compaction, improper joint cutting, curing, finishing, surface irregularities exceeding one‑quarter inch, cracking, honeycombing, or rain‑induced damage further point to construction‑related causes.[2] Environmental exposure also includes low air temperatures below 35–40 °F during placement of pervious concrete or ARBC, which can prevent proper curing and lead to surface defects.[2] While aging is recognized as a contributing factor in climate/material distress, the guidelines do not assign it a specific quantitative threshold beyond its inclusion among material deterioration mechanisms.[1]

Findings, confidence, and next actions

Pavement cores give engineers a direct, documented means of confirming whether an existing pavement meets its design specifications for thickness, density, material composition and strength, allowing the calculation of an effective structural number that can be used to evaluate proposed asphalt or bonded‑concrete‑over‑asphalt overlays [2][1]. By extracting cores near deteriorated joints as well as at random locations, the subbase or subgrade conditions can be verified and the vertical extent of distress beneath the surface quantified, providing the basis for a cost‑effectiveness analysis when concrete patching exceeds ten percent of the pavement area, not counting patches already in good condition [1]. The laboratory results from those cores supply direct measurements of compressive strength, air content and percent patching, which together indicate whether the pavement is a viable candidate for rehabilitation or hot‑in‑place recycling (HIR), and they also determine the exact amount of rejuvenator required for the HIR mix design [1][2]. For overlay designs, core‑derived layer thicknesses and material coefficients are entered into structural analysis procedures to confirm that the existing bottom asphalt layers are free of stripping—a prerequisite for specifying a bonded overlay when the underlying surface is judged fair to good—and to ensure that the proposed overlay will satisfy anticipated traffic loads [1][2]. The acceptance criteria applied to core data include specific dimensional thresholds: any individual depth deficiency greater than one‑quarter inch triggers additional coring, a single core deficient by more than one‑half inch or a uniform deficiency of more than one‑eighth inch across three consecutive locations classifies the section as defective, and the average lot depth must not fall below the design thickness minus 0.5 inch (or minus half an inch for asphalt) with only one sublot allowed to breach that limit [2]. Bulk specific gravity and density values derived from each core must meet contractual lower limits of typically 91 %–92 % of theoretical maximum density, with a percent‑within‑limits (PWL) of at least 100 % for density; entrained‑air content is required to lie between 3.5 % and 8.5 %, and compressive‑strength testing on cylinders taken from cores must achieve the prescribed minimum values with a PWL of at least 55 %; failure to meet these thresholds renders the lot defective [2]. For pervious concrete, oven‑dry density and void content must be within ±5 % of the accepted test section, while macrotexture measurements identify segregation when the average texture depth difference exceeds 0.024 inch and signal flushing when the mean texture depth is 0.006 inch or less, both conditions leading to a defective classification [2]. Despite these capabilities, the conclusions drawn from core data remain limited by their point‑sample nature; because each core represents only a single location, it cannot capture full spatial variability of thickness, density, composition or subgrade quality, necessitating supplemental nondestructive testing or additional sampling to reduce uncertainty between locations and underlying causes of distress [1][2]. When cores are unavailable, reliance on historical Roadway Management System data introduces further doubt regarding actual layer conditions, and the often‑poor condition of existing subbase material may be omitted from structural evaluations, diminishing confidence in overall capacity estimates for overlay designs [1]. The sampling strategy prescribed by the guides typically limits extraction to one random core per sublot unless a deficiency is detected, which can miss localized variations; selection bias, the requirement that cores be taken within 24 hours of placement, and the possibility of core damage or paraffin‑wax coating that precludes retesting for density further reduce representativeness and confidence in the results [2]. Moreover, specifications provide limited guidance on sampling frequency, measurement uncertainty and treatment of non‑repeatable results, and they address only the properties directly tested—thickness, bulk density, asphalt content, air voids, strength and macrotexture—leaving other performance issues such as surface cracking, honeycombing, joint construction errors or curing problems unevaluated [2]. In HIR applications, core‑based assessment addresses only short‑term interim performance and rejuvenator dosing, leaving long‑term structural behavior uncertain, and while cores can verify the absence of stripping for bonded overlays, their point‑specific nature limits the ability to predict long‑term overlay performance across an entire project area [1].

After a pavement core is recovered, the project team expands laboratory results with both destructive and nondestructive investigations that include deflection and faulting measurements, ground‑penetrating radar surveys, friction and roughness testing, MIRA condition assessments, MIT Scan 2 dowel‑bar alignment checks, MIT Scan T2 thickness scans, and tensile‑bond testing. In parallel the measured slab thickness is checked against design tolerances; the average depth of each lot may not be more than one‑half inch below the specified thickness and no individual sublot may fall short by that amount, while a single core low by more than one‑quarter inch or three consecutive cores each low by more than one‑eighth inch also classifies the section as defective. When such a deficiency is identified supplemental six‑inch diameter cores are taken at approximately 100‑foot intervals—or for every 500 tons of material placed—to delineate the extent of the shortfall, and each hole is backfilled with the original mix or an approved non‑shrink grout, compacted, and protected from traffic for at least twenty‑four hours or until pavement temperature drops below 140 °F. A structured monitoring program follows the core evaluation; detailed pre‑construction distress surveys are recorded, post‑construction manual distress surveys are repeated, and additional core sampling is performed for three to five years according to the sampling plans supplied by LTS and NECEPT, with accompanying binder and loose‑box specimens collected to confirm material properties. Core retention follows a systematic plan in which the first core and every tenth thereafter are retained as information samples, while every fifth core is submitted for entrained‑air content testing (PTM 623) and 28‑day compressive‑strength testing (PTM 606/604), with bulk specific gravity, density, asphalt content, gradation, voids, and plastic‑air content also evaluated; the Percent Within Limits for strength and air must be at least 55 % (or 50 % where specified). Density requirements are enforced by material type—base courses must achieve a minimum of 92 % of theoretical maximum density, reinforced cement concrete layers an average of not less than 98 % with no individual test below 96 %, and any mix‑design limit may not be lower than 91 %—and failure for two consecutive lots (or three total) triggers a stop‑work order and requires the contractor to propose corrective actions such as mix‑design adjustments, placement or compaction changes before resuming. The cores are also examined for rut depth, transverse and miscellaneous crack depth, crack width, and raveling; acceptance limits are roughly 1½ in. rutting, less than 1 in. cracking depth with average widths no greater than 0.5 in., and a medium rating for raveling. When the measured values meet those thresholds, the laboratory data are used to calculate the required Hot‑In‑Place Rejuvenator dosage for a typical HIR thickness of 1½ in. (range 1–2 in.) while ensuring that at least 1½–2 in. of underlying asphalt remains in composite sections or 2–3 in. in full‑depth sections; if any limit is exceeded, corrective measures such as additional milling, base repair, or selection of an alternative preservation treatment are implemented before proceeding. The effective structural number of the existing pavement is then determined using the Component Method or a nondestructive testing approach in DARWin and compared with the future‑traffic structural number to size any overlay; when core data indicate that the subbase may be deficient, it is excluded from the structural evaluation on Interstate facilities, while for other routes with design ESALs greater than five million a targeted subbase investigation is required to verify quality and drainability before inclusion. Cores are inspected for stripping in the lower asphalt layers; if stripping is absent and surface condition is judged fair‑to‑good, a bonded fiber‑reinforced concrete overlay can be pursued, otherwise an unbonded overlay is specified, and residual‑strength testing according to ASTM C1609 provides the f150 value that guides macro‑fiber content and any necessary fresh‑mix adjustments for workability and air entrainment. Depending on whether observed distress is load‑related or climate/materials‑related, design checks direct corrective actions such as slab stabilization, dowel retrofits, cross‑stitching, full‑depth patching, installation of base drains, diamond grinding or grooving, and joint cleaning and sealing to preserve performance. When the proportion of concrete requiring patching approaches the ten‑percent threshold a cost‑effectiveness analysis is required before selecting a repair method. Preservation strategies include maintaining polyethylene curing sheets for at least seven days, performing edge‑slump checks with a straightedge, conducting joint wipe tests, and protecting joints with ramps or mats until the pavement attains the required compressive strength and surface quality before opening to traffic; annual performance surveys, nondestructive thickness measurements on small intersections, traffic‑load counts to verify ESAL assumptions, and infiltration testing for pervious concrete (average rate ≥200 in/hr) continue to monitor condition. If a lot is declared defective, the department may apply a reduced overall payment factor of 70 % or order complete removal and replacement of the affected lane width plus at least five feet beyond each end (or a minimum ten‑foot segment between transverse joints); the contractor may request retesting within three weeks, in which case new cores are drilled offset twenty‑four inches longitudinally, tested again for density, air content, strength and gradation, and the retest values are used if the original results lack repeatability.
