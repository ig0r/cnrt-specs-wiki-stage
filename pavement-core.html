Pavement Core

Part of: Pavement Condition Assessment is-subtopic-of
Pavement Core is a topic under Pavement Condition Assessment.
pavement-corescorescore-sampling
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-30

Pavement core is a field‑based investigation that extracts six‑inch (or comparable) cylindrical samples from existing rigid or flexible pavements to directly reveal the condition of each structural layer—including subbase, subgrade, asphalt, concrete, stone‑matrix‑asphalt, pervious concrete and other constructed courses[1][2]. By testing the cores for thickness, density, bulk specific gravity, air‑void content, compressive strength, binder content, fine‑aggregate gradation and macro‑texture, engineers obtain laboratory‑verified data that confirm whether layer dimensions and material properties meet design tolerances and identify hidden distress such as stripping or subgrade weakness[1][2]. These point‑specific measurements feed structural‑capacity calculations (e.g., effective Structural Number) and drive decisions on overlays, repairs, or exclusion of deficient layers from the analysis, making pavement coring the factual foundation for subsequent design and maintenance actions[1][2].

Purpose and investigation objectives

The pavement‑core investigation is employed to characterize the existing pavement system by extracting cylindrical samples that reveal the condition of subbase or subgrade soils as well as the integrity of overlying asphalt, concrete, pervious concrete, stone‑matrix‑asphalt, asphalt‑rubber binder courses, or other constructed layers [1][2]. Cores are taken both adjacent to visibly distressed joints and at randomly selected locations so that hidden deterioration beneath the surface can be identified and the extent of any subgrade weakness quantified [1][2]. The first question addressed is whether the measured thickness of each structural layer matches the design depth within allowable tolerances; a single core may not be more than one‑quarter inch short, three consecutive cores may not collectively fall short by more than one‑eighth inch, and any deficiency of half an inch or greater that cannot be corrected is deemed defective [2]. When a thickness shortfall is found, the investigation records the magnitude of the deficit, its location along the sublot, and determines whether additional coring is required to confirm the extent of the problem [2][1]. For flexible asphalt layers the cores are tested for bulk specific gravity, theoretical maximum specific gravity, and percent compaction, with the requirement that percent compaction not fall below 50 % of the specification limit, or, if only one characteristic is low, not drop below 64 % [2]. The same testing verifies that asphalt binder content lies within contract tolerances and that fine‑aggregate gradation meets the mix formula, expressed as the percent passing a No. 200 (75 µm) sieve and the primary control sieve [2]. When pattern segregation or flushing is suspected, macro‑texture depth is measured; segregation is unacceptable if the difference between segregated and non‑segregated areas exceeds 0.024 inch, and flushing is a defect when texture depth is ≤0.006 inch [2]. For rigid concrete layers the cores provide in‑place density, bulk specific gravity, entrained‑air content, and compressive strength; density must be at least 91 % of the theoretical maximum, air content must fall between 3.5 % and 8.5 %, and compressive strength must meet at least 91 % of the required value for acceptance [2]. Pervious concrete cores are evaluated for oven‑dry density in accordance with ASTM C1754 and for void content, which must be within ±5 % of the accepted test‑section average [2]. When the investigation targets an underlying base or subbase, the cores confirm that the material meets a minimum thickness, achieves a density greater than 92 % of its theoretical maximum, and that asphalt percentage and gradation satisfy the contractor’s mix‑design tolerances [2][1]. The results are entered with appropriate structural coefficients into a Component Method calculation tool such as DARWin to obtain an effective structural number for overlay design; if the subbase is found to be in poor condition it is excluded from the structural evaluation [1]. For existing asphalt pavements the cores also reveal whether stripping has occurred in the lower asphalt concrete layers; absence of stripping and a fair‑to‑good overall condition support a bonded overlay, whereas evidence of extensive deterioration or stripping leads to an unbonded design [1]. The overarching set of questions that the core investigation must answer therefore includes: is the actual pavement depth within tolerance and where are any deficits; does each material meet its prescribed density, air‑content, strength, binder content, gradation, and texture criteria; are subbase or subgrade soils sound or deteriorated; is stripping present in lower asphalt layers; and based on these findings, what remedial action—repair, replacement, bonded overlay, unbonded overlay, or exclusion of a layer from structural calculations—is required [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 10 (pp. 180–188); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 215–217), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 241–358), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 382–462)

Pavement‑core extraction supplies laboratory‑verified measurements—density, bulk specific gravity, asphalt content, fine‑aggregate gradation, layer thickness, entrained air, voids, macrotexture, and concrete compressive strength—that become the factual basis for every subsequent engineering action [1][2]. By coring both distressed joints and random locations, engineers can quantify subbase or subgrade conditions, concrete compressive strength, air content, and the percentage of slab requiring patching; when required patching exceeds ten percent a cost‑effectiveness analysis is triggered to compare patching with alternative treatments [1][2]. The precise layer thicknesses and material coefficients obtained from cores are entered into Component or Non‑Destructive Testing methods to calculate an effective structural number (SNeff), which is then compared with the structural number needed for projected traffic volumes to determine the additional overlay SN and appropriate overlay thickness; if the subbase is found deficient it may be omitted from the structural evaluation, influencing the scope of rehabilitation [1][2]. For asphalt pavements, core results reveal whether stripping or other lower‑layer distresses are present; a fair‑to‑good underlying condition permits design of a bonded concrete overlay, whereas a deteriorated surface leads to an unbonded design and may affect the use of macrofibers [1][2]. Core data also support Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) eligibility by confirming that rutting severity is less than 1½ in, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth is under 1 in with average crack width not exceeding a medium rating of ½ in, and the remaining asphalt layer thickness exceeds the planned milling depth by at least 1½ in, thereby guiding rejuvenator dosage and supplemental milling plans [1]. In Superpave special‑study projects, cores taken after construction and during a three‑to‑five‑year monitoring period provide direct measurements of binder performance across climate, traffic, and structural conditions; these results validate which PG‑binder grades, polymer modifiers, or blending techniques are viable for future design and long‑term maintenance strategies [1]. Density criteria derived from cores require that the lot average be no less than 98 % of maximum wet density, no individual core fall below 96 %, and longitudinal joint zones remain above 91 %; if the percent‑within‑limits drops below 50 % or two consecutive (or three total) lots fail to meet a 100 % density payment‑factor, corrective actions to mix design, placement, or compaction are mandated and incentive or disincentive payments are adjusted accordingly [2]. Entrained‑air content measured in cores must lie between 3.5 % and 8.5 %; values outside this range must be reported within five days and can halt paving until remedial action, directly influencing maintenance scheduling [2]. Asphalt content and the percent passing the No. 200 (75 µm) sieve are evaluated against specified tolerances; when both fall below 85 % the lot is classified as defective and contract payment may be reduced to as low as five percent of the unit price for concrete overlays, prompting design revisions or targeted repairs [2]. Thickness deficiencies identified by cores trigger defect classification: a single core short by more than ¼ in, or a uniform shortfall over three consecutive cores exceeding ⅛ in, deem the pavement defective; for full‑depth repairs the threshold is a half‑inch shortfall, and sections classified as defective are not reimbursed and must be repaired before final acceptance [2]. Pattern segregation indicated by summed deviations of 20 % or more from the Joint Mix Formula, or macrotexture differences exceeding 0.024 in, also leads to defect classification and targeted remedial actions [2]. For pervious concrete, oven‑dry density and void content must stay within ±5 % of the test‑section average and infiltration rates cannot drop below 200 in/hr; thickness tolerance is expressed as an average core length of 3/8 in + 3/4 in, with failure resulting in removal, replacement, or redesign [2]. Core‑derived concrete compressive strength must meet a minimum of 3,500 psi to authorize full payment; values below this threshold trigger repair or replacement actions [2]. All quantitative results feed the multiple‑characteristic formula that determines lot payment, incentives, penalties, and ultimately the allocation of funds for future maintenance, rehabilitation, or reconstruction, enabling engineers to decide whether a segment can remain in service, requires targeted repair, design revision, or replacement [2]. By integrating condition assessment, structural analysis, material performance verification, and threshold testing into a single evidence base, pavement‑core findings support network‑wide planning, detailed design of overlays or new structures, scheduling of preventive maintenance, selection of rehabilitation techniques such as HIR or bonded overlays, and long‑term asset‑management decisions across the pavement system [1][2].

Scope and applicability

Pavement coring is employed on both rigid (Portland‑cement concrete, reinforced cement concrete, plain cement concrete, Class AA overlays, pervious concrete, roller‑compacted concrete, precast slab systems and bonded concrete overlays) and flexible (standard Superpave wearing and binder mixes, rubberized polymer‑modified mixtures, asphalt‑rubberized base courses, conventional asphalt bases with 25 mm or 37.5 mm nominal aggregate, thin lifts placed at the minimum compacted depths often less than 1½ in, PG 64E‑22 binder or wearing courses, high‑traffic mixes designed for more than ten million ESALs, Stone Matrix Asphalt and other specialty SMA courses) when reliable determination of thickness, density, compressive strength, air content, or material properties is required [1][2]. For rigid pavements a core is taken whenever the anticipated slab depth may differ from design by more than a quarter‑inch, when measured density or compressive‑strength values are suspected to fall below acceptance limits, or when entrained‑air content must be shown to lie between 3.5 % and 8.5 %, with additional cores required if any measurement is deficient by the same margin [2]. In flexible pavements a core is mandated whenever material is placed on a stable, uniform base at or above the prescribed compacted depth and when a shortfall greater than one‑quarter inch in layer thickness is suspected, or when non‑destructive testing indicates possible deficiency; cores also support density acceptance that generally must exceed about 92 % of theoretical maximum [2]. Cores are especially appropriate for designing asphalt‑concrete overlays on existing AC pavements, providing accurate layer thicknesses and material properties needed for the Component Method or Non‑Destructive Testing Method, particularly when subbase condition is uncertain—such as a poor‑condition subbase on Interstates or a completely missing subbase on non‑Interstates—or when traffic loads exceed five million 18‑kip ESALs [1]. When a concrete overlay (bonded or unbonded) will be placed over an existing asphalt or composite surface, cores are taken to verify that stripping is not present in the lower asphalt layers; if the underlying condition is fair‑to‑good a bonded design may be pursued, whereas a poor, deteriorated condition typically leads to an unbonded design [1]. For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) treatments cores are justified only when distress is confined to the surface—rutting less than 1½ in., cracks shallower than 1 in. with widths no greater than 0.5 in., medium‑rated raveling and minor corrugation or shoving within the same depth limits—and the existing pavement is structurally sound, well‑drained, free of load‑related base problems, and possesses an asphalt thickness of at least 1½ in. beyond any planned milling depth; suitable candidates include top‑down cracked asphalt structures, stone matrix asphalt, open‑graded friction courses, and pavements that have only limited surface treatments such as seal coats, crack seals or patches [1]. Surface distresses that may trigger coring on flexible pavements include fatigue cracking, pattern segregation, flushing, raveling, weathering, rutting, potholing, transverse cracking, joint and edge deterioration, loss of bond or delamination, all of which prompt a series of six‑inch cores in the distressed area to verify underlying base thickness, density (generally >92 % of theoretical maximum), and asphalt content meet specifications [2]. Pervious concrete slabs are also cored after at least seven days of curing under ambient temperatures above 55 °F to confirm that oven‑dry density and void content satisfy design limits [2]. Sampling frequency follows lot‑size limits, with one random core per roughly 3,000 sq yd for rigid pavements and one core per 500 tons of material placed or per 3,000 yd² of completed work for flexible pavements; extra cores are taken when consecutive locations show depth deficiencies greater than an eighth‑inch (flexible) or a quarter‑inch (rigid) [2]. Cores must be extracted after any surface corrective work is finished and away from obstructions such as manholes, utilities, bridge structures, pavement notches, intersections or gore areas, ensuring that the core represents undisturbed material for accurate evaluation [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84), Ch. 5 (pp. 131–133), Ch. 10 (pp. 180–188); [2] — SECTION 300 BASE COURSES (pp. 215–217), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 241–358), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–464)

Pavement cores deliver the most direct point‑specific information on layer thicknesses, densities and material composition, yet their usefulness is bounded by several limitations identified across the guides. Because a core represents only a single location—often taken near visibly deteriorated joints or at random spots—it cannot capture the full spatial extent of subbase or subgrade distress across an entire slab, so broader non‑destructive investigations such as deflection testing, faulting measurements or ground‑penetrating radar are required to locate which joints need replacement and to assess overall structural performance. The guides also note that each core is limited to a six‑inch diameter sample obtained at most once per sublot unless additional samples are expressly authorized, meaning the data represent only a localized condition and a single core cannot reflect variability across an entire lot, especially when percent‑within‑limits for density (PWLD) or asphalt content (PWLAC) falls below fifty per cent, the overall pavement quality factor drops under ninety, or either the asphalt‑content pay factor or density pay factor is less than eighty per cent; in those cases the lot is deemed defective and reliance on core data alone is insufficient. In addition, logistical, budgetary or site constraints may make cores unavailable, and when cores cannot be obtained the guides recommend using historic pavement data from a Roadway Management System together with the Component Method or a Non‑Destructive Testing Method to estimate an effective structural number for an asphalt concrete overlay. Timing and physical placement restrictions further limit coring; cores must be drilled no later than the day after placement, cannot be taken within one foot of any obstruction such as manholes, utilities or bridge structures, and are prohibited in transition zones where joint construction cannot be consistent. When the asphalt layer has not been placed to the minimum compacted depths specified in Table G, when it rests on an unstable or non‑uniform base, or when the pavement segment is a non‑RPS portion, the specification permits the Representative to waive coring and require density verification by an optimum‑rolling pattern or other quality‑control evidence. For pavement sections smaller than approximately three thousand square yards per traffic lane—typical of intersections—the standard single‑core plan becomes impractical; in those situations two cores may be taken or a non‑destructive thickness measurement such as PTM No. 605 may be used, and additional nondestructive methods can be approved in writing. Sample integrity also imposes strict limits: cores that become coated with paraffin wax during testing cannot be retested, any core that is compressed, bent or otherwise damaged during cutting, handling, transport or storage must be replaced immediately from a nearby location, and when bulk‑density results are non‑repeatable the protocol calls for using retest values; if repeatability cannot be achieved, reliance on the core alone is unreliable. If the difference between in‑place (IA) and laboratory‑tested (HOLA) density averages exceeds the tolerances listed in Table H, an investigation is triggered and the department may shift to laboratory‑tested acceptance samples or other complementary techniques. Warranty provisions further dictate that a pavement must meet the specified minimum thickness, achieve a density greater than ninety‑two per cent of theoretical maximum, and satisfy mix‑design tolerances for percent asphalt and gradation; failure to meet any of these criteria—such as during fatigue‑cracking surveys, observed flushing, or when three consecutive core results fall outside the prescribed limits—necessitates additional investigation to determine the underlying cause. Contractual payment rules add another constraint because the department will not reimburse extra cores if measured depth deviates by more than a quarter‑inch, meaning that significant thickness variations can be missed if only core data are used and prompting the use of nondestructive methods in such cases. Because the unit price for measuring pavement depth already includes both coring equipment and nondestructive testing apparatus, it is advisable to supplement or replace core sampling with nondestructive techniques—such as nuclear density gauges, electrical impedance devices, optimum‑rolling checks, approved nondestructive thickness tests, or other methods authorized in writing—whenever depth variability exceeds the quarter‑inch threshold, rapid sampling beyond a single core is required, or the cost of additional coring cannot be recovered under the contract. Consequently, the guides recommend employing complementary investigation methods whenever the point‑specific nature of cores, their limited spatial coverage, unavailability, procedural constraints, sample integrity issues, performance‑based failures, or payment limitations would otherwise restrict a reliable evaluation of pavement condition. Existing information and records

The reviewer must first collect all documentation that characterizes the existing pavement section, including pre‑construction and post‑construction manual distress surveys as well as any core samples taken during the three‑to‑five‑year period after construction. Automated and manual performance‑survey results such as rut‑depth checks, flushing or pothole surveys, and annual performance analysis reports are also required to provide a quantitative picture of surface condition. The condition surveys should quantify surface distress using the established limits of less than one‑and‑a‑half inches rut depth, cracking depth under one inch with average crack widths not exceeding a medium rating of half an inch, and raveling or weathering limited to a medium rating. Records of short‑term (construction) and long‑term (five‑to‑ten‑year) performance data for the polymer‑modified binders under the region’s climate, traffic regimes and project types must be examined. Prior core test files that detail void content, asphalt percentage, gradation, and dimensions of any six‑inch diameter cores taken when fatigue cracking or other distresses were identified provide a baseline for interpreting new samples. Reports describing the condition of base, sub‑base and subgrade layers together with any earlier sampling or laboratory test results should be gathered. The construction and maintenance history stored in the roadway management system, including documentation of past remedial or emergency repairs, supplies essential context when core data are missing or incomplete. For each core the project file must contain the ECMS project number, lot and sub‑lot identifiers, station and offset location, placement date and mixture type, ensuring traceability of the sample. The daily theoretical maximum specific gravity values required for density calculations per the applicable specification section should be verified for each lot. Test results for both HOLA acceptance samples and IA verification samples, together with any corrective actions recorded in the field inspector’s diary or related logs, are part of the performance record to be reviewed. Average daily traffic counts and the average initial truck factors used for ESAL calculations—such as those listed in Table 7.1—must be incorporated to relate existing structural capacity to anticipated loading. The existing project documentation often fails to provide the quantitative surface‑distress measurements needed to calculate the exact percent of area that must be patched, which is essential because exceeding the ten‑percent threshold triggers a cost‑effectiveness analysis [1]. Likewise, records frequently omit concrete material properties such as compressive strength, air content and frost susceptibility, leaving uncertainty about durability and friction performance [1]. Information on subbase or subgrade thickness, material quality and drainage capability is commonly missing, and in some cases the subbase may be absent altogether, creating inconsistency when structural coefficients are derived for overlay designs [1]. For asphalt sections that use non‑standard polymer‑modified binders, especially experimental grades such as PG 64E‑22, the files rarely contain baseline core results or long‑term distress observations, making it unclear how these materials will perform under regional climate and traffic loads over a three‑to‑five‑year period [1]. The lack of prior control sections and post‑construction distress surveys further limits the ability to compare actual performance against design expectations [1]. When cores are unavailable, reliance on Roadway Management System pavement history data is required, yet those datasets are often incomplete or contradictory, undermining confidence in existing‑pavement inputs [1]. Records also flag lots as defective when overall lot pay factors fall below ninety, or when asphalt‑content or density pay factors drop beneath eighty, and they may show percent‑within‑limits values for weight or asphalt content under fifty, but without coring these indications cannot be verified because laboratory repeatability is uncertain [2]. Acceptance samples are insufficient to confirm in‑place pavement density, asphalt content, gradation or the presence of pattern segregation, and a texture‑depth difference greater than 0.024 in between segregated and non‑segregated zones or an average texture depth of 0.006 in or less signals a defect that must be investigated with at least three cores in each condition [2]. The documentation often lacks timely core extraction, as cores are not taken within one day of placement, and it may omit theoretical maximum specific gravity values or report them more than ±0.030 away from contractor‑provided figures, both of which prevent accurate bulk‑specific‑gravity calculations [2]. Core integrity problems such as damaged samples require immediate replacement cores within twelve inches of the original location, yet records seldom note whether this remedial coring was performed [2]. Thickness data are frequently uncertain; a measured core that deviates by more than one‑quarter inch from design depth—or by an eighth inch at three adjacent locations—cannot confirm compliance and mandates additional coring until the deviation falls within tolerance [2]. Existing warranty or traffic‑count surveys may reveal fatigue cracking but do not provide definitive information on base condition, pavement thickness, density greater than ninety‑two percent of theoretical maximum, or mix composition, necessitating a series of six‑inch cores to measure depth, void content, asphalt percentage and gradation against the contractor’s mix‑design tolerances [2]. Final average densities must not be less than ninety‑eight percent of the maximum wet density, with no sublot test below ninety‑six percent, and reinforced concrete pavements must achieve a compressive strength of three‑thousand‑five‑hundred psi at twenty‑eight days; without core testing these acceptance criteria remain unresolved [2]. Inconsistencies between routine HOLA acceptance cores and less‑frequent IA verification cores leave open whether the mix satisfies compaction requirements or whether equipment calibration, sample integrity or testing procedures are at fault, prompting additional coring for verification [2]. Design files often lack reliable measurements of existing pavement thickness and condition at proposed repair locations, so before a precast slab design can be finalized the actual in‑place thickness and material state must be confirmed by coring [2]. Specifications treat any irregularity exceeding one‑quarter inch or any depth shortfall of half an inch as defective, requiring full‑depth cores at random intervals (one per 3 000 sq yd) and at any suspect spot, which underscores the need for systematic field investigation to close these record gaps [2].

Visual inspection and condition assessment

The Pavement Core examination is directed at visual signs of deterioration that appear on the surface and in the near‑surface layers, focusing on distresses that can be identified through performance surveys before any load‑related or base failures are accepted [1][2]. The primary distress types inspected include fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling or weathering, flushing, potholes, rutting, joint and edge degradation, and loss of bond or delamination [2]. Rutting severity is quantified with a twelve‑foot straight edge across the lane width and must remain below one and one‑half inches; any deeper rut is considered excessive [1][2]. Transverse and miscellaneous cracks are evaluated for depth and aperture, with acceptable crack depth limited to less than one inch and average crack widths not exceeding a medium rating of half an inch [1]. Raveling or weathering may be present only to a medium rating characterized by a rough, pitted surface that may contain loose particles; more severe spalling is outside the acceptable range [1]. Minor corrugation, shoving, slippage, bleeding, and low‑friction conditions are tolerated provided their average depth does not exceed one and one‑half inches and they do not produce measurable loss of traction [1]. Surface irregularities greater than a quarter inch are classified as defective and trigger further investigation [2]. The compacted thickness of each six‑inch diameter full‑depth core is compared with the design thickness, and a shortfall of half an inch or more that cannot be satisfactorily corrected is deemed a defect [2]. When performance surveys flag any of the listed distresses—particularly fatigue cracking that may indicate an unacceptable base condition—a series of six‑inch diameter full‑depth cores is extracted from within the affected zone to verify composition and layer thickness [2]. Random additional cores are taken to confirm that neither the surface‑irregularity tolerance nor the thickness shortfall threshold has been exceeded, and any breach requires immediate corrective action [2].

Pavement cores are employed to classify observed distresses according to their primary cause, separating them into load‑related (or surface‑irregularity) and climate/materials‑related (or depth‑deficiency) categories [1][2]. Once a core is extracted, the inspector assigns each distress to one of two principal groups—surface irregularities such as cracking or faulting, and depth deficiencies such as insufficient layer thickness—following the rating procedures set out in Publication 336 for consistency [2]. Severity is quantified using defined thresholds: surface irregularities exceeding a quarter‑inch tolerance are considered defective, while any layer‑thickness shortfall of half an inch or more is deemed deficient and, if uncorrectable, classified as a defect requiring remediation [2]. In addition to these dimensional limits, the percentage of pavement that would require patching is calculated from the core observations, with a ten‑percent threshold triggering a cost‑effectiveness analysis for alternative treatments [1]. The extent of each distress is measured directly from the core; depth measurements follow PTM No. 737 and rut depths, when applicable, are verified with a twelve‑foot straight edge across the lane width to capture both severity and lateral spread [2]. Cores are taken at random intervals—one full‑depth core per 3,000 square yards of completed base or asphalt concrete—as prescribed by PTM No. 1, and additional cores are placed near visibly deteriorated joints or any locations suspected of deficiency to capture subbase or subgrade conditions [1][2]. For every identified distress the type, measured severity value, calculated extent, and exact geographic position of the core are recorded; location is logged using the Department’s Location Reference System (LRS) for the corresponding State Route segment or partial segment [2]. All findings—including the classification, severity rating, extent derived from core results, and precise location—are entered into a systematic inspection report that tabulates the quantities of visible distress and documents each defect entry whenever defined thresholds are exceeded [1][2].

Field measurements and testing

During a pavement‑core program cylindrical samples, typically six inches in diameter, are extracted at locations defined by the specifications; on longer repairs cores are taken at roughly 100 ft intervals or randomly per 3 000 square yards of completed asphalt [2]. The length of each core is measured on site with a calibrated PTM‑614 apparatus, and when a nondestructive thickness method such as PTM‑605 is used the same tolerance applies, allowing the core depth to be verified without drilling [2]. If a measured depth falls short of the design by more than one‑quarter inch a payment adjustment is triggered, while an individual deficit of half an inch or greater that cannot be corrected renders the section defective; three consecutive cores uniformly low by more than one‑eighth inch also flag the work as defective and require additional coring [2]. The extracted core provides direct measurements of each layer’s thickness and material type, which are entered into the Component Method together with appropriate structural coefficients to compute an effective Structural Number that indicates the remaining load‑bearing capacity of the pavement [1]. Laboratory testing on the cores determines bulk specific gravity (Gmb) and bulk density using PTM‑715 or PTM‑716; these values are compared to the theoretical maximum specific gravity (Gmm) derived from AASHTO T209 as modified by Bulletin 27, and the resulting percent compaction must reach at least 96 % for any individual test and an average of 98 % for the lot, thereby indicating the degree of compaction achieved [2]. Void content is calculated from the same volumetric data, while air‑void analysis on concrete cores confirms that void levels lie within ±5 % of the section average, providing a measure of internal pore structure quality [2]. For concrete pavements every fifth core is tested for entrained‑air content with PTM‑623 and must fall between 3.5 % and 8.5 %; values outside this range require a written evaluation, reflecting the air‑void system’s influence on durability [2]. Cylinders cast from the cores are subjected to compressive‑strength testing according to PTM‑604 at three, seven and twenty‑eight days, with early‑age strengths required to reach 3 000 psi and a minimum of 3 500 psi at twenty‑eight days, thereby indicating the structural capacity of the concrete; when laboratory testing is not feasible strength may be estimated by the maturity method (AASHTO T 413) [2]. Asphalt cores are evaluated for binder content using PTM‑757 or PTM‑702 (Modified Method D) and for fine‑aggregate gradation by sieve analysis, notably the percent passing a No. 200 (75 µm) screen measured with PTM‑739, both of which indicate compliance with mix design specifications [2]. Surface macrotexture, an indicator of skid resistance, is measured in the field with ASTM E965 or PTM‑751; a mean texture depth greater than 0.024 in signals pattern segregation while depths of 0.006 in or less suggest flushing, thereby reflecting surface finish quality [2]. Visual inspections performed according to Publication 336 identify distresses such as fatigue cracking, rutting, flushing, potholes and joint deterioration, providing an overall indication of material placement quality and workmanship [2]. Deflection testing on the slab reveals structural stiffness, while faulting measurements quantify joint movement, together indicating the pavement’s load‑distribution behavior [1]. Ground‑penetrating radar surveys detect anomalies in the subbase or subgrade, offering insight into hidden defects [1]. Surface friction tests assess slip resistance and roughness measurements gauge ride quality, both contributing to an overall condition rating that can be integrated by systems such as MIRA for surface and subsurface assessment [1]. Specialized concrete inspection tools are employed: MIT‑Scan 2 verifies dowel‑bar alignment and MIT‑Scan T2 confirms concrete thickness, each indicating the integrity of joint reinforcement and layer dimensions; a tensile bond tester evaluates the bond strength of any repair material applied to the core, reflecting the effectiveness of repairs [1]. Prior to coring, nondestructive density checks using an approved nuclear gauge (PTM‑402) or electrical‑impedance gauge (PTM‑403) confirm that the mat has achieved target compaction, thereby indicating in‑place density without extraction [2]. Additional nondestructive thickness testing methods beyond PTM‑605 may be requested in writing and, when employed within the DARWin software, can estimate the effective Structural Number through its Non‑Destructive Testing Method; the software also offers Component and Remaining Life calculation routes, with the Component or NDT method recommended for asphalt overlay designs [1]. For pervious concrete sections infiltration tests performed to ASTM C1701 on selected areas verify that permeability meets prescribed minima, indicating the functional performance of the porous structure [2].

The Pavement Core program requires a core‑drilling rig that complies with PTM 606/729 and is equipped with interchangeable bits capable of producing either six‑inch or four‑inch diameter full‑depth cylinders. The drill must be operated in the presence of the Inspector (or Representative) and cores are taken as soon as practicable after final compaction, but no later than the following day. Prior to coring the surface must be clean, cured, dry and free of standing water; vehicular traffic is prohibited for at least twenty‑four hours or until the pavement uniformly cools to 140 °F or less, and ambient temperature must be at least 35 °F (with a seven‑day cure period during which air temperature remains above 55 °F for concrete overlays). Core locations are defined by sublots selected randomly according to PTM 1 (or PTM 729 where noted), each sublot representing roughly two thousand linear feet of lane or up to three thousand square yards. Typically one full‑depth core is obtained from each sublot; a maximum of one core per sublot is allowed unless the Representative authorizes additional sampling, such as one six‑inch core for every five hundred tons placed, five random six‑inch cores on a longitudinal joint for density checks, or three four‑inch cores per 5,000 ft² for more extensive verification. When higher frequency is required, additional cores may be taken at intervals of one hundred feet in both directions from any deficient core. Retest cores consist of two six‑inch cylinders positioned twenty‑four inches downstream in the direction of traffic (or upstream if space limits) and must be reported within three weeks of the original results; the retest location is offset longitudinally by approximately twenty‑four inches, with the opposite direction used if the offset would fall outside the sublot. After coring each cylinder is measured for thickness with a device conforming to PTM 614 (or PTM 737) and tested for in‑place bulk specific gravity and density according to PTM 715 or PTM 716. Entrained‑air content is evaluated against the 3.5 %–8.5 % limit, and depth deficiencies greater than a quarter‑inch (or half‑inch for certain base courses) are deemed defective, prompting further coring until the condition is fully characterized. Information cores are submitted for the first sublot and thereafter for every tenth core. All holes are rinsed and backfilled with matching asphalt or grout within twenty‑four hours of extraction while traffic control remains in effect. [2]

Sampling and laboratory investigation

Pavement core sampling is applied to every structural layer whose quality must be verified, including the concrete slab and underlying subbase or subgrade in rigid pavements, each asphalt lift (binder and wearing courses) down to the top of the existing base in flexible pavements, as well as any asphalt‑treated base, other base‑course layers, hardened pervious‑concrete layers, and concrete overlays. For existing rigid pavement the cores are taken near deteriorated joints and at additional random locations to capture conditions below the surface, while for new construction and repairs core locations are selected according to PTM No. 1 with transitional zones excluded and may be placed randomly within defined lots or sublots. The depth of each core is driven by the need to reach the specific layer being evaluated – through all asphalt lifts to the top of the base for flexible pavements, through the full thickness of a concrete slab to the underlying subbase or subgrade for rigid pavements, and sufficiently deep in pervious‑concrete layers to allow density and void‑content testing within an average length tolerance of three‑eighths to three‑quarters of an inch. The quantity of cores is linked to the amount of visible distress; when distress is extensive a larger, more representative set of samples is required, whereas limited distress may be satisfied with the minimum prescribed numbers. For new flexible pavement the guideline calls for one four‑inch diameter core for every 2,000 linear feet of lane, with a six‑inch core used when bulk specific‑gravity or density testing is needed, and supplemental cores are required for each 500 tons (or where permitted, each 1,000 tons) of material placed. Existing pavement repairs receive at least one full‑depth core per lane for repair sections shorter than 100 ft, and when the repaired segment exceeds 100 ft cores are spaced at intervals not greater than 100 ft. For base‑course or ARBC work a random full‑depth core is taken for each 3,000 square yards of completed work, with additional cores placed wherever the inspector suspects a depth deficiency. When a hardened pervious‑concrete layer is present three four‑inch diameter cores are extracted for every 5,000 square feet of surface, and the cores must meet the specified length tolerance before density and void‑content testing. Concrete overlays on rigid pavements are divided into lots no larger than 3,000 square yards per lane (ramps treated as separate lots); one random core is taken in each lot, with two cores or nondestructive thickness measurements used for intersections or when a lot is smaller than the threshold. The standard sampling frequency limits cores to one per sublot unless the representative authorizes additional cores, and all cores must be extracted no later than the day after placement (or within 24 hours after final compaction) in the presence of the inspector or designated representative. If any measured thickness deviates from design by more than one‑quarter inch for flexible or rigid pavements—or by more than one‑half inch for base‑course layers—additional localized cores are required, placed at 100‑foot longitudinal intervals covering a 50‑foot radius on either side of the deficient line. When depth testing is performed by coring, the first core in each sublot and every tenth thereafter may be submitted as information cores, and an average of three cores can be used to determine acceptability when an individual core exceeds tolerance. After a pavement core is extracted, its thickness is first measured either directly from the cylinder or by nondestructive means; the individual measurements are averaged to obtain a lot‑average depth that must lie within a quarter‑inch of the design thickness and the allowable tolerance for any single core is expressed as an average of three‑eighths inch plus three‑quarters inch with no measurement deviating more than one‑half inch [2]. The core then undergoes a series of standardized laboratory examinations. An entrained‑air‑content test performed in accordance with PTM 623 determines the percentage of air trapped in the mix, and values are required to fall between 3.5 % and 8.5 %; results outside this band trigger a written evaluation of potential durability problems such as reduced freeze‑thaw resistance [2]. For concrete pavements, cylindrical specimens taken from the core are tested for compressive strength at three, seven and twenty‑eight days following PTM 606; early‑age strength must reach at least 3,000 psi and the twenty‑eight‑day strength at least 3,500 psi to confirm that the hardened material has attained the structural capacity required before traffic can be opened [2]. Visual inspection of the core is used to identify durability problems including aggregate degradation, frost damage, and signs of excessive vibration or rolling such as aggregate breakage, which signal the need to halt or adjust construction practices [1][2]. For asphaltic layers, the in‑place binder content is determined using PTM 757, PTM 702, or the modified Method D referenced in PTM 739; the measured binder percentage must meet contractual specifications and may not fall below 85 % of the specified value, otherwise the pavement is deemed defective [2]. Bulk specific gravity (Gmb) and bulk density are obtained with PTM 715, employing PTM 716 when necessary, and these values are compared to the theoretical maximum specific gravity (Gmm) measured by AASHTO T 209 as modified in Bulletin 27; the contractor’s daily Gmm value must agree within ±0.030 of the laboratory result [2]. Percent compaction is calculated from bulk density and Gmm, with acceptance requiring that the percent of theoretical maximum density exceed 92 %; for the overall lot the average density must be at least 98 % of the maximum wet density with no individual core falling below 96 %, while a contractual lower limit of 91 % is also stipulated [2]. A sieve analysis provides fine‑aggregate gradation expressed as the percent passing the No. 200 (75 µm) sieve and the primary control No. 8 (2.36 mm) sieve; if the payment factors for asphalt content, the 75 µm sieve and the 2.36 mm sieve are all equal to 85 % the pavement is considered defective [2]. Surface macro‑texture depth is measured in accordance with PTM 751; a difference between adjacent texture readings greater than 0.024 in indicates pattern segregation, while an average texture depth of 0.006 in or less signifies flushing—both conditions render the section defective [2]. The combination of bulk density, Gmm and air‑void calculations quantifies the percent of unfilled voids, with an acceptance band of ±5 % of the test‑section average, providing a further check on compaction quality and freeze‑thaw durability [2]. Finally, the cores also provide material for evaluating subbase or subgrade soil conditions and the depth of deterioration beneath the surface, information that together informs decisions about rehabilitation scope and method [1].

Structural and functional performance

To evaluate a pavement’s structural capacity and functional condition, cores are taken both adjacent to visibly deteriorated joints and at random locations so that subbase or subgrade conditions and hidden distress can be quantified[1]. The vertical depth of each full‑depth core, representing the actual layer thickness, is measured by drilling in accordance with PTM 606/605 or by nondestructive devices such as PTM 614, 617 or 737, and the measured depth must lie within a quarter‑inch of the design value; any single measurement that exceeds this tolerance triggers additional coring, while a shortfall of one‑half inch that cannot be remedied classifies the section as defective[2]. For flexible pavements the extracted cores are sent to the laboratory where oven‑dry density (ASTM C1754), asphalt content, aggregate gradation and percent unfilled voids are determined according to PTM 715 and AASHTO T 209, with the void content required to be within plus or minus five percent of the average voids measured in an accepted test section, and the in‑place hardened density is also documented[2]. For rigid pavements the same cores provide material for concrete cylinders that are cured and tested at 3, 7 and 28 days, with the 28‑day compressive strength required to reach at least 3 500 psi as the primary indicator of bearing capacity, while air content is recorded for use in the multiple‑characteristic formula applied to lot payment[2]. The remaining asphalt thickness obtained from the core must exceed the milling depth by at least one‑and‑a‑half inches, ensuring sufficient structural layer after resurfacing[1]. Rutting severity measured on the core surface should be less than one‑and‑a‑half inches, and transverse and miscellaneous cracks are recorded with depths under one inch and average widths not exceeding a half‑inch; corrugation, shoving or slippage are also assessed with an average depth below one‑and‑a‑half inches[1]. Friction values derived from the core surface are checked against acceptable low‑friction limits, and roughness or raveling ratings are noted as part of the functional assessment[1]. Adjacent to each coring location macrotexture depth, expressed as Mean Texture Depth (MTD), is measured per ASTM E965 or PTM 751 and must be at least 0.040 in for concrete surfaces; differences greater than 0.024 in between segregated and non‑segregated zones indicate unacceptable pattern segregation, while depths of 0.006 in or less define flushing[2]. Surface tolerance is verified with a straightedge laid parallel to the centerline, and the pavement is accepted only when variation does not exceed 3/16 in; an infiltration test on concrete must achieve a rate of at least 200 in/hr to confirm adequate permeability[2]. Deflection testing and faulting assessments are employed as nondestructive techniques to identify joints that may require replacement, providing additional insight into load‑transfer efficiency and overall structural performance[1]. Finally, ride‑quality evaluations are performed in accordance with contract‑specified procedures, linking measured thickness, compressive strength, texture, infiltration results and surface friction to overall serviceability and incentive payments[2][1].

Pavement cores extracted for evaluation show distress metrics that lie within the limits set by the guidelines, with rutting measured at less than 1½ in., transverse and miscellaneous cracking depths under 1 in., crack widths not exceeding the medium rating of 0.5 in., and raveling severity rated medium. Data interpretation and diagnosis

The integration of a pavement core begins with a systematic visual inspection in which the engineer or field representative records all observable distresses such as rutting, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, pattern segregation, flushing, fatigue cracking, and joint anomalies, quantifying each against defined limits—rut depth less than 1½ in., crack depth under 1.0 in. and average width not exceeding ½ in., and macro‑texture differences greater than 0.024 in. (or ≤0.006 in. for flushing) signaling a defect[1][2]. These quantified observations are entered into a tabular format to establish the exact extent of surface deterioration[1]. Concurrently, non‑destructive field measurements are performed, including deflection testing, faulting measurement, ground‑penetrating radar scans, friction and roughness assessments, as well as in‑situ density determination with an approved nuclear or impedance gauge, and surface macro‑texture evaluation per PTM 751[1][2]. The results of these tests are used to locate joints or zones that may require replacement or slab stabilization and to verify whether the observed surface symptoms correspond to underlying structural deficiencies[1]. Core extraction follows, with at least one 6‑inch diameter core taken from each sublot both adjacent to visibly deteriorated joints or pattern‑segregation areas and from random locations to capture variability of subbase, base, and subgrade conditions; each core’s depth is measured on site, labeled, sealed, and inspected for security tape integrity before shipment[1][2]. In the laboratory the cores are evaluated for bulk specific gravity (Gmb), bulk density, void content, asphalt percentage, and gradation using PTM 715/716, AASHTO T209, PTM 702, PTM 739 and related methods, with percent compaction calculated as a percent of the daily theoretical maximum specific gravity averaged from two contractor‑provided values[2]. Acceptance criteria require density to exceed 92 % of the theoretical maximum (or a Percent Within Limits of at least 91 %), a density payment factor of ≥ 100 %, air‑content between 3.5 % and 8.5 %, gradation deviations whose summed absolute differences from any two sieves do not reach 20 %, and asphalt content and percent passing the No. 200 sieve to be within Joint Mix Formula limits; cores falling outside these limits are deemed defective[2]. Additional laboratory tests may determine compressive strength, air content, material composition, and the quantity of rejuvenator required for design purposes[1]. The laboratory results are cross‑referenced with the visual‑field observations, the pavement’s construction and maintenance history, and traffic volume records (ESALs) to confirm whether distress is confined to the surface or reflects deeper structural issues[1]. Performance data gathered from annual surveys quantify the extent of distress—such as more than 30 % of a segment exhibiting two or more low‑severity defects—and verify accumulated ESALs, and these metrics are considered together with the visual observations, field measurements, and laboratory results[2]. When the integrated dataset indicates that the percent of area requiring patching exceeds the 10 % threshold (excluding existing sound concrete patches), a cost‑effectiveness analysis is triggered to compare simple patching against more extensive rehabilitation alternatives[1]. Likewise, if combined evidence surpasses defined performance thresholds, the pavement is classified as defective and must be removed and replaced; if all values remain within specification, observed distress may be attributed to causes beyond the contractor’s control and no remedial action is required[2]. This holistic approach ensures that quantified surface symptoms, measured material properties, and long‑term performance trends jointly drive repair strategy selection, aligning design decisions with actual pavement condition[1][2].

The pavement cores indicate that the distress is most likely attributable to a combination of load‑related problems such as overloading and climate or material issues including inadequate drainage, environmental exposure, aging and material deterioration. The presence of a weak or deteriorated support layer may be revealed when fatigue cracking prompts drilling of additional 6‑inch diameter cores within the distressed zone, indicating unacceptable base conditions. When cumulative equivalent single axle loads recorded during the warranty period exceed the projected 20‑year design life, overload is considered a cause of rutting or base distress beyond the contractor’s responsibility. Environmental exposure such as flooding and freeze‑thaw cycles can also contribute to the observed condition, especially when acting together with other factors. [1][2]

Findings, confidence, and next actions

Pavement cores, extracted from strategically selected points such as near deteriorated joints and random locations, give engineers a direct means of confirming subbase or subgrade conditions and quantifying the vertical extent of distress beneath the surface. Laboratory analysis of the recovered cylinders yields concrete percent patching, compressive strength, air content, bulk specific gravity, density, asphalt content, fine‑aggregate gradation and void characteristics, allowing comparison with contract tolerances. When the measured bulk density exceeds 92 % of the theoretical maximum (or when a Percent Within Limits is at least 91 %), the mix satisfies the density acceptance criterion; similarly, entrained‑air values must lie between 3.5 % and 8.5 % for concrete overlays, with any deviation prompting written evaluation. Thickness verification proceeds by comparing each core’s depth to the design thickness, permitting a quarter‑inch deviation before the slab is deemed defective work and treating an eighth‑inch shortfall across three consecutive cores as another defect; the individual depths are then averaged to produce a lot‑average pavement depth used in payment calculations. For rehabilitation strategies such as hot‑in‑place recycling, the cores taken from the candidate segment confirm that surface distress limits are satisfied and provide the precise amount of rejuvenator required for the mix design, supporting an immediate suitability decision. In overlay design, the layer thicknesses and material coefficients derived from cores enable calculation of an effective structural number, giving confidence that a proposed asphalt or bonded concrete‑over‑asphalt overlay will meet anticipated traffic loads; additionally, cores can demonstrate that the bottom asphalt layers are free of stripping, which is a prerequisite for specifying a bonded overlay when the existing surface is judged fair to good. If the percent patching required on the project exceeds ten percent—excluding patches already in good condition—a separate cost‑effectiveness analysis must be performed before proceeding. The structural evaluation may omit a subbase that is known or suspected to be of poor quality, which reduces confidence in the overall capacity estimate for an overlay design. Because each core represents only a point sample, it cannot capture the full spatial variability of the pavement; consequently, nondestructive testing or additional coring is required to reduce uncertainty about conditions between locations and underlying causes of distress. When cores are unavailable, reliance on historical Roadway Management System data introduces further doubt regarding actual layer conditions. Practical constraints such as paraffin‑wax coating of samples, damage that necessitates replacement within twelve inches, limited testing windows of twenty‑four hours and the discarding of extreme values at a five‑percent significance level can reduce the effective sample size, sometimes leaving fewer than three valid cores for a lot. Lack of repeatability may require retest cores taken from slightly displaced locations, adding uncertainty to the representativeness of the data. Contractual provisions that prohibit additional coring when a depth deficiency exceeds one quarter‑inch, together with the Representative’s discretion to waive core requirements when quality‑control records appear satisfactory, limit the ability to verify larger‑scale deficiencies. Core results address only short‑term performance aspects such as interim structural adequacy and rejuvenator dosing for HIR; long‑term structural behavior and durability of overlays remain uncertain. Moreover, core data do not provide information on performance attributes such as cracking resistance, rutting potential, ride quality or long‑term durability, leaving those aspects of pavement condition unresolved. After a pavement core is recovered, the project team expands the laboratory results with both destructive and non‑destructive investigations to verify subgrade conditions, slab integrity and bond quality, employing deflection and faulting measurements, ground‑penetrating radar surveys, friction and roughness tests, MIRA condition assessments, MIT Scan 2 dowel‑bar alignment checks, MIT Scan T2 thickness scans, and tensile‑bond testing. The immediate laboratory program also requires that the in‑place density of each core be measured within twenty‑four hours according to PTM 715 or PTM 716 and compared with a lower specification limit of 91 percent (or greater than 92 percent for base layers), while percent asphalt, gradation and unfilled voids are evaluated using PTM 702, AASHTO T‑209 and PTM 739; any individual test falling below 96 percent of the maximum wet density or any lot average dropping beneath 98 percent triggers a stop in paving and a cause investigation with corrective measures such as adjustments to roller speed, pattern, moisture content or mix composition before work can resume. Every fifth core is sent to Laboratory Testing Services for entrained‑air analysis, and values outside the 3.5 %–8.5 % range require a written evaluation within five days and may also halt paving until an acceptable remedy is approved, while 28‑day compressive strength testing must meet at least 3 500 psi (or the minimum 3 000 psi required before traffic opening) or else initiate an investigation of mix design and placement that can lead to removal and replacement of the deficient section at no extra cost. Core thickness is compared with the design thickness, and a shortfall greater than one‑quarter inch classifies the work as defective, activating a targeted follow‑up coring program in which additional six‑inch diameter cores are drilled at prescribed intervals—typically every one hundred feet along the lane or for each 500 tons of material placed—to map the extent of the deficiency; if any core is more than one‑half inch short, the slab is declared defective and must be repaired by adding material until the surface is within a quarter‑inch of design, after which verification cores are taken and all coring holes are backfilled, compacted and sealed within twenty‑four hours using a mix that matches the original job‑mix formula. The proportion of concrete requiring patching is quantified, and when this percentage approaches the ten‑percent threshold a cost‑effectiveness analysis is required before selecting a repair method; depending on whether observed distress is load‑related or climate/materials‑related, design checks direct corrective actions such as slab stabilization, dowel retrofits, cross‑stitching, full‑depth patching, installation of base drains, diamond grinding or grooving, and joint cleaning and sealing to preserve performance. Core measurements of rut depth, transverse and miscellaneous crack depth, crack width and raveling are compared against Hot‑In‑Place Rejuvenator acceptance limits—rutting less than about 1½ in., cracking shallower than roughly 1 in. with average widths no greater than 0.5 in., and raveling not exceeding a medium rating—and when the core meets these thresholds the laboratory data are used to calculate the required rejuvenator dosage and to verify that the planned HIR thickness (typically 1½ in., ranging from 1 to 2 in.) leaves at least the minimum underlying asphalt layer (1½–2 in. for composite pavements or 2–3 in. for full‑depth sections); if any measured value exceeds the limits, corrective measures such as additional milling, base repair, or selection of an alternative preservation treatment are implemented before proceeding. Macrotexture measurements that reveal pattern segregation exceeding a texture difference of 0.024 in. or flushing with an average texture depth ≤0.006 in. deem the pavement defective, and the prescribed corrective action is removal and replacement of the full lane width plus at least five feet beyond each end, with a limited test section first placed to confirm that adjusted roller settings, mix temperature or other parameters have eliminated the defect. When percent‑within‑limits values for PWLD or PWLAC fall below fifty per cent, the lot is classified as defective; the District Executive may apply an overall lot pay factor of seventy per cent or order full removal and replacement, and a written retest request with supporting data and offset cores can be submitted within three weeks for a repeat evaluation. Longer‑term design checks use thickness data from full‑depth cores taken at each repair location (minimum one per lane for repairs under 100 ft and at least every 100 ft for longer sections) to verify subbase condition, select certified bedding material and design load‑transfer dowel bars for precast concrete slabs, while the effective structural number of the existing pavement is determined using the Component Method or a non‑destructive testing approach in DARWin and compared with the future‑traffic structural number to size any overlay; if cores indicate that the subbase may be deficient, it is excluded from the structural evaluation on Interstate facilities, whereas for other routes with design ESALs greater than five million a targeted subbase investigation is required before inclusion. Cores are also examined for stripping in the lower asphalt layers, and if stripping is absent and surface condition is judged fair‑to‑good, a bonded fiber‑reinforced concrete overlay can be pursued; otherwise an unbonded overlay is specified, with residual‑strength testing according to ASTM C1609 providing the f150 value that guides macro‑fiber content and any necessary fresh‑mix adjustments for workability and air entrainment, and the overlay design incorporates joint design criteria ensuring appropriate saw‑cut depth and timing so that fiber‑reinforced concrete joints engage early and limit premature cracking. A structured monitoring program follows the core evaluation: detailed pre‑construction distress surveys are recorded, post‑construction manual distress surveys are repeated together with additional core sampling for three to five years, supplemental cores follow the sampling plan supplied by LTS and NECEPT, and accompanying binder and loose‑box specimens are collected to confirm material properties; annual distress surveys monitor fatigue cracking, transverse cracking, raveling, rutting, flushing, potholing and bond loss, and if more than thirty per cent of a segment exhibits two or more low‑severity distresses, microsurfacing is applied. Preservation actions maintained throughout construction include completing surface corrections such as diamond grinding or grooving before depth testing, ensuring proper curing conditions, sealing transverse and longitudinal joints with approved hot‑pour or silicone sealants, prohibiting vehicular traffic on newly compacted sections for at least twenty‑four hours or until the surface temperature falls below 140 °F, and halting vibration if aggregate breakage or binder flushing is observed in cores, followed by a review of compaction procedures, possible mix adjustments and a demonstration strip until all parameters meet specification.
