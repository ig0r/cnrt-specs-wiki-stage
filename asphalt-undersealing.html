Asphalt Undersealing

Part of: Slab Stabilization is-subtopic-of
Asphalt Undersealing is a topic under Slab Stabilization.
asphalt-undersealingundersealing
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Asphalt undersealing is a slab‑stabilization treatment applied to Portland‑cement concrete slabs that have lost support because voids have formed beneath them due to pumping action, subgrade consolidation or settlement, producing distress such as transverse joint faulting, corner breaks and blow‑holes[1]. The process involves drilling vertical holes (≤1½ in.) at affected joints or cracks and injecting 30–50 gal of hot liquid asphalt—heated to 380 °F‑450 °F and pumped at 25‑45 psi—so that the grout fills the voids and restores full support[2]. During injection slab lift is continuously monitored and must not exceed 0.05 in., a splash shield protects traffic, and the pavement remains closed for at least 30 minutes after pumping to allow the asphalt to cool before reopening[2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

In the context of slab stabilization as described in section 4.5 of the Pavement Policy Manual, asphalt undersealing is intended to treat loss of support in Portland‑cement concrete slabs that arises from voids developing beneath the slab due to pumping action, subgrade consolidation, or settlement; these voids produce excessive slab deflections and associated distress such as transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes), corner breaks, and overall slab settlement [1]. According to section 679.3(c) of the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications, the treatment is applied to slabs that exhibit voids or unstable base material beneath them—a condition that can cause the pavement to lift upward—and the grout injection is required to fill those cavities while monitoring slab movement so that upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is not permitted [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–604)

Slab stabilization using hot‑asphalt undersealing addresses loss of slab support caused by pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement that create voids beneath concrete slabs, which manifest as joint faulting, corner breaks and excessive deflection. By drilling holes at joints or cracks and pumping liquid asphalt into each hole—typically between 30 and 50 gallons per hole—the voids are filled so that full support is restored, thereby restoring the pavement’s structural integrity and reducing the potential for future pumping, faulting and cracking. Consequently, the objective of Asphalt Undersealing is to restore functionality of the slab. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Distress characterization and causes

The distress that Asphalt Undersealing seeks to remediate originates from pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement that may create small voids beneath the concrete slab, especially near transverse joints, cracks and outer corners; these voids reduce support, causing excessive slab deflections, joint faulting, corner breaks and diagonal cracking. Water entering through unsealed joints or cracks exacerbates the pumping, so joint and crack sealing together with functional subsurface drains are required to limit infiltration. Asphalt undersealing addresses the weak support by pumping hot liquid asphalt into drilled holes—typically between 30 and 50 gallons per hole—and allowing it to cool for a minimum of 30 minutes before traffic returns, thereby filling voids and restoring full support without increasing the pavement’s design structural capacity. When no preliminary testing has been performed, at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Applicability and treatment selection

Asphalt undersealing is suitable for Portland cement concrete slabs that exhibit loss of support such as transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a joint or crack, corner breaks, or general slab settlement. When these distresses are present, hot‑asphalt can be pumped into each drilled hole—typically between 30 and 50 gallons per hole—to fill the voids, provided that safety equipment is used to protect traffic and crew. Traffic must remain closed until the asphalt cools, which the source specifies as a minimum of 30 minutes after pumping, and joint and crack sealing should be performed concurrently while subsurface drains are kept in good condition. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Asphalt undersealing is used as a grout material in slab stabilization to fill voids and restore support, but it does not correct pavement surface depressions, increase the pavement’s design structural capacity, or eliminate faulting; therefore when those deficiencies are present, another maintenance, preservation, rehabilitation, or repair approach should be selected instead of asphalt undersealing. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Condition assessment and timing

To decide whether asphalt undersealing is required, a visual inspection looks for loss‑of‑support signs such as transverse joint faulting, fines accumulating near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, corner breaks, and slab settlement; when any of these distresses are observed, voids beneath the concrete are presumed present and the pavement is a candidate for undersealing. If no preliminary testing has been performed, the guidance calls for stabilizing at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints. These observations together provide the assessment needed to locate the holes that will be drilled and filled with hot asphalt or other grout material. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Asphalt undersealing is applied as a slab‑stabilization measure when voids beneath the concrete have caused loss of support, which is signaled by transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, corner breaks, or overall slab settlement. When any of these conditions are observed, hot‑asphalt undersealing—pumping liquid asphalt into drilled holes on both sides of the joint—is used to fill the voids and restore support, with typically 30 to 50 gallons pumped per hole and traffic reopened only after the asphalt cools for a minimum of 30 minutes. If no preliminary testing has been performed, at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

Materials and repair details

Asphalt undersealing consists of pumping hot liquid asphalt beneath a concrete slab on both sides of each joint [1]. The operation requires a fully insulated asphalt transport tanker equipped with a removable access hatch, an overflow pipe of at least 2 inches in diameter and suitable strainers at the outlet to the pumps [2]. A self‑contained reversible underseal pump is used together with a flexible metal circulating hose of minimum 1½ inches size and a quick cut‑off valve for recirculation when asphalt is not being injected [2]. Supporting equipment includes a pressure water distributor trailer having a capacity of at least 50 gallons, compressor units capable of delivering no less than 100 cfm, core drilling gear that produces smooth vertical holes, tapered injection nozzles with three‑way valves, cylindrical pavement plugs tapered to be driven no more than 2 inches into each hole, slab‑lift measurement devices accurate to 0.001 inch, and standard hand tools [2]. Each grout injection hole is drilled vertically, round, and to a diameter not larger than 1½ inches, deep enough to penetrate the stabilized base, and then cleared of debris with compressed air for approximately 5 to 15 seconds [2]. Between 30 and 50 gallons of hot liquid asphalt are pumped into each drilled hole [1]. The asphalt must be heated to a temperature between 380 °F and 450 °F, and any material exceeding 500 °F is rejected [2]. Injection is performed at a pump pressure ranging from 25 psi to 45 psi while a splash shield of no less than 15 sq ft protects traffic and crew [2]. Safety equipment, including the splash shield, is required to protect traffic and crew from hot material that may squirt out of the pavement during injection [1][2]. Slab lift is monitored so that upward movement does not exceed 0.05 inch during the operation [2]. After injection any excess asphalt is removed, and the slab must remain closed for a minimum of 30 minutes before it may be reopened to traffic [1][2]. The entire procedure shall follow the guidelines set forth in Publication 72M, RC‑26M, and Publication 408 Section 679 as referenced by the Pavement Policy Manual [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–604)

Performance of asphalt undersealing depends on the presence of voids beneath the concrete slab—especially near transverse joints or cracks—and on site conditions that favor a liquid‑asphalt grout [1]. The asphalt grout must be heated to a temperature between 380 °F and 450 °F before pumping; any material heated to 500 °F or higher shall be rejected [2]. During injection the asphalt must be kept circulating so that its viscosity remains suitable for placement [2]. Injection is carried out at a pressure ranging from 25 psi to 45 psi [2]. Any upward movement of the slab exceeding 0.05 inch during stabilization is prohibited [2]. Grout holes are required to be vertical, round, and no larger than 1½ inches in diameter, and they must be deep enough to penetrate any stabilized base material [2]. If water or thin mud is encountered within the cavity, it must be cleared by blowing air before injection, while water applied around the hole during pumping prevents seepage [2]. Because the material is pumped as hot liquid asphalt, appropriate safety equipment must be used, and traffic may not be reopened until the asphalt has cooled for at least 30 minutes after pumping [1]. Typical volumes of grout range between 30 and 50 gallons per hole [1]. Effective operation also requires joint and crack sealing to limit water infiltration, and subsurface drainage systems must remain in good condition to avoid further pumping of water into the slab [1].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–604)

Treatment procedures

Preparation for asphalt undersealing begins with establishing the required traffic control and safety equipment, and work is typically confined to a single lane at a time (source [1]). The pavement is then drilled at the affected joints or cracks; holes are made vertical, round, and not larger than 1½ inches in diameter, extending to a depth sufficient to penetrate any stabilized base, while the drill is backed out a compressed‑air blast is introduced for approximately 5 to 15 seconds (source [2]). Prior to injection, water is applied around each hole and a splash shield of at least 15 sq ft is erected to contain runoff (source [2]). The asphalt material is heated in an insulated tanker to a temperature between 380°F and 450°F; any material exceeding 500°F is rejected, and the hot mix is circulated before pumping (source [2]). Placement involves pumping the hot liquid asphalt on both sides of each joint; the typical volume is between 30 and 50 gallons per hole, and the pump pressure is maintained between 25 psi and 45 psi (source [1][2]). During injection the slab lift is monitored so that upward movement does not exceed 0.05 inch, with pumping halted if seepage occurs and excess material removed after each hole (source [2]). After placement the slab must remain closed to traffic until the asphalt cools, which requires a minimum of 30 minutes before reopening; this closure also allows coordination with joint sealing and subsurface drainage maintenance (source [1][2]).

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–604)

The undersealing process calls for pumping equipment to inject liquid asphalt into drilled holes and requires crew safety gear because the hot material can squirt from the pavement [1]. In addition, a full suite of specialized tools is specified, including an insulated transport tanker with a removable access hatch and a 2‑inch minimum overflow pipe, a reversible underseal pump with quick‑connect hoses, a pressure water distributor trailer holding at least 50 gallons, a compressor capable of delivering 100 cfm, core drilling gear equipped with diamond‑tip barrels, tapered nozzles fitted with three‑way valves, cylindrical pavement plugs and high‑resolution lift‑measurement devices [2]. The work should be sequenced so that drilling is performed immediately before stabilization, allowing both the drilling and stabilizing crews to share the same Maintenance and Protection of Traffic (MPT) setup and confining operations to a single lane at any one time [1]. According to the specification, the sequence begins with drilling vertical holes no larger than 1½ in., blowing compressed air for 5–15 seconds while backing out the drill, heating the asphalt to between 380°F and 450°F (material above 500°F must be rejected), circulating the heated mix, and then injecting it at a pressure of 25‑45 psi while monitoring slab lift so that it does not exceed 0.05 in.; a splash shield covering at least 15 sq ft must protect passing traffic during injection [2]. After pumping, traffic must remain closed until the asphalt has cooled for at least 30 minutes [1]. The grout must be allowed to set and traffic may not be permitted onto the pavement for a minimum of 30 minutes after grouting operations unless a field quality‑control plan or the manufacturer’s recommendation permits an earlier opening [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–604)

Quality and workmanship

The inspection should confirm that the asphalt material is heated to between 380°F and 450°F (and any batch above 500°F must be rejected), that drilling produces vertical, round holes no larger than 1½ inches and deep enough to reach the stabilized base, that compressed air is blown through each hole for 5‑15 seconds to clear debris, and that a splash shield of at least 15 sq ft protects traffic during injection. During installation the pump pressure must be kept between 25 psi and 45 psi while a lift‑monitoring device capable of detecting 0.001 inches records slab movement, ensuring upward movement does not exceed 0.05 inch; plugs must be driven to the proper depth and excess material removed. After grouting the work is considered complete only when the pavement has not risen beyond the limit, all plugs are in place, and traffic is withheld for at least 30 minutes (or as required by the field QC plan and manufacturer) before reopening. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–604)

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10), a satisfactory Asphalt Undersealing is considered achieved when the injected asphalt fills the voids beneath the slab sufficiently to restore full support. This condition is indicated by the absence of loss‑of‑support signs such as transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks, small depressions (blow holes) at shoulders, corner breaks, and slab settlement. Traffic may only be reopened after the asphalt has cooled for a minimum of 30 minutes following pumping. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 Specifications requires that drilling holes be vertical, round, and no larger than 1½ inches in diameter, with depth sufficient to penetrate any stabilized base. The asphalt grout must be heated to a temperature between 380°F and 450°F, and any material heated to 500°F or more is rejected. Pumping shall be performed at a pressure of 25 psi to 45 psi while a splash shield covering at least 15 sq ft protects traffic. Slab lift must be monitored with equipment capable of detecting movement to 0.001 inch, and upward movement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed. The pavement may not be opened to traffic for less than 30 minutes after grouting unless a field QC plan or the manufacturer’s recommendation permits earlier opening. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–604)

Performance and service life

Asphalt undersealing is applied during slab stabilization to fill the voids that develop beneath concrete slabs from pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement, which otherwise cause transverse joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and eventual slab breakup. By pumping liquid asphalt (typically between 30 and 50 gallons per hole) into drilled holes and allowing it to cool for a minimum of 30 minutes before traffic is reopened, the voids are filled, full support is restored and slab deflections are reduced, thereby restoring structural integrity and reducing the potential for future pumping, faulting and cracking. Although slab stabilization does not correct surface depressions or increase the pavement’s design structural capacity, the improved support improves the pavement’s condition and functionality and contributes to longer durability by limiting further distress. Consequently, the expected service life of the treated section is extended insofar as the restored integrity reduces future deterioration, provided that at least 25 percent of transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized if no preliminary testing has been performed. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92)

A common failure in asphalt undersealing is excessive slab lift; the specification states that upward movement of the pavement greater than 0.05 inch is not allowed, so any lift beyond this limit indicates a failed grout injection. Another recurring distress is premature asphalt seepage from joints or cracks before the material has congealed, which requires halting pumping until the asphalt sets. These problems are influenced by the injection pressure (required to be between 25 and 45 psi), the asphalt temperature (must be heated to 380°F–450°F and any mix above 500°F is rejected), and proper hole preparation such as blowing compressed air to clear water and applying water around the hole to prevent sticking. Maintaining these procedural controls and continuously monitoring lift are essential to avoid the identified failures. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–604)

Monitoring and follow-up

The treated area should be checked with vertical‑movement testing equipment that can measure slab lift to 0.001 inch, providing a positive means of monitoring lift as required; any upward movement greater than 0.05 inch must not occur. After the grout has set, traffic may not be allowed onto the pavement for at least 30 minutes unless the contractor’s approved field QC plan or the manufacturer’s recommendations permit earlier opening. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 603–604)

Additional maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation is indicated when the slab beneath the asphalt underseal shows loss of support manifested by transverse joint faulting, accumulation of fines near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow‑holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, corner breaks, or overall slab settlement [1]. If, after the asphalt grout injection, the pavement exhibits an upward movement exceeding 0.05 inch, the treatment is out of tolerance and further maintenance, repair, or rehabilitation is required [2].
