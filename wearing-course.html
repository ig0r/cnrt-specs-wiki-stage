Wearing Course

The wearing course is the uppermost pavement layer that provides a smooth, durable, skid‑resistant surface for vehicle traffic, transfers loads to the underlying structure, and protects it from moisture and environmental damage; it may be placed as hot or warm mix asphalt, roller‑compacted concrete, or other specified materials in accordance with design thickness, material, and performance requirements.

Purpose and design objectives

The wearing course is installed as the top layer of a pavement structure to protect the underlying layers from traffic‑induced wear while providing a surface texture that yields acceptable ride quality [1]. In roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) applications the wearing course may serve as the final layer adjacent to reinforced or plain cement concrete pavements, and when an exposed RCC surface will carry vehicles at a speed limit of 35 mph or greater the surface must be textured by diamond grinding [1]. For flexible base (FB) wearing courses the design seeks a surface that can withstand traffic‑induced abrasion yet remain sufficiently flexible for low‑volume roadways; FB‑1 is recommended for low‑volume roads with highly flexible existing pavement structures and may be used independently on roadways having an average daily traffic (ADT) of 1,500 or less, whereas for roadways with ADT greater than 1,500 a combination of binder and wearing courses must be employed [1]. Because FB‑1 and FB‑2 surfaces have high void content, the specification requires placement of a seal coat or surface treatment after a minimum of three months of warm‑weather traffic densification to protect the voidy matrix from premature abrasion [1]. The pavement surface is required to maintain an adequate level of friction throughout its service life; it must possess sufficient surface texture (low‑speed gradient) to prevent water pressure buildup at the tire/pavement interface at the posted speed limit and must retain these properties under traffic and environmental conditions [1]. Within Superpave paving courses the wearing course is intended to resist permanent deformation (rutting) and low‑temperature thermal cracking, and when the estimated equivalent single axle load (ESAL) range exceeds 10 million ESALs the district should specify a polymer‑modified binder (PG 64E‑22) for the wearing course to avoid rutting [1]. When joint faulting in an existing concrete pavement exceeds one‑half inch, the design calls for placement of a thin (1‑inch) Superpave fine‑graded wearing course (9.5 mm) at the joint or milling/grinding of the joints to provide uniform support and mitigate fault‑related distress [1]. Stone Matrix Asphalt (SMA) is prescribed as a wearing course on roadways with greater than 30 million ESALs because its stone‑to‑stone contact and rich mortar binder create a tough, stable, rut‑resistant surface that meets durability requirements [1]. The asphalt rubber gap‑graded (AR‑GG) wearing course is specified to supply a durable, wear‑resistant surface for pavements carrying heavy traffic, particularly interstates or similar routes with ADT greater than 25,000 and ESALs exceeding 10 million over a 20‑year design life (and at least 50,000 sq yd), where its higher cost can be justified by the performance benefits [1]. Cold‑recycled base mixtures have higher void content and are less resistant to abrasion; therefore a wearing surface is imperative to provide protection and a smooth riding surface that meets performance expectations [1]. The specifications also define the wearing course as the element that must deliver a durable, smooth riding surface capable of resisting traffic wear and preventing surface defects such as edge breakdown, flushing, and pattern segregation; consequently immediate finishing of exposed outer edges is required to eliminate sharp, ragged, or open edges [2]. Macro‑texture measurements not greater than 0.006 in indicate a flushed condition that is considered defective, and the difference in average texture depth between segregated and non‑segregated areas must not exceed 0.024 in to avoid unacceptable pattern segregation [2]. To satisfy the 60‑month warranty performance criteria, the wearing course must be placed to the required thickness, and any core taken within 24 hours after final compaction that shows a depth deficiency greater than 1/4 in for a single core or more than 1/8 in uniformly across three consecutive cores is deemed defective work that must be corrected [2]. The material used in the wearing course must incorporate aggregate with the specified SRL designation from a source listed in Bulletin 14 and be designed with the Superpave Gyratory Compactor so that the surface layer can endure traffic wear for the warranty period [2]. When applied as a slurry seal, the wearing course is provided to combat deterioration of the pavement surface by sealing cracks, filling voids, and correcting surface erosion; depending on the type it may be required only to supply a minimum wearing surface for severely eroded areas, to provide a new moderate wearing surface, or to build up a crown for improved ride quality [2].

For roadways where the average daily traffic does not exceed 2,000 vehicles and the speed limit does not exceed 55 mph, the wearing course is required to function as a surface capable of supporting the specified traffic loads for an estimated design life of 20 years. In roller‑compacted concrete applications the thickness may be selected between 4 inches and 18 inches so that compressive strengths can range from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi. Control joints in such RCC sections shall be spaced at 15–20 feet for sections less than 8 inches thick or at a distance equal to the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet for thicker sections. When an exposed RCC surface is located on a route with a speed limit of 35 mph or greater, the surface must be textured by diamond grinding, and a rougher texture shall not be used where International Roughness Index measurements determine ride‑quality payments. Fiber‑bound (FB) wearing courses may be employed independently on roadways having an ADT of 1,500 or less, whereas for roadways with ADT greater than 1,500 a combination of binder and wearing courses must be used. When a deflection‑based design is applied to FB overlays the structural coefficient shall be taken as 0.20. Because FB‑1 and FB‑2 surfaces have high void content, a seal coat or surface treatment shall be placed after a minimum of three months of warm‑weather traffic densification, although this may be deferred up to three years if the underlying pavement is sound; FB Modified surfaces may defer such treatment for at least four years. The asphalt wearing course must provide an adequate level of friction throughout its service life, develop sufficient surface texture (low‑speed gradient) to prevent water pressure buildup at the tire/pavement interface at the posted speed limit, and retain these properties under traffic and environmental conditions. Superpave wearing courses shall be designed for the climatic and traffic conditions of their location by selecting an appropriate performance‑grade binder and using the 20‑year design ESALs to prevent permanent deformation (rutting) and low‑temperature thermal cracking. Durability criteria require specifying a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder when the estimated ESAL range exceeds ten million in order to avoid pavement rutting. Safety and serviceability objectives include maintaining the required skid‑resistance level of the asphalt mix. When placed as overlays, the wearing course shall be installed at a uniform depth that meets the minimum thickness of 1½ inches for Superpave overlays unless prior PDAU approval is obtained, and it may only be applied to sound asphalt pavements where less than 2 % of the pavement requires patching, surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded to good, and no structural upgrade is required. A PG 64E‑22 binder shall be used for standard 6.3 mm wearing courses but not for 9.5 mm Fine Grade mixes. The Safety Edge shall be incorporated on the outside edge of the wearing or binder course when the total depth is at least 1½ inches and not more than 5 inches, providing a 30° taper to aid vehicle recovery. Cold‑recycled base courses require a wearing layer because of their higher void content; the wearing layer shall be placed after at least one week of curing under low humidity high temperature conditions and its compacted thickness shall be no less than 3 inches and no more than 5 inches. Because recycled mixes are less resistant to abrasion, they should not be used on high‑ADT roads unless traffic can be detoured until the base gains adequate strength. Stone matrix asphalt wearing courses must function as a tough, stable, rut‑resistant surface and structurally achieve stone‑to‑stone contact to supply the required strength. Durability of SMA is attained by providing a rich mortar binder and selecting an aggregate gradation that meets or exceeds the minimum voids in mineral aggregate so that the minimum binder content can be used, while also targeting the desired air‑void level and confirming resistance to moisture susceptibility and asphalt cement draindown. Crumb‑rubber modified dense‑graded wearing courses shall serve the same functional role as a dense‑graded surface that would normally use a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, and their structural contribution should be evaluated using the Superpave layer coefficients. The thickness of CRMAB layers should not exceed 2½ inches; they are to be placed only in the upper layers of the pavement where their superior performance is best utilized, and they shall not be used in temporary lanes or shoulders unless the lane and shoulder are paved in one pass. For safety and cost‑effectiveness CRMAB mixes are limited to Interstates or Interstate look‑alikes with more than 25,000 ADT, more than ten million ESALs for a 20‑year design life, and greater than 50,000 sq yd. In long‑life asphalt pavements the wearing course shall be a highly durable surface such as SMA, providing the durability needed for the perpetual‑pavement concept, limiting fatigue cracking in the base and restricting maintenance to periodic resurfacing rather than full‑depth repairs. Hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) wearing courses must remediate rutting and surface distress, improve appearance and extend service life; structurally they are typically 1½ inches thick with a possible range of 1 to 2 inches, and for composite pavements at least 1 to 2 inches of existing asphalt must remain beneath the HIR layer (or 2 to 3 inches for full‑depth asphalt), with the pre‑existing asphalt thickness exceeding any milling depth by at least 1½ inches. Durability requirements for HIR specify suitability for any ADT and use as a short‑term interim wearing surface pending a permanent surface course, with traffic able to be opened as soon as the pavement is cooled to the required temperature. Safety and serviceability criteria for HIR limit rutting severity to less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth to less than 1.0 inch with average crack width ≤ ½ inch, raveling not to exceed a medium rating, minor corrugation, shoving or slippage to an average depth < 1½ inches, while allowing minor bleeding and low friction numbers. When the wearing course is used as an Ultra‑Thin Bonded Concrete Overlay it is treated as a non‑structural layer of 2 inches asphalt depth that should provide a smooth riding surface while resisting traffic wear, be capable of eliminating rutting or other material problems, and milling and overlay depths of 1½ inches or 2 inches may be exceeded to achieve the necessary corrective depth. Micro‑surfacing or Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course should be placed early in the maintenance cycle to improve skid resistance, ride quality and fill ruts before advanced distresses emerge. The wearing course shall be placed at the specified laying temperature and spread across the full lane width, and its outer edges must be worked immediately after placement to eliminate sharp, ragged or open edges. Its compacted depth shall not exceed six inches; if a greater depth is required it shall be built in two or more layers each between three and six inches while compaction is adjusted to satisfy density acceptance and avoid roller marks, shoving or aggregate breakage. A watertight joint at curbs shall be sealed with hot asphalt or an approved emulsified binder over at least twelve inches, and the surface must avoid pattern segregation (texture difference less than 0.024 in) and flushing (surface texture depth greater than 0.006 in). The safety edge, constructed of the same material as the wearing course, shall have a minimum depth of 1.5 inches, a total depth not over five inches, and an angle of 26 to 40 degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope; it must be inspected each day and paving stopped if the shape requirement is not met. The wearing course is considered defective if surface irregularities exceed 3/16 inch when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge across the pavement width, if depth deficiency exceeds 1/4 inch, or if three or more adjacent core samples show a shortfall of more than 1/8 inch. Vehicular traffic or loads shall not be placed on newly compacted courses for 24 hours or until the course uniformly cools to a temperature of 140 °F or less. The 6.3 mm wearing‑course shall be placed only when surface and air temperatures are above 50 °F (or, if a weather‑limit exception is granted, the surface may be as low as 50 °F while the air temperature must not fall below 40 °F) and never on wet surfaces. Compaction shall be performed immediately after placement to meet the optimum rolling pattern, and mat density shall be verified using an approved nuclear or electrical impedance gauge in accordance with PTM 402/403. Aggregate breakage shall be prevented by avoiding vibratory rollers unless it can be demonstrated that no breaking occurs; cores shall be monitored for breakage on every lot, steel‑wheeled rollers of at least 10 tons shall be kept clean and moist to prevent material adhesion, pneumatic‑tire rollers are prohibited, and rollers must be equipped with watering or soapy systems; all roller marks shall be eliminated to achieve a smooth riding surface. The Superpave asphalt wearing course must meet mixture‑acceptance limits such that the payment factor for asphalt content, percent passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve and percent passing the 2.36 mm (No. 8) sieve are each at least 85 %; if density acceptance is based on cores, the in‑place density percent‑within‑limits shall not fall below 50 %, otherwise the lot is deemed defective. The wearing course shall be placed on a binder course whose surface irregularities are corrected and, if possible, kept free of traffic contamination; any binder that cannot be cleaned shall be replaced to the Representative’s satisfaction. It shall be applied as soon as practical after the binder course without a tack coat between lifts, spread with a track paver or mechanical spreader to achieve the design cross‑section and grade while using automatic sensors for profile control. Compaction is limited to no more than four passes of a 10‑ton steel‑wheeled static roller, and over‑compaction that eliminates free drainage or crushes aggregate shall be avoided; placement may not occur when the air or surface temperature is below 50 °F. Within 24 hours after final compaction a core of at least 4 inches diameter shall be taken every 2,000 ft; if any single core shows a depth deficiency of more than 1/4 inch that cannot be satisfactorily corrected, or if three consecutive cores are uniformly deficient by more than 1/8 inch, the area is deemed defective and the contractor must immediately correct the pavement until the depth throughout the repair zone is within 1/4 inch of the required depth. The thin‑lift 6.3 mm asphalt wearing course shall satisfy the mixture‑acceptance and density‑acceptance requirements described in Section 412.4, meaning that its asphalt content and the percent passing the 75 µm sieve are each evaluated by a payment factor and the pavement will be considered defective if both of those factors equal 85 %; consequently the design must achieve values that keep at least one of them above this threshold, and density shall be verified using the optimum‑rolling‑pattern test so that a density payment factor can be applied. The mixture shall be produced so that its volumetric properties meet the tolerances set out in Table C, ensuring the required air voids and VMA at Ndesign; its gradation and asphalt content shall fall within the single‑sample and multiple‑sample limits of Table B for each QC sample, and if the total binder exceeds 7 % the draindown sensitivity shall be measured and fibers added whenever the result exceeds the limits in Table A. Acceptance also requires that at least 90 % of daily printed ticket asphalt‑content values are within 0.2 percentage points of the Joint Mix Formula, and any failure to meet these tolerances triggers immediate corrective action, possible suspension of production, and a written explanation before resuming work. The polymer‑modified micro‑surfacing mix shall contain a polymer modifier capable of making a mix which cures fast enough to allow traffic to be placed on the pavement within 1 hour without damaging the surface, thereby satisfying functional and safety objectives. For wearing courses in this section the specification calls for aggregate with at least the SRL designation specified; fine aggregate shall be Type B or better and a final gradation shall conform to Table A, ensuring structural capacity and serviceability. The filler shall be supplied so that the percent by weight passing the 75 µm (No. 200) sieve complies with the requirement, supporting durability. A QC Plan, as specified in Section 106, shall be prepared and submitted for review at the start of the project and at least annually thereafter; mixing equipment shall be calibrated, daily run‑sheet records shall be kept, and each shipment shall be certified within one working day after testing to provide the necessary quality‑control and serviceability assurances. The wearing course used for a temporary asphalt pavement wedge transition shall be designed according to the Superpave Asphalt Mixture Design, Wearing Course (Scratch) with performance grade PG 64S‑22, a 9.5 mm mix identified as SRL‑L, and it must be suitable for traffic ranging from 0.3 to less than 3 million ESALs, providing the durability objective that the wearing course must satisfy. Scope and applicability

The wearing course is used on any asphalt‑concrete surface regardless of base type, so it applies to all pavement types that have an asphalt concrete wearing surface[1]. It may be placed on flexible‑pavement projects over standard bases or over membrane systems provided the mixture temperature follows the membrane manufacturer’s recommendation[2]. For a rigid pavement first asphalt overlay the required thickness is four inches when a 9.5 mm Wearing Course mix is used or four and one‑half inches when a 12.5 mm mix is used, while for flexible pavement resurfacing the required thickness is one‑and‑a‑half inches with a 9.5 mm mix or two inches with a 12.5 mm mix[1]. Low‑volume roads that have highly flexible existing pavement structures are appropriate for FB‑1 and FB‑2 wearing courses; FB‑1 is a cold‑mix with softer asphalt recommended for such conditions, whereas FB‑2 requires stationary mixing and may use PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement in a hot mix[1]. FB wearing courses may be placed as the sole surface on roadways having an ADT of 1 500 or less, while roadways with ADT greater than 1 500 must employ a combination of binder and wearing courses[1]. Roller‑compacted concrete can serve as the final wearing course for concrete shoulders adjacent to reinforced or plain cement concrete pavements, for temporary median crossovers and widening areas used during construction, and for roadways with an ADT of less than or equal to 2 000 and a speed limit of 55 mph or less; it may also be placed as a base beneath an asphalt wearing course, and if the RCC pavement will have an exposed surface and a speed limit of 35 mph or greater the surface must be textured by diamond grinding[1]. The coarse aggregate used in an asphalt wearing course shall have a Skid Resistance Level (SRL) determined from the anticipated average daily traffic, and only aggregates with the required SRL or better may be used; high‑SRL designs are prohibited on non‑wearing courses, leveling courses, and shoulders[1][2]. For surfaces expected to carry more than ten million ESALs the specification calls for PG 64E‑22 polymer‑modified binder in the wearing course, especially where severe rutting conditions exist, while for an estimated ESAL range of 0.3 to less than three million ESALs the district may specify an ESAL range of less than 0.3 million for the wearing and binder courses when cracking is a concern, average truck speed exceeds 30 mph, or rutting is not a concern; fiber‑modified courses may be used optionally with PG 58S‑28, PG 64S‑22, or PG 64E‑22 binders[1]. For paved‑shoulder projects classified as Type 1 a Superpave asphalt wearing course designated SRL‑L may be used with PG 64S‑22 binder when the anticipated traffic does not exceed 0.3 million ESALs, and when traffic falls between 0.3 and less than three million ESALs the Asphalt Wearing Course specified in Section 413.2 is appropriate[2]. Stone Matrix Asphalt wearing courses are recommended when a project involves at least 50 000 square yards—quantities nearer 100 000 sq yd make SMA especially suitable—and for Interstates, Interstate look‑alike highways, or high‑speed freeways; they are also advised for existing roadways that carry greater than 30 million ESALs and where durable aggregates, a high asphalt content, and a fiber or polymer‑modified binder can be provided, but SMA should be avoided in locations requiring extensive handwork or frequent paver adjustments such as intersections, and it is not suitable where the underlying pavement has problems[1][2]. Asphalt Rubber Gap‑Graded (AR‑GG) wearing courses may be considered for new construction or overlay projects on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements that would otherwise require a gap‑graded wearing or binder course with polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder when the project meets high‑traffic criteria of Interstates and Interstate look‑alikes with more than 25 000 ADT, more than ten million ESALs (20‑year design life), and an area greater than 50 000 square yards; the mix uses a gap‑graded aggregate skeleton with high air voids, a rubberized binder that must meet ASTM D6114 Table 1 Type II, contains crumb rubber constituting at least 15 % by weight of the total blend, may incorporate RAP or RAS, must be produced by the wet process, and if warm‑mix asphalt is used it must include a chemical warm‑mix additive (foaming not allowed); layer thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches and AR‑GG should not be used where significant handwork or raking is required, in shoulders unless the lane and shoulder are paved in one pass, or in temporary lanes or crossovers, and it should only be employed when cost‑effective because it is a premium mixture[1]. Crumb Rubber Modified Asphalt Binder (CRMAB) mixtures are recommended for projects that would normally use dense‑graded wearing or binder courses with polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, may be used in the upper layers of new construction or overlay sections on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements, and are appropriate for Interstates and Interstate look‑alikes exceeding 25 000 ADT, ten million ESALs (20‑year design life), and 50 000 square yards; the mixture may contain RAP or RAS as specified, must follow the wet process, layer thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches, and CRMAB should not be used in shoulders unless the lane and shoulder are paved in one pass, nor where significant handwork or raking is required, nor in temporary lanes or crossovers[1]. When a cold‑recycled asphalt base course is used, a wearing surface is appropriate because these mixes have higher void content; it must be placed only after a minimum of one week of curing under favorable weather (low humidity, high temperature), on projects where traffic can be controlled or detoured during the curing period, and not on roads carrying 200 or more daily ESALs, with the underlying recycled base compacted to at least three inches but no more than five inches before the wearing course is applied[1]. A hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) interim wearing surface may be used when the existing pavement exhibits only surface‑level distress meeting specified limits for rutting, cracking, raveling, corrugation, shoving, slippage, bleeding or friction; it is suitable for any level of ADT but must be followed by a permanent surface course before the construction season ends, and all durable markings, large mastic patches (>5 ft²), and over‑banded rubberized joint sealants must be removed prior to treatment[1]. For an unbonded Portland cement concrete overlay that cannot be cost‑effectively repaired, a nominal depth of one inch of Superpave Wearing Course, 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix serves as the interlayer material; if concrete joint faulting exceeds one‑quarter inch milling is required, and if faulting exceeds one‑half inch a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch), 9.5 mm mix should be placed at the joint[1]. An Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course may be applied on flexible pavements as a preservation treatment to improve skid resistance, ride quality, or fill ruts and must be placed early in the maintenance cycle before advanced distresses emerge; on concrete (rigid) pavements it can be provided as an ultra‑thin bonded concrete overlay with an asphalt depth of two inches serving as a non‑structural wearing course, all within the thickness and condition limits set by the preservation guidelines[1]. If the compacted thickness of the wearing course exceeds six inches it shall be constructed in two or more layers each between three and six inches deep, and a safety edge of the same material may be formed on the outer pavement edge with a depth of at least one‑and‑a‑half inches[2]. When installed adjacent to flush curbs, gutters or other abutting structures the mixture is set uniformly higher so that after compaction the finished surface sits slightly above the edge[2]. Where mechanical spreaders cannot be used hand tools may be employed for placement and screeding[2]. For flexible‑pavement projects a wearing course may be joined to existing pavement using notched wedge joints when the nominal maximum aggregate size does not exceed 19.0 mm; the joint shall be sealed with hot asphalt of the class and type designated for the wearing course, or alternatively with emulsified asphalt identified as PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT, applying sealant evenly at least six inches on each side to obtain a watertight connection[2]. PG 64E‑22 mixtures are specified for surfaces expected to carry more than ten million ESALs and for thin layers such as 4.75 mm thick or any wearing course placed at a compacted depth of less than one‑and‑a‑half inches, and paving may occur only between April 1 and October 15, with an extension allowing placement through November 15[2]. Plant‑mixed 6.3 mm thin asphalt overlay courses used as the top layer on flexible pavements shall not be placed from October 1 to March 31 and may not be placed when surfaces are wet or when air or surface temperature is 50°F or lower unless an exception is granted that requires a minimum surface temperature of 50°F and a minimum air temperature of 40°F[2]. Construction must use steel‑wheeled rollers each weighing at least ten tons, a material transfer vehicle for delivery, and immediate compaction to achieve the specified rolling pattern and mat density[2]. For pervious asphalt pavement systems the wearing course is placed after the binder course and correction of surface irregularities; placement may occur only when air or surface temperature is not lower than 50°F, and compaction shall be performed with smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode using one to four passes without a tack coat between lifts, limiting compaction to no more than four passes so that surface porosity is retained[2]. When gravel is used as the coarse aggregate for an asphalt wearing course it must contain at least 85 % crushed particles with a minimum of two fracture faces; absorption may not exceed 3.0 % for Type A use or 3.5 % for Type B use[2]; steel‑slag aggregate that has been accepted by the LTS may also be placed in asphalt surface courses, including wearing‑course applications[2]. A slurry‑seal wearing course is appropriate for flexible pavements requiring crack sealing, void filling or correction of surface erosion (Type I), more severe erosion or a minimum wearing surface (Type II), or a new moderate wearing surface or crown buildup (Type III); the mix shall use emulsified asphalt of Class SS‑1h or CSS‑1h together with fine aggregate meeting the SRL designation, filler as specified, and water free of harmful soluble salts with hardness not exceeding 150 ppm unless softened, and shall be designed and tested according to ASTM D3910[2]. The 6.3 mm wearing‑course mixture may also be used for scratch courses, leveling courses and driveway adjustments, for mixtures employed by Department maintenance forces or purchased by local municipal governments, and for placements of less than 350 tons in a continuous operation unless the Representative directs otherwise; it shall not be placed directly on top of membrane systems[2]. For asphalt‑concrete pavements covered by Section 496’s 60‑month warranty the wearing‑course design approach is applied; the contractor must provide material that satisfies the warranty performance criteria, aggregate shall have the specified SRL designation from a source listed in Bulletin 14 (or an equal or better blend limited to 50 % by weight), Stone Matrix Asphalt mixes may be submitted for consideration, and the mix design shall be established volumetrically using the Superpave Gyratory Compactor to generate JMF values together with a QC plan approved under Section 106 before work begins[2]. For paved‑shoulder projects classified as Type 1 a Superpave asphalt wearing course designated SRL‑L may be used; the design shall follow the provisions of Sections 413.2, 410.2 or 412.2 and employ PG 64S‑22 binder when the anticipated traffic does not exceed 0.3 million ESALs unless otherwise indicated[2]. When a pavement design calls for a wearing layer using PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement and the anticipated traffic falls between 0.3 and less than three million ESALs, the Asphalt Wearing Course specified in Section 413.2 is appropriate, or it may be used when the project documentation specifically indicates its use[2]. For pervious asphalt pavement systems that employ a 9.5‑mm wearing‑course layer the wearing course described in Section 420 is intended; it requires Asphalt Cement, Class PG 64H‑22 or PG 64E‑22 as specified in Section 702 and approved polymer additives or ground‑tire rubber may be used to meet the required PG grade[2]. The Wearing Course design described in Section 412 is appropriate for thin‑lift wearing courses that are constructed using plant‑mixed, dense‑graded asphalt concrete with a nominal maximum aggregate size of 6.3 mm and placed over a prepared surface[2]. The Wearing Course described in Section 410 is appropriate for standard construction as well as Rapid Placement System projects when a plant‑mixed asphalt layer is placed on a prepared surface; the design shall employ a volumetric mixture developed with the Superpave Gyratory Compactor and then modified to a fine‑graded (FG) mixture[2]. A 4.75‑mm Asphalt Wearing Course (SRL L) with PG 64S‑22 binder is appropriate when projected traffic does not exceed less than 0.3 million ESALs, as referenced in Section 413.2[2]. For flexible‑pavement projects where a plant‑mixed Superpave thin asphalt overlay is specified, the 6.3‑mm Wearing Course shall use aggregate from approved producers that meets at least the SRL designation called out in the section and satisfies void content, sand equivalent, abrasion loss, shape and crushed‑fragment limits; Reclaimed Asphalt Pavement or Recycled Asphalt Shingles are prohibited, fly ash may not be used when the design traffic is greater than or equal to three million ESALs, and hydrated lime may constitute up to two percent of the total dry aggregate weight unless otherwise shown on the plans[2]. The Wearing Course may also be used on flexible‑pavement projects that employ a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt micro‑surfacing system as the top layer; in such cases the mix shall use Class CQS‑1hPM emulsified asphalt together with a latex‑based polymer modifier capable of curing fast enough to permit traffic within one hour, fine aggregate of Type B or better, coarse aggregate that meets the SRL designation (blends may be used at fifty percent by weight each), filler of non‑air‑entrained Type 1 Portland cement, water free of harmful soluble salts with hardness not exceeding 150 ppm unless softened, and a certified mix design and QC plan must be submitted before use[2]. For a temporary asphalt pavement wedge transition the Wearing Course (Scratch) design is specified when the project calls for a Superpave Asphalt Mixture Design using PG 64S‑22, traffic ranging from 0.3 to less than three million ESALs, a 9.5 mm mix and SRL‑L as required by Section 413[2]. The Superpave wearing‑course design described in Section 413 applies to the top layer of a flexible pavement where the aggregate blend shall meet at least the specified SRL designation and may be formed from two aggregates blended fifty percent each; fine and coarse aggregates must satisfy uncompacted void, sand equivalent, abrasion, flat‑and‑elongated piece, and fractured‑face limits set out in AASHTO M 323, RAP may be incorporated provided a minimum of five percent cold‑milled or crushed asphalt is used, recycled asphalt shingles may be added at five percent by mass only if shredded to pass a 9.5 mm sieve and kept separate by felt type with proper certification, fly ash cannot be used as filler when the design traffic is greater than or equal to three million ESALs, and 4.75‑mm mixtures must not be placed in applications that require an SRL designation higher than L[2].

When an existing asphalt pavement shows poor drainage, base failure, uneven surface, insufficient structural capacity, low friction, or deteriorated shoulders, designers are directed to consider alternatives such as base repair, scratch/leveling course, binder course, friction treatments, or shoulder reconstruction instead of a simple wearing‑course overlay [1]. Under the Life‑Cycle Cost Analysis guidelines, an alternate pavement design is justified when agency‑only life‑cycle cost difference reaches ten percent or more, or when the total including user delay costs reaches twenty percent or more; projects with substantial non‑pavement items may not be suitable for alternate pavement type bidding and therefore favor the conventional wearing‑course structure, and a unique extraordinary circumstance must exist to support a costlier alternative [1]. Roller Compacted Concrete (RCC) may serve as a final wearing course only on roadways whose average daily traffic does not exceed two thousand vehicles and whose posted speed limit is fifty‑five miles per hour or less; if either the ADT exceeds that value or the speed limit is higher, a different pavement design should be chosen, and RCC also should not be selected where the International Roughness Index will be measured for ride‑quality incentives [1]. For FB (fine‑graded) surface courses, wearing courses may be used independently on roadways having an ADT of one thousand five hundred or less; when ADT is greater than one thousand five hundred a binder and wearing combination must be used instead, FB Modified courses are prohibited on Federal‑aid projects, and a structural coefficient of 0.20 must be applied for FB overlays [1]. When the projected twenty‑year design ESALs for a segment exceed ten million, the district should specify a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder for the wearing course to avoid rutting; conversely, if the ESAS range falls between 0.3 and less than three million, the district may lower the specification to an ESAL range of less than 0.3 million for the wearing and binder courses when asphalt cracking is a regional problem, average truck speed exceeds thirty miles per hour, or rutting is not a concern, provided that frequent stopping conditions, intersections, or steep hills with slow truck traffic are not present [1]. A thin Superpave wearing‑course overlay less than one‑and‑a‑half inches thick on non‑Interstate pavements shall be used only if the existing surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than two percent patching, both surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded to good, and no structural upgrade is required; otherwise a thicker flexible overlay, a rigid concrete overlay, or a leveling course must be selected, and only a leveling course may be employed to correct longitudinal profile or re‑establish proper cross‑section [1]. When project quantities are below fifty thousand square yards SMA becomes cost‑prohibitive and a conventional dense‑graded Superpave wearing course is preferred; quantities approaching one hundred thousand square yards make SMA viable, but projects that require extensive handwork, many changes to paver configuration such as at intersections, or have issues in the underlying pavement should avoid SMA because it is generally more expensive due to its durable aggregates, higher asphalt content, and typically a modified binder [1]. AR‑GG gap‑graded mixtures should be avoided when a project demands extensive handwork or raking because the mixture’s stiffness hampers placement; they are not warranted for shoulders unless lane and shoulder can be paved in one pass, nor for temporary lanes or crossovers where a premium mix is unnecessary, and the layer thickness of an AR‑GG wearing course must not exceed two and a half inches; because AR‑GG is a premium material that typically costs more than conventional Superpave mixes, it should only be used when cost‑effectiveness can be demonstrated, otherwise a standard wearing‑course design is preferable [1]. Crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder dense‑graded mixtures share the same limitations: they should not be used for projects where significant handwork or raking is required due to mixture stiffness, where the higher premium cost cannot be justified, in lower pavement sections, in shoulders that are not paved together with the lane, on temporary lanes or crossovers, or when the required thickness would exceed two and a half inches [1]. Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) mixtures are considered a relatively short‑term interim wearing surface and require a permanent surface course before the end of the construction season; they are acceptable only if distress is limited to the surface, rutting severity is less than one and a half inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth is less than one inch, average crack width does not exceed a medium rating of one‑half inch, raveling or weathering does not exceed a medium rating, corrugation/shoving/slippage depth is less than one and a half inches, the asphalt thickness is at least one and a half inches greater than the milling depth, and no rubber or fiber material is present in the top two inches; otherwise a longer‑lasting solution is preferred [1]. When an overlay calls for a concrete layer bonded to existing asphalt pavement—or to an AC/PCC composite pavement—the standard wearing‑course design is not appropriate and the project must use the bonded concrete overlay mechanistic‑empirical design procedure because the 1993 AASHTO Pavement Design Procedures do not account for bonding effects; if the required minimum thickness cannot be met because a parking lane and critical curb reveal are present, the wearing course may be tapered to one inch at the curb with careful compaction and joint sealing [1]. When an existing concrete slab is visibly rocking or otherwise unstable, an unbonded concrete overlay with full‑depth repairs is recommended rather than relying on a wearing course; similarly, if joint faulting exceeds one half inch, a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) or milling of the joints is required instead of the standard wearing course, and if fracturing of the existing concrete pavement is preferred, only an asphalt overlay using the “AC over Fractured PCC” design module should be employed [1]. Preservation limits dictate that an overlay exceeding two inches on existing flexible pavements or more than four and a half inches with a Superpave 9.5 mm mix (or more than five inches with a Superpave 12.5 mm mix) on concrete surfaces must be treated as a full design rather than a preservation wearing‑course treatment; likewise, if rutting or material problems require milling and overlay depths beyond the one‑and‑half to two inch range allowed for preservation, or cold in‑place recycling would need to go deeper than the four‑inch maximum, an alternative structural approach is required [1]. Micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing‑course treatments are only acceptable early in the maintenance cycle before advanced distresses emerge; concrete pavements that would need patching of more than ten percent of the area must use other restoration methods unless a cost‑effective justification can be shown [1]. When the intended wearing‑course thickness exceeds six inches in compacted depth, the specification requires constructing it in two or more layers of approximately equal depth, with no layer less than three inches or more than six inches thick, and may call for a binder, leveling, scratch, or additional leveling course before completing full‑depth paving; thus a multi‑layer or alternative course approach becomes preferable under those thickness conditions [2]. The 6.3 mm wearing‑course overlay may not be placed during the prescribed winter windows for many districts, and placement is prohibited when the surface is wet or when the air or surface temperature falls to fifty degrees Fahrenheit or lower, unless a written exception is granted that also imposes a minimum surface temperature of fifty degrees Fahrenheit and an air temperature of forty degrees Fahrenheit; projects encountering such temporal or thermal limits must consider an alternative pavement design or construction method rather than the specified wearing‑course overlay [2]. If the air or surface temperature is lower than fifty degrees Fahrenheit, pervious pavement mixtures—including the wearing course—must not be placed, compaction must be limited to no more than four passes and must not over‑compact the material to the point that it is not free draining; otherwise the pervious wearing course would fail its functional criteria and another approach should be considered [2]. When a pavement layer is to be placed directly on top of a membrane system as defined in Section 467 or Section 680, the 6.3 mm wearing‑course paving mixture is prohibited, so an alternative design must be selected [2]. The Superpave Asphalt Wearing Course (SRL‑L) is limited to a performance grade of PG 64S‑22 and traffic levels less than three hundred thousand ESALs; when the anticipated loading exceeds that threshold or a different grade is required, the specification directs that an alternative design approach be used instead of the wearing course [2]. The Asphalt Wearing Course specified in Section 413.2 is limited to traffic levels between 0.3 and less than three million ESALs; when the projected cumulative load falls below that lower bound or meets or exceeds the upper bound, the wearing‑course design is not indicated and a different pavement structure—such as an Asphalt Base Course for higher loads—should be considered [2]. The specification bars the use of reclaimed asphalt pavement or recycled asphalt shingles in the 6.3 mm Wearing Course; therefore another pavement design must be chosen when such materials are required [2]. When the design traffic is greater than or equal to three million Equivalent Single Axle Loads, fly ash cannot be used as filler for this wearing course, prompting a different mix approach [2]. Under Section 413, using a 4.75 mm asphalt mix for the wearing course is forbidden when an SRL designation higher than L is required; consequently a different surface layer must be designed [2]. If the available aggregates cannot meet the required SRL without blending, another pavement design approach may be preferred over a standard wearing course [2].

Design inputs and requirements

The design of a Roller Compacted Concrete (RCC) wearing course must incorporate site‑specific traffic data such as an average daily traffic not to exceed 2 000 vehicles and a speed limit not to exceed 55 mph, must consider material characteristics including a water‑to‑cement ratio as low as 0.30 (typically 0.40), aggregate size less than 1 inch, and compressive strength ranging from 4 000 psi to 10 000 psi, must address environmental factors by requiring surface texturing when the exposed RCC surface will carry speeds of 35 mph or greater, and must identify geotechnical information on the existing subgrade and subbase—whether un‑improved, rehabilitated, or newly constructed—to verify that these layers can support RCC compaction [1]. The design of a cold‑mix FB‑1 wearing course may be used independently on roadways having an ADT of 1 500 or less, whereas for roadways having ADT greater than 1 500 a combination of binder and wearing courses must be used; the material specification calls for high‑void cold‑mix FB‑1 for low‑volume roads with highly flexible existing pavement structures, while hot‑mix FB‑2 must be produced at a stationary plant using either PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement, and the design assumes an existing pavement that is structurally sound and in satisfactory condition, allowing seal‑coat application to be postponed for up to three years when those conditions are met [1]. The Superpave wearing course requires site‑specific information that the existing pavement surface is asphalt, that its structural condition is sound with less than 2 % requiring patching, and that surface and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded to good; traffic data must verify that the average daily traffic falls within the limits shown in Tables 10.1 and 10.2 for the selected overlay thickness; material specifications call for a minimum mix depth of 1½ inches for thin overlays on non‑Interstates, with a PG 64E‑22 binder required for a 6.3 mm wearing course but not permitted for a 9.5 mm fine‑grade wearing course; environmental considerations such as freeze‑thaw cycles and moisture intrusion are evaluated to decide if additional thickness or an interlayer is needed; and geotechnical information on subgrade water conditions and drainage capacity must be confirmed before placement [1]. The AR‑GG (asphalt rubber gap‑graded) wearing course may be applied only on Interstate or Interstate look‑alike corridors that have average daily traffic greater than 25 000 vehicles, cumulative design ESALs greater than 10 million over a 20‑year design life, and a project area exceeding 50 000 square yards; material requirements specify the use of an asphalt rubber binder for gap‑graded wearing or binder courses that would normally employ a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, may include RAP or RAS as permitted by the SSP, must be produced using the wet process, and any warm‑mix additive used must be of chemical type (foaming is not allowed); existing‑condition information permits application to new construction or overlays on existing asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements, while projects requiring significant handwork or raking should be avoided [1]. The CRMAB wearing course also requires an Interstate or Interstate look‑alike site with average daily traffic greater than 25 000 vehicles, cumulative ESALs exceeding ten million over the 20‑year design life, and a project size of more than fifty thousand square yards; material details state that the project would otherwise employ a dense‑graded wearing or binder course with polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, may contain RAP or RAS as specified in the SSP, must be produced by the wet process, and layer thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches; existing‑condition data should identify whether the work is new construction or an overlay on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavement [1]. The Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) wearing course requires a comprehensive site assessment that documents the existing pavement surface condition—including rutting severity less than 1½ inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth less than 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding ≤ ½ inch, and raveling/weathering limited to a medium rating—as well as the condition of the base, subbase and subgrade; project selection also calls for sampling and laboratory testing of the existing pavement material to determine rejuvenator needs, and a review of construction and maintenance history together with traffic data to establish appropriate design inputs; additional site‑specific constraints such as ensuring any paving fabric is at least 1½ inches below the cut depth are considered during the wearing‑course design [1]. The Asphalt Wearing Course (SRL‑L) specified in Pennsylvania’s Department of Transportation specifications requires a Superpave asphalt mixture using PG 64S‑22 binder and is limited to traffic levels under 0.3 million ESALs; no additional site, environmental, geotechnical or existing‑condition requirements are provided in this excerpt [2].

The wearing course is defined as a plant‑mixed asphalt layer placed on a prepared surface and designed using the Superpave volumetric mixture system developed with the Superpave Gyratory Compactor and modified to be a fine‑graded (FG) mixture [2]. The appropriate performance‑grade binder shall be selected in accordance with the specifications for the particular course, such as PG 64S‑22 for low‑traffic applications or polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 when design ESALs exceed ten million to mitigate rutting [1]. For new construction and overlay projects the minimum thickness of a Superpave wearing course shall be one‑and‑a‑half inches, and any thinner placement on Interstate facilities requires prior PDAU approval; on non‑Interstate routes this minimum may be used only when the existing surface is asphalt, structurally sound with less than two percent patching, and drainage conditions are satisfactory [1]. The first asphalt overlay on a rigid pavement (excluding scratch or leveling courses) shall be four inches thick when a 9.5 mm mix is used or four‑and‑a‑half inches thick when a 12.5 mm mix is used, while resurfacing of flexible pavements shall be one‑and‑a‑half inches thick with a 9.5 mm mix or two inches thick with a 12.5 mm mix [1]. In‑place density for both standard and Restricted Performance Specification (RPS) wearing‑course construction must fall between ninety‑two percent and ninety‑eight point five percent of the target field density, and performance is measured by the Percent Within Limits (PWL) procedure of Publication 408 Section 413.3(j) [1]. Mixture acceptance is further controlled by laboratory thin‑film separator (LTS) testing of asphalt content and No. 200 sieve passing in accordance with PTM No. 757 or PTM No. 702, and density verification shall be performed using an approved nuclear gauge or electrical impedance gauge as specified in the specifications [2]. A lot is deemed defective if the payment factor for asphalt content, percent passing the No. 200 sieve, and percent passing the No. 8 sieve are all eighty‑five percent, or if core density yields a PWL less than fifty percent; when PWL is greater than or equal to ninety the pay factor shall be calculated as 100 + 0.4(PWL − 90), and when PWL is between fifty and ninety the pay factor shall be 70 + 0.75(PWL − 50) with the overall lot pay factor being a weighted sum of the individual factors [2]. The design must also satisfy skid‑resistance requirements; each approved coarse‑aggregate source receives a Skid Resistance Level (SRL) designation based on polishing resistance and friction testing, and the appropriate SRL for the anticipated average daily traffic shall be selected from Table 5.4, with SRL values higher than required prohibited on non‑wearing courses, leveling courses, and shoulders [1]. A safety edge shall be provided on all outside edges of wearing or binder courses with a depth of at least one‑and‑a‑half inches but not greater than five inches, adding roughly one percent to material quantities [1][2].

Roadways with an average daily traffic of fifteen hundred vehicles or less may receive an independent wearing course, whereas routes exceeding that volume require a combination of binder and wearing courses; when a deflection‑based design program is used a structural coefficient of zero point two shall be applied to FB (cold‑mix) courses [1]. Roller Compacted Concrete (RCC) may serve as the final wearing course adjacent to reinforced or plain cement concrete pavements, but only on roadways with an ADT of two thousand vehicles or less and a speed limit of fifty‑five miles per hour; if the RCC surface is exposed and the speed limit is thirty‑five miles per hour or greater, the surface must be textured by diamond grinding [1]. FB cold‑mix wearing courses are mixed at a stationary plant, may use PG 58S‑28 or PG 64S‑22 asphalt cement, must be placed hot, and require a seal coat after a minimum of three months of warm‑weather traffic densification (postponement up to three years is permitted when the underlying pavement is sound) [1].

The Asphalt Rubber Gap‑Graded (AR‑GG) wearing course shall use an asphalt rubber binder that meets the physical requirements of ASTM D6114 Table 1 Type II and contain at least fifteen percent crumb‑rubber modifier by weight; the mixture must be produced by the wet process in which the CRM is blended with the binder before aggregate addition, and its layer thickness shall not exceed two‑and‑a‑half inches. AR‑GG may be used for new construction or overlays on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements, but for new construction it is required to serve as the wearing course; selection is limited to projects that meet Interstate‑type criteria of ADT greater than twenty‑five thousand vehicles, cumulative ESALs greater than ten million over a twenty‑year design life, and an area greater than fifty thousand square yards [1]. Crumb Rubber Modified Asphalt Binder (CRMAB) mixtures may be considered for projects that would otherwise use dense‑graded wearing or binder courses with polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder; they are placed only in the upper layers of the pavement section, shall not exceed two‑and‑a‑half inches in thickness, must be produced using the wet process, and when a warm‑mix is used a chemical warm‑mix additive shall be employed while foaming is prohibited. CRMAB may contain RAP or RAS as specified in the SSP, and the same traffic and area thresholds that apply to AR‑GG (ADT > 25 000, ESALs > 10 million, area > 50 000 sq yd) govern its use [1]. Stone Matrix Asphalt (SMA) wearing courses are governed by Publication 408 Section 419 and Item Number 0419‑0000; SMA is considered only when the project involves at least fifty thousand square yards (with quantities nearer to one hundred thousand or more making it cost‑effective), is especially recommended for Interstates, Interstate look‑alike highways, high‑speed freeways, or existing roads carrying greater than thirty million ESALs, and the mix must satisfy SMA design steps including proper aggregate selection, gap‑graded gradation that yields stone‑on‑stone contact, minimum VMA compliance, appropriate asphalt content for target air voids, and evaluation of moisture susceptibility and binder draindown. SMA shall be avoided in areas requiring extensive handwork, frequent paver adjustments such as intersections, or where underlying pavement problems exist [1]. Hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) asphalt may be used as an interim wearing surface provided that rutting severity is less than one‑and‑a‑half inches, transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth is less than one inch with an average crack width not exceeding a medium rating of one‑half inch, and raveling/weathering severity does not exceed the medium rating; the HIR layer is typically one‑and‑a‑half inches thick (range one to two inches), and underlying pavement must retain at least one‑and‑a‑half inches of asphalt below the HIR depth for composite pavements or two to three inches for full‑depth pavements. Projects may not use rubber or fiber materials within the top two inches, must remove durable markings and any mastic patches larger than five square feet, and cannot have extensive crack sealant or overbanded rubberized joint seal; minor corrugation, shoving, slippage, bleeding, and low friction are acceptable provided their average depths stay below one‑and‑a‑half inches [1].

Placement of the wearing course shall be performed during daytime delivery at laying temperatures listed in Table A; material outside those temperature limits shall not be used. If the compacted depth exceeds six inches, construction shall occur in two or more layers each having a compacted thickness between three and six inches, and the mixture may not be added when it is segregated, below the minimum temperature, or exhibits an asphalt‑content deficiency [2]. A safety edge shall be provided on all outside edges of wearing (or binder) courses with a depth of at least one‑and‑a‑half inches but not greater than five inches and an angle between twenty‑six degrees and forty degrees [2]. At curbs, gutters, and other abutting structures the surface shall be sealed with hot asphalt over a minimum width of twelve inches from the curb [2]. Pattern segregation is deemed unacceptable when the macrotexture difference exceeds zero point zero two four inch, and flushing is considered defective when the measured texture depth is less than or equal to zero point zero zero six inch; notched wedge joints may be used for wearing and binder courses when the NMAS mixture size does not exceed nineteen millimeters [2]. Loose material shall be removed before traffic is opened, surface‑tolerance testing with a twelve‑foot straightedge shall deem any irregularity greater than three‑sixteenths inch defective, and depth control shall require that the finished course not be short by more than one‑quarter inch or by more than one‑eighth inch in three or more adjacent cores; newly placed wearing courses may not carry vehicular traffic or loads for twenty‑four hours or until the course uniformly cools to a temperature of one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit or less, with alternate routes provided as directed [2].

A paving‑operation quality‑control (QC) plan on Form CS‑413 shall be prepared and submitted before the pre‑construction conference, and a preplacement meeting shall be scheduled at least two weeks before placing asphalt mixtures to review the specification, the QC plan, the sequence of paving operations, mixture acceptance, density acceptance, and sample custody [2]. Temporary traffic control shall follow Publication 213 and FHWA Traffic Engineering Manual guidance, and project constraints such as drainage, base repair, and shoulder condition are evaluated under the general considerations of Chapter 2 Section 2.1 of the Pavement Policy Manual [1].

After final compaction the wearing‑course depth shall be verified by drilling a core for each two thousand linear feet of lane within twenty‑four hours; any single core showing a depth deficiency greater than one‑quarter inch that cannot be satisfactorily corrected renders the area defective, and a uniform deficiency greater than one‑eighth inch across three consecutive cores also constitutes defective work. The contractor shall immediately correct pavement that is deficient in depth until the entire repair zone is within one‑quarter inch of the required depth, with additional coring as needed to confirm compliance [2].

Preservation treatments may include ultra‑thin bonded concrete overlays placed to a depth of two inches as non‑structural wearing courses, and scratch overlays on existing concrete may not exceed four‑and‑a‑half inches when a Superpave 9.5 mm wearing‑course mix is used or five inches when a 12.5 mm mix is used; projects exceeding those limits must follow the 3R criteria. Micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses are permitted only to improve skid resistance, ride quality, or rut filling and must be applied early in the maintenance cycle before advanced distresses appear; all preservation treatments shall be shown cost‑effective according to PennDOT’s Pavement Management System [1].

Structural section and dimensions

The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10) states that the first asphalt overlay placed on a rigid pavement shall be four inches thick when a 9.5 mm Wearing Course mix is used and shall be four‑and‑a‑half inches thick when a 12.5 mm mix is used. For flexible pavements the resurfacing layer shall be one‑and‑a‑half inches thick with a 9.5 mm mix or two inches thick with a 12.5 mm mix. When the Wearing Course is provided as roller‑compacted concrete, the design thickness may range from four inches to eighteen inches; control joints for layers less than eight inches thick shall be spaced fifteen to twenty feet and for layers eight inches or greater shall be spaced at a distance equal to the layer thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet. The Manual further specifies that asphalt rubber gap‑graded Wearing Courses shall not exceed two‑and‑a‑half inches in thickness, and the same maximum thickness of two‑and‑a‑half inches applies to crumb‑rubber‑modified asphalt binder dense‑graded courses; these mixtures are advised not to be used in shoulders unless the lane and shoulder are paved in a single pass. Hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) applications typically use a wearing layer that is one‑and‑a‑half inches thick but may vary between one and two inches; when placed over a composite pavement the specification requires at least one to two inches of existing asphalt beneath the HIR depth, while full‑depth asphalt pavements must retain a minimum of two to three inches below the HIR layer, and the total asphalt thickness must be at least one‑and‑a‑half inches greater than any milling depth. For an asphalt overlay that first covers cement concrete or brick pavement, the design shall include at least two‑and‑a‑half inches of binder material and the wearing course shall meet the minimum thickness indicated for the selected material; in parking lanes the wearing course may be tapered to a one‑inch depth at the curb. An unbonded Portland cement concrete overlay normally uses a nominal one‑inch Superpave Wearing Course with a 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix, and if joint faulting exceeds one‑half inch a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) of the same mix is placed at the joint. An ultra‑thin bonded concrete overlay used as a non‑structural wearing course shall have an asphalt depth of two inches. The Manual notes that no other explicit dimensional requirements are given for overlays, bases, subbases, treated‑soil, shoulders, edge‑support, or widening. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Publication 408/2026 specifies a standard thin‑asphalt overlay wearing course thickness of 6.3 mm and indicates that this is the only dimension required for that course, with no separate dimensions prescribed for base, subbase, treated‑soil, shoulder, edge‑support or widening. For pervious asphalt pavement systems the required wearing‑course thickness is 9.5 mm using PG 64H‑22 or PG 64E‑22 binder; lifts of a pervious wearing course shall not exceed four inches in compacted thickness and each test section must cover at least 225 square feet. If any wearing or binder course is to be placed to a compacted depth greater than six inches, the specification requires it to be built in two or more lifts of approximately equal depth, with no individual lift thinner than three inches nor thicker than six inches. A safety edge shall be provided on the outside of the wearing (or binder) course with a minimum depth of 1.5 inches and a total edge depth not to exceed five inches. Where the wearing course abuts curbs or gutters it shall be laid uniformly higher so that after compaction the surface sits slightly above the structure, and a hot‑asphalt seal must extend at least twelve inches from the curb. High‑performance courses such as PG 64E‑22 mixes, those designed for more than ten million ESALs, and 4.75 mm wearing courses may be placed at compacted depths less than 1.5 inches; these may be paved between April 1 and October 15, whereas all other courses may be placed from April 1 to October 31. Quality verification for the wearing course requires that within twenty‑four hours after final compaction a core of four inches (or larger) diameter shall be taken for each 2,000 linear feet of lane; any single core showing a deficiency greater than one‑quarter inch or a uniform deficiency greater than one‑eighth inch across three consecutive cores that cannot be corrected renders the work defective and must be remedied until the depth is within one‑quarter inch of the required value. For slurry‑seal wearing courses the only dimensional requirement concerns dry‑aggregate application rates: Type I requires six to ten pounds per square yard, Type II ten to fifteen pounds per square yard, and Type III at least fifteen pounds per square yard; no other layer dimensions are stipulated. An asphalt‑rubber gap‑graded wearing course is proportioned by selecting a gap‑graded aggregate that omits many fine fractions and blending reclaimed ground tire rubber into the binder so that the crumb rubber modifier constitutes at least 15 % of the total blend weight, with the resulting asphalt‑rubber binder required to satisfy the physical requirements of ASTM D6114 Table 1 Type II; because the gap‑graded aggregate creates a high void content, a higher binder content and thicker film thickness can be used without compromising stability, helping meet structural strength, durability, constructability and performance goals comparable to polymer‑modified binders [1]. Selection of an AR‑GG wearing course should begin by identifying projects that would otherwise employ a gap‑graded wearing or binder layer using a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder and that meet the traffic and size thresholds of more than 25,000 ADT, more than ten million ESALs for a 20‑year design life, and an area greater than 50,000 square yards; the mix must be produced by the wet process with the CRM blended into the binder before aggregate addition, may incorporate RAP or RAS as specified in the SSP, shall not exceed a layer thickness of 2½ inches, and structural layer coefficients from Table 10.6 are applied for pavement design; constructability considerations dictate that AR‑GG should not be used where significant handwork or raking is needed because of its stiffness, should not be placed in shoulders unless lane and shoulder are paved in a single pass, and should be avoided on temporary lanes or crossovers, while warm‑mix asphalt may employ only a chemical additive and foaming is prohibited [1]. Selection and proportioning of the wearing‑course mixture for CRMAB begins with obtaining current ADT, projected growth and truck percentage so that the appropriate asphalt material type can be chosen from suitability tables that relate ADT to leveling, wearing or base courses; the chosen material must then be placed in a layer whose thickness falls within the minimum‑and‑maximum limits given for asphalt overlays, and the wearing course shall have at least the minimum thickness indicated for that material; when an asphalt overlay is applied for the first time over cement concrete or brick, the design shall include a minimum of 2½ inches of binder before the wearing course [1]. CRMAB binders should be considered for projects that would normally use dense‑graded wearing or binder courses with polymer modified PG 64E‑22 binder, and they are recommended only when the higher cost can be justified by performance benefits; they should be placed in the upper layers (wearing and binder courses) of new construction or overlays on asphalt, concrete, or composite pavements, avoiding applications that require significant handwork or raking because of mixture stiffness [1]. Use of CRMAB is especially appropriate for Interstates and Interstate look‑alikes with traffic exceeding 25 000 ADT, cumulative ESALs greater than 10 million over a 20‑year design life, and pavement areas larger than 50 000 square yards, and the layer thickness must not exceed 2½ inches while structural coefficients from Table 10.6 are applied in the structural design [1]. For an unbonded PCC overlay the specification calls for a Superpave Wearing Course made from a 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix placed to a nominal depth of 1 inch, which provides the required structural support and durability; if existing concrete joints show faulting greater than ½ inch, a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) with the same 9.5 mm gradation shall be placed at those locations or the joints shall be milled/grinded to reduce the faulting, and when joint faulting exceeds ¼ inch a geotextile interlayer may be used after milling to ensure proper separation and drainage [1]. For flexible pavements an Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course or micro surfacing is used early in the maintenance cycle to improve skid resistance, ride quality and rut filling, with a maximum asphalt depth of 2 inches when applied as a non‑structural wearing course [1][2].

Structural details and interfaces

The Wearing Course may incorporate control joints when the underlying pavement is roller‑compacted concrete; the Department requires that such joints be placed in shoulders so they align with the adjacent concrete mainline, while elsewhere their use is optional but must appear on the plans if provided. Joint spacing for RCC depends on slab thickness: where the RCC layer is less than 8 inches thick the control joints should be spaced between 15 feet and 20 feet, and where the RCC layer is 8 inches or greater the spacing equals the pavement thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet. No forms, steel reinforcing, or dowel bars are required for the RCC wearing course, and if the surface is exposed and the posted speed limit is 35 mph or higher the surface must be textured by diamond grinding. The Wearing Course shall also include a Safety Edge on the outside edge of the pavement and shoulders; the edge must be built from the same material as the course with a minimum depth of 1½ inches and a total depth not to exceed 5 inches, and the finished edge angle shall lie between 26° and 40° measured from the pavement cross‑slope. Where curbs or sidewalks are encountered the Safety Edge is omitted, but the outer edges must be worked immediately to remove raggedness and any breakdown repaired. Adjacent to flush curbs, gutters or other abutting structures the wearing course shall be placed uniformly higher so that after compaction the surface sits slightly above the edge; the exposed surface shall then be sealed with a hot‑asphalt material of the class listed in Table A (or an approved emulsified asphalt) over a minimum width of 12 inches from the curb to form a watertight gutter. When reflective cracking is a concern, an asphalt paving interlayer may be placed between the existing surface and the new Wearing Course; acceptable interlayers include needle‑punched geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grids, or composite reinforcing grids, provided the interlayer is compatible with the asphalt to provide a strong internal adhesive or mechanical bond. In unbonded concrete overlays the Wearing Course consists of a Superpave mix placed over a geotextile interlayer; concrete joints that exhibit faulting greater than ¼ inch shall be milled, and if faulting exceeds ½ inch a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix shall be placed at the joint or the joint shall be milled/grinded to reduce the faulting. The nominal thickness of the Wearing Course is one inch using a 9.5 mm fine‑graded Superpave mix, and drainage of any interlayer must be provided either by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain; the interlayer shall extend through the shoulder when shoulders are constructed or reconstructed and be tied to the mainline. The specification permits notched wedge joints in the Wearing Course when the nominal maximum aggregate size (NMAS) of the mixture is 19.0 mm or smaller, provided the joints are built according to the Standard Drawing, all loose material is removed before traffic opens, and the abutting asphalt is placed within one working day if the joint faces opposing traffic or within two working days for same‑direction traffic. Transverse joints may be sawed perpendicular to the centerline; they should include straight bulkheads and the joint face may be painted with a thin coating of the PG‑Binder used in the course or PG 64S‑22 before fresh mix is placed, although painting is not required for scratch courses. At locations where the Wearing Course meets existing pavement—such as paving notches, lane additions, or utility openings—the joint shall be sealed with hot asphalt material of the class and type designated for the Wearing Course, applying at least 6 inches of sealant on each side; alternatively an approved emulsified asphalt (PennDOT Material Class TACK or NTT/CNTT) may be used provided excess is removed and covered with a light layer of acceptable dry sand. For pervious‑asphalt systems the vertical surfaces of curbs, structures, gutters and adjoining pavements that will contact the asphalt shall be coated with a thin uniform layer of emulsified asphalt (AASHTO SS‑1, CSS‑1, SS‑1h, CSS‑1h, or TACK) applied in two or more applications; no tack coat is to be placed between lifts of the pervious asphalt and this section does not prescribe any specific longitudinal or transverse joints, edge widening, notches, transitions, connections, or other structural details for the Wearing Course. The temporary asphalt pavement wedge transition required for full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slabs shall be constructed with the Superpave Asphalt Mixture Design identified as Wearing Course (Scratch), PG 64S‑22, suitable for 0.3 to less than 3 Million ESALs, using a 9.5 mm mix and SRL‑L; no other longitudinal or transverse joints, interlayers, membranes, edge or widening details, notches, connections, or additional structural features are prescribed. For Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) wearing courses crack sealant may be placed on longitudinal and transverse joints, but extensive crack sealant and overbanded rubberized joint seals are not acceptable; if paving fabric is used it must be a minimum of 1½ inches below the specified HIR cut depth. When an asphalt overlay (including an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course) is placed over concrete pavement, transverse joints of the overlay should be sawed and sealed over the existing concrete joints, except when the treatment is micro‑surfacing. The subgrade and subbase should be separate construction items capable of supporting RCC compaction during construction. For a friction‑course (FB) wearing surface, a minimum of three months of warm‑weather traffic densification is recommended before applying either a seal coat or a surface treatment; the applications may be postponed for up to three years when the underlying pavement is structurally sound and the FB surface is satisfactory, and a seal coat or surface treatment may not be required on an FB Modified surface for at least four years. A structural coefficient of 0.20 must be used for FB courses in overlay design. Before placing a new wearing course over an existing pavement, a separate scratch or leveling course shall be placed to provide a uniform working platform; the leveling course shall be at least one inch thick and may be counted in structural calculations. Edge transitions are treated with a Safety Edge taper of about thirty degrees (tolerance twenty‑six to forty degrees) and a depth not less than one and a half inches and not more than five inches for both wearing and binder courses. When an interlayer is employed between asphalt layers, the material must be compatible with the asphalt, thermally stable, and provide a strong internal adhesive or mechanical bond within the asphalt matrix. If a cold‑recycled base is used beneath a wearing course, at least one week of curing under low humidity and high temperature is required before the wearing layer is placed; because the recycled mix retains higher voids, a wearing surface is imperative, and the compacted thickness of the wearing course must be no less than three inches and no more than five inches. The interface of a hot‑in‑place recycled (HIR) wearing course must avoid thin layers; when cutting less than one and a half inches, the HIR layer should not be placed over a thinner base, and for composite pavements a minimum of one to two inches of asphalt is required beneath the HIR depth, while for full‑depth asphalt pavements a minimum of two to three inches is required beneath the HIR depth; any paving fabric present must remain at least one and a half inches below the cut depth. If a geotextile interlayer is used over existing concrete, any concrete joint with faulting greater than one‑quarter inch must be milled; if faulting exceeds one‑half inch, a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix should be placed at the joint or the joints milled/grinded to reduce faulting, and the standard wearing‑course thickness in this situation is a nominal depth of one inch of a Superpave Wearing Course, 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix; proper drainage of the interlayer is achieved by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain, with the interlayer extending through any shoulder that is constructed or reconstructed and being tied to the mainline. Sawing and sealing of overlay transverse joints over existing concrete joints is recommended except when the overlay is micro‑surfacing. Overlay projects on existing flexible pavements whose thickness exceeds two inches will not be eligible for consideration as Pavement Preservation, and scratch overlays on existing concrete surfaces that exceed four and a half inches when a Superpave 9.5 mm Wearing Course mix is used or exceed five inches when a Superpave 12.5 mm Wearing Course mix is used are also not eligible for consideration as Pavement Preservation. At locations where the wearing‑course meets flush curbs, gutters or other abutting structures, the mixture is placed uniformly higher so that after compaction the finished surface sits slightly above the edge; harmful material is removed, the area cleaned and sealed with hot asphalt (or permitted emulsified asphalt) extending at least twelve inches from the curb to create a watertight joint. A safety‑edge of the same wearing‑course material is then formed on the outer edge with a depth of not less than one and a half inches and not greater than five inches, shaped as a wedge whose final angle ranges from twenty‑six degrees to forty degrees measured from the pavement cross‑slope. For connections between the wearing‑course and an adjacent lane or existing pavement where the nominal maximum aggregate size is 19.0 mm or smaller, the contractor may use notched wedge joints constructed as shown on the Standard Drawing; transverse joints are cut perpendicular to the centreline, the joint face is painted with a thin coating of binder (or PG‑64S‑22) before fresh mix is placed, except for scratch courses, and the joint shall be sealed with hot asphalt or emulsified asphalt applied at least six inches on each side after cleaning away loose material to achieve a watertight joint. Where traffic flows opposite the adjacent lane, the abutting asphalt mixture shall be placed within one working day; where traffic flows in the same direction, placement shall occur within two working days. Prior to placing a 6.3 mm wearing‑course, the existing pavement surface must be cleaned by removing loose material, foreign debris, excess joint sealer and crack filler, and, where practical, traffic is kept off the cleaned area; potholes and gouges greater than one inch in depth are repaired, and any cracks or joints exceeding one eighth inch in width are filled and sealed with PennDOT‑specified fillers. For pervious asphalt systems, irregularities in the binder course must be corrected and traffic avoided on the binder course when practical; contaminated binder that cannot be cleaned to the Representative’s satisfaction shall be removed or replaced, and all existing vertical surfaces that will contact the asphalt are coated with a thin, uniform layer of emulsified asphalt (AASHTO SS‑1, CSS‑1, SS‑1h, CSS‑1h, or TACK) applied in two or more applications; no tack coat is placed between lifts of any pervious asphalt, and the wearing course is then placed as soon as practical after the binder course has been installed. When an ultra‑thin bonded wearing course is specified, a polymer‑modified emulsified asphalt membrane is placed directly beneath the hot‑mix asphalt concrete and the two layers are laid together in a single operation with one paving machine; the 6.3 mm wearing‑course mixture must not be placed directly on top of that membrane, and its use with membranes shall follow the provisions in Section 467 or Section 680. The interface between a full‑depth precast or prestressed concrete slab and its supporting layers is achieved by a bedding and grout system in which fine‑grained granular bedding material shall not exceed a thickness of ½ inch, cement‑treated bedding material shall not exceed a thickness of 2 inches and must attain a minimum compressive strength of 500 psi at the time of opening to traffic; grout used for transverse dowels, longitudinal tie bar slots and lifting insert ports must be pumpable into the slots and achieve a minimum compressive strength of 2 500 psi before the slabs are opened to traffic, while cementitious bedding grout must reach at least 1 000 psi before opening and 2 500 psi within twelve hours. Drainage, environment, and accessibility

The wearing course is required to maintain adequate friction and surface texture throughout its service life so that water pressure does not build up at the tire‑pavement interface, a condition that must be retained under traffic and environmental conditions [1]. When a thin Superpave Wearing Course overlay less than 1 ½ inches is specified, the design must verify that both surface drainage and subsurface drainage are good or will be upgraded to good as part of the project; if this cannot be demonstrated the overlay shall not be placed [1]. To mitigate moisture‑related distress, an interlayer such as geotextile paving fabric or a composite reinforcing grid may be incorporated; these provide a moisture barrier to surface water and can help reduce damage from freeze‑thaw cycles, particularly in harsh climates where a thicker overlay with an interlayer may delay cracking [1]. The Stone Matrix Asphalt wearing‑course design includes a final evaluation of the mixture’s moisture susceptibility and asphalt cement draindown sensitivity, directly addressing potential water degradation of the surface layer [1]. Along curbs, gutters and other abutting structures the wearing course is placed uniformly higher so that after compaction the finished surface sits slightly above the edge; the adjoining surface is cleaned, harmful material removed and sealed with a hot‑asphalt binder of the class listed in Table A applied at a minimum width of 12 inches to fill all voids and create a watertight joint [2]. The apparent moisture content of the wearing‑course mixture shall not exceed 0.5%; if any coarse aggregate exhibits water absorption greater than 2.0% (AASHTO T 85), the mix must be sampled according to PTM No. 1 and a moisture‑susceptibility analysis performed, using a compatible heat‑stable amine‑based liquid anti‑strip additive or an approved alternate [2]. Paving of wearing courses is limited to the prescribed paving season; for PG 64E‑22 and other high‑traffic or thin wearing courses placement may occur from April 1 through October 15 (or to November 15 with an approved extension), while other courses may be placed from April 1 to October 31, and placement is prohibited when the surface is wet or when air or surface temperature is 40 °F or lower [2]. During colder periods trucks delivering the mix must be insulated on all sides, double‑walled, or heated whenever the air temperature falls below 50 °F from October 1 to April 30 [2]. For pervious asphalt pavement systems the wearing course shall not be placed when air or surface temperature is lower than 50 °F; the subgrade must be excavated to undisturbed soil without compaction so that permeability is retained, any irregularities in the binder course must be corrected before placement, no tack coat may be applied between lifts, and the wearing course should be placed as soon as practical after the binder course with compaction limited to not more than four passes to preserve surface porosity [2]. The pervious 9.5 mm wearing course shall use an asphalt cement of class PG 64H‑22 or PG 64E‑22; moisture susceptibility shall be evaluated by ASTM D3625 on the finished mix with a minimum of 90 % coating, and when required an anti‑strip additive not less than 0.25 % by weight of the asphalt cement shall be incorporated; if AASHTO T305 indicates additional stabilization is needed, cellulose fibers are added at a dosage rate between 0.2 % and 0.4 % of the total mix [2].

The wearing course must satisfy a range of accessibility, safety, geometric and user performance criteria that vary according to pavement type and design context. When roller‑compacted concrete is used as the final wearing course, the design shall limit traffic to an ADT of ≤ 2,000 and a speed limit of ≤ 55 mph; if the RCC pavement will have an exposed surface and a speed limit of 35 mph or greater, the pavement surface must be textured by diamond grinding. For RCC pavements less than 8 inches thick, control joints shall be spaced every 15 to 20 feet, whereas for RCC pavements 8 inches or greater the joint spacing shall equal the thickness multiplied by 2.5 feet. The first asphalt overlay on rigid pavements shall be 4 inches thick when a 9.5 mm Wearing Course mix is used or 4.5 inches thick when a 12.5 mm mix is used, and all resurfacing of flexible pavements shall be 1.5 inches thick with a 9.5 mm mix or 2 inches thick with a 12.5 mm mix. A Safety Edge shall be used as a standard pavement edge treatment on the outside edge of asphalt pavements and shoulders, with a taper depth of at least 1½ inches; it shall not exceed five inches total depth, shall be shaped to an angle of 26–40 degrees from the cross‑slope, and shall be inspected each day for compliance. Immediately after placement the outer edges shall be worked to eliminate sharp, ragged or open edges and any edge breakdowns shall be repaired; where the course meets curbs, gutters or other abutting structures the surface is set slightly above the edge, cleaned and sealed with hot (or permitted emulsified) asphalt over a minimum twelve‑inch width to form a watertight gutter. The wearing course must meet geometric tolerances that limit surface irregularities to no more than 3/16 inch when checked with a 12‑foot straightedge, and its thickness may not be short by more than 1/4 inch (or by more than 1/8 inch at three or more adjacent core locations) without being classified as defective. No vehicular traffic or loads shall be permitted on the freshly compacted wearing course for 24 hours or until its temperature falls to 140 °F or lower, and alternate routes must be provided during this period. The mixture shall be spread and struck off across the full lane width, or as much width as practical, with screed adjustments that achieve the required cross‑section depth; if its compacted thickness exceeds six inches it must be placed in two or more layers each between three and six inches. For pervious asphalt pavement systems the wearing course shall be placed on a properly conditioned binder surface and built so that its profile follows the design grade line, with transverse grade monitored by fully automatic sensors; compaction shall be performed with smooth steel‑wheeled rollers in static mode and limited to no more than four passes to preserve surface porosity and free drainage, and the mixture shall be allowed to cool to 100 °F before any subsequent layer is placed, while tack coat may be applied only to existing vertical surfaces. The wearing course shall develop an adequate amount of friction between tire and pavement and shall have sufficient surface texture (low‑speed gradient) to prevent water‑pressure buildup at the posted speed limit; these frictional properties shall be retained under traffic and environmental conditions throughout the life of the surface, and the appropriate Skid Resistance Level designation for the coarse aggregate shall be selected using Table 5.4 based on anticipated or current ADT as indicated on design approval forms. Friction‑based wearing courses may be used independently on roadways having an ADT of 1,500 or less; for roadways having ADT greater than 1,500 a combination of binder and wearing courses must be used. A minimum of one week of curing under favorable weather conditions is required before placing the wearing course, and its compacted depth shall fall between a minimum of 3 inches and a maximum of 5 inches; these mixtures are not suitable for high ADT roads unless detours can be established until adequate structural strength is obtained. The asphalt rubber gap‑graded (AR‑GG) wearing course is intended for Interstates or Interstate look‑alikes with an ADT greater than 25,000 vehicles, cumulative design ESALs exceeding 10 million over a 20‑year design life, and a pavement area larger than 50,000 square yards; under these conditions the layer thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches, pavement markings shall have the wet film thickness increased by 5 mils for gap‑graded surfaces, and it should not be used where significant handwork or raking is required, nor in shoulders unless lane and shoulder are paved in one pass, nor in temporary lanes or crossovers. The crumb rubber modified asphalt binder dense‑graded (CRMAB) wearing course shall be limited to Interstates and Interstate look‑alikes that carry more than 25,000 ADT, exceed ten million ESALs for a 20‑year design life, and occupy more than 50,000 square yards; the layer thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches, it should not be used in shoulders unless lane and shoulder are paved in one pass for efficiency, and it shall not be placed on temporary lanes or crossovers where a premium mix is not warranted. For a Hot‑In‑Place Recycled (HIR) wearing course the existing surface distress shall remain within defined limits: rutting severity shall be less than 1½ inches; transverse and miscellaneous cracking depth shall be less than 1.0 inch with an average crack width not exceeding ≤ ½ inch; minor corrugation, shoving or slippage shall have an average depth under 1½ inches. Additional safety‑related actions include removing raised pavement markers and durable markings, eliminating mastic patches larger than 5 sq ft, and ensuring only minor bleeding or low‑friction numbers are present. The asphalt layer must be at least 1½ inches thicker than any milling depth (and paving fabric, if present, must lie at least 1½ inches below the cut). When an asphalt overlay is placed over cement‑concrete or brick pavement for the first time a minimum of 2½ inches of binder material must be provided; where a parking lane exists and curb reveal is critical, the wearing course may be tapered to a 1‑inch depth at the curb—only within that parking lane—but the travel lanes must still retain the required minimum thickness, and careful attention must be given to compaction and to sealing the pavement/curb joint in those tapered sections. For an unbonded concrete overlay the Wearing Course shall be placed at a nominal depth of 1 inch using a Superpave Wearing Course, 9.5 mm Fine‑Graded mix as the standard thickness; if joint faulting exceeds ½ inch, a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch), 9.5 mm mix must be placed at the joint or the joints should be milled/grinded to reduce faulting. Proper drainage of the interlayer shall be ensured by daylighting the interlayer or installing a vertical subdrain, and the interlayer must extend through the shoulder when shoulders are constructed or reconstructed and tied to the mainline. An Ultra‑Thin Bonded Wearing Course may be applied in a manner that improves skid resistance, ride quality, and/or fills ruts, thereby addressing safety and user performance, while any leveling courses used with the wearing course must satisfy the minimum cross‑slope requirements. Verification and expected performance

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, May 2015 Edition, Change No. 10), the completed wearing course is verified through the Percent Within Limits (PWL) quality measure, which requires field density to fall within the target range of 92.0 % to 98.5 % for both Standard and Restricted Performance Specification (RPS) wearing courses; contractors must achieve consistent density results near the middle of that range to meet performance and payment criteria. Districts must determine the appropriate method for density acceptance by following Publication 408 Section 413.3(j), typically using pavement core testing, and this requirement is recorded as a Special Provision in the bid documents so that compliance with project specifications can be confirmed [1]. The Department of Transportation requires that the completed wearing course also be verified through the submission of a certified mix design on the appropriate form—TR‑448A for conventional asphalt concrete, the standard JMF for pervious asphalt, or Form TR‑483 for micro‑surfacing—signed by a Certified Asphalt Level 2 Plant Technician and delivered within the specified lead times (at least 7 calendar days before its use for Section 496, at least three weeks before mixture production for pervious asphalt under Section 420, and at least two weeks before placement for micro‑surfacing under Section 483) [2]. A quality‑control plan prepared in accordance with Section 106 must be submitted at the start of the project, and work may not commence until both the QC plan and the relevant mix design have been approved by the Representative [2]. During production, plant‑laboratory testing of materials, proportions, and the mixture is performed to verify conformance with the uniformity requirements of the applicable specification and to obtain material certifications for asphalt content, gradation, air voids, RAP limits or additive dosages where required; certified results of these QC tests are provided to the Representative within 48 hours of each material sample for conventional asphalt, and copies of the certifications are given to the Inspector‑in‑Charge within one working day after testing for pervious asphalt and micro‑surfacing [2]. For each batch, daily sampling is required to test asphalt content, gradation, theoretical maximum specific gravity, apparent moisture content, and volumetric properties against the tolerances in Tables A and B; asphalt content and gradation must satisfy the multiple‑sample limits, apparent moisture may not exceed 0.5 %, and any variation in theoretical maximum specific gravity of 0.030 or more triggers immediate notification to the DME/DMM [2]. After placement, mixture acceptance is confirmed by lot‑by‑lot evaluation and density acceptance is assessed from pavement cores; measured values are compared with the upper and lower specification limits in Table J and the percent within limits is calculated as described in Section 106.03(a)3 [2]. Payment factors are derived from the PWL results: when PWL ≥ 90, the pay factor for a specific parameter shall be PF = 100 + 0.4(PWL‑90); when 50 ≤ PWL < 90, the equation PF = 70 + 0.75(PWL‑50) shall be used, and the resulting Overall Lot Pay Factor is evaluated to confirm compliance with contract payment criteria [2]. Constructability requirements state that no compacted layer shall exceed six inches; if the total compacted depth exceeds six inches the work shall be executed in two or more layers of approximately equal depth, each layer being not less than three inches nor greater than six inches [2]. A safety edge must be constructed to a depth of at least 1.5 inches but not greater than five inches, and the finished edge angle measured from the pavement cross‑slope extended shall lie between 26° and 40° [2]. Notched wedge joints permitted for NMAS mixtures of 19.0 mm or smaller shall be placed in accordance with the standard drawing within the one‑day (opposing traffic) or two‑day (same direction) window [2]. Performance inspection includes immediate edge finishing and sealing a minimum twelve‑inch strip along curbs with hot or emulsified asphalt, measurement of pavement surface macrotexture where the difference between segregated and non‑segregated areas must not exceed 0.024 inch and texture depth must be greater than 0.006 inch, and examination of the finished surface with a twelve‑foot straightedge; any irregularity exceeding 3/16 inch is deemed defective work [2]. Depth is verified by measuring each sublot or by calculating mass per square metre, and pavement that is deficient in depth by more than 1/4 inch, or uniformly deficient by more than 1/8 inch at three consecutive core locations, shall be considered defective and corrected [2]. Within twenty‑four hours after final compaction, in the presence of the Inspector, a minimum‑size core shall be drilled for each two thousand linear feet of lane and its depth measured; any single core showing a depth deficiency greater than 1/4 inch that cannot be satisfactorily corrected designates the area as defective work, and immediate correction shall be made until the repaired zone is within 1/4 inch of the required depth [2]. The course shall not be opened to vehicular traffic for at least twenty‑four hours or until it uniformly cools to a temperature of 140 °F or less [2]. Within twenty‑four hours after paving completion, the contractor shall submit Form CS‑413EQC to the Representative together with all required documentation; for extended‑season work the Department conducts a spring manual performance survey no later than May 1, evaluating fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling/weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond/delamination, and will issue final acceptance only after any remedial repairs are completed and the Final Acceptance Certificate is granted [2].

The wearing course is expected to provide a design service life of 20 years for each of the specified pavement types[1]. For roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) wearing courses, durability is linked to a compressive strength that may range from 4,000 psi to 10,000 psi, which should resist traffic‑induced distress[1]; ride quality is to be evaluated by the International Roughness Index (IRI), although RCC shall not be selected where IRI measurement determines payment incentives because its surface tends to be rougher and varies with placement equipment[1]. When an exposed RCC surface serves as the final wearing course on roads with a speed limit of 35 mph or greater, diamond grinding must be performed to achieve an acceptable texture[1]. For asphalt wearing courses that provide friction, the pavement surface should have an adequate level of friction throughout its life and develop sufficient surface texture (low‑speed gradient) to prevent water pressure buildup at the posted speed limit; friction performance is measured with the AASHTO T 242 test method and the results are used to assign a Skid Resistance Level (SRL) designation for the coarse aggregate, which must be selected according to the anticipated or current average daily traffic as shown in Table 5.4 and documented on design‑approval forms[1]. For Superpave paving courses, the wearing course is expected to meet its performance over a 20‑year design life by resisting permanent deformation (rutting) and low‑temperature cracking; these distress‑resistance targets are evaluated through the Superpave volumetric mixture‑design procedure together with advanced performance testing and prediction models that incorporate the project’s calculated 20‑year equivalent single axle loads (ESALs), and the outcomes are measured against the specified volumetric criteria as well as the results of the performance tests and predictive models[1]. In SECTION 413—SUPERPAVE MIXTURE DESIGN, STANDARD AND RPS CONSTRUCTION OF PLANT‑MIXED ASPHALT COURSES WITH PERCENT WITHIN LIMITS AND LTS TESTING (PWL‑LTS), the performance of the wearing course is judged by meeting density acceptance requirements, which are verified through core density tests performed in accordance with PTM No. 715; the mixture must meet the asphalt‑content and 75 mm sieve limits, determined by laboratory analyses per PTM No. 757/702[2]; macrotexture depth measured with PTM No. 751 must not differ more than 0.024 inch between segregated and non‑segregated zones and must be greater than 0.006 inch to avoid flushing[2]. Surface smoothness is checked with a 12‑foot straightedge and any irregularities that are more than 3/16 inch are considered defective; thickness accuracy is verified by core drilling and density acceptance samples per PTM No. 737 (or mass calculations per PTM No. 1), and pavement deficient in depth by more than 1/4 inch or more than 1/8 inch in three or more adjacent cores is deemed defective work[2]; the course must remain free of traffic for at least 24 hours or until it uniformly cools to a temperature of 140°F or less before opening to vehicles[2]. A spring manual performance survey conducted in accordance with Publication 336 will be performed to assess fatigue cracking, transverse and miscellaneous cracking, raveling or weathering, rutting, flushing, potholing, joint and edge deterioration, and loss of bond or delamination; acceptance (or requirement for remedial action) will be based on that evaluation before a Final Acceptance Certificate is issued[2]. In SECTION 412—SUPERPAVE MIXTURE DESIGN, CONSTRUCTION OF PLANT‑MIXED 6.3 mm THIN ASPHALT OVERLAY COURSES each lot must satisfy the asphalt‑content and 75 µm sieve tolerances set in Table B as verified by LTS testing per PTM No. 757 or PTM No. 702; field density is accepted when the mixture does not move under the compaction equipment and meets the optimum rolling pattern established with a control strip using an approved nuclear gauge according to PTM 402, and the 6.33 mm paving mixture must be compacted to achieve the optimum rolling pattern requirements and to eliminate all roller marks, with any deviation requiring corrective action identified by monitoring for aggregate breakage or flushing[2]. In SECTION 420—PERVIOUS ASPHALT PAVEMENT SYSTEM the wearing course is expected to provide a high‑permeability surface that meets an infiltration rate greater than 200 inches per hour as measured by ASTM C1701 on two test sections, to retain the designed structural thickness within a tolerance of not more than 1/4‑inch less than the design thickness, and to preserve its porosity by limiting compaction to not more than four roller passes; compliance is verified by averaging the infiltration results, checking the compacted thickness in the field, counting roller passes during construction, and ensuring the surface cools to at least 100°F before placing subsequent lifts[2]. Under SECTION 496—ASPHALT CONCRETE PAVEMENT, 60‑MONTH WARRANTY the wearing course must retain its functional condition and resist typical distresses throughout the 60‑month period; compliance is demonstrated by a volumetric mix design that meets the specified JMF tolerances together with moisture‑sensitivity data such as tensile strength ratio or Hamburg Wheel Tracking results and other volumetric properties (VMA, VTM, VFA), and certified test results must be provided to the Representative within 48 hours of each material sample[2].
