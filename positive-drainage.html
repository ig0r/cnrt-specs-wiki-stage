Positive Drainage

Part of: Subsurface Drainage is-subtopic-of
Positive Drainage is a topic under Subsurface Drainage.
positive-drainage
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Positive Drainage is a design and construction requirement that removes water from pavement structures, preventing loss of support caused by moisture‑induced erosion or pumping of the subbase and thereby protecting structural integrity and extending service life[1][2]. It is achieved by providing a continuous positive slope—through shaping and fine‑grading compacted layers, sealing surfaces each workday with a smooth‑drum roller, and maintaining a positive cross‑slope on rigid pavements—so that surface runoff can flow freely to an appropriate outlet[1][2]. The system is typically realized with an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB/CTPB) over a suitable subbase where a positive outlet drainage can be supplied; when such an outlet cannot be provided, the design shifts to a dense base with longitudinal edge drains[1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Positive Drainage is required so that water can be removed from a pavement structure, thereby avoiding the loss of support that occurs when moisture causes erosion or pumping of the subbase material and protecting the pavement’s structural integrity. Positive Drainage is also employed to counter the engineering problem of inadequate water removal from rigid pavements, which can diminish pavement life, by supplying a slope or gradient that facilitates runoff and improves drainage quality, thus supporting longevity and performance. For pavements evaluated under section 8.6, designers should specify drainage layers only where an appropriate positive outlet drainage can be achieved, and when such conditions are not met they may consider a dense base with longitudinal edge drains; the loss of support factor is used to correct the effective k‑value for potential erosion or pumping of the subbase material. For new construction or reconstruction projects described in section 8.10 that use a subbase consisting of treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system and positive drainage, the overall drainage coefficient C_d is taken as 1.1, reflecting the intended improvement in drainage performance. Positive Drainage is used to prevent water accumulation on embankments or fills that could cause instability such as rutting, displacement, or shear waves. The specification requires shaping compacted layers so they drain during construction, fine‑grading the surface each day and rolling with a smooth drum roller to create continuous positive drainage and reduce infiltration; furrows that would block free surface‑water flow are prohibited. Positive Drainage is intended to address surface water infiltrating the subgrade, which can degrade pavement performance and durability, and the specification requires sealing all constructed or disturbed subgrade surfaces at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller in static mode so that continuous positive drainage is maintained and infiltration minimized. Construction specifications require maintaining a positive slope for drainage during placement and compaction of the subbase, ensuring that the subgrade or stabilization layer is built with a slope that directs water away. Positive Drainage is required to eliminate water pooling on concrete pavements caused by an insufficient cross‑slope; the specification calls for restoring the original pavement cross‑slope, correcting any area where deviation exceeds ¼ inch in 12 feet, and providing a positive cross‑slope so the pavement drains, thereby preventing slurry or runoff from crossing traffic lanes or entering gutters and protecting both ride quality and structural integrity. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–163); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 150–174), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 404–405)

Positive Drainage must achieve an appropriate positive outlet drainage so that water is removed from the pavement surface; consequently drainage layers are permitted only where such an outlet can be provided, otherwise a dense base with longitudinal edge drains should be used. The design shall employ either an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base as the drainage layer because Open Graded Subbase is prohibited for concrete pavements. The effective subbase to roadbed soil resilient‑modulus ratio shall not exceed 4, and loss‑of‑support factors must be applied to correct the k‑value for potential erosion or pumping, thereby protecting structural integrity and durability. In the pipe underdrain and pavement base drain system, Positive Drainage is required to provide a gravity‑flow system that attains positive drainage within 24 hours of trenching. Structurally, the system shall use fittings with deflection angles of 45 degrees or less, backfill with fine aggregate Type A placed in hydraulically compacted lifts not greater than 9 inches, apply water spraying at a rate of 1.0 gallon per linear foot, and provide a minimum of 6 inches of geotextile overlap to maintain pipe integrity. Durability is addressed by specifying Class 1 geotextile, sealing pipe ends to prevent foreign‑material entry, and installing fittings that preclude soil intrusion and protect joints from damage. Safety objectives include prohibiting open trenches during precipitation or overnight, limiting any unattended trench exposure to no more than 2 inches depth, and barricading all trenches in accordance with code requirements. Serviceability is ensured by furnishing smooth, non‑restricting transitions, supplying outlet pipes with galvanized wire‑mesh shields, and maintaining continuous drainage flow throughout the system. During construction of embankments or fills, Positive Drainage requires shaping layers so they drain, fine‑grading to eliminate low spots, and sealing the surface each workday by rolling with a smooth‑drum roller to promote continuous positive drainage and reduce water infiltration; edge furrows that would block free surface‑water runoff are not permitted. Compaction must achieve a stable condition without rutting, displacement or shear wave, thereby supporting structural integrity and durability of the drained pavement. For subsurface drain outlets, Positive Drainage must provide positive gravity flow from the outlet, with a uniformly firm and smooth trench bottom and pipe placed on 2 inches of satisfactorily tamped embankment material; connections shall be limited to a maximum 45‑degree bend. Durability is addressed by backfilling in 4‑inch layers of satisfactory material that are thoroughly tamped, and protecting plastic pipe ends from sunlight with concrete endwalls or suitable backfill. Safety and serviceability are satisfied by installing outlet opening protection and marking each subsurface drain outlet with a red marker embedded at least 18 inches for visibility to approaching traffic. Positive Drainage must maintain a positive slope for drainage as required in the Class 4 stabilization installation of geotextiles, fulfilling the functional objective of allowing water removal from the pavement surface. The subgrade shall be sealed at the end of each workday using a smooth‑drum steel‑wheel roller in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage and minimize infiltration, and the subgrade grade and slope shall be regularly checked to ensure the required cross‑slope is present so water can flow off the surface as intended. Diamond grinding of concrete pavement must provide a positive cross slope so the pavement drains, with uniformity verified at 100‑foot intervals longitudinally and any deviations greater than 1/4 inch in 12 feet corrected, thereby meeting serviceability and structural consistency requirements; each test location and maximum depth of depression or misalignment shall be documented. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

According to Section 8.10 of the Pavement Policy Manual, Positive Drainage may be applied to rigid (concrete) pavement projects—particularly Interstate and Expressway designs—when a permeable base such as an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base or Cement Treated Permeable Base is placed over a 2A subbase and the site can achieve appropriate positive outlet drainage; in those situations the drainage layer must be specified, and Open Graded Subbase is prohibited for concrete pavement projects. Section 8.6 of the same manual states that Positive Drainage is also appropriate for new‑construction or reconstruction rigid pavement projects when the design incorporates a subbase consisting of treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system and the positive drainage feature, in which case the overall drainage coefficient is taken as C_d = 1.1; this approach does not apply to rigid pavements built solely on unbound aggregate nor to rehabilitation projects lacking significant drainage improvements. Section 610 of the Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications indicates that Positive Drainage is appropriate for roadway pavements requiring a subsurface drainage system when the pipe underdrain or pavement base drain can be installed after fine grading and after the adjacent pavement has been constructed, except where placement would occur beneath curb sections or other special locations. The same section prescribes that such installations use Class 1 geotextile, perforated pipe with perforations down (or semicircular pipe with the smooth circular portion at the bottom), prefabricated pavement base drains set in a trench extending at least 2 inches into the subgrade without disturbing the subbase, backfilled with fine‑aggregate Type A in hydraulically compacted lifts not exceeding 9 inches thick, each lift sprayed with water at 1.0 gallon per linear foot, splice joints overlapped by a minimum of 6 inches of geotextile, and an outlet pipe and fittings installed to establish positive drainage within 24 hours of trenching. Section 206 specifies that Positive Drainage is applied when constructing embankments or fills composed of soil, Type 1 granular, Type 2 granular, rock, shale or other random material; the method requires shaping each compacted layer so it drains during construction, fine‑grading to eliminate low spots, and sealing the surface at the end of each workday with a smooth drum roller to maintain continuous positive drainage and limit infiltration, while edges must be free of furrows that would block surface runoff. Section 212 requires Positive Drainage for stabilization work that employs Class 4 Type C geotextile, mandating fine‑grading of the subgrade, placement of the geotextile without wrinkles with rolls overlapped at least two feet, and placement and compaction of the full depth of subbase while preserving a positive slope for drainage. Section 210 directs that Positive Drainage be applied to the subgrade surface of any pavement during construction or maintenance by sealing the constructed or disturbed subgrade at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode, thereby promoting continuous positive drainage and reducing water infiltration; when the completed subgrade has remained in place for more than 60 days or at least one winter season, the entire subgrade surface must be compacted and proof rolled again before installing a Class 4 Type A geotextile separator and subbase. Finally, Section 514 mandates that Positive Drainage, expressed as a positive cross‑slope, be provided when diamond grinding rigid concrete pavements; the transverse tolerance clause requires restoration of the original pavement cross‑slope, maintenance of a uniform cross‑slope, and verification of cross‑slope uniformity at 100‑foot intervals longitudinally. [1][2]

When a site cannot provide an appropriate positive outlet for water, drainage layers such as ATPB or CTPB should not be used and the design must shift to a dense base with longitudinal edge drains because drainage layers are only specified where positive outlet drainage can be achieved; additionally, open‑graded subbase (OGS) is prohibited for concrete pavements and only ATPB or CTPB may be employed when a drainage layer is called for. In cases where the pavement is built on unbound aggregate only, or where a rehabilitation project shows substandard existing drainage and significant drainage upgrades are not planned, the design should rely on an overall drainage coefficient of C_d = 1.0 rather than employing positive drainage, making an alternative approach preferable. [1]

Design inputs and requirements

According to the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), positive drainage in rigid pavement design is governed by material specifications that prohibit the use of Open Graded Subbase for any concrete pavement project and require that only Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base be used when a drainage layer is specified; all Interstate and Expressway designs must include an ATPB or CTPB layer on a 2A subbase, and drainage layers should be specified only where appropriate positive outlet drainage can be achieved, otherwise a dense base with longitudinal edge drains shall be considered [1]. The same manual sets the overall drainage coefficient requirement in Chapter 8 Subsection 8.10, stating that for new construction or reconstruction projects employing a subbase consisting of treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system and positive drainage, a drainage coefficient of C_d = 1.1 shall be used, while rigid pavements constructed on unbound aggregate only, as well as rehabilitation projects where no significant drainage improvements are proposed, must use a drainage coefficient of C_d = 1.0 [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification 408/2026 governs positive‑drainage systems through several sections; under Section 610—Pipe Underd rain and Pavement Base Drain the system for pavement underdrains and base drains must be installed in accordance with the requirements set out in Section 610.3, all drainage cores are required to use Class 1 geotextile as referenced in Section 212.3(b), the trench shall follow the Standard Drawings and extend at least 2 inches into the subgrade, backfill must be fine‑aggregate Type A placed in hydraulically compacted lifts not exceeding 9 inches with each lift sprayed with water at a rate of 1.0 gallon per linear foot, trenches may not remain open greater than 2 inches deep overnight and must be barricaded according to 67 PA Code Chapter 203, and connections and outlet piping must follow the manufacturer’s approved details as specified in Section 615.3 so that a functional positive‑drainage path is achieved within 24 hours of trenching [2]. Section 206—Placement and Compaction of Embankment and Fill requires that the design shape compacted layers to drain during construction, fine‑grade at the end of each workday to eliminate low spots, seal the surface with a smooth drum roller to maintain continuous positive drainage and limit water infiltration, and prohibit furrows at embankment or fill edges that would block free surface‑water flow [2]. Section 615.3 further mandates that the trench be excavated with a uniformly firm and smooth bottom to provide positive gravity flow, pipe be placed on 2 inches of satisfactorily tamped embankment material, backfill be placed in 4‑inch layers of satisfactory embankment material with thorough tamping, portions under pavement or shoulder be compacted as specified in Section 601.3(f), connections may not exceed a 45‑degree bend, plastic pipe ends require sunlight protection, and each outlet must be marked with a red marker embedded at least 18 inches from the surface [2]. Under Section 212—Geotextiles the specification for Class 4 Stabilization directs that a positive slope be maintained when the subbase is placed over the fabric to ensure water can drain away, and securing pins may be eliminated for slopes flatter than 6:1 provided alternative anchoring such as aggregate or rock is used [2]. Section 210—Subgrade specifies that all subgrade surfaces constructed or disturbed at the end of each workday must be sealed with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller in static mode and fill materials placed and compacted to the top‑of‑subgrade elevation as specified in Section 206.3(b); grade and slope are checked using a string line at longitudinal intervals not more than 25 feet (or up to 50 feet with an automatic grading machine and up to 500 feet with GPS or laser‑controlled equipment), any irregularity exceeding 1/2 inch must be corrected before further work, and if more than 10 percent of all acceptance tests exceed 1/2 inch in any phase the Representative may require stakes and stringline as specified in Section 210.3(c) [2]. Finally, Section 514—Diamond Grinding of Concrete Pavement requires that the design restore the original pavement cross‑slope and maintain a uniform cross‑slope verified at 100‑foot intervals along the length of the pavement; any area where the deviation exceeds greater than 1/4 inch in 12 feet must be corrected, and the specification explicitly requires providing a positive cross slope so the pavement drains [2].

Design methods and criteria

Positive Drainage employs a weighted loss‑of‑support calculation LS1·D1/(D1+D2)+LS2·D2/(D1+D2) to determine an effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k) for combined base and subbase layers. The design manual specifies that a drainage layer may be an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) only when appropriate positive outlet drainage can be achieved; where such outlet conditions cannot be met, a dense base with longitudinal edge drains shall be considered. Use of Open Graded Subbase (OGS) for any concrete pavement project is prohibited. In rigid‑pavement design the overall drainage coefficient C_d represents the effect of positive drainage on pavement life, and when a subbase of treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate is installed together with an underdrain system and positive drainage, C_d shall be assigned a value of 1.1; if the pavement consists only of unbound aggregate, or if rehabilitation does not include major drainage improvements, the model uses C_d equal to 1.0, which corresponds to the AASHO Road Test fair rating. Positive Drainage is achieved through a series of construction practices that differ by structural element. After fine grading, the trench for a pipe underdrain shall be excavated without disturbing the subbase beneath the adjacent roadway pavement, and a Class 1 geotextile is required for all base‑drain installation. The prefabricated drain shall be set vertically in the trench extending at least 2 inches into the subgrade, then backfilled with fine aggregate Type A using hydraulically compacted lifts not to exceed 9 inches, each lift being sprayed with water at a rate of 1.0 gallon per linear foot. Connections and fittings must be installed to prevent soil intrusion and provide smooth, non‑restricting flow, and a minimum of 6 inches of geotextile overlap is required between sections. For embankments or fills the compacted layers shall be shaped so that water can flow off the surface, then fine‑graded to eliminate low spots, and at the end of each workday the finished surface shall be rolled with a smooth‑drum roller to create a sealed, continuously draining face; edge furrows that would impede free surface drainage are prohibited. Positive Drainage for subsurface drain outlets requires excavating a trench with a uniformly firm and smooth bottom to provide positive gravity flow, placing the outlet pipe on a 2‑inch layer of satisfactorily tamped embankment material, joining sections with bands or split couplings, and after acceptance backfilling the trench in 4‑inch layers of satisfactory embankment material that are thoroughly tamped. Connections to underdrains or base drains must use a maximum 45‑degree bend (or a series of such bends), may include concrete endwalls or protective measures for plastic pipe, and the outlet opening shall be protected and marked with a red subsurface drain outlet marker embedded at least 18 inches from the surface. The constructed or disturbed subgrade surface shall be sealed at the end of each workday using a smooth‑drum steel‑wheel roller operated in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage and limit water infiltration. Grade and cross‑slope are verified with a taut string line stretched transversely across the subgrade, supplemented by template and 10‑foot straightedge checks, and any surface irregularities that exceed ½ inch shall be corrected by loosening, adding or removing material followed by compaction. When grading is performed with GPS or laser‑controlled equipment a machine‑control grading QC plan must be submitted at least one week before the preconstruction conference to confirm that the control system meets acceptable tolerances. During concrete pavement diamond grinding the original pavement cross‑slope shall be restored and a uniform cross‑slope maintained across the slab; the cross‑slope is inspected at 100‑foot longitudinal intervals, and any segment where the deviation exceeds ¼ inch over a 12‑ft length must be corrected before providing a positive cross‑slope to ensure water drains from the surface. [1][2]

Positive Drainage in rigid pavement design is governed by a set of material, geometric, and performance criteria that differ according to the structural layer configuration and construction practice. The drainage layer shall be an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB); Open Graded Subbase is prohibited for concrete pavements [1]. Such a drainage layer may be specified only when an appropriate positive outlet drainage can be achieved; if a positive outlet cannot be provided, the design should use a dense base with longitudinal edge drains instead [1]. When a rigid pavement incorporates an underdrain system together with positive drainage and employs a subbase of treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate, the overall drainage coefficient shall be assigned C_d = 1.1; pavements constructed only on unbound aggregate, as well as rehabilitation projects that do not incorporate significant drainage improvements, shall be assigned C_d = 1.0 [1]. In the pipe underdrain and pavement‑base drain system, positive drainage must be achieved by cutting a trench to the width shown on the Standard Drawings, extending it at least 2 inches into the subgrade, and backfilling with Type A fine aggregate placed in hydraulically compacted lifts not exceeding 9 inches; each lift shall be sprayed with water at a rate of 1.0 gallon per linear foot [2]. Trenches deeper than 2 inches must never be left open overnight, and all pipe connections shall use fittings whose deflection angles do not exceed 45 degrees, provide a minimum geotextile overlap of 6 inches, and have the outlet pipe installed within 24 hours of trenching to maintain continuous gravity flow and prevent soil intrusion [2]. For embankments and fills, positive drainage must be achieved by shaping each compacted layer so that it drains during construction, fine‑grading the surface at the end of every workday to eliminate low spots, sealing it with a smooth drum roller, and ensuring that no furrows are left at the edges that would block free surface‑water runoff [2]. In subsurface drain outlets, positive drainage must be achieved by excavating the trench so that its bottom is uniformly firm and smooth, laying pipe on at least 2 inches of satisfactorily tamped embankment material, backfilling in 4‑inch layers with thorough tamping, and compacting the portion under pavement or shoulder as required by Section 601.3(f); connections shall use a maximum 45‑degree bend (or series of such bends), be protected according to the standard drawings, include endwalls for plastic pipe against sunlight, and the outlet shall be identified with a red marker embedded a minimum of 18 inches from the surface [2]. The stabilization treatment described in Class 4 of the geotextile specifications requires that a positive slope be maintained to provide drainage; no additional numerical thresholds, safety factors, or reliability limits are stipulated for this condition [2]. Subgrade work must seal any constructed or disturbed surface each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode, and the finished subgrade grade and slope shall be maintained within tolerances that prevent surface irregularities from exceeding 1/2 inch; any area exceeding this limit must be loosened, corrected, and re‑compacted before further placement [2]. The subgrade grade and cross‑slope shall be inspected at transverse intervals of not more than 25 feet (or up to 50 feet when an automatic grading machine is used, or up to 500 feet with GPS/laser control), and acceptance testing shall include random cross‑section checks at least every 500 feet; no more than 10 percent of those tests may exceed the 1/2‑inch threshold, otherwise additional staking and stringline verification is required [2]. For diamond‑grinded concrete pavements, the design criteria for positive drainage require that the original pavement cross‑slope be restored and a uniform positive cross‑slope provided so water can drain; inspections shall occur at 100‑foot intervals along the length, and any segment where deviation exceeds ¼ inch over a 12‑ft run must be corrected [2].

Structural section and dimensions

According to Section 615‑3 of the Incidental Construction specifications, Positive Drainage calls for the drainage pipe to be laid on a minimum of 2 inches of satisfactorily tamped embankment material, with the trench then backfilled in 4‑inch layers that are thoroughly tamped; any portion of the outlet located beneath pavement or shoulder must be compacted as required elsewhere, and the subsurface drain outlet marker must be embedded at least 18 inches from the surface. No other specific dimensions for pavement, base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab, edge‑support, or reinforcement are prescribed in this section. [2]

Positive Drainage design for embankments and fills selects lift thicknesses based on material type—soil and Type 1 granular material are limited to compacted lifts of no more than 6 inches, Type 2 granular material to no more than 8 inches, confined areas to 4 inches, and rock layers not exceeding the average size of the larger rock and capped at 36 inches—while each layer is placed in uniform horizontal lifts across the full width, compacted to the required density, then shaped to drain during construction; fine grading removes low spots and a smooth‑drum roller seals the surface each workday to maintain continuous positive drainage and limit water infiltration, and edges must be free of furrows that would impede free drainage. Materials and mixture design

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, positive outlet drainage in rigid pavement designs requires that the drainage layer consist of an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) placed over a 2A subbase, and the use of Open Graded Subbase is prohibited. The manual further specifies base elastic moduli of 15,000 psi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30,000 psi for normal (summer/fall) conditions, and 50,000 psi for frozen conditions, with the stipulation that these values be adjusted if necessary so that the ratio of subbase to roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed four. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications mandate the use of Class 1 geotextile in all pavement‑base drain installations. Perforated pipe must be installed with its perforations facing downward, or a semicircular pipe may be used with the smooth circular portion at the bottom, and any fittings shall not exceed a deflection angle of 45 degrees. The backfill material for the drain trench shall be fine aggregate, Type A, placed in hydraulically compacted lifts no more than nine inches thick, each lift being sprayed with water at a rate of one gallon per linear foot. Outlet piping is required to incorporate galvanized wire‑mesh shields, and splice connectors must be sized so that they do not restrict flow while providing at least six inches of geotextile overlap. For subsurface drain outlets the trench bottom shall be uniformly firm and smooth, the pipe shall rest on two inches of satisfactorily tamped embankment material, and backfill shall be placed in four‑inch layers of satisfactory embankment material with thorough tamping; where plastic pipe is used its ends shall be protected from sunlight by satisfactory endwalls and backfill, and concrete endwalls may be constructed when outlets are not tied to existing drainage structures. The specifications also require that fill materials be placed and compacted to the top of subgrade elevation in accordance with Section 206.3(b) for the specific material type, that the finished subgrade surface be sealed each workday using a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode, and that a Class 4, Type A geotextile be installed continuously over the completed subgrade for lane widths of eight feet or greater before the subbase is placed. Positive Drainage designs require that the drainage layer be an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base and expressly prohibit the use of Open Graded Subbase; such a permeable base may be specified only when a suitable positive outlet drainage can be provided, otherwise a dense base with longitudinal edge drains must be considered. The thickness and type of sub‑base are selected by weighting loss‑of‑support factors for each layer according to their depths, and the combined effective k‑value is calculated using a base elastic modulus of 15,000 psi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30,000 psi for normal (summer/fall) conditions, or 50,000 psi for frozen conditions. In proportioning the subbase material, the ratio of the subbase resilient modulus to the roadbed soil resilient modulus must not exceed four, thereby avoiding an artificial design condition. Positive Drainage also mandates the use of Class 1 geotextile for all pavement‑base drain installations, with geotextile joints overlapping by at least six inches to ensure continuity. The drainage trench must be backfilled with fine aggregate Type A placed in hydraulically compacted lifts that do not exceed nine inches, each lift being sprayed with water at a rate of 1.0 gallon per linear foot and extending at least two inches into the subgrade; trench width follows the standard drawings and connections are protected from soil intrusion. Uniform horizontal layers are placed using material from the same borrow source: soils and Type 1 granular material are laid in compacted six‑inch lifts, Type 2 material in lifts up to eight inches, and rock layers are limited to an average stone size not exceeding thirty‑six inches. Except for the top three feet, each layer is compacted to at least ninety‑seven percent of its maximum dry density, while the upper three feet must reach one hundred percent of that density; moisture contents are kept within ±2 % of optimum for Type 1 granular material and between –3 % and optimum for soil. After each lift the surface is fine‑graded and rolled with a smooth drum roller to create a continuous positive drainage plane that reduces water infiltration and supports structural durability and constructability. Structural details and interfaces

In accordance with Section 610, all pipe‑underdrain and pavement‑base drain joints shall be installed following the manufacturer’s recommendations; fittings used at these joints must have deflection angles of 45 degrees or less, splice connectors are to be fitted so that they do not restrict flow, each geotextile joint must provide a minimum of 6 inches of overlap, and both the upgrade end of the pipe and all joints and fittings shall be sealed to prevent entry of foreign material while being protected from damage during construction. Section 615 requires that the subsurface drain outlet be assembled using pipe joints formed with connecting bands or split couplings, and it shall be linked to the underdrain or pavement‑base drain by suitable couplings, fittings or special connectors limited to a maximum 45‑degree bend or a series of such bends; where the outlet is not tied into existing drainage, concrete endwalls are provided to protect the opening, plastic pipe ends must be shielded from sunlight with satisfactory endwalls and backfill material, the completed outlet is marked with a red subsurface drain outlet marker placed on the traffic‑facing side of the opening and embedded at least 18 inches. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 521–526)

In designing the interfaces for a positively drained rigid pavement, the subbase is treated as both subbase and base course and its depth is added to the base depth so that a weighted‑average loss of support factor is calculated using LS1D1/(D1+D2)+LS2D2/(D1+D2) and the resulting effective k‑value should be adjusted if necessary so that the ratio of the subbase to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed 4 [1]. Drainage layers shall be limited to Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) and may be specified only where appropriate positive outlet drainage can be achieved; otherwise a dense base with longitudinal edge drains should be considered, while Open Graded Subbase is prohibited for any concrete pavement project and ATPB and CTPB must be bid as alternates on Interstate and Expressway designs that require a 2A subbase layer [1]. Positive‑drainage design requires that an underdrain or pavement base drain be joined to new or existing drainage structures using satisfactory connections, with fittings limited to deflection angles of 45 degrees or less and splice connectors placed so they do not restrict flow; each joint shall have at least 6 inches of geotextile overlap and transitions between the drainage core and outlet pipe shall be smooth and non‑restricting while all fittings shall preclude soil intrusion and backfill may not enter any connector [2]. The trench for a prefabricated pavement base drain shall extend at least 2 inches into the subgrade and be backfilled with fine aggregate Type A in hydraulically compacted lifts to maintain full contact with the geotextile [2]. When constructing interfaces between pavement layers, fill materials, structures or adjacent features, each compacted layer must be shaped to drain during construction, fine‑graded to eliminate low spots and then sealed at the end of every workday by rolling with a smooth drum roller so that continuous positive drainage is promoted and water infiltration is reduced; furrows at the edges of embankments or fills that would block free surface‑water drainage are not allowed [2]. The trench for a subsurface drain outlet shall be excavated so that its bottom is uniformly firm and smooth, providing positive gravity flow, the pipe placed on a 2‑inch layer of satisfactorily tamped embankment material and the trench backfilled in 4‑inch layers of satisfactory embankment material, each thoroughly tamped, with the portion beneath the pavement or shoulder compacted as required by Section 601.3(f); connections to an underdrain or pavement base drain shall use suitable couplings or fittings and be limited to a maximum 45‑degree bend (or series of such bends), with concrete endwalls or protective endwalls for plastic pipe ends, and the outlet is marked with a red marker embedded at least 18 inches from the surface [2]. At each interface where a geotextile separates pavement layers or supports structures, the fabric shall be laid flat and unstretched, overlapped according to class‑specific minimums—at least 6 inches on roll ends and edges for layer separation, at least 2 feet between adjacent rolls for stabilization—with upslope fabric placed overtop downslope fabric; installation shall preserve a positive slope across the interface to promote drainage, where slopes are flatter than 6:1 pins may be omitted provided aggregate or other acceptable means secure the fabric, otherwise steel pins spaced no more than 6 feet apart shall be used, and no equipment may travel directly on the geotextile until it is fully covered with the specified backfill or subbase, the cover material being placed promptly to prevent exposure [2]. To achieve positive drainage at each interface, the subgrade shall be compacted and then sealed at the end of every workday using a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller in static mode, creating a continuous draining surface and limiting water infiltration; before any subbase is placed, a Class 4 Type A geotextile shall be laid over the finished subgrade across the full lane width (8 feet or greater) and installed continuously in the longitudinal direction, with its transverse edges kept between a minimum of 6 inches and a maximum of 12 inches from the interior edge of the pavement base drain, and it must not be disturbed during base‑drain installation, while any surface irregularities that exceed 1/2 inch shall be corrected by adjusting material and re‑compacting [2]. Positive drainage is attained by restoring and maintaining a uniform positive cross‑slope on the pavement surface so water can run off; any cross‑slope deviation greater than 1/4 inch in 12 feet shall be corrected, grinding work shall stay at least 2 feet away from existing curbs, bridge barriers and roadway barriers to protect adjacent structures, and all slurry or residue must be removed without allowing it to flow into gutters or other drainage facilities [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Positive Drainage design requires that a drainage layer be limited to Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) and should be specified only where a positive outlet drainage path can be achieved; if such an outlet cannot be provided, the design should use a dense base with longitudinal edge drains. [1] The effective subgrade reaction is calculated using climate‑adjusted seasonal base elastic modulus values of 15,000 psi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30,000 psi for normal (summer and fall) conditions, and 50,000 psi for frozen winter conditions, these moduli being combined with subbase properties while ensuring that the ratio of subbase to roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed four. [1] For new or reconstructed rigid pavements that incorporate a subbase consisting of treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system and positive drainage, the overall drainage coefficient shall be taken as C_d = 1.1; for rigid pavements constructed on unbound aggregate only, or for rehabilitation projects where significant drainage improvements are not proposed, the coefficient remains at C_d = 1.0. [2] Drainage outlets are installed concurrently so that positive gravity flow is established within 24 hours of trenching, and all joints and fittings are protected from damage and soil intrusion. [2] Moisture conditions are controlled by keeping soil within –3 % of its optimum moisture content, maintaining Type 1 granular material within ±2 % of its optimum moisture content at the time of compaction, and keeping Type 2 granular material in a surface‑damp condition during compaction. [2] Where the foundation is saturated, swampy, or otherwise unstable, the specification requires constructing the embankment with rock to provide a stable base. In Section 610—Pipe Underdrain and Pavement Base Drain, Positive Drainage installation must follow safety measures that include barricading trenches at all times per 67 PA Code Chapter 203, must not leave any trench greater than 2 inches in depth open overnight, and must seal pipe ends to keep foreign material out; geometric requirements dictate the use of fittings whose deflection angles are 45 degrees or less, require a trench width as shown on the Standard Drawings with a depth extending at least 2 inches into the subgrade, call for backfilling in hydraulically compacted lifts no greater than 9 inches maximum while spraying each lift with water at 1.0 gallon per linear foot, and require overlapping Class 1 geotextile by at least 6 inches between sections. Under Section 206—Placement and Compaction of Embankment and Fill, Positive Drainage must be achieved by shaping the layers of compacted embankment or fill so they drain during construction, then fine‑grading to eliminate low spots and sealing the surface each workday with a smooth drum roller to promote continuous positive drainage and reduce water infiltration; additionally, any furrows at the edges of embankments or fills that would prohibit free surface‑water flow are not allowed. In Section 615—Subsurface Drain Outlets, Positive Drainage must be constructed so that the trench bottom is uniformly firm and smooth to ensure positive gravity flow in the outlet; the pipe shall rest on a minimum of 2 inches of satisfactorily tamped embankment material, be backfilled in 4‑inch layers with thorough tamping, and any connections to underdrains or base drains must not exceed a maximum 45‑degree bend (or series of such bends); for safety the outlet opening shall be protected and clearly marked by installing a red subsurface drain outlet marker on the side facing approaching traffic and embedding it at least 18 inches. Section 212—Geotextiles requires that Positive Drainage be achieved by maintaining a positive slope for drainage, ensuring water can flow off the surface. According to Section 210—Subgrade, Positive Drainage is achieved by sealing the constructed or disturbed subgrade at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller in static mode to promote continuous drainage and limit water infiltration; geometric control requires reference grade lines on both sides of the subgrade and regular checks of grade and cross‑slope using a taut string line or template, with longitudinal inspections not exceeding 25 feet (or up to 50 feet when an automatic grading machine is used and up to 500 feet with GPS/laser control), and any surface irregularities that exceed 1⁄2 inch must be corrected by adjusting material and re‑compacting, thereby ensuring the required slope for drainage while supporting safety and accessibility by preventing water pooling that could affect vehicle traction. In Section 514—Diamond Grinding of Concrete Pavement, Positive Drainage must be achieved by providing a positive cross‑slope that allows water to run off the pavement and by maintaining a uniform cross‑slope checked at 100‑foot intervals, correcting any deviation greater than 1/4 inch in 12 feet; the work shall not permit grinding slurry or residue to flow across traffic lanes or into gutters or other drainage facilities, protecting user safety, and equipment may not be operated within 2 feet of existing curbs, bridge barriers, or roadway barriers, preserving required clearances. Verification and expected performance

The completed positive drainage is checked through a series of inspections and measurements that address the pipe underdrain and base drain, the embankment or fill, the subgrade, and any concrete pavement that has been diamond‑grinded. For the pipe system, the Inspector‑in‑Charge must inspect the pipe before backfill is placed; all joints are required to follow the manufacturer’s recommendations, connections must be satisfactory with fittings limited to deflection angles of 45 degrees or less, geotextile seams must have at least 6 inches of overlap, and all fittings and outlet pipes must be sealed so soil cannot enter the core, thereby ensuring continuity of flow. Verification also includes confirming that trench depths, backfill lifts, water‑spray rates and protection measures such as not leaving trenches greater than 2 inches deep open overnight were observed, which together demonstrate compliance with constructability, performance and project requirements. For embankments or fills, the positive drainage is verified by confirming that the layers have been shaped to drain during construction, that any low areas have been fine‑graded out, and that the surface has been sealed at the end of each workday with a smooth drum roller to promote continuous drainage and reduce water infiltration; no furrows at the edges of embankments or fills that prohibit free drainage of surface water are allowed. For subgrade work, the surface is sealed each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode to maintain continuous drainage, after which the finished grade and cross‑slope are checked using a taut string line across the reference grade lines at longitudinal intervals of not more than 25 feet (or not more than 50 feet when an automatic grading machine is used and not more than 500 feet when GPS or laser control is employed). Any surface irregularities that exceed 1⁄2 inch are corrected by loosening, adding or removing material, compacting the area and re‑checking for grade and slope; when GPS or laser equipment is used a grading QC plan must be submitted, the machine control system calibrated each workday, and critical hinge points, centerline, lane edges and shoulders tested as required, with acceptance granted only after all measured deviations are within the 1⁄2 inch tolerance. For concrete pavements that have undergone diamond grinding, the original cross‑slope is restored and a uniform positive cross‑slope is provided; its uniformity is checked at 100‑foot longitudinal intervals and any segment where the deviation exceeds 1/4 inch over a 12‑foot length must be corrected. Documentation of each test location with the maximum depression or misalignment is then submitted to the Representative. [2]
