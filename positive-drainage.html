Positive Drainage

Part of: Subsurface Drainage is-subtopic-of
Positive Drainage is a topic under Subsurface Drainage.
positive-drainage
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Positive Drainage is a pavement‑design and construction approach that obliges water to be removed from the pavement structure so that moisture‑induced erosion or pumping of the subbase does not diminish the effective modulus of subgrade reaction, thereby preserving structural support and long‑term performance[1][2]. It achieves this by providing a positive gravity outlet—through an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB/CTPB) together with appropriately sloped pavement cross‑slopes and underdrain systems installed so that drainage is established within 24 hours of trenching—and by daily sealing of all constructed or disturbed subgrade, embankment and fill surfaces with a static‑mode smooth‑drum steel wheel roller to maintain continuous runoff[1][2]. The method is characterized by the requirement for a verifiable positive outlet (otherwise a dense base with edge drains is used), the prohibition of Open‑Graded Subbase in concrete pavements, a design drainage coefficient of C_d = 1.1 when such a system is employed, and strict cross‑slope tolerances (no more than ¼ inch deviation over 12 ft) to ensure water can drain efficiently[1][2]

Purpose and design objectives

Positive Drainage is required so that water can be removed from a pavement structure, thereby avoiding the loss of support that occurs when moisture causes erosion or pumping of the subbase material and preserving the effective modulus of subgrade reaction. For pavements evaluated under section 8.6, drainage layers shall be specified only where an appropriate positive outlet drainage can be achieved; otherwise a dense base with longitudinal edge drains may be considered, and the loss‑of‑support factor is used to correct the effective k‑value for potential erosion or pumping of the subbase material. Positive Drainage is also employed to counter the engineering problem of inadequate water removal from rigid pavements by supplying a slope or gradient that facilitates runoff, improves drainage quality, and supports longevity and performance. For new construction or reconstruction projects that use a treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system and positive drainage, the overall drainage coefficient C_d is taken as 1.1 to reflect the intended improvement in drainage performance. Throughout the pavement system Positive Drainage is employed to prevent water accumulation and infiltration that would otherwise degrade the performance of subgrade, embankments, fills, and surface layers. In the pipe underdrain and pavement base drain (Section 610) subsurface outlet piping shall be installed concurrently with the base drain so that positive drainage is established within 24 hours of trenching, and all fittings and connections must preclude soil intrusion into the drainage core to maintain uninterrupted gravity flow. In the subgrade (Section 210) sealing each constructed or disturbed surface daily with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller in static mode promotes continuous positive drainage and minimizes infiltration of surface water, thereby protecting the pavement structure. For embankments and fills (Section 206) compacted layers shall be shaped to drain during construction, fine‑graded to eliminate low spots, sealed each workday with a smooth drum roller, and furrows at edges that would block free surface‑water runoff are prohibited, all to prevent water accumulation that could undermine stability, cause rutting, or lead to displacement. In concrete pavement (Section 514) a uniform cross‑slope shall be provided; any area with deviations greater than 1/4 inch in 12 ft must be corrected and a positive cross‑slope established so that slurry or runoff does not cross traffic lanes or enter gutters. [1][2]

Positive Drainage design in rigid‑pavement context must achieve appropriate positive outlet drainage so that water is removed from the pavement surface; therefore drainage layers are permitted only when such outlet can be provided and otherwise a dense base with longitudinal edge drains should be considered [1]. It is required to produce positive gravity flow from subsurface drain outlets by excavating trenches with a uniformly firm and smooth bottom, and to provide a positive cross‑slope on the pavement surface that enables water to run off; the original cross‑slope shall be restored and kept uniform, with checks at 100‑foot intervals, and any deviation greater than ¼ inch over a 12‑ft length must be corrected [2]. Each compacted layer of embankment or fill must be shaped so that it drains during construction, fine‑graded to eliminate low spots, and edge conditions must not include furrows that would prohibit free drainage of surface water [2]. The pipe for subsurface outlets shall rest on at least 2 inches of satisfactorily tamped embankment material, and the trench shall be backfilled in 4‑inch layers of satisfactory material with thorough tamping; the portion under pavement or shoulder is to be compacted as specified in Section 601.3(f) while providing outlet opening protection [2]. The ends of plastic pipe must be protected from sunlight deterioration by using suitable endwalls and backfill, and all constructed or disturbed subgrade surfaces shall be sealed at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode to limit water infiltration; similarly the embankment or fill surface is sealed each day with a smooth drum roller [2]. Continuous positive drainage must prevent standing‑water hazards, furrows that could trap water are not allowed, and each subsurface drain outlet shall be marked on the traffic‑facing side with a red marker embedded at a minimum of 18 inches (or as recommended by the manufacturer) [2]. These construction practices ensure long‑term drainability, limit water ingress that could degrade the pavement structure, and preserve the functional performance of the drainage system over the service life [2]. The effective subbase to roadbed soil resilient‑modulus ratio shall not exceed 4, and loss‑of‑support factors must be applied to correct the k‑value for potential erosion or pumping, thereby protecting structural integrity and durability [1]. Only Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) may be used as the drainage layer because Open Graded Subbase is prohibited for concrete pavements [1].

Scope and applicability

Positive Drainage may be applied to rigid concrete pavement projects, particularly on Interstate and Expressway alignments, when a permeable base such as an Asphalt Treated Permeable Base or Cement Treated Permeable Base is placed over a 2A subbase and the site can achieve appropriate positive outlet drainage; in those cases the drainage layer should be specified and Open Graded Subbase is prohibited. For new construction or reconstruction of rigid pavements, Positive Drainage is appropriate when the design incorporates a subbase consisting of treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system and the positive drainage feature, and the overall drainage coefficient is taken as C_d = 1.1; this approach is not applied to rigid pavements built solely on unbound aggregate nor to rehabilitation projects lacking significant drainage improvements. Positive Drainage is appropriate wherever a pipe underdrain or pavement base drain is installed for roadway pavements, provided the pipe is placed after fine grading and the base drain is placed after the adjacent roadway pavement is constructed, except in curb sections or other special locations, with trenches excavated without disturbing the subbase. The design for such underdrain or base‑drain installations requires fittings whose deflection angles shall not exceed 45 degrees, the use of Class 1 geotextile for all base‑drain construction (and when indicated for pipe underdrain), and prefabricated pavement base drains set in a trench at least 2 inches into the subgrade, backfilled with fine aggregate Type A using lifts no greater than 9‑inch hydraulically compacted and sprayed with water; outlet piping must be installed concurrently to establish positive drainage within 24 hours of trenching. Positive Drainage is also applied to any pavement project in which the subgrade has been constructed or disturbed, regardless of the specific pavement type, because the specification requires sealing those surfaces each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operating in static mode; this requirement applies to all fill materials placed to top of subgrade elevation and to any completed subgrade that must be maintained and protected. Positive Drainage is required whenever an embankment or fill is constructed, regardless of whether the material is soil, Type 1 granular, Type 2 granular, rock, shale or random material; each horizontal lift shall be shaped to drain during construction, fine‑graded to eliminate low spots and rolled with a smooth drum roller at the end of each workday, and edges may not contain furrows that would block free surface‑water runoff. For diamond grinding of rigid concrete pavements, Positive Drainage expressed as a positive cross‑slope is required; the original pavement cross slope shall be restored and a uniform cross slope maintained, any area where deviation exceeds 1/4 inch in 12 feet shall be corrected, and a positive cross slope provided so the pavement drains. [1][2]

When a site cannot provide an appropriate positive outlet for water, drainage layers such as ATPB or CTPB should not be used and the design must shift to a dense base with longitudinal edge drains because drainage layers are only specified where positive outlet drainage can be achieved; additionally, open‑graded subbase (OGS) is prohibited for concrete pavements and only ATPB or CTPB may be employed when a drainage layer is called for. In cases where the pavement is built on unbound aggregate only, or where a rehabilitation project shows substandard existing drainage and significant drainage upgrades are not planned, the design should rely on an overall drainage coefficient of C_d = 1.0 rather than employing positive drainage, making an alternative approach preferable. [1]

Design inputs and requirements

In the rigid pavement design guidance, Open Graded Subbase (OGS) is prohibited for any concrete pavement project and only Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) may be used when a drainage layer is specified [1]. All Interstate and Expressway designs must include an ATPB or CTPB layer on a 2A subbase [1]. Drainage layers should only be specified where appropriate positive outlet drainage can be achieved, and if that condition cannot be met a dense base with longitudinal edge drains should be considered [1]. For new construction or reconstruction projects that employ a subbase consisting of treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system and positive drainage, the specification calls for using a drainage coefficient of C_d = 1.1 [1]. Rigid pavements constructed on unbound aggregate only, as well as rehabilitation projects where no significant drainage improvements are proposed, must use a drainage coefficient of C_d = 1.0 [1]. Pipe underdrain and pavement base drain shall be placed after fine grading, with trench widths shown on the drawings and a depth extending at least 2 inches into the subgrade; all pavement‑base drains must use Class 1 geotextile per Section 212.3(b) and splice connections shall not restrict flow, while fittings are limited to 45° deflection angles [2]. Backfill shall be fine aggregate Type A placed in hydraulically compacted lifts no greater than 9 inches, sprayed with 1.0 gallon of water per linear foot, and trenches may not remain open longer than 2 inches deep overnight or during precipitation; they must be barricaded according to 67 PA Code Chapter 203 [2]. Subsurface drain outlet construction mandates that the outlet trench be excavated with a uniformly firm, smooth bottom to ensure positive gravity flow, that pipe be laid on 2 inches of satisfactorily tamped embankment material, and that backfill be placed in 4‑inch layers of satisfactory material with thorough tamping [2]. Connections shall use a maximum 45‑degree bend or series of such bends and be compacted as specified in Section 601.3(f); the outlet opening shall be protected according to the Standard Drawings, including endwalls for concrete or plastic pipe and red markers embedded at least 18 inches from the surface [2]. Outlet pipe and fittings shall follow Section 615.3 and be installed concurrently so that positive drainage is established within 24 hours of the beginning of trenching [2]. Subgrade construction requires sealing all subgrade surfaces each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operating in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage and limit water infiltration; after the completed subgrade has been in place for more than 60 days or at least one winter season it shall be compacted and proof rolled again before installing the Class 4, Type A geotextile separator [2]. Any grade or slope irregularities that exceed 1/2 inch must be corrected by loosening, adding or removing material and then re‑compacted, with verification using string lines, templates or straightedges [2]. Placement and compaction of embankment or fill shall shape layers to drain during construction; low areas shall be removed by fine grading and the surface sealed at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum roller, and furrows at the edges of embankments or fills that would prohibit free drainage are not allowed [2]. Diamond grinding of concrete pavement shall restore the original pavement cross‑slope, maintain a uniform cross‑slope checked at 100‑foot intervals, correct any deviation greater than 1/4 inch over a 12‑ft length, and provide a positive cross‑slope so the pavement drains [2].

Design methods and criteria

Positive Drainage employs a weighted loss‑of‑support calculation LS1·D1/(D1+D2)+LS2·D2/(D1+D2) to compute an effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k) for combined base and subbase layers. The design manual requires that a drainage layer be an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) only when appropriate positive outlet drainage can be achieved; otherwise a dense base with longitudinal edge drains should be considered, and the use of Open Graded Subbase (OGS) for any concrete pavement project is prohibited. In rigid‑pavement design the overall drainage coefficient C_d represents the effect of positive drainage on pavement life, and when a subbase of treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate is installed together with an underdrain system and positive drainage, C_d is assigned a value of 1.1; if the pavement consists only of unbound aggregate, or if rehabilitation does not include major drainage improvements, the model uses C_d equal to 1.0, the same value that was observed for the AASHO Road Test condition which corresponds to a fair rating for drainage quality. Section 210 of the Pennsylvania specifications achieves positive drainage on subgrade by sealing all constructed or disturbed subgrade surfaces at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode, creating a continuous draining surface; grade and slope are then verified using a string line stretched transversely across the subgrade and measuring vertical distances at longitudinal intervals of not more than 25 feet (or up to 50 feet when an automatic grading machine is used), and by employing templates with leveling devices or a 10‑foot straightedge for cross‑slope checks, while any surface irregularities exceeding 1/2 inch must be corrected before proceeding. Section 206 requires that each compacted lift of embankment or fill be shaped so that water can flow off the surface, fine‑graded to eliminate low areas, and sealed at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum roller; edge furrows that would block free surface runoff are not allowed. Section 610 specifies that after fine grading, pipe underdrain is placed and pavement base drain is installed only after adjacent roadway pavement is constructed; trenches are excavated without disturbing the subbase, with trench depth extending at least 2 inches into the subgrade and width as shown on the Standard Drawings; backfill is placed in hydraulically compacted lifts not exceeding 9 inches, each lift being sprayed with water at a rate of 1.0 gallon per linear foot; fittings are limited to deflection angles of 45 degrees or less, Class 1 geotextile is used with a minimum overlap of 6 inches between sections, and all connections must be sealed to prevent soil intrusion, ensuring continuous gravity flow through the drainage core. Section 615 requires that the outlet trench bottom be uniformly firm and smooth to ensure positive gravity flow, that the outlet pipe be laid on a two‑inch layer of satisfactorily tamped embankment material, that joints be made with bands or split couplings, and that backfilling be performed in four‑inch layers of satisfactory embankment material with thorough tamping; connections to underdrains or base drains must use a maximum 45‑degree bend or a series of such bends, concrete endwalls may be installed where needed, plastic pipe ends are protected from sunlight, and each outlet is marked with a red subsurface drain outlet marker embedded at least eighteen inches on the side facing approaching traffic. Section 514 mandates that diamond grinding of concrete pavement restore the original pavement cross slope and maintain a uniform cross slope across the work area; cross‑slope measurements are taken at 100‑foot intervals longitudinally, and any segment showing a deviation greater than 1/4 inch over a 12‑foot length must be corrected to reestablish a positive cross slope that allows water to drain, with each test location and the maximum depth of depression or misalignment documented as part of the drainage verification. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–163); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 149–167), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 395–396), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 509–514)

In rigid pavement design, the drainage layer shall be an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) and Open Graded Subbase is prohibited for concrete pavements [1]. Such a drainage layer may be specified only when an appropriate positive outlet drainage can be achieved; if a positive outlet cannot be provided, the design shall use a dense base with longitudinal edge drains instead [1]. When a rigid pavement incorporates an underdrain system together with positive drainage and employs a subbase of treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate, the overall drainage coefficient shall be assigned C_d = 1 . 1 [1]. Rigid pavements constructed only on unbound aggregate, as well as rehabilitation projects without significant drainage improvements, shall be assigned an overall drainage coefficient of C_d = 1 . 0 [1]. Positive drainage in the base‑drain trench required by Section 610 shall be achieved by extending the trench depth at least 2 inches into the subgrade and sizing the trench according to the Standard Drawings [2]. Backfill for these trenches shall be placed with fine aggregate Type A in lifts not exceeding 9‑inch (maximum) hydraulically compacted lifts, each lift being sprayed with water at 1.0 gallon per linear foot, and a Class 1 geotextile shall provide a minimum of 6 inches of overlap between sections [2]. The pipe or prefabricated drain is installed after fine grading; perforations on perforated pipe are placed down, all joints and fittings must be sealed to preclude soil intrusion and must not restrict flow, and connections and outlet piping shall be installed so that positive drainage is established within 24 hours of the beginning of trenching for a given section, with trenches deeper than 2 inches not left open overnight [2]. In Section 615, positive drainage at subsurface drain outlets is achieved by excavating the outlet trench so that the bottom is uniformly firm and smooth to ensure positive gravity flow; the pipe shall be laid on top of 2 inches of satisfactorily tamped embankment material, and any bends are limited to a maximum 45‑degree bend or series of 45‑degree bends [2]. After placement, backfill shall be placed in 4‑inch layers using satisfactory embankment material that is thoroughly tamped, and the portion of the outlet under pavement or shoulder shall be compacted as specified in Section 601.3(f); a red subsurface drain outlet marker must be embedded at a minimum of 18 inches on the side of the opening facing approaching traffic [2]. In Subgrade construction (Section 210), the subgrade must be compacted as specified in Section 206.3 and each day’s disturbed surface shall be sealed with a static‑mode smooth‑drum steel wheel roller to maintain continuous positive drainage; any surface irregularities that exceed 1/2 inch shall be corrected by loosening, adding or removing material and re‑compacting, and acceptance testing requires that no more than 10 % of the checks exceed this 1/2‑inch limit, otherwise the Representative may require stakes and stringline to be established [2]. Positive drainage during embankment or fill placement (Section 206) shall be achieved by shaping each compacted layer so it drains during construction, fine‑grading to eliminate low spots, sealing the surface at the end of every workday with a smooth drum roller, and ensuring that no furrows exist at the edges of the embankment or fill that would block free surface‑water runoff [2]. Diamond grinding of concrete pavement (Section 514) shall provide a positive cross slope so the pavement drains; cross‑slope uniformity shall be checked at 100‑foot intervals longitudinally, and any area where the deviation exceeds ¼ inch in a 12‑foot length must be corrected to restore the original cross slope [2].

Materials and mixture design

Positive drainage specifications for rigid pavement designs require that the drainage layer consist of an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) placed over a 2A subbase, while the use of Open Graded Subbase is prohibited. The subbase type, its thickness, and the associated loss‑of‑support factor must be provided to compute the effective modulus of subgrade reaction, with base elastic moduli prescribed as 15,000 psi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30,000 psi for normal (summer/fall) conditions, and 50,000 psi for frozen conditions; these values should be adjusted if necessary so that the ratio of subbase to roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed four. For pipe underdrain installations, perforated pipe is placed after fine grading with the perforations facing downward and a semicircular pipe oriented so that its smooth circular portion rests at the bottom, and joints shall follow the manufacturer’s recommendations; fittings may have deflection angles of 45 degrees or less. Class 1 geotextile, as defined in Section 212.3(b), is used for all pavement‑base drain construction and when indicated for pipe underdrain construction. The trench behind the pipe is backfilled with fine aggregate Type A using hydraulically compacted lifts not exceeding nine inches, each lift being sprayed with water at a rate of one gallon per linear foot. Outlet pipes are furnished with galvanized wire‑mesh shields and splice connections must provide a minimum six‑inch overlap of geotextile to preclude soil intrusion. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 8 (pp. 161–162); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 509–510)

In rigid pavement designs that require positive drainage, the drainage layer may be limited to an Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) and the use of Open Graded Subbase is prohibited; such a permeable base is specified only when a suitable positive outlet can be provided, otherwise a dense base with longitudinal edge drains must be used [1]. The thickness and type of the sub‑base are determined by weighting loss‑of‑support factors for each layer according to their depths, and the combined effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k) is calculated using a base elastic modulus of 15 000 psi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30 000 psi for normal summer/fall conditions, or 50 000 psi for frozen conditions [1]. In proportioning the subbase material the ratio of the subbase resilient modulus to the roadbed soil resilient modulus shall not exceed four, thereby avoiding an artificial design condition [1]. For all base‑drain installations a Class 1 geotextile is selected, and fine‑aggregate Type A is required as the backfill material; the backfill is placed in hydraulically compacted lifts that do not exceed nine inches in thickness and each lift is sprayed with one gallon of water per linear foot to achieve the required density [2]. Pipe underdrains are installed after fine grading, with perforations facing downward and a semicircular pipe oriented so that the smooth circular portion lies at the bottom; all fittings must have deflection angles of forty‑five degrees or less, trench depth must extend at least two inches into the subgrade, and splice connectors are required to be installed so they do not restrict flow or permit soil intrusion [2]. The construction sequence limits open‑trench time and all joints and fittings are sealed in accordance with manufacturer recommendations, which supports structural integrity, durability, constructability, and performance [2]. When constructing embankments or fills, the appropriate earth‑work material—soil, Type 1 or Type 2 granular, rock, shale, or random fill—is selected and placed in uniform horizontal lifts whose compacted thickness is limited to six inches for soil and Type 1, eight inches for Type 2, and up to thirty‑six inches for rock [2]. Each lift is placed within its optimum moisture range—soil within –3 % of optimum, Type 1 ±2 %, and Type 2 surface damp—and compacted with the specified equipment to at least ninety‑seven percent of the maximum dry density, increasing to one hundred percent in the top three feet; compaction speed does not exceed 1.5 ft/s and rollers must operate a minimum of six seconds over the surface [2]. After each lift the surface is fine‑graded to eliminate depressions and rolled with a smooth drum roller to seal it, shaping the layer to drain continuously and thereby protecting structural integrity, durability, constructability, and overall performance [2].

Structural details and interfaces

In SECTION 610—PIPE UNDERDRAIN AND PAVEMENT BASE DRAIN, 610.3 CONSTRUCTION, Positive Drainage requires that pipe joints be installed according to the manufacturer’s recommendations and that all fittings have deflection angles of 45 degrees or less; splice connectors must be placed so they do not restrict flow. Each joint should be sealed at the upgrade end to keep foreign material out and protected from damage during construction, and a minimum of 6 inches of Class 1 geotextile overlap must be provided between sections to prevent soil intrusion. Transitions between pipe sections, fittings, and outlet piping must be smooth and nonrestricting to flow, and any termination without an outlet must include a fitting that blocks soil entry. The installed outlet pipe and its fittings are to be provided with galvanized wire‑mesh shields as shown on the Standard Drawings. In SECTION 615—SUBSURFACE DRAIN OUTLETS, 615.3 CONSTRUCTION, Positive Drainage requires that the outlet pipe be joined by connecting bands or split couplings, and where it meets an underdrain or pavement base drain it must be linked with suitable couplings, fittings or special connectors limited to a maximum 45‑degree bend (or series of such bends). Where outlets are not tied into new or existing drainage structures, concrete endwalls should be provided for protection, and if plastic pipe is used the ends must be shielded from sunlight using satisfactory endwalls and backfill. The completed outlet is then marked with a red subsurface drain outlet marker embedded at least 18 inches deep on the side of the opening facing approaching traffic. [2]

In a positively drained rigid pavement the subbase is treated as both subbase and base course, and its depth is added to the base depth so that a weighted‑average loss of support factor is calculated as LS1·D1/(D1+D2)+LS2·D2/(D1+D2) [1]. The resulting effective modulus of subgrade reaction (k) shall be adjusted, if necessary, so that the ratio of the subbase resilient modulus to the roadbed soil resilient modulus does not exceed 4 [1]. Drainage layers at this interface may be limited to Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB), and they may be specified only where appropriate positive outlet drainage can be achieved; otherwise a dense base with longitudinal edge drains should be considered [1]. Open‑graded subbase is prohibited for any concrete pavement project, and ATPB and CTPB must be bid as alternates on Interstate and Expressway designs that require a 2A subbase layer [1]. Positive Drainage design requires each interface to allow water to move freely from one pavement component to the next. When an under‑drain or pavement‑base drain is joined to a new or existing drainage structure, the connection shall use satisfactory fittings limited to deflection angles of 45 degrees or less, and all fittings and connections shall be installed to preclude soil intrusion into the drainage core or outlet piping while providing continuity of drainage flow [2]. At this interface a geotextile shall provide a minimum of 6 inches of overlap between sections [2]. At subsurface drain outlets the trench bottom shall be uniformly firm and smooth to permit gravity flow, the pipe shall rest on two inches of satisfactorily tamped embankment material, and it shall be joined to the under‑drain or pavement‑base drain with couplings that do not exceed a 45‑degree bend; backfill shall be placed in four‑inch layers of satisfactory embankment material that are thoroughly tamped [2]. The interface between the compacted subgrade and the overlying subbase shall be treated by sealing all subgrade surfaces at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode to promote continuous positive drainage and to minimize infiltration, followed by placement of a Class 4 Type A geotextile across the full lane width of eight feet or greater; the geotextile shall be installed continuously in the longitudinal direction with its transverse edges positioned between six inches and twelve inches from the interior edge of the pavement‑base drain, and any surface irregularities exceeding one‑half inch shall be corrected by loosening and adding or removing material before re‑compaction [2]. Each layer of compacted embankment or fill shall be shaped to drain during construction, fine‑graded to eliminate low spots, and sealed at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum roller while reducing water infiltration; edges must be formed without furrows that would block free surface‑water flow, and uniform material from the same borrow source shall be placed in consistent lifts before compaction [2].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Positive Drainage design specifies that a drainage layer may be limited to Asphalt or Cement Treated Permeable Base (ATPB or CTPB) and should be used only where a positive outlet drainage path can be achieved; when such an outlet cannot be provided, the design should employ a dense base with longitudinal edge drains. [1] The effective subgrade reaction is calculated using climate‑adjusted seasonal base elastic modulus values of 15,000 psi for spring‑thaw conditions, 30,000 psi for normal (summer and fall) conditions, and 50,000 psi for frozen winter conditions, and the ratio of subbase to roadbed soil resilient modulus shall not exceed four. [1] For new or reconstructed rigid pavements that incorporate a subbase consisting of treated permeable base or dense asphalt base over unbound aggregate together with an underdrain system and positive drainage, the overall drainage coefficient shall be taken as C_d = 1.1; for rigid pavements constructed on unbound aggregate only, or for rehabilitation projects where significant drainage improvements are not proposed, the coefficient remains at C_d = 1.0. [1] In pipe underdrain and pavement base drain installations, positive drainage is achieved by placing the pipe after fine grading and installing the base drain only after the adjacent roadway pavement has been constructed; trenches are excavated so that the subbase is not disturbed, all trench openings are kept closed during precipitation or overnight, and the upstream end of each pipe or drain shall be sealed to prevent entry of foreign material while Class 1 geotextile is required for the entire base‑drain system. [2] Backfill shall consist of fine aggregate Type A placed in hydraulically compacted lifts not exceeding 9 inches, each lift sprayed with water at 1.0 gallon per linear foot; trenches deeper than 2 inches must not be left open overnight, and connections and outlet fittings are to be installed so that flow is smooth, non‑restricting, and protected from soil intrusion, establishing positive gravity flow within 24 hours of the beginning of trenching. [2] For subgrade work, positive drainage is maintained by sealing every constructed or disturbed subgrade surface at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller in static mode, which promotes continuous positive drainage and minimizes infiltration of surface water; the subgrade grade and cross‑slope are regularly checked with string lines, templates and straightedges, and any surface irregularities that exceed 1/2 inch must be corrected by loosening, adding or removing material and then re‑compacting. [2] After the completed subgrade has been in place for more than 60 days or at least one winter season, it must be compacted and proof rolled again before the Class 4, Type A geotextile separator is placed, ensuring that prolonged moisture exposure does not degrade drainage performance. [2] In embankment and fill placement, positive drainage is achieved by shaping each compacted lift so that it drains during construction, then fine‑grading to eliminate depressions and sealing the surface each day with a smooth drum roller; furrows that would block free surface runoff are not permitted. [2] Moisture conditions are controlled by keeping soil within –3 % of its optimum moisture content, Type 1 granular material within ±2 % of optimum, and Type 2 granular material in a surface‑damp condition at the time of compaction; where the foundation is saturated, swampy, or frozen, rock fill must be used or frozen material must be removed before placement to prevent instability. Positive Drainage installations must follow the geometric constraints specified for pipe and trench work, using fittings with deflection angles of 45 degrees or less, providing a trench having a width as shown on the Standard Drawings and a depth extending at least 2 inches into the subgrade, barricading trenches at all times according to the requirements of 67 PA Code Chapter 203, and not leaving trenches greater than 2 inches in depth open overnight. Verification and expected performance

For subgrade (Section 210—Subgrade > 210.3 Construction) the completed positive drainage is verified by sealing the subgrade at the end of each workday with a smooth‑drum steel wheel roller operated in static mode to maintain the intended slope and limit water infiltration. The finished surface is then inspected for grade and cross‑slope using a taut transverse string line, measuring the vertical distance to the surface at longitudinal intervals not more than 25 feet; where an automatic grading machine is used the interval may be increased to not more than 50 feet, and when GPS or laser control is permitted the interval may be up to 500 feet provided a machine‑control QC plan is submitted, the equipment calibrated each day, and representative testing performed at hinge points and random locations. Any measured irregularities that exceed 1/2 inch are corrected by loosening the surface and adding or removing material, followed by compaction and a re‑check of grade and slope.

For embankment and fill (Section 206—Placement and Compaction of Embankment and Fill > 206.3 Construction) verification requires confirming that each compacted layer has been shaped to drain during construction, that the surface has been fine‑graded to eliminate low areas, and that it is sealed at the end of every workday with a smooth drum roller to maintain continuous positive drainage and reduce water infiltration. Edge furrows that would impede free surface‑water runoff must be absent; their absence demonstrates compliance, constructability, performance, and consistency with project requirements.

For concrete pavement diamond grinding (Section 514—Diamond Grinding of Concrete Pavement > 514.3 Construction) verification consists of restoring the original pavement cross‑slope and confirming a uniform cross‑slope throughout the grind area. Uniformity is checked at longitudinal intervals of 100 feet, and any segment showing deviations greater than 1/4 inch in 12 feet must be corrected to reestablish a positive cross‑slope that allows drainage. The contractor must also provide documentation for each test location noting the maximum depth of depression or slope misalignment, thereby demonstrating compliance with the project’s tolerance and performance requirements.
