Crack and Seat

Part of: Rehabilitation is-subtopic-of
Crack and Seat is a topic under Rehabilitation.
crack-and-seatcrack-and-seatcracking-and-seating
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-29

Crack‑and‑Seat is a concrete‑pavement rehabilitation technique applied when jointed slabs have become faulted, pumping or rocking to the extent that conventional patching is no longer economical, with the purpose of restoring functional performance and delivering a structural enhancement that extends service life [1]. The method intentionally fractures the existing slab into fragments roughly 12–24 in (no piece exceeding about 30 in) and seats those pieces in the underlying base or subbase using a minimum‑50‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller, thereby creating a uniform, stable support layer that limits differential movement at joints, cracks and punchouts and reduces reflective cracking of any subsequent overlay [2]. Successful implementation requires a sound subgrade, adequate drainage correction, dry pavement conditions and ambient temperatures between 45 °F and 90 °F before the overlay is placed [1][2].

Purpose and treatment objectives

Crack‑and‑seat is applied to concrete pavements that are so severely cracked, faulted or have free‑moving slabs that patching is no longer economical. By fracturing the slab into smaller pieces and seating them in the base or subbase, the method creates a uniform, stable support and limits differential movement at joints, cracks and punchouts. This stabilization reduces the likelihood of reflective cracking developing through any subsequent overlay. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

The Crack‑and‑Seat technique is employed primarily to restore the functional performance of a distressed concrete pavement while delivering a structural enhancement that extends its service life. By intentionally cracking and seating the existing slab, large free‑moving sections are broken into smaller units, which reduces differential movement at joints, cracks and punchouts and consequently limits reflective cracking in any overlay applied thereafter. This creates a more uniform, stable support layer that improves ride quality and load distribution, effectively repairing the distressed condition and prolonging the pavement’s useful period. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139)

Distress characterization and causes

The crack‑and‑seat technique is used on jointed concrete pavements where slabs are rocking, pumping or faulted—i.e., at transverse joints and cracks that have become free‑moving. The distress is extensive enough that slab movement or faulting is present across the pavement surface, making conventional patching uneconomical and indicating a severe condition of differential movement. Because such movement can lead to reflective cracking in any overlay and degrade rideability, the method breaks the slabs into smaller pieces and seats them to provide a uniform support layer, thereby reducing safety and operational impacts. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Crack‑and‑seat is used when a concrete pavement has become unstable because slabs are moving, faulting or pumping—symptoms that arise from inadequate subgrade support and drainage problems that allow water infiltration to undermine the base. By intentionally fracturing the slab into smaller pieces and seating them in the underlying base, the method reduces the size of free‑moving slabs, limits differential movement at joints and cracks, and therefore lessens reflective cracking in the overlay. The process provides a more uniform, stable support layer that is superior to simply placing an unbonded overlay on a poorly supported pavement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Applicability and treatment selection

Crack‑and‑Seat is appropriate for existing jointed plain concrete pavements (JPCP) or jointed reinforced concrete pavements (JRCP) that have become faulted, pumped, rocking, or otherwise so distressed that patching is no longer economical and the slabs are moving freely. The method works by breaking the pavement into pieces roughly 12 to 24 inches in size and seating those fragments in the underlying base or subbase to create a more uniform support layer for an overlay. For the treatment to be viable, the existing subgrade must be stable enough to receive the seated pieces, and any drainage deficiencies should be corrected—often by installing additional subgrade drains—before or during the operation. Site conditions must exclude nearby utilities and structures; specifically, no culverts, cross‑pipes, or utility sleeves may be within ten feet horizontally or vertically of the slab, and the work area should be at least forty feet away from bridge approaches, decks, or tunnel portals. The subgrade must also be such that the use of sonic vibratory equipment will not cause liquefaction during the seating process. Environmental requirements include dry pavement conditions for the pneumatic‑tire roller operation and air temperatures between 45 °F and 90 °F with a non‑frozen surface at the time of post‑fracture testing. Traffic must be closed to vehicles after the fracturing step and remain closed until the overlay is placed, ensuring that no loads are applied while the pieces are being seated. The equipment specifications call for a minimum 50‑ton roller equipped with a variable‑force impact device, which must be available to meet the procedural requirements of the method. When these pavement, site, environmental, and traffic conditions are satisfied, the guides consider Crack‑and‑Seat an appropriate rehabilitation technique. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Crack‑and‑Seat should not be selected when the rehabilitation plan calls for an unbonded concrete overlay, because the cracking and seating operation adds cost without improving performance and is unnecessary for such overlays. It is also inappropriate when a flexible asphalt overlay will be placed over an existing jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) that is to remain unbonded, since the same support can be achieved with the unbonded overlay or by rubblizing, making crack‑and‑seat redundant. Where the subgrade exhibits poor conditions or high moisture content, methods such as rubblizing provide better structural support than crack‑and‑seat and therefore should be preferred. The technique must be avoided whenever the pavement is located close to existing utilities or fixed objects: within ten feet horizontally of culverts, cross‑pipes, or utility sleeves; within three feet of manholes, inlets, or similar appurtenances; and within forty feet of bridge approaches, decks, or tunnel portals. Additionally, if the work requires sonic vibratory equipment, crack‑and‑Seat cannot be used when such vibration could liquefy a susceptible subgrade, necessitating an alternative rehabilitation method. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Condition assessment and timing

To decide if crack‑and‑seat is required, a visual inspection for slab movement such as rocking, faulting or pumping is first performed; the presence of movement triggers slab stabilization or jacking and indicates that cracking and seating may be needed. A Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) test must also be conducted on the existing concrete pavement before designing any overlay to quantify structural deficiency. When the pavement is so distressed that patching is uneconomical, these inspections and the FWD results justify applying crack‑and‑seat. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Crack‑and‑seat is used when a concrete pavement has deteriorated so far that patching is no longer economical—typically when slabs are rocking, pumping, faulted or otherwise free‑moving, causing extensive differential movement at joints and cracks. In this condition the pavement is fractured into roughly 12‑ to 24‑inch pieces and rolled into the base or subbase to create a stable, uniform support before applying an overlay. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98)

Materials and repair details

The Crack‑and‑Seat rehabilitation begins by fracturing the existing concrete slab into fragments roughly twelve to twenty‑four inches long, with no single piece exceeding thirty inches, and when a slab longer than twenty feet is encountered it is saw‑cut across the lane width to a depth of about plus or minus one inch—approximately two‑thirds of the slab thickness—to define the fracture zone[1][2]. Impact equipment capable of delivering a controlled variable force is employed to initiate full‑depth cracking while preserving aggregate interlock[2]. After fracturing, a pneumatic tire roller weighing at least fifty tons and equipped with four to seven rubber‑tired wheels inflated to a minimum of one hundred pounds per square inch is driven over the area for three passes to firmly seat the fragments in place[2]. Any voids that develop are backfilled with Subbase No. 2A and then covered with a Flexible Base Replacement layer before a tack coat is applied and the final asphalt overlay is laid, while patching prior to overlay also utilizes flexible base‑course replacement rather than new concrete patches[1][2]. A continuous pavement base drain is installed and any deficient areas of the base are repaired with a Base Course or Superpave Base Course as required[1]. A leveling course at least one inch thick—often placed over a bond‑breaker when needed—is then applied to correct cross‑slope before the overlay[1]. The overlay may be an unbonded concrete layer designed according to the appropriate design procedures, or an asphalt layer whose thickness is dictated by design; asphalt material is permissible for Crack‑and‑Seat projects[1][2]. Full‑depth asphalt shoulders are provided where specified, while concrete shoulders are used when the original pavement had concrete shoulders and otherwise the shoulder type matches the existing configuration[1]. Exposed reinforcing steel or joint plates must be removed, and all necessary repairs to utilities, drainage systems, bridge approaches or other pavement damage are performed as part of the procedure[2]. Guide rails, drainage structures, and any required fill widening to maintain shoulder width and bridge clearances are reset or replaced as needed[1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Crack‑and‑seat can be applied only when the existing concrete slab exhibits sufficient distress—such as faulting, extensive cracking, or free‑moving slabs—that makes conventional patching uneconomical [1]. The treatment relies on breaking the slab into fragments roughly twelve to twenty‑four inches long and no larger than about thirty inches across so that each piece can be reseated to form a more uniform bearing surface [1][2]. Prior to fracturing, any continuous slab longer than twenty feet must be saw‑cut to that maximum length across the lane width, overlying asphalt must be removed, and debonded joint seals cleared to provide adequate clearance for equipment and utilities [2]. The concrete must be unreinforced because reinforcing steel interferes with the intended reduction in slab size and differential movement [1][2]. After fracturing, the average elastic modulus of the broken concrete layer must fall within the range specified in Table A, a condition verified by falling‑weight deflectometer testing on a one‑thousand‑foot trial section [2]. Seating of the fragments is achieved with a minimum fifty‑ton pneumatic roller that makes at least three passes in the prescribed rolling pattern, and this operation requires a stable subgrade capable of supporting the load without excessive deformation [2]. Adequate subgrade drainage is also required because poor drainage can undermine the uniform support that the reseated fragments are intended to provide [1]. The method is compatible with flexible (asphalt) overlays, which benefit from reduced differential movement and consequently experience less reflective cracking; however, it offers no performance advantage for unbonded concrete overlays and would add unnecessary cost in those cases [1]. When a crack‑and‑seat project is patched before overlaying, a flexible base‑course replacement should be used rather than concrete patches to maintain compatibility with the treated slab [1]. Sonic vibratory tools may be employed only when their use will not cause liquefaction of the underlying subgrade, ensuring that the fracturing process does not compromise subgrade stability [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Treatment procedures

Preparation begins by confirming that the concrete slab is too deteriorated for conventional patching and by correcting any drainage or subgrade deficiencies that could impair the new structure [1]. Any existing asphalt overlay must be stripped away, loose joint seals removed, and slabs longer than twenty feet saw‑cut to approximately one inch deep—about two‑thirds of the slab thickness—while taking care to avoid utilities and other structures [2]. The concrete is then intentionally cracked or broken into fragments roughly twelve to twenty‑four inches long using controlled‑force equipment to produce full‑depth cracks across the surface [1][2]. Immediately after fracturing, the broken pieces are kept wet for visual inspection and a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller, inflated to provide proper contact pressure, is driven over the lane in a pattern that gives each spot at least three passes, with vibratory rollers avoided, to seat the fragments firmly into the underlying base or subbase [1][2]. Seating continues until the pieces are embedded and a uniform, stable support surface is achieved [1][2]. Where the original base is deficient, it is rebuilt with an appropriate Base Course or Superpave Base Course material before further work proceeds [1]. A continuous pavement‑drain system is installed beneath the repaired base to ensure proper water evacuation [1]. Over this seated slab and repaired base a leveling course at least one inch thick, incorporating a bond‑breaker layer, is placed and compacted while correcting any cross‑slope irregularities [1][2]. The leveling course must attain the required strength—often observed by allowing curing time as specified—before the final overlay is applied, with placement typically occurring within one week and protected from freezing conditions [1][2]. A tack coat is then applied to the leveled surface, followed promptly by an unbonded concrete or asphalt wearing surface of the designed thickness, which is finished with full‑depth shoulders that meet required widths and clearances [1][2]. Finally, guide rails, drainage structures, and any necessary fill widening are reset or replaced to complete the rehabilitation [1].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

The Crack and Seat method requires a pre‑fracturing sequence that begins with removal of any overlying asphalt, stripping loose joint seals and sawcutting slabs to about one inch shy of two‑thirds the slab depth, followed by establishing at least one 1,000‑foot‑long test section to fine‑tune the variable‑force fracturing equipment. After full‑depth cracking is achieved, a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller (four to seven wheels, ≥100 psi inflation) must make at least three passes over every spot while avoiding wet conditions, and subsequent falling‑weight deflectometer testing may only be done when temperatures are between 45 °F and 90 °F. Traffic is barred on the fractured lane after cracking and seating until an asphalt overlay is placed, with only limited crossing permitted for ramp access, and bridge crossings by the roller require district engineer approval. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Quality and workmanship

Crack‑and‑Seat work is inspected at every stage: the fracturing equipment must be capable of a controlled impact without spalling, and pre‑fracture preparation requires removal of overlying asphalt, loose joint seals and sawcutting slabs to the specified depth; a minimum one‑thousand‑foot test section is fractured and visually confirmed that all cracks are visible before full production, then a minimum 50‑ton pneumatic‑tire roller seats the broken slab with at least three passes over each spot while confirming no vertical movement of the substrate; the seated pavement’s elastic modulus is verified by falling‑weight deflectometer testing against Table A limits, and any unsuitable or unstable material and exposed reinforcement are removed, the base backfilled with Subbase No. 2A and patched with Flexible Base Replacement before the surface is swept, tack‑coated and overlaid within a week, with traffic prohibited until these inspections are satisfied. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

A satisfactory crack-and-seat treatment is confirmed when the existing concrete is fractured into fragments roughly 12 to 24 inches long and these pieces are compacted or rolled into the underlying base or subbase, creating a uniform and stable support for the overlay [1][2]. The cracking operation must produce continuous full-depth cracks that extend across the entire pavement width while avoiding spalling, punching, or shattering, and every crack must remain visible on the surface [2]. No individual fragment may exceed 30 inches in any dimension, ensuring that all pieces stay within the prescribed size limits [2]. The process must effectively break up free-moving slabs so that differential movement at joints, cracks, and punchouts is minimized [1]. After fracturing, a rolling pattern is required that guarantees each point of the surface receives at least three roller passes, and visual observation of the roller’s vertical deflection must show no substrate movement, confirming proper seating [2]. Any necessary patching should be performed with flexible base-course material rather than concrete patches to preserve the integrity of the seated layer [1]. Finally, a falling-weight deflectometer test on a representative 1,000-foot section (and subsequent production lots) must produce an average elastic modulus for the fractured layer that falls within the limits defined in the applicable table, providing quantitative confirmation of satisfactory treatment [2].

Performance and service life

When a concrete slab has deteriorated beyond economical patching, the crack‑and‑seat method is employed. The pavement is fractured into roughly twelve‑ to twenty‑four‑inch fragments and compacted into the underlying base or subbase, creating a uniform, stable support that limits the size of free‑moving slabs and curtails differential movement at existing cracks, joints, and punchouts. This more consistent backing reduces both the frequency and severity of reflective cracking in any subsequently placed overlay, which translates to an improved surface condition and functional performance compared with leaving the distressed concrete untouched. Because the overlay rests on a support level that exceeds what a conventional subbase can provide, the rehabilitated section exhibits greater durability. As a structural rehabilitation technique, crack‑and‑seat delivers the enhancements defined for 3R work—extending the pavement’s service life while boosting its structural and functional capacity. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139)

The most frequent treatment failures in a Crack‑and‑Seat rehabilitation are an incomplete or overly aggressive fracture that leaves pieces larger than allowed or yields a concrete modulus above the specified upper limit, and inadequate seating that permits residual vertical movement of the slab. These problems stem from using unsuitable impact equipment, operating under wet or temperature‑restricted conditions, ignoring proximity constraints to utilities or structures, and failing to achieve the required roller pressure and pass count; when they occur the contractor must stop work, adjust the fracturing pattern, re‑fracture, or perform additional base repair until visual deflection ceases and modulus values fall within Table A limits. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Monitoring and follow-up

After a Crack‑and‑Seat treatment the crew must first verify that the fractured slab is stable by watching for any vertical deflection of the surface while the roller makes its passes; if movement is seen, base repair and backfill are required. The stabilized pavement is then subjected to falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD) testing at regular intervals (center of lane every 50 feet or less) to confirm that the elastic modulus of the fractured layer falls within the approved range, with testing postponed if the surface is frozen or temperatures are outside the 45–90 °F window. Once the FWD results are acceptable, the surface is swept clean, traffic is prohibited, and an asphalt leveling course must be placed within one week, ensuring the newly seated pavement is never left exposed to freezing conditions. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

When post‑fracturing inspections reveal that the roller‑induced vertical deflection still shows movement of the substrate, or when falling weight deflectometer testing indicates the fractured concrete’s elastic modulus falls outside the prescribed range—particularly above the upper limit in Table A—the cracked and seated area is deemed unsatisfactory and must undergo additional repair such as base removal, replacement with Subbase No. 2A, and flexible‑base patching before overlay placement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)
