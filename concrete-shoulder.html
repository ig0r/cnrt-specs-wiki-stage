Concrete Shoulder

Part of: Shoulder is-subtopic-of
Concrete Shoulder is a topic under Shoulder.
concrete-shouldersconcrete-shouldertied-concrete-shoulderstied-shoulders
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-29

A concrete shoulder is a dedicated concrete element placed adjacent to a rigid pavement that functions as a durable load‑bearing extension, supplying a smooth riding surface for bicycles, heavy trucks and turning movements while transferring edge loads into the main slab to prevent excessive deflection, differential heave, settlement and horizontal separation[1]. It is tied to the pavement through a continuous joint system—control joints aligned with the mainline and spaced no more than twenty feet—and its structural capacity is provided by full‑depth Portland‑cement concrete on high‑volume freeways or roller‑compacted concrete on low‑traffic roads, with slab thickness ranging from four to eighteen inches and compressive strength between 4,000 and 10,000 psi[1][2]. These shoulders must match the material layers of the adjoining pavement, incorporate the load‑transfer coefficient J in design, meet strict edge‑slump tolerances (≤¼ in. over a twelve‑foot straightedge), and are expected to deliver roughly twenty years of service life by adding stiffness at the pavement edge and reducing distress progression[1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

Concrete shoulders are provided adjacent to rigid pavements primarily to address the structural and functional deficiencies that occur at the pavement edge. When truck traffic exceeds a design hourly volume of 150, or when vehicles are expected to occupy the shoulder on sharp curves or turn lanes, the shoulder supplies a durable load‑bearing surface that transfers edge loads into the slab, thereby preventing excessive deflection, differential heave, settlement and horizontal separation. By tying the shoulder to the main slab and accounting for its contribution in the load‑transfer coefficient J, designers avoid an arbitrary one‑inch reduction in required slab thickness and achieve the intended durability and rideability. The shoulder also offers lateral support to rehabilitated or overlaid pavements, preserving structural continuity and reducing future maintenance. To limit stress concentrations at the outer edge, joint spacing in the concrete shoulder is limited to 20 ft, and control joints are aligned with those of the adjacent mainline pavement. In low‑traffic (ADT ≤2 000) and lower‑speed (≤55 mph) roads, roller‑compacted concrete may be used as a maintenance‑free wearing surface that matches the main slab’s joint pattern while providing high compressive strength without reinforcement. When existing shoulders are already concrete, the design requires a concrete shoulder of matching type to retain geometry, proper width and bridge clearances; fill widening is employed where necessary. For widened lanes on low‑volume rural Interstates (ADT <10 000) where an incident‑management lane is not required, a concrete slab up to 14 ft wide may be paired with a partial asphalt or RCC shoulder, and a tied shoulder can be provided as a 13‑ft outside lane striped at 12 ft to further control slab deflection. Collectively these provisions satisfy the performance needs of structural stability, edge load transfer, drainage, safety for bicyclists and turning vehicles, and long‑term serviceability of the roadway edge. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 60–62), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 8 (pp. 164–166)

The concrete shoulder is required to function as a durable load‑carrying extension of the rigid pavement that supplies a smooth riding surface for bicycles, accommodates heavy truck traffic and permits vehicle turning movements at intersections, tight‑radius ramps and sharp curves. Structurally the shoulder must be provided full‑depth on interstates and other limited‑access freeways, while Type 1‑F, 1‑S or 1‑SP sections are specified for arterials, collectors and local roads where bicycle activity is expected; joint spacing may not exceed twenty feet and all control joints shall align with those of the adjacent mainline pavement. A minimum three‑foot width is prescribed for a Type 6 (or 6‑F, 6‑S, 6‑SP) shoulder when only the first few feet of an existing high‑type paved shoulder are distressed, and for widening projects the pavement structure shall be extended into the shoulder by two feet when average daily traffic exceeds five thousand or the adjusted truck traffic index surpasses five hundred, otherwise by one foot. The slab thickness is limited to a range of four to eighteen inches and must achieve a compressive strength between 4,000 and 10,000 psi, matching that of the mainline cement concrete; roller‑compacted concrete may be used provided it meets the same dimensional and strength criteria. All longitudinal and pavement‑shoulder joints are required to meet a surface‑tolerance of no more than one quarter inch when measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge, to be cleaned and sealed, and to have control joints that correspond with the mainline joints. The shoulder surface must receive a full‑width transverse texture using rectangular grooves of the prescribed size, and on roadways with speed limits of 35 mph or higher any exposed concrete shall be diamond‑ground to provide adequate skid resistance. Durability objectives include resisting edge slumping at the lane‑shoulder interface within the allowable limit, enduring a design hourly volume of at least 150 trucks on two‑lane routes, maintaining performance under adverse drainage conditions, and achieving an approximate twenty‑year design life through proper subgrade and subbase compaction. Safety is further ensured by adjusting or replacing guide rail and drainage structures as necessary, preserving adequate shoulder width through fill widening, verifying bridge clearances, and providing a surface that remains usable for turning movements and incident‑management traffic on high‑volume corridors. The increased stiffness of a tied concrete shoulder reduces edge stresses, limits deflection, and prevents differential heave, settlement or horizontal separation between pavement and shoulder, thereby extending service life and delaying the first maintenance cycle for long‑life concrete pavement projects. Where two‑way ADT is under 10 000, widened slabs with asphalt or RCC shoulders may be considered as alternative treatments, provided they satisfy the same functional and structural criteria. [1][2]

Scope and applicability

Concrete shoulders are employed wherever project specifications require a dedicated concrete element adjacent to the travel lane, and they may be constructed either as plain cement‑concrete (PCC) or as roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) provided that the supporting base conditions and material requirements are satisfied. [2] The PCC option calls for Class AA cement concrete together with the prescribed joint sealing material, curing covers or compounds, admixtures, dowels, expansion‑joint filler and longitudinal joint material, and it is placed on a prepared surface such as an asphaltic leveling course over rubblized concrete, a treated base of cement or asphalt, or a No. OGS subbase while protecting the underlying substrate during placement. [2] The RCC option follows the requirements set out in Section 502 (including 502.2 and 502.3) and is installed exactly as shown on the project plans. [2] Across the guides, concrete shoulders are considered appropriate for rigid pavement systems—specifically jointed plain cement‑concrete or Long‑Life Concrete Pavement—when the project involves new construction, reconstruction, or rehabilitation that retains or replaces such concrete pavements. [1] For any new construction or reconstruction that employs a rigid pavement structure, Portland Cement Concrete shoulders must be provided, with full‑depth PCC shoulders required on Interstate and other limited‑access freeways and Type 2 concrete shoulders called for on arterials, collectors and local roads that adjoin the rigid slab. [1] In rehabilitation projects that use the 3R method on existing concrete pavements, a concrete shoulder may be added to give lateral support or to create a low‑maintenance edge, but joint spacing shall not exceed twenty feet. [1] Concrete shoulders are also specified for bonded and unbonded concrete overlays, crack‑and‑seat overlays, rubblization reconstruction, and full remove‑and‑replace reconstruction of concrete pavements. [1] When the existing shoulder is already concrete, a concrete shoulder is required; if the existing shoulder is of another material, the new shoulder should match that material. [1] For low traffic volumes—average daily traffic of two thousand vehicles or less—and design speeds of fifty‑five miles per hour or lower, RCC may be used as an alternate to plain cement concrete for a shoulder that serves as the final wearing course adjacent to reinforced or plain cement‑concrete pavement, provided tied shoulders are not required. [1] The same low‑volume condition permits RCC to be placed as a base course beneath an asphalt wearing surface. [1] A special arrangement that combines a widened concrete slab not exceeding fourteen feet in width with a partial asphalt or RCC shoulder is suitable for low‑volume rural Interstate or Expressway segments carrying two‑way traffic below ten thousand vehicles per day where an incident‑management lane is not needed; in such cases tied concrete shoulders can be provided, and the outside lane may be thirteen feet wide and striped at twelve feet to limit slab deflection while maintaining conventional lane widths for the remaining lanes. [1] On urban Interstate or Expressway corridors, plain cement‑concrete shoulders that are tied to the mainline concrete pavement are mandatory; they must be full‑depth with dowelled joints and their material composition must match the mainline from subgrade to surface. [1] For rural Interstates, the choice depends on anticipated work‑zone or incident‑management use: if such support is not expected, tied concrete shoulders with undowelled JPCP joints may be provided; if it is expected, a full‑depth cement‑concrete shoulder with dowelled joints should be constructed. [1] One guide notes that concrete shoulders are not prescribed for projects involving flexible (asphalt) pavements, which require other shoulder types, whereas the other guide permits concrete shoulders adjacent to flexible pavements when the project specifications call for a dedicated concrete element and the base conditions are met. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 60–62), Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 8 (pp. 165–166); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 577–578)

A concrete shoulder is appropriate only when the pavement structure, traffic characteristics, and construction constraints allow it to meet a series of stringent limits. When the roadway employs a flexible pavement rather than a rigid one, the standard full‑depth concrete shoulder is omitted in favor of Type 1 (or its variants) shoulders, especially where serious drainage problems are anticipated, truck traffic on two‑lane roads exceeds a Design Hourly Volume of 150, or regular shoulder use is expected at tight‑radius curves, ramp intersections, or turning movements. For collectors and local roads with flexible pavements, a same‑material Type 3 shoulder may also be selected. Concrete shoulders are further limited by joint spacing; the spacing must never exceed twenty feet, and if this limit cannot be achieved—or if the existing shoulder is itself a flexible pavement—alternative shoulder types become mandatory. On interstates and other limited‑access freeways that require excavation or scarifying of the existing shoulder, a Type 6 series shoulder is specified, while only cleaning and patching calls for a Type 7 shoulder. In low‑volume settings, when average daily traffic is five thousand vehicles or fewer (or ADTT ≤ 500), the widening extension into the shoulder may be reduced to one foot—or omitted entirely—provided an alternative such as a Type 6, 7, or recycled shoulder is used.

Traffic volume and speed also dictate suitability. Roller‑compacted concrete shoulders are acceptable only on roadways with an ADT of two thousand vehicles per day or less and a posted speed limit of fifty‑five miles per hour or lower; they are discouraged where the International Roughness Index will be measured for ride‑quality incentives, and if the RCC surface is exposed on roads with speeds of thirty‑five miles per hour or greater, mandatory diamond grinding must be performed—if this finishing step cannot be provided, another shoulder type is preferred. When frost heave differentials are expected, increasing slab thickness offers limited mitigation, and designers are directed to consult dedicated frost‑design provisions; tied concrete shoulders also preclude the one‑inch slab‑thickness reduction that might otherwise be applied because their effect is already incorporated in the load‑transfer coefficient.

Finally, on low‑volume rural interstates or expressways with two‑way ADT below ten thousand and little demand for an incident‑management lane, a widened concrete slab limited to fourteen feet in width, using a twelve‑foot travel‑lane stripe, combined with an adjacent asphalt or RCC shoulder is preferred over a full concrete shoulder. This alternative leverages the reduced stresses associated with lower traffic volumes and the lack of anticipated heavy shoulder usage, making the durability advantages of a full concrete shoulder unnecessary. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 60–62), Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99), Ch. 8 (pp. 164–166)

Design inputs and requirements

Design of a concrete shoulder using roller‑compacted concrete must confirm that the location is adjacent to reinforced or plain cement concrete pavement, that the existing subgrade and subbase are capable of supporting RCC compaction, and that traffic conditions do not exceed an average daily traffic of 2,000 vehicles with a speed limit of 55 mph or less. The material specification calls for a very dry mix of cement, aggregate (maximum size under one inch), and water with a low water‑cement ratio—typically around 0.40 but as low as 0.30—and a compressive strength target between 4,000 and 10,000 psi. Because service life is influenced by construction materials, environmental conditions, and traffic volumes, the design phase should also evaluate site‑specific climate impacts and anticipate a typical service life of about twenty years. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 98–99)

The design of concrete shoulders is governed by a hierarchy that begins with project‑specific provisions, proceeds through geometric and construction specifications, and culminates in performance constraints imposed by the applicable standards. [1] For any new or reconstructed facility having a rigid pavement, the shoulder must be constructed of Portland cement concrete, with full‑depth PCC required on Interstate and other limited‑access freeways and Type 2 shoulders prescribed for arterials, collectors and local roads. [1] The geometric definitions are taken from Publication 13 Design Manual Part 2 Chapter 4, while the detailed shoulder types are defined in Publication 72M (RC‑25M) and further clarified in Chapters 8.14 and 8.15 of the manual. [1] When a flexible pavement is placed, the paved portion must extend at least one foot into the future shoulder area, increasing to two feet when average daily traffic exceeds five thousand vehicles or the truck factor exceeds five hundred; an option exists to pave the entire shoulder. [1] Rehabilitation of rigid pavements may add concrete shoulders for lateral support, but joint spacing shall never exceed twenty feet and a minimum three‑foot width of Type 6, 6‑F, 6‑S or 6‑SP must be provided when only the first few feet adjacent to the travel lane are distressed. [1] Existing concrete shoulders dictate that new shoulders match the existing material, otherwise the shoulder type should be consistent with the adjacent pavement and designed in accordance with Chapter 8 for full reconstruction or replacement and Chapter 10 for bonded or unbonded overlays. [1] Diamond grinding is required to improve ride quality, and when an exposed RCC surface serves a posted speed of thirty‑five miles per hour or higher, diamond grinding must also be performed. [1] Roller‑compacted concrete shoulders are permitted where tied shoulders are not required, with thickness ranging from four to eighteen inches and compressive strength approximately four‑ to ten‑thousand psi; control joints must align with those on the adjacent mainline concrete pavement. [1] Widened slab alternatives may be used only up to a maximum width of fourteen feet and must be paired with either a Type 1 asphalt or an RCC right shoulder to achieve the required effective shoulder width, a configuration allowed solely on low‑volume rural Interstates or expressways where two‑way ADT is under ten thousand vehicles, using the load‑transfer coefficient given in Table 8.2 for widened lanes with asphalt or RCC shoulders. [1] Projects employing Long‑Life Concrete Pavement require plain cement concrete shoulders, while urban Interstate and expressway sections must have full‑depth PCC shoulders with dowelled transverse joints; rural routes may use tied undowelled JPCP shoulders unless ADT exceeds ten thousand vehicles, at which point full‑depth dowelled shoulders become mandatory. [1] All concrete shoulder materials must match the mainline pavement from subgrade to surface, and the specifications for dowelled shoulders are found in Publication 408 Sections 501 or 506, whereas non‑dowelled or RCC shoulders are governed by Section 658. [1] Concrete shoulders must conform to the same rigid‑pavement specifications that apply to the main slab, as set out in Section 500 of the specifications. [2] Edge slump at the interface between shoulder and pavement shall not exceed a quarter inch and joint tolerances are limited to a quarter inch when measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge. [2] The transverse texture grooves required on the shoulder surface must be rectangular, ranging from three‑thirty‑seconds to three‑sixteenths of an inch in width and from one‑eighth to three‑sixteenths of an inch deep, providing a mean texture depth of at least 0.040 in. [2] All curing, joint sealing, surface‑finish and defect‑remediation requirements that apply to the primary pavement are also imposed on the concrete shoulder. [2] Construction shall follow the Standard Drawings and the detailed specifications in Section 501.3 when plain cement concrete is used, or Section 502.3 for roller‑compacted concrete shoulders. [2] During placement the crew may not damage the prepared surface; any damage must be repaired to the Representative’s satisfaction at no extra cost. [2] When slip‑form paving is employed, transverse and longitudinal contraction joints are to be accurately marked, sawed with approved equipment, then cured and protected in accordance with the joint specifications. [2] Material provisions for concrete shoulders are set out in Section 658.2 of the incidental construction specifications, and plain cement‑concrete shoulders shall be built with Class AA cement as defined in Section 704. [2] Joint sealing materials must meet the requirements of Section 705.4, while curing covers and approved curing compounds are required by Sections 711.1 and 711.2(a), with admixtures prescribed in Section 711.3. [2] The supporting base courses must satisfy the criteria of Sections 303.2, 350.2 and 360.2, and dowels, expansion‑joint fillers and longitudinal joint materials are prescribed in Sections 709.1(a), 705.1 and 705.2 respectively; roller‑compacted concrete shoulders follow the provisions of Section 502.2. [2]

Design methods and criteria

Concrete shoulder design follows the same overall procedures as the adjacent pavement, requiring that the shoulder material be compatible from subgrade through surface and that a continuous base drain be provided when appropriate [1]. When an existing shoulder is already concrete, a new concrete shoulder must be installed; otherwise the material may be matched to the existing type [1]. Full‑depth reconstruction of a concrete shoulder employs the methods outlined in Chapter 8, while bonded or unbonded concrete overlays are designed according to the requirements of Chapter 10 [1]. Roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) shoulders are sized using the PennDOT RCC design procedures set out in Chapter 8, Section 8.15, with allowable slab thickness ranging from four to eighteen inches and selected on the basis of traffic volume and concrete compressive strength typically between 4,000 and 10,000 psi [1]. An empirical joint‑spacing rule governs control‑joint placement: slabs eight inches thick or greater receive joints spaced at 2.5 times the slab thickness in feet, whereas thinner slabs use a fixed spacing of fifteen to twenty feet [1]. The DARWin calculation routine is used to compute the required slab thickness, incorporating the influence of tied concrete shoulders through the load‑transfer coefficient J taken from Table 8.2 for widened lanes with asphalt or RCC shoulders; after computation the thickness is rounded to the nearest half‑inch, and for pavements thicker than seven inches with an area less than 30,000 yd² it is rounded to the nearest whole inch [1]. Geometric limits are imposed on widened concrete slabs so that they may not exceed fourteen feet in width and must be striped at twelve feet from the edge; when tied shoulders are employed a thirteen‑foot‑wide outside lane also striped at twelve feet is permitted to control deflection [1]. Subgrade and base layers must be capable of supporting the compaction required for RCC shoulders, ensuring adequate support for the designed slab thickness [1]. Urban interstate and expressway projects require full‑depth plain cement concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints that are tied to the main pavement, as specified in Publication 72M, whereas rural interstates may use either tied undowelled shoulders or full‑depth dowelled shoulders depending on anticipated work‑zone traffic; if the two‑way average daily traffic is below ten thousand vehicles, widened slabs with asphalt or RCC alternatives are allowed per Section 8.14 [1]. Construction of plain cement concrete shoulders follows detailed procedural steps that prohibit damage to the prepared surface while transporting or depositing concrete ahead of the paver, limit vehicle travel to the immediate paving area, and require repair or replacement of any damaged areas at no additional cost to the department [2]. Transverse and longitudinal contraction joints must be sawed, cured, and protected in accordance with prescribed standards, with the location of transverse joint centers accurately marked using tacks or other approved methods before sawing [2]. Sawing equipment must be equipped with guides, blade guards, water‑cooling, and depth‑control features to ensure precise joint cutting, and slip‑form paving operations require that extra equipment and parts be provided both prior to concrete placement and during the sawing process [2]. Roller‑compacted concrete shoulders are constructed as shown on the plans and in accordance with Section 502.3, following the same design thickness and joint‑spacing criteria described for RCC shoulders [1][2].

Concrete shoulders are designed according to roadway classification and pavement type, with rigid pavements requiring Portland‑Cement Concrete (PCC) shoulders and flexible pavements using specified concrete types depending on functional class [1]. Full‑depth PCC shoulders are mandated for Interstate and other limited‑access freeways, while Type 2 concrete is prescribed for arterials, collectors and local roads; when the adjacent pavement is flexible, Type 1 (or its variants) shoulders are used on interstates and arterials, on routes with serious drainage problems, on two‑lane highways where design hourly truck volume exceeds 150, on curves sharper than about 12°‑30′, and within a corridor extending 250 ft upstream to 150 ft downstream of intersections [1]. For collectors and local roads with flexible pavement, either Type 3 concrete shoulders or the same surface material as the main pavement may be provided [1]. Joint spacing for concrete shoulders shall not exceed twenty feet, and control joints on roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) shoulders must align with those of the adjacent mainline concrete pavement; RCC joint spacing follows a 15–20 ft rule for sections under eight inches thick or equals 2.5 times the pavement thickness in feet for thicker sections [1]. When an existing shoulder is already concrete, the new design must retain a concrete shoulder; if the existing shoulder is of another material, replacement may match that material [1]. In widening projects, the pavement structure must extend into the shoulder by two feet when average daily traffic exceeds 5,000 vehicles or adjusted daily truck traffic exceeds 500, and by one foot otherwise; the widened concrete slab is limited to a total width of fourteen feet, striped at twelve feet, with the remaining width serving as an effective shoulder together with a Type 1 asphalt or RCC shoulder [1]. Tied shoulders are accounted for through the load‑transfer coefficient (J); when tied, the usual one‑inch reduction in required slab thickness is not applied, and calculated slab thicknesses are rounded to the nearest half inch, or to the nearest inch for slabs thicker than seven inches and less than 30,000 yd², while remaining within the minimum and maximum depths prescribed [1]. RCC shoulders are limited to roadways with ADT of 2,000 vehicles or fewer and a posted speed limit of 55 mph or less; their thickness is designed between four and eighteen inches using standard Penn‑DOT concrete pavement design procedures, targeting a compressive strength of approximately 4,000 to 10,000 psi and an intended service life of twenty years [1]. Full‑depth plain cement concrete shoulders with dowelled transverse joints are required for projects employing Long‑Life Concrete Pavement on urban interstates and expressways; rural low‑volume corridors may use tied shoulders with undowelled joints or RCC shoulders, provided the materials from subgrade to surface match those of the mainline pavement [1]. No separate factor‑of‑safety or reliability coefficient is prescribed beyond compliance with these design thresholds and the underlying Penn‑DOT specifications, which embed the assumed safety and reliability requirements [1][2]. At the pavement‑shoulder interface edge slumping must be limited to a maximum of one‑quarter inch, and the elevation difference across the joint may not exceed the same quarter‑inch tolerance when measured with a twelve‑foot straightedge positioned centrally and perpendicular to the joint [2]. Any transverse texture applied to the shoulder shall consist of rectangular grooves whose width ranges from three‑thirty‑seconds to three‑sixteenths of an inch and whose depth lies between one‑eighth and three‑sixteenths of an inch [2]. The concrete material shall be Class AA cement concrete as defined in the applicable specifications, and joint sealing materials must conform to the prescribed sections [2]. Proper curing and protection are required through approved covers and a curing compound meeting the stipulated criteria, with any admixtures receiving prior approval [2]. The shoulder’s base courses shall comply with subgrade preparation, aggregate quality, and thickness provisions; dowels—either deformed or plain bar types—must be installed in accordance with joint detailing requirements, while expansion joints are to be filled with premolded material and longitudinal joints must employ the designated filler system [2]. When roller‑compacted concrete is used for the shoulder, it is governed by its own set of provisions that parallel those for RCC pavements elsewhere in the guidelines [2].

Structural section and dimensions

The concrete shoulder that employs roller‑compacted concrete must be built to a pavement thickness ranging from four inches up to eighteen inches. Control joints are aligned with those of the adjoining mainline concrete pavement; when the RCC layer is less than eight inches thick, joint spacing is limited to fifteen‑to‑twenty feet, while for sections eight inches or thicker the spacing equals 2.5 times the slab thickness expressed in feet. The shoulder does not require formwork, steel reinforcement, or dowel bars and rests on a prepared subgrade and subbase capable of sustaining the required compaction. Where the shoulder is provided as a widened concrete slab, its overall width may not exceed fourteen feet and it is striped at twelve‑foot intervals; an alternative tied‑concrete shoulder can be formed as a thirteen‑foot‑wide outside lane, also striped at twelve feet, while conventional layouts retain twelve‑foot lanes with a tied concrete shoulder. No explicit dimensions are prescribed for the pavement base, subbase, treated‑soil layer, edge support, or additional reinforcement. [1]

Concrete shoulders built with roller‑compacted concrete are sized using the standard PennDOT concrete pavement design procedures, which allow a structural thickness anywhere between four and eighteen inches depending on traffic and subgrade conditions. The required thickness is achieved in one or more lifts that match the paver’s width, and because RCC does not need forms or reinforcement, the layer configuration is simply compacted lift(s). Control joints are placed to align with the mainline concrete pavement; when the shoulder is under eight inches thick the joints are spaced about fifteen to twenty feet, while for thicknesses of eight inches or more the spacing equals 2.5 times the slab depth in feet. These joint locations provide the necessary tie‑ins between the RCC shoulder and the adjacent pavement. [1]

Materials and mixture design

Concrete shoulders are required to be constructed of Portland‑cement concrete and the specification must call for a Class AA cement‑concrete mix as the baseline material [1][2]. The constituent materials shall include Portland cement, a dense aggregate gradation whose maximum particle size does not exceed one inch, and water; limited admixtures and optional air‑entraining agents may be incorporated when the shoulder is placed as roller‑compacted concrete (RCC) [1][2]. For RCC shoulders the mix design must employ a low water‑to‑cement ratio, typically between 0.30 and 0.40, producing a dry, no‑slump mixture comparable to damp gravel [1]. This proportioning yields compressive strengths in the range of approximately four thousand to ten thousand pounds per square inch for RCC shoulders [1]. Where a plain cement‑concrete shoulder is used, the material constituents must match those of the adjoining mainline pavement throughout all structural layers, from subgrade to surface, with no separate proportioning beyond the requirements already defined for the mainline [1]. The specification must also identify the joint sealing material, curing covers and protective compound, and any required admixtures that are to be used in the shoulder construction [2]. Required dowel type—either deformed or plain bar—and the premolded expansion‑joint filler as well as the longitudinal joint material for the shoulder shall be explicitly called out in the design documents [2]. Supporting base courses for the shoulder must be described, and when a roller‑compacted concrete shoulder is employed the specification shall reference the provisions of Section 502.2 [2]. For dowelled shoulders the applicable joint provisions are those found in Publication 408 Sections 501 or 506, while non‑dowelled and RCC shoulders must comply with Publication 408 Section 658 [1].

The selection and proportioning of the concrete‑shoulder mixture is driven by the need to provide adequate structural capacity, durability against traffic and environmental exposure, ease of construction, and long‑term performance. The mix uses the same cement, aggregate and water base as the adjacent mainline pavement, ensuring that every layer from subgrade through surface is identical; this material matching ties the shoulder to the pavement and reduces edge stresses. For roller‑compacted concrete shoulders the water‑to‑cement ratio is reduced to about 0.40 and may be as low as 0.30, producing a damp‑gravel consistency with essentially no slump that can be placed by a paver and compacted with rollers without forms or reinforcement. The aggregate is densely graded with a maximum size of less than one inch, air entrainment may be incorporated when required, and admixtures are limited to those needed for workability or durability. This proportioning yields compressive strengths typically between 4,000 psi and 10,000 psi, satisfying structural demands for the anticipated traffic loads. Thickness is determined by state design procedures—PennDOT guidelines prescribe sections from four to eighteen inches—to meet required capacity and service life under expected traffic and climate conditions. Full‑depth concrete shoulders on high‑volume corridors are constructed with dowelled transverse joints in accordance with Publication 72M, while rural sections may use undowelled tied joints when work‑zone support is not needed; both approaches conform to the relevant provisions of Publication 408, thereby ensuring constructability and long‑term serviceability. Structural details and interfaces

The concrete shoulder is integrated with the adjacent pavement through a continuous joint system that includes both control joints and contraction joints, all of which must be treated in the same manner as the mainline joints [1][2]. Control joints are required to line up with those of the adjoining concrete pavement and may not be spaced more than twenty feet apart; for reinforced cement concrete (RCC) shoulders thinner than eight inches the recommended spacing is fifteen to twenty feet, while for thicker slabs the spacing equals 2.5 times the slab thickness in feet, a rule that also satisfies the general twenty‑foot limit [1]. All longitudinal joints that intersect the shoulder and the pavement‑shoulder interface must be cleaned and sealed, and any failed transverse joint must be repaired or resealed before the shoulder slab is placed [1]. Transverse and longitudinal contraction joints are sawed, cured and protected in accordance with the specified sections; the centre of each transverse joint is precisely located by marking with tacks or an approved method prior to cutting, and the sawing equipment must be equipped with guides, a blade guard, water‑cooling and depth‑control features [2]. The joint system incorporates sealing material as prescribed in Section 705.4 (options a, b, or d) together with a premolded expansion‑joint filler defined in Section 705.1, and longitudinal joint material completes the assembly [2]. For full‑depth Portland cement concrete shoulders dowel bar retrofits are installed at mid‑panel cracks and transverse joints to provide load transfer, using deformed and plain steel dowels that conform to the relevant specifications; in urban interstate applications these transverse joints are routinely dowelled, whereas rural sections may remain undowelled unless work‑zone support is required [1][2]. RCC shoulders are placed without forms, steel reinforcing bars or dowel bars, relying on the slab thickness and joint design for structural performance [1]. When a tied concrete shoulder is used, the design employs the load‑transfer coefficient (J) prescribed for widened lanes with asphalt or RCC shoulders [1]. The shoulder rests on the prescribed base courses identified in Sections 303.2, 350.2 and 360.2, which serve as transition layers between the subgrade and the concrete slab [2]. During construction the placed concrete is protected with curing covers, an appropriate curing compound, and any required admixtures to ensure proper strength development [2]. The shoulder slab width is limited to fourteen feet; a pavement stripe is placed at twelve feet, and when a tied shoulder is provided an outside lane of thirteen feet may be striped at the same twelve‑foot location to limit slab deflection [1]. All materials comprising the shoulder—from subgrade through base courses to surface concrete—must match those of the adjoining mainline pavement, ensuring continuity of structural properties across the interface [1].

Concrete shoulders are tied directly to the adjacent pavement so that the interface behaves as a continuous slab, requiring the same subgrade, subbase and base courses beneath the shoulder as under the mainline pavement.[1][2] Load transfer across the shoulder‑pavement edge is provided by steel dowels—either deformed or plain—installed in the longitudinal joint, ensuring continuity between new concrete and the existing lane; dowelled joints are required on high‑volume urban interstates or work zones while undowelled joints may be used on low‑traffic rural sections.[1][2] Joint layout is carefully defined: transverse contraction joints are accurately marked, sawed with guided equipment and spaced no more than twenty feet apart, with roller‑compacted concrete shoulders following the same rule as the mainline—typically fifteen to twenty feet for lifts under eight inches thick or a distance equal to the slab thickness multiplied by 2.5 for thicker sections.[1][2] Longitudinal joints that incorporate the shoulder interface must be aligned with those of the adjoining concrete pavement and inspected with a straightedge, with any deviation not exceeding one quarter inch measured over a twelve‑foot span.[1][2] All joint ends are sealed to prevent cement mortar intrusion, using premolded expansion‑joint filler for transverse joints and dedicated longitudinal joint material for the continuous edge, after which the joints are protected and cured with appropriate compounds or covers; cleaning and sealing of longitudinal and pavement‑shoulder joints also preserves a continuous base drain.[1][2] The subgrade and subbase are constructed as separate items capable of supporting the concrete during placement, and the base course beneath the shoulder follows the same specifications as that under the mainline pavement.[1][2] When a concrete shoulder is provided adjacent to rigid pavements or on Interstates and other limited‑access freeways, a full‑depth shoulder is required; for flexible pavements the type of shoulder (Type 1, 1‑F, 1‑S, or 1‑SP) is selected based on traffic conditions, with higher‑type sections beginning about 250 ft before an intersection and terminating roughly 150 ft downstream.[1] On two‑lane roads where the design hourly truck volume exceeds 150, a concrete shoulder must be provided, and when average daily traffic reaches five thousand vehicles (or ADTT ≤ 500) the structural layer of a new lane extends into the shoulder by one foot, increasing to two feet for higher traffic volumes, thereby creating a continuous structural connection.[1] In areas with heavy turning movements such as driveway entrances, exits or intersections, out‑to‑out paving of the shoulder with the same material as the main lane is strongly recommended; the widened slab shall not exceed fourteen feet in width and is striped at twelve feet, while a tied shoulder may include a thirteen‑foot‑wide outside lane also striped at twelve feet to limit slab deflection.[1] Guide rail and drainage structures located at the shoulder edge are adjusted or replaced as needed, and fill widening is employed where necessary to retain the designed shoulder width and verify bridge clearances.[1] The design adopts the load‑transfer coefficient (J) specified for widened lanes with asphalt or RCC shoulders, as given in Table 8.2 of the referenced specifications, ensuring appropriate transfer of stresses across the interface.[1] During concrete placement traffic is restricted to the immediate paving zone so that the prepared surface beneath the shoulder is not damaged; any damage must be repaired to the representative’s satisfaction at no additional cost.[2]

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Concrete shoulder design must incorporate a continuous pavement base drain to convey water away, clean and seal all longitudinal and pavement‑shoulder joints to prevent moisture intrusion, and adjust or replace guide rail and drainage structures as needed; when an overlay is used, concrete shoulders are required if the existing shoulders are concrete, otherwise they may match the existing shoulder type. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70)

Concrete shoulders are required wherever a rigid‑pavement system is constructed and, on flexible‑pavement projects, the pavement may be widened two feet into the shoulder to provide a concrete edge [1]. Full‑depth plain cement concrete shoulders are mandatory on Interstate and other limited‑access freeways, while arterials, collectors and local roads employ Type 2 or Type 6 (including 6‑F, 6‑S, 6‑SP) sections that must provide at least three feet of width adjacent to the travel lane [1]. Minimum shoulder widths are specified as three feet for the Type 6 families, with extensions into the shoulder limited to one foot when average daily traffic is five thousand vehicles or less (and an ADT truck count of five hundred or fewer) and two feet for higher traffic volumes [1]. The overall slab width of a widened concrete section may not exceed fourteen feet, and where tied shoulders are used a thirteen‑foot outside lane is striped at twelve‑foot intervals to limit deflection [1]. Joint spacing shall never exceed twenty feet and control joints must align with the joint pattern of the adjoining mainline pavement [1]; additionally, the longitudinal joint between pavement and shoulder must be finished within a tolerance of no greater than one‑quarter inch and edge slumping at the lane‑shoulder interface may not exceed one‑quarter inch [2]. The shoulder surface is required to receive transverse texture using rectangular grooves whose width ranges from three‑thirty‑seconds to three‑sixteenths of an inch and depth from one‑eighth to three‑sixteenth of an inch [2]. When the design hourly volume of trucks exceeds 150, or when turning movements are expected on tight‑radius ramps, curves sharper than about 12°‑30′, and intersections where vehicles may need the right‑hand shoulder, a paved concrete segment extending roughly 250 feet upstream and 150 feet downstream of the intersection is required to give bicyclists a smooth surface and to permit optional rumble‑strip treatment [1]. Safety and user performance are enhanced by tying the shoulder to the adjacent pavement, which adds stiffness, reduces edge stresses, limits differential settlement and improves incident‑management capability [1]. Urban interstates require full‑depth dowelled joints, whereas rural corridors may use undowelled tied shoulders unless the shoulder serves work‑zone traffic control on high‑volume routes [1]. For low‑volume roads with an ADT of two thousand or less and speed limits up to 55 mph, roller‑compacted concrete is permissible; however, if the exposed surface carries a posted speed of 35 mph or greater it must be diamond‑ground to provide adequate texture [1]. All full‑depth shoulders use the same material layers as the mainline pavement from subgrade to surface, ensuring consistent structural performance and durability [1].

Verification and expected performance

The completed concrete shoulder is verified by first checking edge slumping during placement with a 12‑foot straightedge and confirming that the slump between lane and shoulder does not exceed one‑quarter inch; the same straightedge test is repeated after the concrete has hardened to ensure the limit remains satisfied. A second straightedge inspection across the longitudinal joint where the pavement meets the shoulder must also stay within a one‑quarter‑inch tolerance when measured perpendicular to the joint. The shoulder’s surface texture is then examined to confirm that transverse grooving was applied over the full width and that groove dimensions fall between three‑thirty‑seconds and three‑sixteenths of an inch in width and between one‑eighth and three‑sixteenths of an inch in depth, meeting the prescribed texture criteria. These checks collectively demonstrate constructability, performance, and adherence to the project specifications for the concrete shoulder. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 362–375)

The concrete shoulder is expected to provide a design service life on the order of twenty years, a duration attributed to the low water‑cement ratio mix that yields high compressive strength and consequently greater durability [1]. Because the shoulder adds stiffness to the edge of the pavement, slab deflection is limited, which in turn extends the structural life of the overall pavement relative to conventional concrete sections [1]. The design also targets enhanced distress resistance; control joints aligned with the mainline pavement and spaced according to thickness‑based guidelines, together with the inherent rigidity of the shoulder, are intended to reduce differential heaving, settlement, and horizontal separation at the carriageway edge [1]. Dimensional performance is monitored through strict tolerances: edge slumping adjacent to the shoulder must not exceed one‑quarter inch when measured with a 12‑foot straightedge on the six‑inch strip next to the pavement edge, both during placement and after hardening [2]. The longitudinal joint between pavement and shoulder is required to be smooth within one‑quarter inch, also verified with a 12‑foot straightedge across the joint [2]. Surface texture must achieve at least a mean texture depth of 0.040 in., determined by ASTM E965 on four locations per lane, while transverse grooves are checked for compliance with specified width and depth ranges [2]. Ride quality may be assessed with International Roughness Index surveys if contractually required; otherwise it is considered not applicable because the typical roller‑compacted concrete surface is rougher than conventional pavements and such shoulders are generally avoided where IRI‑based payment incentives apply [1][2]. Performance verification relies on long‑term monitoring that records slab deflection magnitudes to document stiffness effects and tracks the interval before the first maintenance intervention as an indicator of distress progression, confirming that the time to initial repair exceeds that observed in conventional concrete pavements [1]. When ride‑quality criteria are stipulated, compliance would be confirmed through IRI surveys conducted according to contract specifications [1][2].
