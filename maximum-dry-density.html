Maximum Dry Density

Part of: Compaction is-subtopic-of
Maximum Dry Density is a topic under Compaction.
maximum-dry-density
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

Maximum Dry Density is the laboratory‑derived greatest dry unit weight that a soil or granular material can attain when compacted at its optimum moisture content under a specified compactive effort, as determined by the Moisture‑Density Test (PTM No. 106) on representative samples[1][2]. It serves as the reference density for field compaction of subgrade soils, embankment fills, Type 1 granular material, and plant‑mixed asphalt courses, with in‑place densities required to reach at least 97 % of this value (or 100 % in the upper three feet) while keeping moisture within a narrow band around optimum[2]. The target values are verified on site using calibrated nuclear or electrical‑impedance gauges per PTM 402/403, and any failure to meet the prescribed density or moisture limits mandates reshaping, recompaction, and retesting before work can continue[1][2]

Purpose and quality objectives

Maximum Dry Density serves as the benchmark for assessing the compaction performance of subgrade soils, quantifying the greatest dry unit weight attainable when the soil is compacted at its optimum moisture content under a specified compactive effort. The value is obtained from the laboratory Moisture‑Density Test (PTM No. 106) performed on representative 50‑lb bags and reflects the ability of well‑graded or coarse‑grained soils to achieve higher densities, which in turn influences CBR values and overall subgrade stability. [1] Maximum Dry Density also functions as the reference density that governs the evaluation of compaction quality for both granular fill materials and asphalt mixtures. In Section 206, the optimum moisture content and maximum dry density for each embankment or fill material are first established by laboratory testing (PTM No. 106); field verification with a calibrated nuclear gauge in accordance with PTM No. 402 then requires compacted layers to reach not less than 97 % of that maximum, while the upper three feet must attain 100 %; if the measured dry density falls short, the area must be reshaped, re‑compacted and retested until the required percentage is obtained. [2] In Section 208, the same reference density controls special rolling: material in embankment foundations or existing embankments more than three feet below the proposed subgrade elevation is compacted to 97 % of maximum dry density, whereas material constituting structure foundations, subgrades, or lying within three feet of the proposed subgrade elevation is compacted to 100 % of maximum dry density, with compaction performed at a moisture content between minus 3 % of optimum and optimum moisture content. [2] Within Section 413, the theoretical maximum density derived from a material’s maximum dry density is employed for mat‑density acceptance; field gauges and pavement cores taken within one day of placement are used to calculate percent of theoretical maximum density from bulk specific gravity (Gmb) and bulk density measurements, and the inspector may require that a target density be met, mandating a new optimum‑rolling pattern if it is not achieved and triggering a stop‑paving order when the density payment factor percentage of ≥ 100 is not met for two consecutive lots. [2]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 150–170), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 276–280)

Maximum Dry Density is used as a quality check to confirm that the subgrade soil can be compacted to its highest achievable dry unit weight at the optimum moisture content, which underpins reliable CBR values and resilient modulus estimates; if the soil cannot reach this density it signals a potential problem of insufficient compaction and reduced bearing capacity. The requirement is verified through the Moisture Density Test (PTM No. 106) as part of the laboratory suite that supports subgrade evaluation. For embankments the specification requires compacting the full width of the fill to not less than 97 % of the maximum dry density, with the upper three feet required at 100 %, and moisture must be kept within –3 % of optimum. For Type 1 granular material the same percentages apply with moisture within ±2 % of optimum. If the in‑place density measured by a nuclear gauge does not meet these limits, the area must be reshaped, re‑compacted or replaced and retested before further placement. For structure foundations, subgrades and any material within three feet of the proposed subgrade elevation, the specification requires achieving 100 % of the maximum dry density; meeting these targets together with moisture content between minus 3 percent of optimum and optimum is intended to prevent excessive permanent deflection or rutting, which is defined as greater than 1/2 inch for existing subgrades and greater than 1 inch for embankment foundations. For plant‑mixed asphalt courses, mat‑density acceptance procedures verify that the compacted mixture reaches its maximum dry density so that the mixture will not move under the compaction equipment and will satisfy the percent‑of‑theoretical‑maximum‑density limits set for each lot. If the calculated density falls below the required density payment factor of ≥ 100 % for two consecutive lots or three total lots, paving must be stopped and the cause investigated before work can resume. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 150–170), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 276–280)

Scope and applicability

Maximum Dry Density is applicable to the subgrade soil material that forms the roadbed in any pavement reconstruction project, including rubblized projects, where it governs the compaction process used to achieve a stable base [1]. The required maximum dry density and optimum moisture content are obtained from laboratory moisture‑density testing identified as PTM No. 106 on representative samples taken from cut, fill or at‑grade locations, and these values are used together with CBR results to confirm that the subgrade can be compacted to the required level [1]. Well‑graded and coarse‑grained soils are expected to reach higher maximum dry densities, whereas poorly graded or fine‑grained soils will achieve lower values, influencing the selection of compaction effort and any needed subgrade improvement [1]. For embankment and fill placement, Maximum Dry Density is applied when placing and compacting soil and Type 1 granular material (Section 206—Placement and Compaction of Embankment and Fill) [2]. The contractor must first determine the optimum moisture content and maximum dry density according to PTM No. 106, then compact each lift to at least 97 % of that density, with the upper three feet required to reach 100 % of the maximum dry density [2]. Compaction of soil is performed with a pad‑foot roller and compaction of Type 1 granular material with a smooth‑drum vibratory roller, using uniform horizontal lifts not exceeding six inches and maintaining moisture within –3 % of optimum for soil or ±2 % of optimum for Type 1 material [2]. For embankment foundations, existing embankments deeper than three feet below the proposed subgrade elevation, structure foundations, subgrades, and any material within three feet of the proposed subgrade elevation (Section 208—Special Rolling), Maximum Dry Density is used as the target density, with compaction to at least 97 % for embankment foundations or existing embankments greater than three feet below, and to 100 % for structure foundations, subgrades, or material within three feet of the proposed subgrade elevation [2]. Fine‑grained or cohesive soils are compacted with a sheepsfoot, pad foot, or pneumatic roller operated in static mode, while granular materials are compacted with a smooth steel drum vibratory roller; moisture content must be between minus 3 % of optimum moisture content and optimum moisture content, and if the moisture exceeds optimum it must be reduced by disking, scarifying, or other means before compaction [2].

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 150–170)

Section 206.3(a) states that maximum dry density is a required compaction target for soil (Section 206.3(b)1.a) and for Type 1 granular material (Section 206.3(b)1.b); in both cases the in‑place dry density must be at least 97 % of the maximum dry density, except in the upper three feet where it must reach 100 %, with moisture kept within –3 % of optimum for soil or ±2 % of optimum for Type 1, and a quality‑control plan that includes moisture‑density testing is mandatory. For Type 2 granular material, rock, shale, random material and the other categories listed in Sections 206.3(b)1.c‑f, compaction acceptance is based on a non‑movement condition rather than on maximum dry density, so the maximum dry density requirement does not apply; these materials must simply be compacted to a stable condition with no rutting, displacement or shear wave, and Type 2 material must be surface damp at placement. In confined areas where the material size exceeds 2 inches, larger‑size rock, shale or random material is prohibited, further limiting the applicability of maximum dry density criteria. Section 208.3 requires that material used for embankment foundations or existing embankments more than 3 feet below the proposed subgrade elevation be compacted to at least 97 % of maximum dry density, while material for structure foundations, subgrades, or any layer within 3 feet of the proposed subgrade elevation must be compacted to 100 % of maximum dry density; in all cases the moisture content must lie between minus 3 % of optimum moisture content and optimum moisture content, and any material that is above optimum must be dried before compaction. When moisture‑density test data are not available a test‑strip approach may be used, but special rolling cannot substitute for these compaction procedures, vibratory drum rollers shall not be used on fine‑grained soils, and special rolling is prohibited on newly constructed subgrades, making reliance on maximum dry density in those situations inappropriate. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 150–170)

Test methods and procedures

The Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10) states that Maximum Dry Density is determined by conducting the Moisture Density Test identified as PTM No. 106, which provides the optimum water content for a given compactive effort [1]. The Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification likewise requires that the maximum dry density for soils and granular materials be established in the laboratory by performing the Moisture Density Test designated as PTM No. 106 to obtain the optimum moisture content and corresponding maximum dry density [2]. Once these target values are known, field verification is performed with an approved nuclear density/moisture gauge calibrated in accordance with AASHTO T310 (or, for Troxler gauges, per PTM No. 418); the calibration must be done every twelve months and recorded on Form TR‑4276AC [2]. In‑place moisture and density are then measured by applying PTM No. 402 using the direct transmission method, or alternatively by using an approved electrical‑impedance gauge in accordance with PTM No. 403; the gauge operator must hold a current license and provide an ASTM D2950 calibration certificate together with training records [2]. The approved nuclear gauges include Troxler Electronics Model 3411B or 4640B, Campbell Pacific Nuclear Model MC‑2, Seaman Nuclear Model MC‑2, TransTech Systems PQITM Model 300 or 301, and Troxler Electronic Laboratories PaveTracker™; each must be calibrated annually in accordance with ASTM D2950 [2]. For fine‑grained or cohesive soils the compaction equipment specified includes a sheepsfoot or pad foot roller having a minimum contact pressure of 160 psi, or a pneumatic‑tire roller with a load between 26 and 28 tons; these rollers are operated in static mode at a maximum travel speed of two miles per hour [2]. Granular materials are compacted with a smooth steel‑drum vibratory roller that must develop a minimum dynamic force of 50,000 lb and also may not exceed two miles per hour [2]. The material shall be compacted to at least 97 % of the maximum dry density for embankment foundations or existing embankments more than three feet below the proposed subgrade elevation, and to 100 % of the maximum dry density for structure foundations, subgrades, or any material within three feet of the proposed subgrade elevation; for soil lifts compaction shall achieve at least 97 % of the maximum dry density except in the top three feet where 100 % is required [2]. Moisture content must be maintained between minus three percent of the optimum moisture content and the optimum level for fine‑grained soils, while Type 1 granular material requires moisture to be kept within plus or minus two percent of optimum; if moisture exceeds optimum it must be reduced by disking, scarifying, or other approved means before compaction [2]. After establishing the target density, the field crew together with the Representative and a certified asphalt field technician establishes an optimum‑rolling pattern that is documented on Form TR‑4276B or C, and the course is compacted accordingly; the pattern may be revised if subsequent density checks indicate that the target has not been met [2]. Within one day of placement a six‑inch core is taken from each sublot, labeled, and shipped to Laboratory Test Services where PTM No. 715 (and PTM No. 716 when required) are used to determine bulk specific gravity and bulk density; the bulk specific gravity is verified by AASHTO T209 as modified in Bulletin 27, and acceptance is based on the calculated percent of theoretical maximum density according to the specified forms and thresholds [2]. Acceptance testing follows the frequencies set out in Table B; any failure triggers immediate reshaping and recompaction with a retest, and if the retest still fails the gauge’s standard count must be checked per PTM No. 418 or AASHTO T310 and the moisture‑density curve may be re‑tested with PTM No. 106 to establish new target values [2]. Verification testing by an independent gauge is required on one of the first three acceptance tests and thereafter at least every ten acceptance tests, and the independent readings must be within plus or minus 2.0 lb/ft³ for wet density and within plus or minus 0.8w for moisture when compared with the contractor’s gauge [2]. When in‑place moisture‑density data are not yet available a test‑strip approach using AASHTO T191 or AASHTO T310 is performed after each pass, and special rolling may proceed only after three consecutive passes show an increase of less than one percent in measured moisture density and the material appears stable [2].

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 141–143); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 150–170), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 276–280)

For Section 206—Placement and Compaction of Embankment and Fill, calibration of the nuclear density/moisture gauge is required annually; gauges must be calibrated every 12 months and a sheet showing the current annual calibration with projected monthly decay limits must accompany each gauge, and daily standardization is recorded on Form TR‑4276AC. The testing technician must be a properly trained Nuclear Equipment Operator who carries a valid nuclear gauge operator certification while performing QC and acceptance tests. Density and moisture measurements must be made at least 30 feet from any other radioactive source and in an area where influences affecting gauge performance are minimized; if the standard count on the reference block does not meet the calibration limits, the gauge must be diagnosed, repaired or replaced before work may continue. For Section 413—Superpave Mixture Design, Standard and RPS Construction of Plant‑Mixed Asphalt Courses with Percent Within Limits and LTS Testing (Mat Density Acceptance), valid mat density measurements for Maximum Dry Density require an approved nuclear or electrical impedance gauge that has a current annual calibration certificate meeting ASTM D2950, and the gauge must be operated only by a licensed nuclear gauge operator who has documented training; any gauge that is damaged or repaired must be recalibrated before use. No additional environmental controls are stipulated in this section. [2]

Sampling and testing frequency

Maximum dry density serves as the benchmark that dictates where and how often moisture‑density testing is performed. In embankments and fills (Section 206), soil and Type 1 granular material must be compacted to not less than 97 % of the maximum dry density, except for the top three feet which must reach 100 %. These compaction targets require that any location failing to meet the specified percentage be reworked, reshaped, recompacted and retested, thereby determining the sampling locations. The contractor’s quality‑control plan must contain at least one start‑up frequency cycle before shifting to production testing; the minimum testing frequency is prescribed by Table A, while acceptance tests are carried out at the frequencies listed in Table B at Representative‑directed locations, and QC tests may not be placed at the same spot as an acceptance test. After a passing acceptance result, the Representative conducts verification testing on one of the first three acceptance tests and thereafter on at least one verification test for every ten acceptance tests.

For plant‑mixed asphalt courses (Section 413), the Inspector selects different sample locations in each sublot according to PTM No. 1, PTM No. 729 and PTM No. 746 and drills a 6‑inch diameter core as soon as possible but no later than the day following placement; only one pavement core per sublot may be obtained for quality‑control purposes unless the Representative permits additional cores. The core is tested for bulk specific gravity and density to assess whether the achieved dry density meets the theoretical maximum. When a non‑movement or optimum‑rolling pattern acceptance procedure is used, density may be verified on‑site with an approved nuclear or electrical impedance gauge after establishing the optimum‑rolling pattern in the presence of the Representative and the certified field technician.

When moisture‑density test data are unavailable (Section 208), verification of in‑place density is required by employing a test‑strip method. After each compaction pass the crew checks moisture and density at several pre‑determined random locations in accordance with AASHTO T 191 or T 310, sampling multiple points on each pass, and continues this routine until three consecutive passes show less than a 1 % increase in measured density; at that point the material is considered stable and special rolling may proceed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 150–170), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 276–280)

For materials placed and compacted under Sections 206.3(b)1.a and 1.b the contractor must prepare a compaction‑control QC Plan that defines the sampling frequency, which may be set by the Contractor but must not be less than the minimum shown in Table A; at least one start‑up frequency cycle is required before shifting to production frequencies. QC measurements must be taken at locations different from acceptance test points because QC tests cannot be performed at the same location as an acceptance test nor replace an acceptance test, and acceptance testing is carried out at the frequencies directed in Table B. All moisture‑density readings are obtained with a calibrated nuclear gauge per PTM No. 402, with daily standardization recorded, and any failed sample triggers rework of the defined area followed by retesting. To represent the compacted mix for maximum dry density, the Inspector selects distinct core locations in each sublot according to PTM No. 1, PTM No. 729 and PTM No. 746, then drills a 6‑inch diameter pavement core no later than the day after placement; only one core per sublot is taken unless the Representative permits more, and any damaged core must be replaced from within 12 inches of the original spot. The cores are identified with project, lot, sublot, station, offset, date, mixture type and classified as acceptance samples (Sample Class AS) for bulk specific gravity and density testing. For verification of theoretical maximum specific gravity, the LTS randomly selects one loose‑mix sample from the lot according to PTM No. 1, and if its result differs from the contractor’s daily Gmm by more than ± 0.030, the laboratory value is used for calculating percent of theoretical density. When moisture‑density test data are not available, the contractor must use a test‑strip approach; after each pass of the compaction equipment the in‑place moisture and density are measured at several pre‑determined random locations using AASHTO T 191 or AASHTO T 310. The measurements are considered representative when the moisture‑density values do not increase by more than 1 % over three consecutive passes and the material appears stable, allowing special rolling to proceed. All measurements must be taken while the material moisture content is between minus 3 % of optimum moisture content and optimum moisture content; if the material exceeds optimum it must be dried before further compaction. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 150–170), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 276–280)

Acceptance criteria and tolerances

In the embankment and fill placement requirements of Section 206, compaction shall achieve not less than 97 % of the maximum dry density for all layers except the top three feet, which shall be compacted to 100 % of the maximum dry density and kept within –3 % of optimum moisture content; for Type 1 granular material the same density percentages apply and moisture shall be maintained within ±2 % of optimum moisture content, while Type 2 granular material is required only to be in a surface‑damp condition but still compacted to a non‑movement condition. Acceptance testing uses the direct transmission method with a calibrated nuclear gauge, and any failure to meet these density or moisture limits triggers rework, reshaping, recompaction and retesting; verification of each acceptance test shall show wet‑density readings within ±2.0 lb/ft³ and moisture readings within ±0.8w, where w is the percent moisture content, otherwise gauge calibration checks and corrective actions are required. Section 208 specifies that material in embankment foundations or existing embankments more than three feet below the proposed subgrade elevation shall be compacted to 97 % of maximum dry density, whereas structure foundations, subgrades, or material within three feet of the proposed subgrade elevation shall be compacted to 100 % of maximum dry density; in all cases the in‑place moisture shall be between minus 3 % of optimum moisture content and optimum moisture content, and any material above optimum moisture content shall be dried before compaction. When moisture‑density test data are not available, acceptance is based on a test‑strip approach in which the measured moisture density must not increase by more than 1 % over three consecutive passes of the compaction equipment and the material must appear stable. For mat density acceptance under Section 413, courses that are less than one inch deep or weigh ≤ 110 lb/yd² may be accepted by non‑movement only if the mixture does not move under the rollers; when an optimum‑rolling pattern is used, the density measured with an approved gauge shall agree with the laboratory Gmm within ±0.030, and if the difference exceeds that amount the laboratory value becomes the acceptance Gmm. Pavement cores taken no later than one day after placement are used to calculate percent of theoretical maximum density rounded to the nearest tenth of a percent, and any core result outside the lower and upper specification limits in Table I shall be subjected to an extreme‑value analysis at the 5 % significance level; paving shall be stopped if two consecutive lots or three total lots fail to meet a density payment factor of ≥ 100. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 150–170), SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 276–280)

The specification evaluates maximum dry density in three distinct ways. For individual in‑place tests, Section 206.3(a) requires that compacted soil and Type 1 granular material achieve not less than 97 % of the maximum dry density in every lift except the upper three feet, which must reach 100 % of the maximum dry density; each acceptance test is compared directly to those limits and any failure obligates immediate rework, reshaping, recompaction and retest. Section 208 reinforces this by stating that an individual result must meet 97 % of the maximum dry density for embankment foundations or existing embankments more than three feet below the proposed subgrade elevation and 100 % for structure foundations, subgrades or material within three feet of the proposed subgrade elevation; additionally the measured moisture‑density may not increase by more than 1 % over three consecutive compaction passes before special rolling can proceed. For pavement cores, Section 413.3(j) converts each core density to a percent of theoretical maximum density, rounds it to the nearest tenth of a percent and checks it against the lower and upper specification limits; if a result falls outside those limits an extreme‑value analysis is performed at the 5 % significance level and the value may be discarded provided that at least three test results remain for the lot. Lot‑average density is addressed in Section 413.3(j) where the rounded sublot percentages are summed, rounded again to the nearest tenth of a percent, and compared with the acceptance criteria set out in Section 413.4(a)4 or (b). A minimum of three valid cores is required for a lot; if two consecutive lots or three total fail to meet the density payment‑factor percentage of ≥ 100, paving must be stopped until corrective action is approved. Variability is monitored without reliance on averages in the embankment and fill provisions. Section 206 mandates verification testing on one of the first three acceptance tests and at least every tenth thereafter; the independent nuclear density/moisture gauge reading must be within ±2.0 pcf wet density and within ±0.8 w moisture of the contractor’s gauge, providing a check on variability. The requirement in Section 208 that moisture‑density not increase by more than 1 % over three passes also serves to limit variability during compaction. [2]

Quality control and process monitoring

The laboratory and field program should verify that the soil classification, gradation curve, percent passing the #200 sieve, Atterberg limits, optimum moisture content, and the achieved maximum dry density are all within the ranges expected for the material to keep Maximum Dry Density consistent during production and construction [1]. Because well‑graded, coarse‑grained soils generally yield higher densities than poorly graded or fine‑grained soils, these parameters must be tracked to ensure the compactive effort is applied at the correct moisture condition [1]. During production and construction the moisture content shall be kept between minus 3 percent of the optimum moisture content and the optimum value for soils, and Type 1 granular material shall be maintained within plus or minus 2 percent of its optimum moisture at the time of compaction [2]. Density shall be compacted to not less than 97 percent of the maximum dry density for all layers except the top three feet where 100 percent of the maximum dry density shall be achieved [2]. In‑place moisture and density shall be determined with a nuclear gauge calibrated every twelve months in accordance with PTM No. 402, and verification is acceptable when independent gauge readings are within ±2.0 lb/ft³ for wet density and within ±0.8w for moisture compared with the contractor’s gauge [2]. Moisture‑density measurements shall follow AASHTO T 191 or T 310 after each equipment pass, and compaction may continue only until the measured moisture‑density increase is no more than 1 percent over three consecutive passes and the material shows stability [2]. Persistent excess moisture or insufficient density shall trigger drying or additional recompaction, and stability shall also be judged by permanent deflection limits—greater than one‑half inch for subgrade or greater than one inch for embankment foundations indicates unsatisfactory conditions that require stabilization [2]. Core samples taken within one day of placement shall be used to determine bulk specific gravity and bulk density, which together with the contractor’s daily theoretical maximum specific gravity provide the percent of theoretical maximum density that must meet or exceed the department‑specified acceptance level; paving shall be stopped if cores are not obtained within twenty‑four hours or if the density payment factor percentage is less than ≥ 100 for two consecutive lots or three total lots [2].

Nonconformance and corrective action

When acceptance testing shows that the in‑place dry density does not meet the specified maximum dry density, the contractor must promptly rework, reshape and recompact (or remove and replace) the unsatisfactory area and obtain a passing acceptance retest before proceeding to the next location. If the retest still fails, the gauge standard count is checked; any gauge that does not meet its annual calibration or daily standard must be diagnosed, repaired or replaced prior to further work. Should the gauge be satisfactory yet density remain out of limits, a new moisture‑density test per PTM No. 106 is performed to see whether the material’s moisture‑density relationship has changed, and if so new target values are established for all subsequent testing. If the original targets remain valid, the contractor must review operations, adjust procedures within the specification, and amend the QC Plan to achieve acceptable results. When inspection or testing shows that the target density has not been met, the contractor must develop a new optimum‑rolling pattern as directed and the Representative may suspend paving until the pattern is followed. If density results for two consecutive lots or for a total of three lots fail to reach the required density payment factor percentage of ≥ 100, or if cores are not taken within one day, paving must be stopped, the operation reviewed to determine the cause, and a corrective plan submitted; paving may only resume after the Representative reviews and authorizes the proposed solution. When testing shows that the compacted soil does not meet the required maximum dry density or moisture limits, the work must be re‑compacted to the specified percentages—97 % of maximum dry density for embankment foundations or existing embankments more than 3 ft below the proposed subgrade and 100 % for structure foundations, subgrades, or material within 3 ft of the proposed subgrade—and the moisture content must be brought between minus 3 % of optimum and optimum; any material above optimum moisture must be dried by disking, scarifying, or other means before compaction. If a test‑strip approach shows that in‑place density does not increase by more than 1 % over three consecutive passes, the material is deemed too wet and must be dried and recompacted prior to special rolling. Should the Representative deem the subgrade stability unsatisfactory—permanent deflection greater than ½ inch for existing subgrade or greater than 1 inch for embankment foundations—the subgrade must be stabilized either by additional compaction or by undercutting, excavating and replacing the unsuitable material according to the prescribed undercut depths and geotextile placement. [2]

Documentation and reporting

For Maximum Dry Density documentation, the Pavement Policy Manual requires inclusion of Moisture Density Test results (PTM No. 106) showing max dry density and optimum moisture content, together with soil classification per AASHTO M 145, gradation data, percent passing #200 sieve, Atterberg limits and Group Index for each sample [1]. Sampling information must record that three 50‑pound bags of representative material were submitted from cut, fill or at‑grade locations to capture site variability [1]. All laboratory results must be certified by the PennDOT BOCM MD Laboratory Testing Section, and when CBR values exceed 10 or fall below 5 they must be reviewed and approved by the District Geotechnical Engineer and the Chief Geotechnical Engineer; corrective action requires verification that the CBR test results are supported by these companion laboratory tests and, if they lie outside typical ranges, further evaluation or subgrade improvement shall be undertaken [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania specifications add that the primary inspection record for maximum dry density is the compaction control QC Plan documented on Form CS‑206 together with daily gauge standardization sheets [2]. In‑place moisture and density measurements performed with a calibrated nuclear gauge in accordance with AASHTO T 310 or PTM No. 418 are entered on Form TR‑4276AC, which also records the optimum moisture content and target dry density derived from PTM No. 106 and specifies required compaction levels of at least 97 % of maximum dry density, rising to 100 % for the top three feet [2]. Acceptance test results recorded at the frequencies shown in Table B are compared with these targets; any failure triggers corrective actions such as reshaping, recompacting, or retesting and a retest log together with a new moisture‑density curve is documented [2]. Verification testing performed by the Representative using an independent gauge is logged on Form TR‑4276AD and must fall within ±2.0 lb/ft³ wet density and ±0.8 % moisture; discrepancies require checking the gauge standard count and repairing or replacing the gauge before work resumes [2]. The nuclear equipment operator’s valid certification and the annual calibration certificate for each gauge are retained as part of the record, with the calibration certificate required to conform to ASTM D2950 [2]. For mat‑density acceptance the documentation must include the optimum‑rolling pattern forms (TR‑4276B or TR‑4276C) signed by the Representative, a copy of the nuclear gauge annual calibration certificate together with the operator’s training record, and detailed identification of each density‑acceptance core—including project number, lot and sublot, station and offset, placement date and mixture type [2]. Test results from the Laboratory Testing Services for bulk specific gravity (Gmb) and bulk density of each core, as well as daily theoretical maximum specific gravity values used to calculate percent of theoretical density, are recorded; any deviation beyond ±0.030 triggers use of the LTS Gmm value and a new rolling pattern, and if the target density is not achieved the Inspector shall establish a revised optimum‑rolling pattern and paving may be suspended [2]. If density for two consecutive lots or three total lots falls below the density payment factor percentage of ≥ 100, or if sample security is compromised, paving must be stopped, replacement cores obtained, and a written investigation submitted before work can resume [2]. When special rolling is performed, inspection records must note the roller type and load and that the Representative was present to approve or disapprove stability [2]. Test results shall also include in‑place moisture‑density measurements taken at random locations after each pass according to AASHTO T 191 or T 310, recorded as a percentage of maximum dry density—97 % for embankment foundations deeper than three feet and 100 % for structure foundations, subgrades or material within three feet—and moisture content kept between minus 3 % of optimum moisture content and the optimum [2]. Acceptance criteria require that the measured moisture‑density does not increase by more than 1 % over three consecutive passes before special rolling proceeds; permanent deflection greater than one‑half inch for subgrade or greater than one inch for embankments triggers corrective action, which may include drying material above optimum moisture content, additional compaction, undercutting or excavating to the specified depth of one foot up to three feet, and placement of the required geotextile classes and backfill as directed [2].

Relationship to long-term performance

The specification ties the measured density to durability by requiring compacting embankment foundations or existing embankments more than 3 feet below the proposed subgrade elevation to 97% of maximum dry density and compacting structure foundations, subgrades, or material within 3 feet of the proposed subgrade elevation to 100% of maximum dry density while keeping moisture between minus 3% of optimum moisture content and optimum moisture content; when these densities are achieved the subgrade is considered stable, whereas instability is defined by permanent deflection or rutting greater than 1/2 inch for existing subgrades or greater than 1 inch for embankments, which would trigger corrective actions. Consequently, meeting the 97%/100% density criteria indicates that the pavement should resist excessive settlement and rutting, supporting the expected service life. [2]
