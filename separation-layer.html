Separation Layer

Part of: Unbonded Concrete Overlay is-subtopic-of
Separation Layer is a topic under Unbonded Concrete Overlay.
separation-layerasphalt-separation-layerlayer-separationgeotextile-interlayer
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-29

The separation layer is a thin interlayer—either a fine‑graded asphalt film at least one inch thick or a Class 4 Type A (or C) non‑woven geotextile—installed between pavement courses to prevent mixing of adjacent layers and preserve each material’s function[1][2]. By creating a debonding interface it isolates the new overlay from an existing slab, controls water movement and erosion, and allows the overlay to act as an independent structural element even when underlying concrete exhibits faulting or instability[1]. The layer must extend across the full lane width (minimum eight feet), be placed on a clean, prepared surface with prescribed overlaps and elevation tolerances, and be protected from excessive temperature, traffic loading, and damage to ensure proper drainage and long‑term durability[1][2].

Purpose and design objectives

The separation layer is installed between pavement courses to prevent mixing of adjacent layers, thereby preserving each course’s function and managing water movement and erosion within the structure (section 2.9) [1]. In unbonded concrete overlays a fine‑graded asphalt separation layer with a minimum thickness of 1 inch is placed between the existing concrete pavement and the new overlay so that the two slabs do not bond, addressing the need to restore structural capacity on moderately to significantly deteriorated pavements while avoiding bond‑related problems such as differential movement or reflective cracking (section 4.9) [1]. The layer is also required when joint faulting exceeds ¼ inch (or more than ½ inch for additional treatment), allowing the new slab to move independently of the existing pavement, providing uniform support and proper drainage to address faulting of unstable, visibly rocking slabs (section 10.7) [1]. For unbonded concrete overlays the separation layer creates a debonding interface so that unstable or moving slab fragments, which would otherwise cause non‑uniform support of the overlay, do not transmit forces to the new pavement (section 548.3) [2]. Placement of a geotextile (or asphalt) interlayer after repairing and cleaning the base also preserves free drainage through the layer (section 548.3) [2]. When dissimilar materials such as subgrade and subbase are present, a Class 4‑Type A geotextile specified as the separation layer provides a barrier that keeps each material’s integrity and functionality intact or improved, and it is required in all subgrade‑strengthening treatments to maintain structural performance (section 6.2) [1].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 62–63), Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 10 (p. 185); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462)

The separation layer required for an unbonded concrete overlay rehabilitation shall be a fine‑graded asphalt material that is at least 1 inch thick or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile placed on the existing concrete pavement so that the new overlay can function as an independent structural element and restore capacity without relying on bond with the underlying slab. The layer shall act as a fabric that separates two dissimilar materials, preserving or improving the integrity and functionality of each material, and it must also provide lateral restraint along the base‑subgrade interface to reduce load stresses on the subgrade. For bridge‑approach applications the separation layer must be installed as a continuous Class 4, Type A sheet that fully covers the prepared subgrade and lay‑back slope, is laid flat, pulled tight and anchored within ±½ inch of the required elevation, thereby maintaining the required vertical spacing between layers. Splicing of any geosynthetic is prohibited; adjacent rolls shall overlap a minimum of 12 inches while edges may not overlap within four feet of a wrapped face, ensuring structural continuity and preventing intermixing of materials. The material shall be protected from temperatures greater than 140°F, direct sunlight and debris during delivery, storage and handling, and any geosynthetic showing tears, punctures, flaws or other damage must be rejected and replaced at no additional cost, addressing durability objectives. No vehicles are allowed on the exposed geosynthetic until a minimum of 6 inches of loose backfill has been placed, and sudden braking or sharp turning of equipment on stabilized backfill is prohibited, satisfying safety requirements during construction. The separation layer shall be placed across the entire lane width—at least eight feet—for all completed subgrades as stipulated in Publication 408 Section 210.3(b), and it must maintain a positive slope for drainage, thereby contributing to long‑term serviceability and durability of the pavement structure. When a geotextile interlayer is used, joints with greater than 1/4‑inch faulting shall be milled; if faulting exceeds 1/2 inch a Superpave Wearing Course or milling/grinding of the joint shall be required, ensuring durability by preventing water accumulation and maintaining serviceability through control of joint faulting. The interlayer must extend through any reconstructed shoulder and be tied to the mainline, providing continuous shoulder integration that supports safety and structural performance. For general geotextile applications the layer shall be placed on a prepared surface free of puncture hazards, rolled out loosely without wrinkles, overlapped at least six inches on roll ends and edges, and must be covered with backfill or subbase within two weeks to prevent exposure, thereby supporting serviceability through proper compaction and drainage. Pins or other acceptable means may be used to secure the fabric with spacing not exceeding six feet where required, and when rocks larger than one foot are placed they shall rest on a minimum six‑inch bedding layer to avoid damage to the fabric, further enhancing durability. In unbonded concrete overlay over a concrete‑surfaced pavement the separation layer shall act as a debonding and drainage medium by being placed flat on the repaired surface, overlapped 8 inches ± 2 inches, limited to no more than three layers, and extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge so that it terminates in a drainable layer without impairing free drainage; it shall be kept clean, damp but not saturated, secured per the manufacturer’s instructions, protected from construction traffic, sharp turns and braking, and its surface temperature shall never exceed 120°F before concrete placement, collectively ensuring functional separation, structural continuity, durability against temperature and mechanical damage, safety during construction, and long‑term serviceability of the pavement system. Scope and applicability

In PennDOT experimental pavement construction (section 2.9) a separation layer is appropriate when a geotextile is employed for drainage, layer separation, erosion control, sediment control, or to reinforce soft subgrade soil and aggregate layers; these uses are treated as standard practice, while use of a geotextile to add structural reinforcement beyond those functions must be classified as an experimental construction item and a project‑specific design shall be prepared using Publication 408, FHWA’s Geosynthetic Design and Construction Guidelines, NTPEP data, and the manufacturer’s product information [1]. For unbonded concrete overlays that are used to restore structural capacity of moderately to significantly deteriorated pavements (section 4.9) a separation layer between the existing concrete slab and the new overlay is required and may be provided by placing a fine‑graded asphalt layer at least 1 inch thick, or alternatively by using a non‑woven geotextile interlayer so that the overlay functions as a new pavement on the stable existing platform [1]. Where subgrade‑strengthening treatments such as cement or lime stabilization or rock capping are applied (section 6.2) a separation layer supplied by a Class 4 Type A geotextile is required and must be placed between the treated subgrade and any overlying aggregate material; it also applies to all completed subgrades that span an entire lane width of eight feet or greater, and a Class 4 Type C geotextile may serve as a separation‑and‑stabilization layer on subgrades whose CBR value is less than three, thereby supporting full‑depth flexible pavement designs and any patching work longer than twenty feet where the subbase is removed for drainage or other concerns [1]. For unbonded Portland cement concrete (PCC) overlays of existing PCC pavements (section 10.7) a separation layer should be considered when the existing pavement cannot be cost‑effectively repaired but can serve as a relatively uniform subbase and when concrete joints exhibit faulting greater than 1/4 inch; in those situations the separation of the concrete layers can be accomplished with a geotextile interlayer or asphalt concrete, and when a geotextile interlayer is specified a nominal depth of 1 inch of a Superpave Wearing Course, 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix is the standard thickness, while if joint faulting exceeds 1/2 inch a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch), 9.5 mm mix should be placed at the joint or the joints milled/grinded to reduce faulting; the interlayer must have proper drainage by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain, and it must extend through any reconstructed shoulder and be tied to the mainline [1]. In Geosynthetic Stabilized Bridge Approach (GSBA) projects, whether for new bridge construction or retrofitting an existing bridge, the separation layer—specified as a Class 4, Type A geosynthetic—is applied after the foundation subgrade has been prepared, proof‑rolled and cleared of deleterious soils; it may be placed over subgrades composed of AASHTO No. 1 or No. 3 coarse aggregate, R‑rock or shot rock fill, and is installed to cover the entire prepared subgrade and excavated layback slope as shown on the standard drawings [2]. In general pavement projects that require a geotextile to prevent mixing of underlying and overlying aggregates, the Class 4—Layer Separation (Type A) geotextile defined in Section 212 is appropriate; it must be placed on a prepared surface free of objects that could puncture it, rolled out loose and unstretched without folds, overlapped at roll ends and edges by at least 6 inches, and covered with subbase or other required cover material within two weeks, with steel pins permissible to secure the fabric against wind lift and the cover material compacted as specified in Section 206.3 [2]. For unbonded concrete overlays of existing concrete‑surfaced pavements where the existing joints exhibit faulting that exceeds the thresholds for each interlayer type, a separation layer is prescribed; a geotextile interlayer is used when mill joints have greater than 1/4 inch faulting, while an asphalt interlayer is required if joint faulting exceeds 1/2 inch and a scratch course or milling is applied to reduce the faulting. Prior to placing either interlayer, all surface defects larger than 4 square inches and 1 inch deep must be filled with an approved repair material, the existing pavement surface must be swept clean, and when a geotextile is used it must be laid flat with edges overlapped 8 inches ± 2 inches, limited to three layers, and extended at least 4 inches beyond the pavement edge; the separation layer may also be placed as a debonding layer in transition zones where treated base course or concrete lies directly beneath the overlay [2].

In the Pavement Policy Manual (section 10.7 UNBONDED PCC OVERLAY OF PCC PAVEMENT), when a project calls for an unbonded concrete structural overlay, the guidance advises that an asphalt separation layer shall not be used as a leveling course and that all grade corrections, including leveling, should be accomplished with the concrete overlay itself, making the concrete‑only approach preferable. The same manual (section 10.7) states that when geotextiles or geogrids are intended to act as soil reinforcement and add structural capacity they are treated as experimental construction items; because such applications require project‑specific design parameters drawn from Publication 408, FHWA’s Geosynthetic Design and Construction Guidelines, NTPEP data, manufacturer data, and other technical references, a non‑experimental alternative is preferred. Also in the Pavement Policy Manual (section 10.7), if the existing concrete pavement is in relatively good condition, the subgrade is stable, and drainage functions properly, a bonded JPCP overlay that relies on structural bonding is favored over an unbonded system that would require a fine‑graded 1‑inch minimum asphalt separation layer or a non‑woven geotextile interlayer; thus another design approach becomes preferable under those favorable conditions. Conversely, the manual (section 10.7) notes that when there is significant structural deterioration, unstable support, stripping of the asphalt, or unresolved drainage problems, the unbonded approach with a separation layer may be appropriate, implying that in the absence of these adverse conditions an alternative design should be selected. Within the same section (10.7), when the subgrade CBR value is less than three, or when sufficient undercut depth cannot be achieved, the guidance recommends using a dual‑function stabilization geotextile (Class 4 Type C) rather than a simple Class 4 Layer Separation (Type A) because it also provides reinforcement; similarly, if lane width is less than eight feet, the standard Class 4 Layer Separation cannot be applied without a special provision, so an alternative treatment must be considered. The manual further limits geotextile interlayers for unbonded PCC overlays to joints faulting more than ¼‑inch; when joint faulting exceeds ½‑inch the design calls for placing a Superpave Wearing Course mix or milling/grinding the joints, which serves as an alternative to using a geotextile separation layer, and unbonded concrete overlays are only appropriate when the existing pavement can serve as a subbase providing relatively uniform support; otherwise another repair or design method must be used instead of a separated‑layer approach. In Publication 408 (section SECTION 548—UNBONDED CONCRETE OVERLAY OF CONCRETE‑SURFACED PAVEMENTS > 548.3 CONSTRUCTION), a geotextile separation layer is not preferred when the existing pavement contains surface defects larger than four square inches and one inch deep that cannot be patched to a flat, tight surface, when the required overlap of eight inches ± two inches or the limit of no more than three layers cannot be satisfied, when free drainage would be impaired, when construction traffic cannot be avoided, or when the fabric temperature is likely to exceed 120°F. The same specification (section 548.3) states that an asphalt interlayer is unsuitable if shallow defects cannot be compacted together with the interlayer material, requiring repair before placement, or if joint faulting exceeds one‑half inch, which mandates a scratch course or milling instead of the interlayer. Design inputs and requirements

The design of a separation layer for an unbonded PCC overlay requires traffic input in the form of rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors for ESAL calculations, material specifications that the interlayer be a geotextile or asphalt concrete and that a nominal 1 inch Superpave Wearing Course (9.5 mm Fine‑Graded) be placed over milled joints with faulting greater than ¼‑inch (or a Scratch mix if faulting exceeds ½‑inch), existing‑condition data such as joint faulting measurements and the presence of visibly rocking slabs, and environmental/drainage provisions that the interlayer be daylighted or provided with a vertical subdrain and extend through any reconstructed shoulder; the existing pavement must also be capable of serving as a relatively uniform support subbase. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (p. 185)

In PennDOT projects a separation layer provided by a geotextile shall be designed in accordance with Publication 408 (Specifications) and the FHWA Geosynthetic Design and Construction Guidelines, with reference to the National Transportation Product Evaluation Program, the geotextile manufacturer’s published product data, and other appropriate technical references when determining properties, design specifications, product selection, and construction procedures. When a geotextile is used as an experimental item, such as for soil reinforcement beyond its standard drainage or layer‑separation functions, the work must be coordinated through the BOCM Materials Division New Products and Innovations Section, an Experimental Item Work Plan must be prepared and approved before the PS&E package submittal, and approval must be obtained from FHWA on Federal‑oversight projects or from the appropriate Bureau Director on non‑federal projects, with FHWA notification when federal funds are used. For unbonded concrete overlays the design calls for a fine‑graded asphalt separation layer that is at least 1 inch thick placed between the existing concrete pavement and the new overlay, or alternatively a non‑woven geotextile interlayer may be used; this requirement is governed by Publication 408 Section 548. The use of a Separation Layer in pavement structures is governed by the specifications in Publication 408 and the material listings in Publication 35, and a Class 4‑Type A geotextile must be provided as the separation layer for all treatments and is required on every completed subgrade across the full lane width as called out in Publication 408 Section 210.3(b); when the underlying subgrade has a CBR value less than three, a Class 4‑Type C geotextile may be used to provide both separation and reinforcement per the same specifications. Design, product selection and construction procedures for these geotextiles must follow Publication 408 Sections 212 and 735 together with Publication 35 (Bulletin 15). When a geotextile interlayer is employed in an unbonded concrete overlay the existing concrete joints must be milled if faulting exceeds ¼‑inch; the overlying Superpave Wearing Course shall have a nominal depth of 1 inch with a 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix as the standard thickness, and if joint faulting exceeds ½‑inch a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix must be placed at the joint or the joints milled/grinded to reduce faulting. Proper drainage of the interlayer shall be achieved by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain, and the interlayer must extend through any shoulder reconstruction and be tied to the mainline. The material‑submittal requirements for a geosynthetic separation layer are set out in Section 106.03(b)3, which call for electronic submission of manufacturer data and approval at least thirty days before construction. Foundation preparation must follow the rules in Section 206.3, requiring proof‑rolling to achieve a uniform, firm surface and repair of any unstable areas prior to placement of the geotextile. Placement of a Class 4, Type A separation geosynthetic over the entire prepared subgrade and excavated layback slope must provide a minimum twelve‑inch overlap on parallel roll edges, prohibit splicing of any geosynthetic, maintain vertical spacing between layers, and be positioned within ±½‑inch of the elevations indicated in the plans; vehicle traffic is prohibited until a minimum of six inches of loose backfill is placed. The surface to receive the fabric must be prepared and any objects that could puncture the geotextile removed, the fabric rolled out in a loose, unstretched condition without folds or wrinkles, and roll‑ends and edges overlapped at least six inches with adjacent material; the fabric must then be covered with subbase or other required cover material within two weeks of placement, the cover placed by back dumping then spread ahead of all equipment, and compacted in accordance with Section 206.3, with steel pins permitted as specified in Section 212.2 to prevent wind lift. For a geotextile interlayer in an unbonded concrete overlay the fabric must be laid flat on a repaired and cleaned existing pavement surface, overlapped eight inches ± two inches, limited to no more than three layers in any location, extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, terminated in a drainable or daylighted condition without impairing drainage, secured according to the manufacturer’s instructions, kept clean and damp but not saturated, protected from construction traffic, and the surface temperature of the fabric must not exceed 120 °F before overlay placement. When an asphalt interlayer is specified, the specification requires a nominal depth of one inch of the Superpave mix design referenced in Section 410.3; joint faulting greater than ½‑inch must be corrected by a scratch course or milling, placement must use a rubber‑tire roller, and the surface temperature of the pavement must not exceed 120 °F before overlay placement, with temperature control methods approved by the Representative. Design methods and criteria

The design of a Separation Layer that employs geotextiles begins with establishing project‑specific geotextile design parameters and then consulting Publication 408, the FHWA Geosynthetic Design and Construction Guidelines, NTPEP, the manufacturer’s published product data, and other appropriate technical references to define material properties, design specifications, product selection, and construction procedures. For unbonded concrete overlays the separation function may be provided either by placing a fine‑graded 1‑inch minimum asphalt separation layer on the existing concrete pavement or, as an alternative, by installing a non‑woven geotextile interlayer in accordance with Publication 408 Section 548. The required geotextile is identified as a Class 4—Layer Separation (Type A) fabric that must extend across the entire lane width, which shall be eight feet or greater according to Publication 408 Section 210.3(b); any road‑widening less than eight feet triggers a special provision, and when the subbase is removed for drainage or other reasons the geotextile must also be placed in patching sections longer than 20 feet and at least eight feet wide as stipulated by Publication 72M RC‑26M. The overlay thickness design follows the Unbonded Concrete Overlay module, using rigid‑pavement Average Initial Truck Factors from Table 7.1 to compute equivalent single axle loads (ESALs), then referencing the future‑traffic pavement thickness in Table 10.8 and the existing effective pavement thickness in Table 10.9 so that the required overlay thickness can be derived and checked against the minimum values in Table 10.10. When a geotextile interlayer is specified, joints exhibiting faulting greater than ¼‑inch are milled before placement; a standard 1‑inch Superpave Wearing Course of 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix serves as the nominal thickness, and if joint faulting exceeds ½‑inch a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) of the same 9.5 mm mix is placed at the joint or the joints are milled/grinded to reduce faulting, with proper drainage achieved by daylighting the interlayer or installing a vertical subdrain. In a Geosynthetic Stabilized Bridge Approach the separation layer is installed by first laying a Class 4, Type A geosynthetic sheet over the entire prepared subgrade and excavated lay‑back slope, pulling it tight, anchoring it until backfill placement, and maintaining its elevation within ±½‑inch of the required levels; splicing is prohibited, adjacent rolls must overlap a minimum of 12 inches along parallel edges while no overlapping is permitted within four feet of a wrapped edge, continuous lengths are preferably run perpendicular to the roadway or extend at least four feet from a wrapped face before any overlap, and vehicular traffic may not travel on the geosynthetic until a minimum of six inches of loose backfill has been placed. For a generic separation layer the contractor must prepare the subgrade and remove any objects that could puncture the geotextile, then roll out the fabric in a loose, unstretched condition without folds or wrinkles; adjacent rolls are overlapped a minimum of six inches at ends and edges, steel pins may be used to secure the fabric before cover placement, cover material is placed by back‑dumping and spread ahead of all equipment, compacted in accordance with Section 206.3, and the geotextile must not remain exposed for more than two weeks before being covered with subbase or other required cover material. In an unbonded concrete overlay the existing pavement is repaired and swept clean prior to installing a separation layer; for a geotextile interlayer joints faulting more than ¼‑inch are milled, the fabric is laid flat on the cleaned surface with edges overlapped by eight inches ± 2 inches, no more than three layers may be placed in any location, the free edge must extend at least four inches beyond the pavement edge and terminate in a drainable layer or daylighted location without impairing drainage, the geotextile is secured per the manufacturer’s instructions, kept clean and damp (but not saturated), protected from construction traffic, and its surface temperature must not exceed 120 °F. When an asphalt interlayer is used in the same context a nominal depth of one inch of Superpave mix design as specified in Section 410.3 is placed, joints faulting more than ½‑inch are scratched or milled to reduce faulting, a rubber‑tire roller is employed, and the pavement surface temperature also must be kept at or below 120 °F. The unbonded concrete overlay section does not prescribe any equations, empirical relationships, or design software for the separation layer. [1][2]

The design of a separation layer in pavement structures is governed by distinct criteria that vary according to the type of work and the applicable specification sections. In experimental pavement construction (section 2.9) when a geotextile is selected as the separation layer, its design parameters must be established on a project‑by‑project basis by consulting Publication 408 specifications, FHWA’s Geosynthetic Design and Construction Guidelines, National Transportation Product Evaluation Program data, the manufacturer’s published product information, and any other appropriate technical references; no generic numeric thresholds, safety factors, or reliability values are prescribed [1]. In rehabilitation of unbonded concrete overlays (section 4.9) the separation layer shall be a fine‑graded asphalt placed on the existing concrete pavement with a minimum thickness of 1 inch, although a non‑woven geotextile interlayer may also be used in lieu of an asphalt separation layer as permitted by Publication 408 Section 548 [1]. In subgrade work (section 6.2) the separation layer shall be a Class 4 Layer‑Separation geotextile (Type A) installed between subgrade and subbase, and it must extend across the entire lane width of at least eight feet as required by Publication 408 Section 210.3(b); a special provision is required when road widening is less than eight feet, and for patching where the subbase is removed the geotextile must be placed in sections longer than 20 feet and at minimum eight feet wide per Publication 72M RC‑26M [1]. In unbonded PCC overlay of PCC pavement (section 10.7) the separation layer shall be provided either by a geotextile interlayer or by asphalt concrete, and it is required when existing concrete joints exhibit faulting greater than ¼ inch; if joint faulting exceeds ½ inch a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix must be placed at the joint or the joint must be milled/grinded to reduce faulting, with the standard thickness of the asphalt concrete interlayer being a nominal depth of 1 inch of a Superpave Wearing Course, 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix, and proper drainage shall be ensured by daylighting the interlayer or installing a vertical subdrain, while the interlayer must extend through any shoulder that is constructed or reconstructed and be tied to the mainline [1]. In geosynthetic stabilized bridge approaches (SECTION 224) the separation layer shall be a Class 4, Type A geosynthetic that completely covers the prepared subgrade and excavated lay‑back slope, with required vertical spacing maintained between it and any other geosynthetic layers; splicing of the material is prohibited, adjacent sections must overlap along parallel roll edges at least 12 inches, edge wraps may not have overlapping within 4 feet of the wrapped edge, and when placed perpendicular to the roadway direction partial rolls must extend a minimum of 4 feet from the wrapped face before an overlap is allowed, the geosynthetic must be laid flat, pulled tight and anchored, positioned within ±½‑inch of the specified elevation, and no vehicles may travel on it until at least 6 inches of loose backfill have been placed [2]. For a separation layer classified as Class 4 – Layer Separation (Type A) in SECTION 212, the geotextile must be laid flat without wrinkles, overlapped at roll ends and edges by at least 6 inches and all adjacent edges by at least 1.5 feet (or 3 feet when placed underwater) with roll‑end offsets of a minimum of 10 feet; anchoring pins may be used at the spacing shown in Table A, but on slopes flatter than 6:1 pins can be eliminated if aggregate or other acceptable means secure the fabric, the fabric must not remain exposed for more than 2 weeks before being covered with subbase or other required cover material, any rock placed on it may not be dropped from a height greater than 1 foot, and when rocks larger than 1 foot are used a minimum 6‑inch bedding layer over the geotextile is required, while equipment must not travel directly on the fabric until it is fully covered and cover material should be placed promptly to prevent slippage [2]. In unbonded concrete overlay of concrete‑surfaced pavements (SECTION 548) the separation layer must be installed on a repaired and cleaned surface with the geotextile placed flat, overlapped by 8 inches ± 2 inches, limited to no more than three layers in any location, and its free edge extended at least 4 inches beyond the pavement edge while terminating in a drainable or daylighted condition that does not impair drainage; the geotextile must be kept clean, damp but not saturated, protected from construction traffic, and the surface temperature of the fabric may not exceed 120°F before the overlay placement, and a nominal depth of 1 inch of Superpave Mix Design is to be placed as specified in Section 410.3 [2].

Structural section and dimensions

In the Pavement Policy Manual (Publication 242, Change No. 10), Section 4.9 Rehabilitation specifies that for unbonded concrete overlays the separation layer shall consist of a fine‑graded asphalt material having a minimum thickness of 1 inch; alternatively a non‑woven geotextile interlayer may be used in place of the asphalt layer, and the manual provides no further dimensional requirements for pavement, base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab, shoulder, edge‑support, or reinforcement [1]. The same manual, in Section 6.2 Subgrade Soils, requires that a Class 4‑Type A geotextile be installed as the separation layer across the entire lane width, which is defined as 8 feet or greater; for patching applications the geotextile must extend for greater than 20 feet in length and at least eight feet in width, and it is mandated for all subgrade‑strengthening treatments [1]. The Department of Transportation Specification Publication 408 (2020), Section 548.3 Construction adds that when a geotextile interlayer is used its edges shall overlap by 8 inches ± 2 inches, the free edge must extend at least 4 inches beyond the pavement edge, and no more than three geotextile layers may overlap in any location [2]. The same specification states that when an asphalt interlayer is specified it shall be placed to a nominal depth of 1 inch of Superpave mix design [2].

The separation layer thickness is established by placing a nominal 1‑inch asphalt interlayer beneath the geotextile and, when rock larger than 1 foot will rest on top of the fabric, providing a bedding layer of at least 6 inches to protect the material; the cover must be applied within two weeks so that exposure does not exceed that period. Materials and mixture design

In the Rehabilitation portion of the Pavement Policy Manual, for unbonded concrete overlays the specification requires a separation layer that consists of a fine‑graded asphalt placed on the existing concrete pavement with a minimum thickness of 1 inch; alternatively, a non‑woven geotextile interlayer may be used in lieu of the asphalt layer. In the Subgrade Soils portion of the same manual, the separation layer must be a Class 4—Layer‑Separation geotextile (Type A) that is applied across the entire lane width (minimum eight feet) and is required for all subgrade strengthening treatments. In the Unbonded PCC Overlay of PCC Pavement portion, when a geotextile is used it is required for joints that have greater than ¼‑inch faulting and the layer should be installed with a nominal depth of 1 inch of a Superpave Wearing Course, 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix as the standard thickness; if joint faulting exceeds ½‑inch the specification calls for placing a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch), 9.5 mm mix at the joint or milling/grinding the joints to reduce faulting. In the Geosynthetic Stabilized Bridge Approach section, the separation layer shall be a Class 4, Type A separation geosynthetic that completely covers the prepared subgrade and lay‑back slope; the material must be free of any defects such as tears or punctures, must be protected from temperatures greater than 140 °F, direct sunlight and debris, and must be stored elevated off the ground, with any damaged material rejected and replaced at no cost. In that same section splicing is not permitted, adjacent sheets shall overlap a minimum of 12 inches along parallel edges while maintaining the required vertical spacing, and placement shall be within ±½‑inch of the design elevation before backfill is applied. For general separation layers in Section 212, the specification calls for a Class 4 (Type A) geotextile placed over a clean, puncture‑free surface in a loose, unstretched condition without folds or wrinkles; roll ends and edges shall overlap at least six inches with adjacent material, the fabric may be exposed for no more than two weeks before cover material is placed and compacted as directed, and when rock larger than one foot is placed a minimum six‑inch bedding layer over the fabric is required. In unbonded concrete overlay applications (Section 548), if a geotextile interlayer is used it shall be placed flat on the cleaned surface, overlapped 8 inches ± 2 inches at the edges, limited to no more than three layers in any location, and extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge; the fabric must be kept clean, damp but not saturated, and its surface temperature may not exceed 120 °F prior to concrete placement. In that same section, when an asphalt interlayer is specified the material shall be a Superpave mix design placed to a nominal depth of 1 inch as referenced in Section 410.3, and the pavement surface temperature also may not exceed 120 °F before overlay placement; if joint faulting exceeds ½‑inch a scratch course or milling must be performed first. Structural details and interfaces

For an unbonded concrete overlay the Separation Layer must be a fine‑graded asphalt film at least 1 inch thick, although a non‑woven geotextile interlayer may be substituted[1]. Joints in the overlay are installed exactly as they would be in a new concrete pavement and they do not have to line up with the existing transverse joints; their spacing is set by the type and thickness of the concrete being placed[1]. When a geotextile interlayer is specified, the contractor must mill any existing concrete joint that shows greater than 1/4‑inch faulting, and if joint faulting exceeds 1/2 inch either a Superpave Wearing Course (Scratch) 9.5 mm mix must be placed at the joint or the joints must be milled/grinded to reduce faulting[1][2]. A nominal depth of 1 inch of a Superpave Wearing Course, 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix is used as the standard thickness for the interlayer[1]. Proper drainage of the interlayer must be provided by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain[1]. The interlayer must extend through the shoulder when shoulders are constructed or reconstructed and be tied to the mainline[1].

In geosynthetic‑stabilized bridge approaches the separation geosynthetic shall be installed without any splicing; adjacent sheets must overlap along parallel roll edges by at least 12 inches, and when a layer edge is wrapped the material must remain continuous with no overlap within 4 feet of the wrapped edge[2]. If partial rolls are used perpendicular to the roadway they must extend a minimum of 4 feet from the wrapped face before an overlap is allowed; otherwise the geosynthetic may be placed in continuous strips parallel to traffic using the same 12‑inch edge overlap, and it shall be laid flat, pulled tight and anchored until backfill placement[2].

For general layer separation the Separation Layer shall be a loose, unstretched geotextile whose roll ends and edges are overlapped at least 6 inches, increasing to at least 1.5 feet where erosion control is required; overlaps shall be arranged so that the upslope fabric lies over the downslope fabric[2]. The fabric shall be anchored with steel pins or other acceptable methods placed along the overlaps, with pin spacing not to exceed 6 feet on any un‑overlapped perimeter, although pins may be omitted on slopes flatter than 6:1 provided aggregate, rock, or an equivalent means secures the material[2]. Cover material must be placed promptly and compacted as specified, and the geotextile shall not remain exposed for more than two weeks[2].

In experimental pavement construction, when a Separation Layer is provided by a geotextile, the interface with adjacent pavement layers must be designed on a project‑by‑project basis using criteria set forth in Publication 408, FHWA Geosynthetic Design and Construction Guidelines, NTPEP data, the manufacturer’s product data, and any other appropriate technical references; these sources are to be referenced to define geotextile properties, design specifications, product selection, and construction procedures. Because PennDOT limits geotextile uses to drainage, layer separation, erosion control, sediment control, and reinforcement of soft subgrade or aggregate layers, the interface design must reflect those functions. For unbonded concrete overlays, the interface is deliberately separated by installing a fine‑graded asphalt separation layer that is at least 1 inch thick on the existing concrete pavement, although a non‑woven geotextile interlayer may be used in lieu of the asphalt layer; this prevents bonding so that the overlay can act as a new pavement with joints designed according to standard new‑pavement criteria. Drainage, environment, and accessibility

The Separation Layer may consist of a Class 4 geotextile identified as a Layer‑Separation (Type A) fabric that isolates the subbase from the subgrade across the full lane width, preserving each material’s integrity and limiting moisture migration. When installed on low‑CBR subgrades the Separation Layer may be the Stabilization (Type C) version, which also requires maintaining a positive slope for drainage. The design should incorporate pavement base drains or other appropriate drains to remove water that could contribute to frost heave, because any solution to frost action must reduce or eliminate excess moisture and freezing temperatures. By providing this separation and ensuring proper drainage, the layer helps mitigate climate‑related effects such as differential heaving and thaw‑weakening of the subgrade. In the geosynthetic stabilized bridge approach (Section 224), drainage is provided by constructing the foundation drain exactly as shown in the bridge structure plan and no separate GSBA drain is added, while a Class 4, Type A separation geosynthetic layer is placed over the prepared subgrade to serve as the Separation Layer. All geosynthetic materials must be shielded from temperatures greater than 140°F, direct sunlight, and debris during delivery, storage, and handling to protect against climatic exposure. The separation‑layer geotextile is rolled out on a clean, prepared surface without wrinkles, with roll‑ends and edges overlapping at least six inches, and it must be covered by subbase or other required cover material within two weeks so that exposure does not exceed that period. Drainage is facilitated by providing smooth side and bottom trench surfaces that prevent the fabric from bridging depressions, by maintaining a positive slope in the backfilled layer, and by anchoring the fabric with steel pins to resist wind lift, thereby limiting moisture infiltration and protecting the layer from climatic exposure. In unbonded concrete overlay applications (Section 548), the geotextile Separation Layer must be placed flat on a cleaned surface with overlaps of eight inches ± 2 inches, limited to three layers in any location, and its free edge extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge where it terminates in a drainable layer or daylighted location so that free drainage is not impaired. Prior to concrete placement the fabric should be kept clean, dampened but not saturated, and its surface temperature must not exceed 120°F, which together address moisture and temperature conditions. Verification and expected performance

The Pavement Policy Manual states that the completed Separation Layer is verified by confirming that a Class 4—Layer Separation (Type A) geotextile has been installed across the entire required lane width of eight feet or greater, as called for in Publication 408 Section 210.3(b). When a road‑widening project provides less than an eight‑foot width, the manual requires inclusion of the special provision prescribed for such narrower configurations. The same manual further requires that both the geotextile material and its installation satisfy the product and placement criteria defined in the relevant Publication 408 specification sections, including Sections 212 and 735, which specify the acceptable class, type, and performance characteristics. The 2020 Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specifications require that, before any fill or additional geosynthetics are placed in a Geosynthetic Stabilized Bridge Approach, the Representative must inspect and approve the prepared foundation—proof‑rolled and conforming to the required profile—to verify readiness for the separation layer. Compliance is then demonstrated by confirming that a Class 4, Type A separation geosynthetic fully covers the subgrade, that vertical spacing to adjacent layers is maintained, that no splicing occurs, that adjacent rolls overlap a minimum of twelve inches, and that the fabric lies within plus‑or‑minus one‑half inch of the elevations shown on the plans. Constructability and performance are verified by ensuring the geosynthetic has been stored protected from temperatures exceeding one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit, sunlight, and debris; that it is placed flat, pulled tight, anchored appropriately, and that aggregate placement does not damage the fabric, with backfill placed at least six inches before any vehicle traffic and any distortion corrected. Consistency with project requirements is confirmed by reviewing material submittals to verify that the geosynthetic specifications have been approved, rejecting any damaged or defective sheets, and documenting that handling, placement, and post‑placement conditions meet all of the detailed criteria set out in Section 224.3. For general geotextile installations covered by Section 212, the specifications call for an inspection after placement to verify that the fabric has been rolled out loose and unstretched with no folds or wrinkles, that all objects capable of puncturing it have been removed, and that roll‑ends and edges overlap at least six inches. The inspector must also confirm that securing pins are installed on the unlapped portions at a maximum spacing of six feet, that steel pins are used where necessary to prevent wind lift before backfilling, and that cover material is placed and compacted in accordance with Section 206.3 within two weeks of exposure. Performance criteria include laying rock larger than one foot on a minimum six‑inch bedding layer over the geotextile and prohibiting equipment travel directly on the fabric until it is fully covered. In unbonded concrete overlay projects, the specifications require inspection of the separation layer to confirm that the geotextile has been laid flat on the repaired surface with edges overlapped by eight inches plus or minus two inches, that no more than three layers of material occur at any point, and that the free edge extends at least four inches beyond the pavement terminating in a drainable layer without impairing drainage. The fabric must be clean, kept damp but not saturated, secured according to the manufacturer’s instructions, protected from construction traffic, and its surface temperature must not exceed one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit before concrete placement.
