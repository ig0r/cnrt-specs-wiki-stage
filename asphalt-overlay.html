Asphalt Overlay

Part of: Overlay is-subtopic-of
Asphalt Overlay is a topic under Overlay.
asphalt-overlayasphalt-overlaysasphalt-overlay-s
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-28

Asphalt overlay is a preservation treatment that adds a new flexible wearing course to an existing pavement whose structural support or ride quality has deteriorated, thereby increasing load‑carrying capacity and extending service life [1]. The process begins with evaluation of drainage, structural condition (often via Falling Weight Deflectometer) and surface defects, followed by milling or scratch‑leveling, patching, sealing and application of a tack coat before placing the hot‑mix asphalt layer typically 1½–2 inches thick [1][2]. By providing a uniform, high‑friction surface on a dry, clean, stable substrate—subject to limits such as less than two percent patching and adequate subdrainage—the overlay restores smoothness, improves water removal, and mitigates further distress while remaining cost‑effective for up to about ten years of service [1][1]

PennDOT publications covering “Asphalt Overlay” also include the following subtopics: Saw and Seal.

Saw and Seal serves as a preparatory input for Asphalt Overlay by sealing transverse and longitudinal joints to prevent water infiltration that could compromise the new layer’s integrity. [1][2] The joint treatment preserves the underlying structure and extends service life, ensuring the substrate meets the stability requirements necessary before placing the hot-mix asphalt wearing course. [1][2]

Purpose and treatment objectives

Asphalt overlay is employed when the existing pavement no longer provides sufficient structural support or a satisfactory riding surface, targeting a range of distress that compromises load‑carrying capacity and ride quality [1]. The treatment corrects structural deficiencies such as thin or weakened layers, spalled concrete sections, crushed joints, and other non‑uniform base conditions by adding a new flexible layer whose required thickness is determined through non‑destructive testing or other accepted design procedures [1]. It also remedies surface‑level problems including longitudinal joint cracks, fatigue cracking, rutting, wheel ruts, local depressions, irregular longitudinal profiles, surface unevenness, reduced friction, shoulder drop‑off, and inadequate drainage that impede water removal and vehicle safety [1]. Prior to placing the overlay, profiling milling is used to remove bumps, corrugations, wheel ruts and other irregularities such as grooves, ridges or gouges, thereby producing a surface free of defects that could jeopardize ride quality and safe operation of vehicles [2]. Areas where spalling or dislodged unsound pavement are identified during milling are patched before the overlay is applied to ensure a stable base for the new wearing course [2]. A scratch or leveling course may then be placed to fill small depressions and re‑establish the proper cross‑section, after which the asphalt overlay restores structural capacity, provides a smooth high‑friction surface, and improves water removal [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (p. 75), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221), Ch. 14 (p. 227); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 342–343)

The primary purpose of an asphalt overlay is to extend the useful service life of an existing pavement by adding a new structural layer that increases load‑carrying capacity while also restoring functional performance and repairing damage incurred during construction or from distress [1][2]. By providing additional thickness—typically one‑and‑a‑half to two inches, with deeper milling allowed when necessary—the overlay corrects structural deficiencies such as rutting or material failures and reestablishes ride quality, skid resistance, and overall support [1]. The treatment also incorporates slab stabilization where needed and targeted patching based on field measurements to anticipate further deterioration between design and construction, thereby slowing subsequent degradation and reducing future maintenance cycles [1]. Contract specifications require that repaired sections conform in smoothness and durability to the adjacent pavement, emphasizing the objective of fixing damage and delivering a uniform riding surface [2]. A typical design assumes a ten‑year service life for the overlay, supported by a five‑year clean‑and‑seal maintenance regime that seals about twenty‑five percent of longitudinal joints and applies roughly five hundred lineal feet per mile of seal coat or micro‑surface treatment on shoulders [1]. Across the guides, the overlay is therefore viewed as both a rehabilitation measure—restoring functionality and repairing distress—and a preservation strategy that slows deterioration and extends pavement life [1][2].

Distress characterization and causes

Asphalt overlays are applied to address distress that originates from a combination of water infiltration, inadequate structural support, material aging, and excessive loading [1][2]. Water infiltration is identified as the primary driver of many overlay‑targeted distresses; moisture enters through insufficient surface or subsurface drainage and through unsealed or damaged waterproofing membranes, leading to loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, crack propagation and freeze‑thaw damage [1][2]. When membrane sheets are not installed with a shingle overlap in the direction of flow, laps remain open, wrinkles or air bubbles persist, and tears may go unrepaired, creating additional pathways for water to reach the structural layer [2]. Construction traffic that contacts the membrane after it has been placed, or delays between membrane installation and overlay placement without proper inspection for punctures, further exacerbate infiltration risks [2]. Inadequate base or subbase material, whether deteriorated or insufficient in thickness, reduces the pavement’s load‑bearing capacity and produces premature mid‑slab cracking and uneven bearing beneath the new asphalt layer [1]. Loss of effective load transfer at transverse joints—caused by crushed or deteriorated joint material that prevents dowel action—allows slab movement or faulting, undermining support for the overlay [1]. Age‑related oxidation of the existing pavement produces alligator cracking across the surface, a symptom of diminished structural integrity that must be addressed before an overlay can perform adequately [1]. Certain aggregates such as Vanport Limestone and chemical reactions like alkali‑silica reaction degrade the matrix and may lower skid resistance, contributing to distress that overlays aim to mitigate [1]. Heavy traffic loads generate rutting, bleeding and additional stresses that accelerate these failure modes, making structural reinforcement through overlay necessary [1]. To ensure the overlay functions, the existing surface must be stabilized by removing failed areas, repairing or replacing base material, restoring load‑transfer joints, sealing cracks, providing adequate subdrainage, and completing membrane installation with proper overlap and prompt repair of defects before the asphalt is placed [1][2].

Applicability and treatment selection

An asphalt overlay may be placed over any existing pavement when the objective is to correct structural deficiencies, surface deterioration, or poor rideability, as long as the underlying layer provides a stable platform. For concrete pavements, a falling‑weight deflectometer test must be conducted before design and adequate subdrainage must exist where subsurface drainage problems are present. When the existing pavement is asphalt, it should be essentially sound—no more than about two percent of the surface requiring patching—and the project should not seek a structural upgrade. The overlay thickness must meet a minimum of one‑and‑a‑half inches unless special approval is granted, and both surface and subsurface drainage must be good or be upgraded to good as part of the work. The underlying base must be stable so that a thin overlay can perform adequately, and traffic volumes must fall within the ADT limits specified in the applicable tables. No specific climate limitations are imposed beyond the requirement for proper drainage. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

An asphalt overlay should be rejected whenever the existing pavement cannot be brought to a condition of stability, proper leveling, sealing and adequate patching before the new surface is placed. Obvious base failures, insufficient structural thickness, or drainage problems that cannot be corrected render an overlay inappropriate, directing the designer toward base repair, milling, scratch‑and‑leveling, heater‑planing, or a full structural overlay rather than a thin wearing course. The overlay also becomes unsuitable when the pavement surface is not asphalt, when more than about two percent of the area requires patching, or when the existing layer exhibits material deficiencies that are confirmed by core testing; in those cases the flawed overlay must be removed and a design for an AC overlay over PCC pavement should be used instead. Thickness limits further constrain preservation‑type overlays: on flexible pavements any design exceeding two inches is outside the preservation envelope unless a mill‑and‑overlay is undertaken to correct rutting or material problems, while on concrete decks overlays thicker than four‑and‑a‑half inches with a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing course—or five inches with a 12.5 mm mix—are not permissible as preservation treatments. When the required patching exceeds roughly ten percent of the surface, the project is no longer cost‑effective for preservation and must shift to more extensive rehabilitation or reconstruction. Likewise, if the distress level has progressed beyond early‑stage symptoms, routine maintenance actions such as crack sealing, joint sealing, spall repair, micro‑surfacing, diamond grinding, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, cold in‑place recycling, or full‑depth patching are preferred over an overlay. Finally, when the functional life of the pavement has been exhausted and its structural capacity cannot be restored by any overlay, total reconstruction is the appropriate response. [1]

Condition assessment and timing

Determining whether an asphalt overlay is warranted begins with a systematic visual and instrumented survey of the existing pavement. Engineers first review the automated pavement‑condition surveys—such as the STAMPP video‑logging system—that record surface distresses including cracking, rutting, edge deterioration and other fatigue signs for every NHS segment and a representative sample of non‑NHS roads. The distress data are entered into the RMS database and run through the treatment matrices; a recommendation for a surface treatment flags the section as a candidate for overlay. A field visual inspection follows to confirm drainage conditions, base integrity, surface roughness that may require milling or a leveling course, friction levels, and shoulder condition. The pavement must exhibit adequate subsurface drainage or be slated for improvement, and any movement of the existing surface under roller pressure must be absent. Structural capacity is quantified with non‑destructive testing, principally Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) measurements performed on the current concrete or flexible pavement. The FWD results provide the structural coefficient and subgrade characteristics needed to calculate the effective existing thickness using the condition‑survey method and to verify that additional structural layer thickness is required. Where visual signs suggest material problems in the existing asphalt, cores are extracted at representative locations. Laboratory analysis of these cores confirms the actual layer thickness, bond quality between the asphalt and any underlying concrete or brick base, and overall integrity; the core results also determine the milling depth needed before placing a new overlay. The overlay is limited to sections where less than two percent of the surface requires patching and where the required overlay thickness does not exceed two inches for preservation projects. If low skid resistance is identified, friction testing is performed and, when necessary, remedial measures are incorporated into the overlay design. Traffic loading information such as ADT or ESALs may be consulted to ensure that the proposed overlay thickness will satisfy future performance requirements. Only after drainage, structural capacity, surface condition, bond integrity, patching extent, friction, and traffic considerations have been verified through these inspections, measurements, and tests does the engineer conclude that an asphalt overlay is required and can delineate its exact location and design thickness. [1]

An asphalt overlay is indicated once the existing pavement no longer satisfies serviceability criteria but still possesses sufficient structural integrity to be preserved rather than reconstructed. The trigger occurs when observable distresses—such as rutting, fatigue cracking, loss of ride quality, inadequate friction, poor drainage, or deteriorated shoulders—have advanced to a level that surface treatment is required, as signaled by the STAMPP pavement‑management system’s surface‑treatment recommendation. For flexible pavements the existing base must be sound, with less than two percent of the area needing patching and adequate (or upgraded) surface and subsurface drainage; non‑destructive testing may be employed when structural capacity is uncertain. In composite asphalt‑over‑concrete sections a typical overlay is scheduled around the twenty‑year service point after milling the wearing course and shoulders, patching roughly eight percent of the concrete area, cleaning and sealing all joints, and then placing a 60 lb/yd² asphalt layer four to four‑and‑half inches thick, with a repeat at about thirty‑five years. For flexible pavements exhibiting rutting or material failures, milling followed by an overlay of one‑and‑a‑half to two inches (deeper if needed to correct the defect) should be applied early in the maintenance cycle before more advanced distresses appear. Overlays thicker than two inches are reserved for cases where additional structural capacity is required and are not considered preservation treatments. The overlay thickness is sized using the AASHTO design method or an equivalent non‑structural preservation approach to provide the necessary increase in structural strength while restoring ride quality. Materials and repair details

Before placing an asphalt overlay the existing surface must be made stable, non‑moving under rollers, fully leveled, sealed and patched; any failed sections are removed and replaced with appropriate base material to restore structural capacity and drainage. For concrete pavements this includes slab stabilization, full‑depth patching with Portland cement concrete or an approved asphalt base course, saw‑cutting and sealing all transverse joints, and providing a minimum one‑inch leveling (or scratch) course to correct cross‑slope and create a uniform platform. The leveling course is typically placed at about sixty pounds per square yard and may be counted as the first structural inch. In flexible pavements the preparation often begins with milling using a self‑propelled machine capable of cutting a full lane up to twelve feet wide in a single pass, followed by sweeping the milled surface clean of loose material. Spalled or otherwise unsound areas are excavated to match the depth of the surrounding pavement, cleaned, coated with an emulsified asphalt tack coat and filled with an asphalt mix of the same type before being compacted to the adjacent grade. The milled surface must be profiled so that smoothness does not vary more than one‑eighth inch over a twelve‑foot length and cross‑slope transitions are within the same tolerance. Design thickness is determined by the AASHTO method in Chapter 10 (or Chapter 12 for preservation), calculating future traffic needs, evaluating effective existing thickness with a condition‑survey method, and then applying the prescribed overlay depth. For rigid pavements the overlay shall be four inches thick when a 9.5 mm wearing‑course mix is used or four‑and‑a‑half inches when a 12.5 mm mix is used. For flexible pavements the required thickness is one‑and‑a‑half inches with a 9.5 mm mix or two inches with a 12.5 mm mix, and Interstate Superpave wearing courses may not be less than 1½ inches without prior approval. Thin overlays can be as thin as one inch using a 6.3 mm grade, while ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses are limited to two inches for non‑structural use. The first lift of asphalt must achieve a minimum compacted thickness of 1½ inches, and the overall overlay must meet the contract‑specified total thickness. Overlay material may be hot‑mix, warm‑mix or Superpave restricted‑performance asphalt and may incorporate reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP). Typical aggregate gradations include nominal sizes of 25, 19, 12.5 and 9.5 mm; the thin‑overlay grade uses a 6.3 mm aggregate with a PG 64E‑22 binder, while the fine‑grade 9.5 mm course uses its compatible binder. When additional structural capacity is required a binder course is added, its thickness being determined by non‑destructive testing or other acceptable procedures. To mitigate reflective cracking an interlayer such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grid, or composite reinforcing grid may be installed between the existing surface and the new overlay, provided it is thermally stable and placed per manufacturer instructions. A tack coat is applied over a clean, dry surface at the manufacturer‑specified rate—typically 0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard for geotextile fabric, 0.02 to 0.05 gal/yd² for fiberglass mesh, or 0.19 to 0.28 gal/yd² for composite grid—and must extend at least six inches beyond each reinforcement edge while the coating remains tacky. Reinforcement is placed while the tack coat is still tacky, avoiding areas where the compacted overlay thickness will be less than 1½ inches, and overlaps must be a minimum of one inch; damaged sections are removed and replaced before hot mix placement. Saw‑cuts are required to control cracking; each cut is exactly one‑eighth inch wide and is made to a depth equal to either one‑third of the total overlay thickness or a minimum of 1½ inches for the initial cuts, and at least one inch (or one‑third of the final course thickness) for the last course, whichever is greater. The cuts are positioned directly over existing transverse joints within plus or minus one inch, and after the asphalt cools the cut reservoirs are cleaned, dried and sealed with a manufacturer‑approved sealant kept between 40 °F and 90 °F; a tape bondbreaker is placed on the bottom of each reservoir but must not extend more than one‑eighth inch up the faces, and the sealant is filled to a level one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface. Edge protection is achieved with a safety‑edge taper of approximately 30°, cut to a minimum depth of 1½ inches and not exceeding five inches; this treatment adds roughly one percent to material quantities. Shoulder improvements may include Type 6 or Type 7 paved shoulders, and surface‑friction treatments are incorporated as needed; drainage structures, continuous base drains, guide rails and other ancillary items are adjusted or replaced in accordance with the overlay design. The overlay is placed at a rate of about sixty pounds per square yard over both mainline and shoulder sections, with saw‑cutting and sealing of all transverse joints. Placement temperature for the hot mix must be between 300 °F and 340 °F, and placement should occur on the same day that any waterproofing membrane has been installed; if an overnight hold is unavoidable construction traffic is kept off the membrane and the surface inspected for punctures before proceeding. Rubber‑tired pavers and trucks may travel on a freshly placed membrane provided they avoid sudden starts, stops or turning while stationary. Hand‑applied asphalt is used only for repairs at overlaps or identified distress areas, and any overlap receives additional hand‑placed material as directed. All repair work, including removal of damaged reinforcement, replacement of cracked sections, and fixing of construction‑traffic damage, is performed at no cost to the Department. Timing constraints require that the first overlay course be placed within seven calendar days after milling, that an overlay over a fractured slab be completed within one week of seating that slab, and that a full overlay after rubblizing and seating be installed within sixty calendar days while avoiding exposure to freezing conditions. Traffic is not permitted on the new surface until it has fully cured. [1][2]

Treatment procedures

Before an asphalt overlay is placed, the existing pavement is evaluated for drainage capability, structural capacity and surface defects, a process that may include field‑loaded deflection testing on concrete slabs and coring to determine the necessary milling depth [1]. The surface is then dried and thoroughly cleaned of dirt, gravel, vegetation, oil and any loose debris, creating a dry, clean deck ready for repair work [2]. All cracks and potholes are repaired and sealed according to the specified details, with the sealant allowed to cure before further operations commence [2]. Areas that cannot provide a stable, uniform base—such as spalled concrete, crushed joints, rutting or other deteriorated sections—are removed by milling, heater‑planing or scratching to a depth of at least one and a half inches, often extending to two inches, while preserving a minimum of one and a half inches of sound asphalt when it is present [1][2]. Full‑depth patches are placed with approved Portland cement concrete or an asphalt base course where needed, slab movement or faulting is corrected by stabilization or jacking, and adequate subdrainage is provided wherever subsurface drainage problems exist [1]. When the existing pavement is concrete, any overlying asphalt is stripped away, the slab may be rubblized to the required fragment size, backfilled and compacted with a pneumatic‑tired roller until vertical deflection under the roller is less than three‑quarters of an inch, after which the surface is swept clean; all transverse joints are sawed and sealed before the new asphalt is laid [1][2]. A non‑tracking tack coat of asphalt material is applied uniformly over the prepared surface at a temperature between 290 °F and 320 °F using a calibrated distributor truck, and if an interlayer system such as paving fabric or grid is required, it is laid immediately while the tack remains sticky, with overlaps trimmed to extend at least one inch [2][1]. Where a leveling or scratch course is indicated, a minimum one‑inch thick layer is placed to create a uniform platform, joints that have lost load transfer are repaired and sealed, and longitudinal as well as transverse joints are cleaned, sawed where necessary and resealed before the overlay is applied [1][2]. The hot‑mix overlay itself is supplied at a temperature between 300 °F and 340 °F (or within the 290 °F–320 °F range for spray applications) and placed on the same day as any membrane or interlayer preparation, with the first lift achieving a minimum thickness of one and a half inches and the overall overlay conforming to the design thickness specified in contract documents, such as a one‑and‑a‑half‑inch wearing course for interstates or a two‑inch ultra‑thin bonded wearing course for non‑structural applications [1][2]. The newly placed material is compacted with rollers to meet density targets measured by the Percent Within Limits quality measure, and any required safety‑edge tapers or reflective‑crack mitigation interlayers are installed according to manufacturer guidance [1]. After placement, sand may be broadcast to absorb bleed‑through and then removed, and once the overlay has cooled below 140 °F saw cuts are made directly over the recorded transverse joints within seven days of placing the wearing course, with cut width and depth set according to the overlay thickness; joint sealing follows only after the reservoir faces are clean and dry, using heated sealant placed no later than six hours after reaching pouring temperature, a tape bond‑breaker applied at the bottom of the joint, and the seal filled to one‑eighth to one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface, all performed within an ambient temperature range of 40 °F to 90 °F unless otherwise authorized [2]. Construction traffic is restricted until the overlay has cured; one guide notes that no separate curing period is required beyond proper compaction and adherence to mix specifications, while another requires traffic be held off until curing is complete and a final cleaning of any remaining loose material is performed before opening the surface to vehicles [1][2]. The process is typically initiated by a pre‑placement meeting with the manufacturer’s technical representative, and the locations of all existing transverse joints are located, referenced and recorded to a tolerance of plus or minus one inch, with the quality control plan amended if this tolerance cannot be achieved [2].

Implementation of an asphalt overlay begins only after the existing pavement has been shown to be stable, meaning it does not move under a roller, and any required base repairs have been completed [1]. The first field activity is surface preparation, which may include milling with a self‑propelled machine up to twelve feet wide equipped with automatic grade‑control, heater‑planing, or the placement of a scratch or leveling course to correct transverse and longitudinal profile irregularities and provide a level platform for the overlay [1][2]. When joints are present, each transverse joint is located and referenced before any tack coat or interlayer material is applied [2]. A tack coat is then sprayed with a calibrated distributor‑truck spray bar, and if an interlayer reinforcement is specified it is placed on the dry surface using mechanical or manual laydown equipment that minimizes wrinkling; wet pavement is prohibited for this step [1][2]. Any waterproofing membrane must be covered with the hot‑mix asphalt on the same day, and if overnight storage becomes unavoidable traffic must be kept off the membrane and a careful inspection for punctures performed before paving resumes [2]. The hot‑mix asphalt is delivered at temperatures between three hundred and three hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit (or maintained within two ninety to three twenty degrees as required) and must be placed immediately after tack coat or reinforcement so that it does not cool below the specified limits before the next layer is laid [2]. Overlay thickness must conform to the pavement‑depth limits set out in the applicable tables, and thin overlays less than one‑and‑a‑half inches on Interstate facilities require prior approval [1]. Placement of the overlay is permitted only under the weather conditions prescribed in Publication 408 Section 413.3(b), which include adequate surface and subsurface drainage and avoidance of freezing temperatures for seated or rubblized material [1][2]. Equipment used throughout the operation includes rollers (including a minimum fifty‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller with four to seven wheels inflated to at least one hundred psi for seating fractured or rubblized concrete), milling machines, heater‑planers, tack‑coat applicators, and non‑tracking asphalt tack coat sprayers where traffic will stop, turn, or move slowly [1][2]. After milling, the milled surface must be thoroughly cleaned and no traffic may travel on it for more than six calendar days; the first overlay course must be placed within seven calendar days of starting the milling operation, with documented weather delays excluded from this count [2]. For fractured concrete pavements an asphalt leveling course is required within one week of seating, and the seated surface must be kept free of freezing conditions; traffic is prohibited except for limited crossing until the overlay is placed [2]. When a slab is rubblized, the rubblized material is seated with a fifty‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller, swept clean, optionally primed, and the overlay must be installed within sixty calendar days while avoiding freezing exposure; traffic is prohibited after rubblizing and seating [2]. Saw cuts for joint sealing are made only after the asphalt course has cooled below one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit, directly above each transverse joint within plus or minus one inch, and sealing material may be placed only when ambient temperature is between forty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit [2]. Throughout construction traffic through the work zone is limited to construction vehicles; regular traffic is diverted lane‑by‑lane in accordance with temporary traffic‑control guidelines, detours are coordinated with the District Traffic Unit and any required maintenance on the detour route is completed before opening it, and where a same‑day overlay cannot be achieved warning signs such as “ROUGH ROAD” or “BUMP” with reduced speed limits are posted [1][2]. The volume of traffic allowed on the overlay construction area must not exceed the average daily traffic limits specified in the applicable tables [1].

Quality and workmanship

Before any overlay is placed the inspector must confirm that the existing pavement substrate is structurally sound and stable, showing no movement when a roller passes over it, and that it has been properly leveled, sealed, patched and evaluated for drainage, with full‑depth patches applied where needed, slab movement stabilized, subdrainage provided, and all transverse joints sawed and sealed; FWD testing is required on concrete pavements to verify structural condition prior to design. [1] The surface that will receive the overlay must be dry, clean and free of debris, oil, vegetation, grooves, ridges or gouges, with all cracks sealed, potholes repaired and loose material removed before any tack coat is applied. [2] Where an interlayer system is used, the contractor must supply the manufacturer’s installation instructions to a qualified technical representative who remains on site throughout the work, and the interlayer material must be delivered by a calibrated distributor truck providing the specified gallons per square yard with a minimum six‑inch extension beyond any reinforcement and at least one‑inch overlap of joints. [2] The interlayer itself is inspected for punctures, protected from traffic if delayed, and must be placed on the same day as the hot‑mix asphalt; if an overnight delay occurs the membrane is examined before placement. [2] The asphalt mix to be placed shall conform to the specified Superpave wearing‑course requirements, including the correct binder grade—such as PG 64E‑22 for a 6.3 mm course—and aggregate gradation, and any approved interlayer material must be compatible and thermally stable with the tack coat applied as called for. [1][2] Mix temperature during placement is continuously monitored to remain within the prescribed limits, typically between 300 °F and 340 °F for membrane‑overlays and not below 290 °F nor above 320 °F under contract specifications. [2] The first compacted lift must achieve a minimum thickness of 1.5 inches, and the overall installed thickness is checked against design values—four inches with a 9.5 mm mix or 4½ inches with a 12.5 mm mix for rigid pavements, and 1½ inches with a 9.5 mm mix or two inches with a 12.5 mm mix for flexible pavements—or against the thickness calculated by the AASHTO design procedure—to ensure required structural strength. [1][2] Compaction and density are verified using the Percent Within Limits methodology, typically through core testing, to confirm that the placed material meets the specified density criteria. [1] Continuous joint sealing is inspected, with transverse joints sawed within a ± 1‑inch tolerance and sealed after the asphalt has cooled below 140 °F; overlaps receive at least one inch of tack coat coverage. [1][2] Safety edges are constructed to the required depth and taper along the outer edge of the pavement and shoulders, providing a standard pavement edge treatment. [1] After paving, the finished surface is measured against contract drawings for overall thickness, excess material or wrinkles are removed, and surface smoothness and cross‑slope are verified to be within 1/8 inch per 12 feet. [2][1] The final overlay is examined for uniform thickness, adequate compaction, proper bond to the underlying base—confirmed by coring where necessary—and sufficient friction, while also confirming that shoulder support, drainage improvements, moisture‑barrier performance and the absence of reflective cracking meet design intent. [1] The first overlay course must be placed within seven calendar days of milling, ensuring timely construction sequencing. [2]

A satisfactory asphalt overlay requires that the existing pavement be stable under rolling, thoroughly dried and cleaned of dirt, gravel, vegetation, oil or loose material, and that any cracks, joints or damaged areas be properly leveled, sealed and patched before new surfacing is placed, with structural soundness indicated by less than two percent of the surface requiring patching and adequate surface and subsurface drainage [1][2]. Saw cuts must be made directly over all underlying transverse joints within a tolerance of plus or minus one inch, overlaps must be at least one inch, and any wrinkles larger than an inch must be repaired prior to paving; for concrete pavements these cuts are essential to create a uniform load‑transfer surface [1][2]. The milled surface must be free of gouges, grooves and ridges and must meet smoothness not greater than one‑eighth inch in twelve feet as well as cross‑slope within the same tolerance, with vertical edges around utilities removed at the close of each workday [2]. When a waterproofing membrane is part of the system, the overlay must be placed on the same calendar day as the membrane; if an unavoidable delay occurs, construction traffic is prohibited on the membrane and a careful inspection for punctures is required, while the first overlay course must be laid within seven calendar days after milling and the entire overlay completed within sixty calendar days after rubblizing and seating to avoid exposure to freezing conditions [2]. A liquid binder (tack coat) is applied at a temperature between 290 °F and 320 °F at a rate of 0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard, extending six inches beyond any reinforcement and forming a uniform film that fully saturates the interlayer material [2]. The hot‑mix asphalt itself must be delivered at a temperature between 300 °F and 340 °F [2]. Overlay thicknesses must satisfy both minimum design requirements derived from the AASHTO structural analysis and programmatic limits: for a first overlay on rigid pavements the layer shall be at least four inches thick when a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing‑course mix is used or four and a half inches with a 12.5 mm mix; flexible pavements require a minimum of one and a half inches with a 9.5 mm mix or two inches with a 12.5 mm mix, and on Interstate projects the Superpave wearing course may not be less than one‑and‑half inches without prior approval; preservation projects impose upper limits of no more than two inches on existing flexible pavements and, on concrete surfaces, no more than four and a half inches with a 9.5 mm mix or five inches with a 12.5 mm mix, while any leveling course must be at least one inch thick [1]. The placed mix must achieve an in‑place density within the target field range of ninety‑two percent to ninety‑eight point five percent, with contractors typically aiming near the middle of this band to avoid reduced payment factors [1]. Where backfill is used it must be placed in lifts not exceeding six inches and compacted until no further movement occurs, and final seating of any rubblized concrete is considered satisfactory when vertical deflection under a 50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller is less than three‑quarters of an inch [2]. If a safety edge is incorporated, its taper shall be between twenty‑six and forty degrees, centered on thirty degrees, with total depth limited to one‑and‑half through five inches [1]. Concrete patching areas must remain at or below ten percent of the total pavement surface unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided [1]. Traffic on freshly placed membrane or milled surfaces is restricted to construction vehicles moving carefully without sudden starts, stops or turns, and no traffic may use a milled surface for more than six calendar days [2].

Performance and service life

An asphalt overlay is applied after a corrective scratch or leveling course (or milling) that levels the existing pavement and creates a stable base for the new wearing surface. By placing a binder course of appropriate thickness, determined through non‑destructive testing or other design procedures, the overlay adds structural capacity that raises durability and allows the pavement to better resist future loads. The treatment also incorporates drainage improvements, base repairs, enhanced surface friction, and shoulder rehabilitation, which together improve functional performance and safety. The new surface restores condition by eliminating cracking, rutting, edge deterioration and other distresses, while the use of Superpave thin‑asphalt mixes—such as 6.3 mm or 9.5 mm fine grades—provides a smoother ride, better drainage, and superior skid resistance compared with emulsified‑asphalt alternatives. When an interlayer such as geotextile fabric or reinforcing grid is included, reflective cracking is mitigated and moisture intrusion is reduced, further extending the overlay’s durability. Because the overlay adds only about 1½ to 2 inches of material, it can be employed as a cost‑effective preservation activity early in the maintenance cycle, before advanced distresses develop. To preserve the restored condition and functionality, a clean‑and‑seal operation is recommended at five years, crack sealing on roughly one‑quarter of the longitudinal joints, and a seal coat or micro‑surface treatment along shoulders at a rate of 500 ft per mile for specified pavement types. These maintenance actions address surface wear and cracking throughout the design period. Taken together, these structural enhancements and periodic upkeep provide an expected service life of approximately ten years, extending the functional lifespan of the roadway beyond that achievable with the original surface and delaying the need for full reconstruction. Asphalt overlays are prone to a variety of distresses that arise when existing pavement condition, preparation practices, and environmental or loading factors are unfavorable. Reflective cracking is identified as the most frequently observed distress in overlays, especially when the overlay rests on concrete slabs that exhibit movement, faulting, or inadequately treated transverse joints, allowing cracks to propagate from the underlying slab into the new surface. Alligator cracking generated by age‑related oxidation of the existing pavement can also transmit through an overlay, creating a network of interconnected fatigue cracks in the new layer. Rutting, bleeding, raveling, stripping, potholes, localized failures, and freeze‑thaw induced deterioration commonly appear when moisture infiltrates the structure or subgrade support is compromised. The likelihood and severity of these failures are governed by the condition and type of pre‑existing distress, overlay thickness, climatic influences such as freeze‑thaw cycles, traffic loading, drainage quality, and whether moisture‑resistant interlayers or adequate surface preparation have been provided. If the existing pavement is not thoroughly cleaned of dirt, gravel, vegetation, oil, or loose debris, the tack coat cannot achieve proper adhesion, resulting in delamination of the new asphalt layer. Placing reinforcement or an interlayer on a wet base similarly prevents bonding and can cause premature separation of the overlay from the underlying surface. Applying the tack coat at temperatures outside the recommended range—either below the minimum or above the maximum—or laying the overlay before the milled surface has fully dried reduces bond effectiveness and predisposes the pavement to early failure. Interlayer fabrics that develop wrinkles exceeding one inch must be sliced and overlapped flat; unaddressed folds act as planes of weakness that promote separation and cracking. Gaps, insufficient overlaps, or damaged reinforcement interrupt continuity and further increase the risk of cracking and loss of structural integrity. The first overlay course should be placed within seven calendar days after milling; exceeding this window often leads to premature failure because the milled surface loses its optimal condition. Traffic must not be allowed on any milled area for more than six calendar days, as early loading can crush the interlayer system and generate surface irregularities that evolve into localized failures. When milling tolerances are not met—specifically a smoothness deviation greater than one‑eighth inch over twelve feet or an incorrect cross‑slope—the resulting grooves or ridges become permanent defects in the overlay. Spalling or dislodgement of unsound underlying pavement during profile milling creates areas that must be patched before the new asphalt is placed; if left unaddressed they lead to localized failure of the overlay. Monitoring and follow-up

After the overlay is placed, the treated segment should enter a ten‑year service plan that includes specific inspections and maintenance actions. At five years the pavement receives a clean‑and‑seal operation, during which roughly one‑quarter of the longitudinal joints are crack‑sealed and the shoulders are treated with a seal coat or micro‑surface applied at about 500 linear feet per mile for the applicable pavement types. In addition, the segment becomes part of the routine annual pavement‑condition assessments conducted through Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (videologging) as required by the STAMPP program; if needed, Falling Weight Deflectometer testing may be performed to verify structural response and confirm that the overlay meets its design objectives. Throughout this period the Bureau of Maintenance Operations continuously performs quality‑assurance checks on the condition data to maintain reliability and accuracy. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (p. 75), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

After an asphalt overlay the pavement must be monitored for a range of distress indicators that signal the overlay has become insufficient and that further maintenance, repair or rehabilitation is required. When five years have passed a clean‑and‑seal operation should be performed, and if about one quarter of the longitudinal joints are cracked a crack‑sealing treatment is called for; similarly shoulders on the specified pavement types (Type 1, 1S, 3, 4, 6 or 6S) should receive seal coat or micro‑surfacing at roughly five hundred linear feet per mile. The appearance of reflective cracking that propagates through the new surface, widespread alligator cracking not confined to wheel paths, rutting, bleeding, raveling, active pumping, joint spalling, edge deterioration, faulting, or any sign of water intrusion such as stripping, potholes, loss of subgrade support and freeze‑thaw damage also indicate that the overlay no longer provides adequate performance. Annual automated condition surveys that show recurring or worsening distress—cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling/weathering, joint spalling or faulting—beyond established matrix thresholds further confirm the need for additional work. When patching or base repair on the overlaid section approaches or exceeds ten percent of the pavement area, the treatment shifts from preservation to rehabilitation and a more extensive intervention such as another overlay is typically required. Persistent skid‑resistance problems identified by friction testing also trigger corrective actions beyond the original overlay. In cases where the existing overlay rests on a concrete base and visual inspection or core analysis reveals material deficiencies, or when the Condition Survey Method calculates an effective existing thickness that indicates insufficient residual capacity, the remaining asphalt must be removed before any new overlay can be placed. Finally, if the STAMPP system flags the segment for surface treatment because observed distresses such as shoulder drop‑off, rutting or fatigue cracking have exceeded condition thresholds, this recommendation likewise signals that additional maintenance, repair or rehabilitation is needed.
