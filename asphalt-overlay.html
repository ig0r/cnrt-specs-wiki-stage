Asphalt Overlay

Part of: Overlay is-subtopic-of
Asphalt Overlay is a topic under Overlay.
asphalt-overlayasphalt-overlaysasphalt-overlay-s
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-29

Asphalt overlay is a preservation treatment applied when an existing pavement no longer offers adequate structural support or a satisfactory riding surface, addressing both load‑carrying and functional deficiencies[1][2]. By adding a new flexible wearing course—typically one‑and‑a‑half to two inches thick—over a prepared, stable base, the overlay increases structural capacity while restoring smoothness, skid resistance and proper drainage[1][2]. The treatment requires removal or repair of surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch, milling or profiling to correct geometric irregularities, sealing joints and stabilizing unstable slabs, and often installing an interlayer to mitigate reflective cracking before the hot‑mix asphalt is placed[1][2]. When these preparatory steps are satisfied, the overlay provides a uniform wearing layer that extends the pavement’s service life by roughly ten years while improving ride quality and reducing future maintenance needs[1]

PennDOT publications covering “Asphalt Overlay” also include the following subtopics: Saw and Seal.

Saw and Seal serves as a specialized joint preparation method for Asphalt Overlay by limiting water infiltration at transverse and longitudinal joints through groove geometry and sealant placement. [1][2] The treatment ensures the overlay’s structural integrity is maintained by preventing crack propagation that would otherwise accelerate deterioration under the new wearing course. [1][2]

Purpose and treatment objectives

Asphalt overlay is employed when an existing pavement no longer provides adequate structural support or a satisfactory riding surface, indicating both load‑carrying and functional deficiencies. The treatment targets structural problems such as thin or weakened layers, spalled concrete sections, crushed joints, and unstable or rocking slabs that would otherwise give non‑uniform support to the new asphalt layer. Surface‑level distresses including longitudinal joint cracks, fatigue cracking, general cracking, potholes, loss of surface integrity, embedded debris, and localized depressions are also remedied by the overlay. Geometric irregularities that impair ride quality—wheel ruts, wheel rutting, bumps, corrugations, local depressions, irregular longitudinal profiles, shoulder drop‑off, and deviations from the designed elevation or cross‑slope—are corrected through milling, profiling, and placement of a leveling or scratch course before the overlay is placed. The overlay also addresses functional problems such as reduced surface friction and inadequate drainage by restoring a smooth, high‑friction wearing surface and re‑establishing proper cross‑slope to promote water removal. Design thresholds cited in the guides require that surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch be filled, joint faulting greater than a quarter‑inch be milled, and the finished surface meet a smoothness tolerance of one‑eighth inch over twelve feet. By adding a new flexible layer whose thickness is determined through non‑destructive testing or other accepted procedures, the overlay increases structural capacity while providing a uniform wearing course that restores serviceability. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (p. 75), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221), Ch. 14 (p. 227); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 317–350), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Across the guides, the primary purpose of an asphalt overlay is to extend the useful service life of an existing pavement by adding a new structural layer that increases load‑carrying capacity while also restoring functional performance and repairing existing distress [1][2]. The overlay accomplishes this by providing additional thickness—typically one and a half to two inches, with deeper milling allowed when needed to eliminate rutting or other material problems—and by correcting structural deficiencies identified in the field [1]. Prior to placement, all surface defects such as cracks, potholes, and unstable slab fragments must be repaired using approved materials including hot‑mix asphalt, cementitious mixes, or comparable repair products, ensuring that the underlying pavement can support the new layer [2]. Stabilization of loose concrete slabs and targeted patching based on measured field conditions are also required to anticipate any further deterioration that could occur between design and construction [1][2]. By addressing these deficiencies, the overlay not only restores ride quality, skid resistance, and overall support but also slows subsequent deterioration and reduces future maintenance cycles, achieving both rehabilitation and preservation goals [1]. The design life assumed for a typical overlay is ten years, supported by a maintenance regime that includes a five‑year clean and seal operation, sealing roughly twenty‑five percent of longitudinal joints and applying about five hundred lineal feet per mile of seal coat or micro‑surface treatment on shoulders [1]. Consequently, the objective combines repair of existing damage, restoration of functional performance, and extension of pavement service life while also moderating further degradation [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–75), Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 6 (pp. 137–139), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 317–319), SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Distress characterization and causes

Asphalt overlays are employed when pavement distress stems from a combination of water infiltration, inadequate support, and structural deficiencies that compromise the existing surface’s ability to carry loads. [1][2] Inadequate drainage is highlighted as a primary mechanism because surface and subsurface water that cannot be removed accumulates, allowing moisture to penetrate the pavement structure and trigger stripping, potholes, crack propagation and freeze‑thaw damage. [1] Weak support arises when the base or subbase material is deteriorated, insufficient, or when concrete slabs are unstable and rock under normal traffic, producing non‑uniform bearing conditions beneath the overlay. [1][2] The guides note that premature mid‑slab cracking of jointed concrete pavements may be caused by inadequate subbase material, while unstable slab fragments that move visibly indicate a loss of uniform support. [1][2] Load transfer deficiencies at transverse joints exacerbate distress; crushed or deteriorated joints prevent effective dowel action, leading to slab movement or faulting that undermines the overlay’s foundation. [1] Excessive joint faulting greater than ¼ inch is specifically called out as a threshold beyond which structural capacity is reduced and must be milled before overlay placement. [2] Age‑related oxidation of the existing asphalt or concrete produces alligator cracking across the surface, signaling diminished structural integrity that accelerates further deterioration. [1] Aggregate‑related problems such as the presence of Vanport Limestone and chemical reactions like alkali‑silica reaction can degrade the pavement matrix and lower skid resistance, contributing to distress. [1] Heavy traffic loads generate rutting, bleeding, and additional stresses that speed the progression of these failure modes. [1] Surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch act as direct pathways for water infiltration and compromise drainage, requiring removal or patching before an overlay can be effective. [2] To mitigate the identified mechanisms, the guides require stabilization or repair of unstable slabs, replacement of failed base material, sealing and patching of cracks, restoration of load‑transfer joints, and provision of adequate subdrainage to create a uniform, stable platform for the new asphalt layer. [1][2] Additionally, installation of a geotextile interlayer overlapped 8 ± 2 inches, extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, and terminated in a drainable layer is prescribed to preserve free drainage while enhancing support. [2] The combined preparatory actions—removing failed areas, repairing or replacing base/subbase, correcting surface irregularities, and ensuring proper drainage—address the underlying mechanisms of water‑related deterioration, weak support, material aging, and load‑induced stresses, thereby allowing the overlay to perform as intended.

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Applicability and treatment selection

An asphalt overlay may be placed over any existing pavement when the purpose is to remedy structural deficiencies, surface deterioration, or poor rideability, provided that the underlying layer offers a stable platform [1]. For concrete pavements, a falling‑weight deflectometer test must be performed before design and adequate subdrainage must exist where subsurface drainage problems are present, while for existing asphalt pavements the surface should be essentially sound with no more than about two percent requiring patching and the project should not aim for a structural upgrade [1]. The overlay thickness is required to be at least one‑and‑a‑half inches in compacted form unless special approval is obtained, and both surface and subsurface drainage must be good or be upgraded as part of the work [1][2]. The underlying base must be stable so that a thin overlay can perform adequately, and traffic volumes must fall within the average daily traffic limits specified in the applicable tables [1]. During construction the pavement surface must be dried, cleaned of dirt, gravel, vegetation, oil, and loose debris, with cracks sealed and potholes repaired to produce a drainable, depression‑free surface before any interlayer or overlay is placed [2]. Traffic is restricted to construction vehicles until the interlayer and overlay have cured; no traffic may travel on the tack coat or interlayer material during this period [2]. Installation is limited by weather: the asphalt material must be applied when ambient conditions meet the manufacturer’s recommendations, with material temperature maintained between 290 °F and 320 °F (and for fiberglass mesh grid surfaces between 40 °F and 140 °F), and rain must not occur before the overlay is placed [2]. The interlayer system must be stored dry and protected from ultraviolet light, precipitation, chemicals, and sparks to preserve its integrity until placement [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 96–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 317–319)

An asphalt overlay should be rejected whenever the existing pavement cannot be brought to a condition of stability, proper leveling, sealing and adequate patching before the new surface is placed. [1] Obvious base failures, insufficient structural thickness, or drainage problems that cannot be corrected also render an overlay inappropriate, directing designers toward base repair, milling, scratch‑and‑leveling, heater‑planing, or a full structural overlay rather than a thin wearing course. [1] When the existing surface is concrete and exhibits unstable slabs or slab fragments that move under traffic, a new asphalt layer would lack uniform support and therefore must be preceded by stabilization or repair of those slabs. [2] Joint faulting that exceeds the limits set for the chosen interlayer—greater than one‑quarter inch for a geotextile interlayer or more than one‑half inch for an asphalt interlayer—requires milling, scratching, or other joint treatment before overlay placement. [2] Surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch must be filled with approved patching material; if they cannot be adequately repaired, alternative methods such as slabjacking, concrete patching, or flexible‑base replacement should be employed instead of a simple overlay. [2] If more than about two percent of the pavement area requires patching, or if core testing reveals material deficiencies in the existing layer, the flawed overlay must be removed and a design for an asphalt‑concrete (AC) overlay over Portland cement concrete should be used rather than a standard AC overlay. [1] Preservation‑type overlays are limited by thickness: on flexible pavements any design exceeding two inches falls outside the preservation envelope unless a mill‑and‑overlay is undertaken to correct rutting or material problems, and on concrete decks overlays thicker than four‑and‑a‑half inches with a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing course—or five inches with a 12.5 mm mix—are not permissible as preservation treatments. [1] When the required patching exceeds roughly ten percent of the surface, the project is no longer cost‑effective for preservation and must shift to more extensive rehabilitation or reconstruction. [1] If distress has progressed beyond early‑stage symptoms, routine maintenance actions such as crack sealing, joint sealing, spall repair, micro‑surfacing, diamond grinding, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, cold in‑place recycling, or full‑depth patching are preferred over an overlay. [1] Finally, when the functional life of the pavement has been exhausted and its structural capacity cannot be restored by any overlay, total reconstruction is the appropriate response.

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 10 (p. 183), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Condition assessment and timing

Determining whether an asphalt overlay is warranted begins with a systematic visual and instrumented survey of the existing pavement, including review of automated condition surveys such as the STAMPP video‑logging system that record cracking, rutting, edge deterioration and other fatigue distresses for all NHS segments and a representative sample of non‑NHS roads. The recorded distress data are entered into the RMS database and processed through treatment matrices, which generate surface‑treatment recommendations that flag candidate sections for overlay consideration. A subsequent field visual inspection confirms drainage conditions, base integrity, surface roughness that may require milling or a leveling course, friction levels, shoulder condition, and verifies that the pavement is stable with no movement under roller pressure. During this inspection crews also evaluate slab stability, noting any slabs or fragments that move or rock under normal traffic because such instability would produce non‑uniform support for an overlay and must be repaired before placement. Joint faulting is measured, with joints exhibiting more than a quarter‑inch of vertical displacement requiring milling, and faults exceeding half an inch triggering the placement of a scratch course or grinding to reduce the fault before the overlay can be applied. Surface defects are quantified, and any defect larger than four square inches in area and deeper than one inch must be filled prior to overlay installation, especially when a geotextile interlayer is used. Structural capacity of the existing pavement is assessed using non‑destructive testing, principally Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) measurements performed on the current concrete or flexible pavement, which provide the structural coefficient and subgrade characteristics needed to calculate effective existing thickness via the condition‑survey method. When visual signs suggest material problems in the existing asphalt, representative cores are extracted and sent to a laboratory where layer thickness, bond quality between the asphalt and any underlying concrete or brick base, and overall integrity are confirmed; these results also determine the required milling depth before overlay placement. The effective existing thickness derived from FWD data and core analysis is compared with design criteria, and an overlay is limited to sections where less than two percent of the surface requires patching and where the proposed overlay thickness does not exceed two inches for preservation projects. If low skid resistance is identified, friction testing is performed and remedial measures are incorporated into the overlay design to achieve acceptable skid‑resistance levels. Traffic loading information such as average daily traffic or equivalent single axle loads may be consulted to ensure that the selected overlay thickness will satisfy future performance requirements. Only after drainage adequacy, structural capacity, surface condition, joint faulting, defect repair, bond integrity, patching extent, friction, and anticipated traffic have been verified through these inspections, measurements, and tests does the engineer conclude that an asphalt overlay is required and can delineate its exact location and design thickness. [1][2]

An asphalt overlay is indicated once the existing pavement no longer satisfies serviceability criteria but still possesses sufficient structural integrity to be preserved rather than reconstructed. The trigger occurs when observable distresses—such as rutting, fatigue cracking, loss of ride quality, inadequate friction, poor drainage, or deteriorated shoulders—have advanced to a level that surface treatment is required, as signaled by the STAMPP pavement‑management system’s surface‑treatment recommendation. For flexible pavements the existing base must be sound, with less than two percent of the area needing patching and adequate (or upgraded) surface and subsurface drainage; non‑destructive testing may be employed when structural capacity is uncertain. In composite asphalt‑over‑concrete sections a typical overlay is scheduled around the twenty‑year service point after milling the wearing course and shoulders, patching roughly eight percent of the concrete area, cleaning and sealing all joints, and then placing a 60 lb/yd² asphalt layer four to four‑and‑half inches thick, with a repeat at about thirty‑five years. For flexible pavements exhibiting rutting or material failures, milling followed by an overlay of one‑and‑a‑half to two inches (deeper if needed to correct the defect) should be applied early in the maintenance cycle before more advanced distresses appear. Overlays thicker than two inches are reserved for cases where additional structural capacity is required and are not considered preservation treatments. The overlay thickness is sized using the AASHTO design method or an equivalent non‑structural preservation approach to provide the necessary increase in structural strength while restoring ride quality. Materials and repair details

Before an asphalt overlay can be placed the existing surface must be stable, non‑moving under rollers, fully leveled, sealed and patched; it also must be dry, clean, free of loose debris and all cracks sealed before any interlayer is installed [1][2]. For concrete pavements any asphalt that overlies a slab is stripped away, after which the concrete is fractured either by crack‑and‑seat for unreinforced slabs or break‑and‑seat for reinforced slabs into pieces ranging from twelve to twenty‑four inches and never exceeding thirty inches in any dimension [2]. Surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch are filled with an approved patching material—acceptable options include asphalt‑based, cementitious, or comparable repair mixes—and compacted to a flat, tight surface [1][2]. When a geotextile reinforcement is used it is laid flat over the prepared surface, overlapped on each side by eight inches plus or minus two inches, limited to three layers in any location, and extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge [2]. A tack coat is then sprayed at manufacturer‑approved rates—0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard for geotextile fabric, 0.02 to 0.05 gallons per square yard for fiberglass mesh, or 0.19 to 0.28 gallons per square yard for composite grid—with the spray extending six inches beyond each side while the coating remains tacky [2]. If an asphalt interlayer is specified, a nominal one‑inch depth of the designated Superpave mix is placed before the overlay [2]. Saw‑cuts are made directly above existing transverse joints within plus or minus one inch; when the total overlay thickness reaches three and a half inches or more each cut is one‑eighth inch wide and deep to at least one and a half inches or one‑third of the overlay thickness, whichever is greater [2]. After the asphalt cools below 140 °F the cut reservoirs are sealed with clean, dry faces using a sealant applied only when ambient temperature is between 40 °F and 90 °F; a tape bond‑breaker may be used but must not project more than one‑eighth inch, and the sealant is filled to just below the pavement surface [2]. The first lift of the overlay must achieve a minimum compacted thickness of one and a half inches, with the hot mix maintained between 290 °F and 320 °F and placed at a rate of 0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard, covering a width equal to the reinforcement fabric plus six inches on each side [1][2]. Placement must occur no later than seven calendar days after milling or within sixty days after rubblizing and seating operations, and the milled surface must be profiled to a smoothness tolerance of one‑eighth inch over twelve feet with cross‑slope matching the same tolerance [2]. Any wrinkles larger than one inch are sliced and overlapped flat, and spalled or dislodged sections are cleaned, tack‑coated with emulsified asphalt, filled with an identical overlay mix, leveled and compacted to match adjacent pavement; damaged interlayer material is removed and replaced, and joint faulting greater than half an inch is corrected by a scratch course or milling [1][2]. Design thickness follows the AASHTO method in Chapter 10 (or Chapter 12 for preservation); required overlay thickness is determined from future traffic needs, effective existing thickness via condition‑survey, and then applied as prescribed [1]. For rigid pavements the overlay shall be four inches thick when a 9.5 mm wearing‑course mix is used or four‑and‑a‑half inches with a 12.5 mm mix; for flexible pavements the required thickness is one‑and‑a‑half inches with a 9.5 mm mix or two inches with a 12.5 mm mix, and Interstate Superpave wearing courses may not be less than 1½ inches without prior approval [1]. Thin overlays as thin as one inch can be placed using a 6.3 mm grade, while ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses are limited to two inches for non‑structural use [1]. The wearing course may be hot‑mix, warm‑mix or Superpave restricted‑performance asphalt and may incorporate reclaimed asphalt pavement (RAP); typical aggregate gradations include nominal sizes of 25, 19, 12.5 and 9.5 mm, with the thin‑overlay grade using a 6.3 mm aggregate and a PG 64E‑22 binder, and the fine‑grade 9.5 mm course using its compatible binder [1]. When additional structural capacity is required a binder course is added, its thickness being determined by non‑destructive testing or other acceptable procedures [1]. To mitigate reflective cracking an interlayer such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grid, or composite reinforcing grid may be installed between the existing surface and the new overlay, provided it is thermally stable and placed per manufacturer instructions [1][2]. Edge protection is achieved with a safety‑edge taper of approximately thirty degrees cut to a minimum depth of one‑and‑half inches and not exceeding five inches; this treatment adds roughly one percent to material quantities [1]. Shoulder improvements may include Type 6 or Type 7 paved shoulders, surface‑friction treatments, and seal coat or micro‑surface applied at about 500 linear feet per mile, while drainage structures, continuous base drains, guide rails and other ancillary items are adjusted or replaced in accordance with the overlay design [1]. Routine preservation calls for a clean‑and‑seal every five years, crack sealing on roughly twenty‑five percent of longitudinal joints, and a seal coat or micro‑surface applied to shoulders at approximately 500 linear feet per mile [1].

The performance of an asphalt overlay depends first on the condition of the existing pavement substrate, which must be stable under rolling, uniformly leveled, sealed and patched before any new mix is placed [1][2]. For flexible pavements the total area requiring patching should not exceed two percent, while concrete slabs may tolerate up to ten percent unless a cost‑benefit justification dictates otherwise, and these limits are verified through non‑destructive testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements [1]. Drainage capability, shoulder integrity, surface friction and the adequacy of the underlying base are evaluated during design, and any deficiencies in drainage or failed bases must be repaired prior to overlay placement [1]. Cracked, spalled or uneven concrete must be cleaned, seated and stabilized; joints that have lost load transfer are saw‑cut within ±1 inch, faulted more than ¼ inch are milled for geotextile interlayers, and faulting exceeding ½ inch requires a scratch course or milling when an asphalt interlayer is used [2][1]. The size of fractured concrete pieces retained in place is limited to lengths between 12 and 24 inches with no dimension greater than 30 inches, and the average elastic modulus of the broken layer must fall within the allowable range specified for the project [2]. Overlay thickness must satisfy structural strength requirements while respecting compatibility constraints; under Superpave Restricted Performance specifications wearing and binder courses must meet minimum thicknesses, and thin overlays less than 1½ inches are allowed only when the existing surface is asphalt, structurally sound (patching <2 %), and drainage is good or will be upgraded [1]. On concrete bases a scratch‑overlay thickness is limited to 4½ inches with a 9.5 mm Superpave mix and to five inches with a 12.5 mm mix, provided the slab has been verified as sound by FWD testing [1]. The interlayer material must be thermally stable, physically durable and chemically compatible with the selected binder; for example a PG 64E‑22 binder is appropriate for standard 6.3 mm courses to create a strong internal adhesive or mechanical bond that mitigates reflective cracking [1]. When a geotextile interlayer is used it must be overlapped eight inches (±2), limited to three layers, kept clean and damp but not saturated, and its surface temperature may not exceed 120 °F; the fabric is placed after milling joints with faulting greater than ¼ inch [2]. Asphalt interlayers employ a one‑inch Superpave mix, and the underlying concrete must be seated so that vertical deflection under a 50‑ton roller is less than 0.75 inch before placement of the overlay [2]. Prior to placing the asphalt mixture an emulsified tack coat or prime coat is applied at a rate of 0.22 to 0.28 gal per square yard to ensure complete saturation of any reinforcement, and the binder temperature must be maintained between 290 °F minimum and 320 °F maximum (or within manufacturer‑specified limits) [2]. No single batch of material may remain at pouring temperature for more than six hours, and ambient air temperature during sealing operations must be between 40 °F and 90 °F to avoid premature distress [2]. Timing constraints require that the milled base be cleaned and the first overlay course placed within seven calendar days of milling (traffic may not travel on a milled surface for more than six days), and after rubblizing and seating the overlay must be installed within sixty days, avoiding exposure to freezing conditions [2]. The first lift of asphalt over an interlayer system must achieve a minimum compacted thickness of 1½ inches, and the finished surface must meet smoothness tolerances of 1/8 inch in twelve feet and edge matching within the same tolerance [2][1]. Aggregate selection also influences performance; aggregates that are Vanport limestone or prone to alkali‑silica reaction must be avoided because they can impair skid resistance and accelerate distress, especially on concrete pavements where such issues may require earlier overlay intervention [1].

Treatment procedures

The guides require an initial evaluation of the existing pavement to verify drainage adequacy, structural capacity, and surface condition, which may include field‑loaded deflection testing on concrete decks and coring to determine milling depth. Areas that cannot provide a stable, uniform base—such as spalled concrete, crushed joints, rutting, or other deteriorated sections—are removed by milling, heater‑planing, or scratching to a depth of at least one and a half inches, often extending to two inches while preserving a minimum of one and a half inches of sound asphalt when it exists. Full‑depth patches are placed with approved Portland cement concrete or an asphalt base course, and slab movement or faulting is corrected by stabilization or jacking before overlay work proceeds. Where subsurface drainage problems are identified, adequate subdrainage is installed to protect the new surface. For concrete pavements any existing asphalt overlay is stripped, the concrete is rubblized, backfilled with No 2A subbase in lifts not exceeding six inches, compacted, and seated with a fifty‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller until vertical deflection under the roller is less than three‑quarters of an inch. The remaining surface is swept clean of loose debris using a power broom, vacuum truck, or equivalent equipment, and its temperature is maintained below one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit prior to tack coat application. A pre‑placement meeting with the manufacturer’s technical representative is held before any overlay work begins. When milling is required, a self‑propelled machine capable of cutting a full lane up to twelve feet wide with automatic grade and slope control removes bumps, corrugations, wheel ruts and other irregularities while establishing the specified elevation and cross‑slope; all milled material and loose debris are removed immediately. Traffic may not travel on the freshly milled surface for more than six calendar days, and the first overlay course must be placed within seven days of milling, preferably on the same day, with rough‑road signage installed if placement is delayed. A calibrated distributor truck then applies a non‑tracking tack coat of asphalt at a temperature between two hundred ninety and three hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit, spreading it uniformly so that the width extends six inches beyond each edge of any reinforcement fabric. While the tack coat is still tacky, an interlayer reinforcement such as geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh or composite grid is laid with one‑inch sheet overlaps and eight‑inch edge overlaps, kept damp but not saturated, broomed or rolled for full contact, and any damaged sections are replaced. If a separate leveling or scratch course is required, a minimum one‑inch thick layer of suitable material is placed to provide a uniform working platform before the binder and/or wearing course is laid. The hot mix asphalt overlay is then placed to the design thickness—typically a minimum of one and a half inches for the first lift—and compacted with rollers until density targets measured by the Percent Within Limits quality measure are achieved. Safety‑edge tapers or reflective‑crack mitigation interlayers may be installed according to manufacturer guidance before final compaction. After the overlay has cooled below one hundred forty degrees Fahrenheit, each existing transverse joint is located, saw‑cut directly above it within plus or minus one inch width and depth, cleaned, dried, fitted with bond‑breaker tape, and sealed with manufacturer‑approved material placed just below the pavement surface. Excess tack coat may be sanded off before overlay placement, and no additional curing period is prescribed beyond these finishing operations; traffic may be reopened once the material has set and has not been exposed to freezing conditions. The entire overlay must be completed within the schedule limits specified—typically within sixty calendar days after rubblizing and seating for concrete decks, and with the first course placed within seven days of milling for asphalt bases—to ensure proper bond and performance. Implementation of an asphalt overlay begins after a pre‑placement conference that defines the work sequence and confirms that the existing pavement is stable, meaning it does not move when a roller passes over it.[1][2] Any required base repairs are completed first, followed by surface preparation which may involve sweeping or vacuuming loose debris with a power broom, vacuum truck, or comparable device, then milling, heater‑planing, or applying a scratch or leveling course to correct transverse and longitudinal profile irregularities and provide a level platform.[1][2] When a rigid concrete base is present, the existing asphalt overlying it must be milled away using a self‑propelled milling machine capable of cutting a full lane up to twelve feet wide in one pass and equipped with automatic longitudinal, transverse, grade and slope control; the same equipment may also be used for concrete milling if required.[2] After milling, the surface is cleared of loose material and traffic is prohibited on the milled area for more than six calendar days, with the first overlay course required to be placed within seven calendar days of the start of milling, weather stoppages excluded from that count.[2] If placement cannot occur on the same day as milling, rough‑road warning signs and possibly reduced speed limits are installed to protect traffic while the surface remains exposed.[2] When an interlayer material is specified, it is placed after a calibrated distributor truck spray bar applies a tack coat while the tack coat remains tacky; the interlayer is then laid with mechanical or manual laydown equipment designed to produce a smooth, wrinkle‑free surface and compacted with a rubber‑tire roller, or a 50‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller when rubblizing concrete.[1][2] For geotextile fabrics the material must be kept flat, overlapped eight inches, free of folds, and its surface temperature must not exceed 120 °F before placement, with misting or other approved cooling methods used as needed.[2] The asphalt overlay itself is placed at paving temperatures between 290 °F and 320 °F (or within any specific grid limits) and no rain is permitted until the interlayer has dried; sealing material may be applied only when ambient air temperature is between 40 °F and 90 °F unless an exception is granted.[2] Thin overlays less than 1½ in on Interstate facilities require prior approval and must comply with pavement depth limits set out in Table 9.5, while all placement must occur under the weather conditions prescribed in Publication 408, Specifications, Section 413.3(b).[1] Traffic through the work zone is managed according to temporary traffic‑control guidelines; detours are coordinated with the District Traffic Unit when feasible and any required maintenance on the detour route is performed before it is opened to traffic.[1] The volume of traffic allowed on the overlay construction area must not exceed the average daily traffic limits specified in the applicable tables, and only construction vehicles may travel on fresh material, interlayer, or milled surfaces until they have cured or been sealed.[1][2] For projects involving rubblizing of concrete, mechanical or sonic vibratory equipment breaks the slab, a pneumatic‑tired roller seats the broken material, and the overlay must be completed within sixty calendar days while ensuring that the seated surface is never exposed to freezing conditions.[2] Throughout the operation joint referencing is required; a quality‑control plan must detail personnel, equipment, and methods for locating existing transverse joints, with saw‑cutting permitted only after the asphalt course has cooled below 140 °F.[2] Any damage caused by construction traffic must be repaired at no cost to the Department, reinforcing the restriction that public or equipment traffic is barred from newly placed geotextile, interlayer, or milled surfaces until they are properly cured.[2]

Quality and workmanship

Before placement the asphalt mix must be positively identified and labeled in accordance with ASTM D4873, kept dry and protected during storage, and placed within the temperature range specified for the selected interlayer system; the mix itself is required to conform to the designated Superpave wearing‑course specifications, including the correct binder such as PG 64E‑22 for a 6.3 mm course and the appropriate aggregate gradation, while any approved interlayer material must be compatible and thermally stable with the tack coat applied as called for [1][2]. The inspector first verifies that the existing pavement is stable—showing no movement under a roller—and that it has been properly leveled, sealed, patched, and provided with adequate drainage; full‑depth patches are placed where needed, slab movement is stabilized, subdrainage is installed, and all transverse joints are sawed and sealed, with FWD testing confirming the structural condition of any concrete pavement prior to design, while the surface is also cleaned of dirt, gravel, vegetation, oil, and loose material and any cracks, potholes or defects larger than four square inches by one inch deep are sealed with an approved patching material [1][2]. When a geotextile interlayer is used its edges must overlap eight inches plus or minus two inches, no more than three layers may be installed, it must extend at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, remain free of folds, tears and excess moisture, and its surface temperature must not exceed 120 °F before the overlay is laid [2]. Existing transverse joints are located and referenced within a plus‑or‑minus one‑inch tolerance; saw cuts are made directly over those joints to widths and depths dictated by the total overlay thickness, joint faces are cleaned and dried before any sealing material is applied, sealant is heated and poured within the manufacturer’s safe temperature range, not held at pouring temperature for longer than six hours, bond‑breaker tape extends no more than one‑eighth inch up the face, and the sealant is filled to within a quarter of an inch below the pavement surface [1][2]. During placement the installer follows the technical representative’s instructions using calibrated equipment such as a distributor‑truck spray bar, achieves proper coverage width, lays the mix smoothly without wrinkles, provides overlaps of at least one inch, and ensures that the finished overlay meets contract‑specified thickness—four inches with a 9.5 mm mix or 4½ inches with a 12.5 mm mix for rigid pavements, and 1½ inches with a 9.5 mm mix or two inches with a 12.5 mm mix for flexible pavements—or the thickness calculated by the AASHTO design procedure—and that the minimum compacted first lift is not less than 1½ inches [1][2]. Compaction and density are verified using the Percent Within Limits methodology, typically through core testing, and continuous joint sealing and the construction of safety edges to the required depth and taper are also inspected [1]. After placement the finished surface is examined for uniform thickness, adequate compaction, proper bond to the underlying base—confirmed by coring where necessary—and sufficient friction; smoothness, edge continuity and cross‑slope are measured to verify that deviations do not exceed one‑eighth inch over twelve feet, and the overlay is checked for absence of traffic damage or excess material [1][2]. The completed work must also satisfy design intent for shoulder support, drainage improvements, moisture‑barrier performance, and show no signs of reflective cracking or other distress that could reduce service life [1]. Vertical edges around utilities are eliminated each day, with warning signs posted if the overlay cannot be placed on the same day [2].

The existing pavement must be stable, showing no movement under a roller, and it must be clean, dry, properly leveled, sealed and patched before any new surfacing is placed, with less than two percent of the surface requiring patching and adequate surface or upgraded drainage. All cracks, potholes or other defects larger than four square inches by one inch shall be repaired, unstable slabs stabilized, and the surface free of gouges, grooves and ridges. When the overlay is to be placed over a concrete slab, every transverse joint and any previously patched joint must be sawed and sealed so that the new asphalt forms a uniform load‑transfer surface, with saw cuts located within plus or minus one inch of the original joint line. If a geotextile separation layer is specified, it must lie flat, may not exceed three plies at any location, must extend at least four inches beyond the pavement edge and its edges must overlap by eight inches plus or minus two inches; the material temperature prior to placement shall be below 120 °F. Where an asphalt interlayer is required, a nominal one‑inch Superpave mix is placed, and any joint faulting greater than one‑half inch must be corrected with a scratch course or milling before the overlay proceeds. A tack coat applied at the manufacturer‑approved rate of between 0.22 and 0.28 gallons per square yard shall cover the entire prepared surface and extend six inches beyond each side of reinforcement, while the liquid asphalt temperature must be maintained between 290 °F and not exceed 320 °F during placement. The overlay thickness must satisfy both minimum design requirements derived from the AASHTO structural analysis and programmatic limits; for a first overlay on rigid pavement the layer shall be at least four inches thick when a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing‑course mix is used or four and a half inches with a 12.5 mm mix, while flexible pavements require a minimum of one and a half inches with a 9.5 mm mix or two inches with a 12.5 mm mix, and the first compacted lift over any interlayer system may not be less than one‑and‑a‑half inches. On Interstate projects the Superpave wearing course may not be placed thinner than one‑and‑a‑half inches without prior approval, and preservation projects impose upper limits of two inches on existing flexible pavements and no more than four and a half inches (9.5 mm) or five inches (12.5 mm) on concrete surfaces; any leveling course used must be at least one inch thick. The hot‑mix asphalt must be placed so that the in‑place density falls within the target field range of ninety‑two percent to ninety‑eight point five percent, with contractors typically aiming for values near the middle of this band to avoid reduced payment factors. The mix itself shall conform to the specified Superpave design, such as a 6.3 mm wearing course using a PG‑64E‑22 binder, and any safety edge incorporated into the overlay must be built with a taper between twenty‑six and forty degrees, centered on thirty degrees, and a total depth limited to one‑and‑half through five inches. Milling of the existing surface must produce a profile that does not deviate more than one‑eighth inch in smoothness or cross‑slope over any twelve‑foot length, and the milled base must be completely free of loose material before traffic is allowed; traffic may not travel on a milled surface for more than six calendar days, and the first asphalt course must be placed within seven calendar days of the start of milling, with rough‑road warning signs required if placement does not occur the same day. When joint reservoirs are sealed, the sealing material shall be filled to a level between one‑eighth and one‑quarter inch below the pavement surface, tape may not extend more than one‑eighth inch up the face, and sealing may only be performed when ambient temperature is between forty and ninety degrees Fahrenheit. After rubblizing and seating, vertical deflection measured under a fifty‑ton pneumatic‑tired roller must be less than three‑quarters of an inch; any area exceeding this limit requires undercutting and recompaction, and the overlay must be completed within sixty calendar days of finishing the rubblizing work. Core inspection shall verify that milling depths have removed rutting or defective material, and concrete patching areas must remain at or below ten percent of the total pavement surface unless a cost‑effectiveness justification is provided. Prior to opening the finished overlay to traffic the surface shall be swept clean of all loose material that could create a hazard or be redeposited into the texture. [1][2]

Performance and service life

An asphalt overlay is applied after a corrective scratch or leveling course (or milling) that levels the existing pavement and creates a stable base for the new wearing surface. By placing a binder course of appropriate thickness, determined through non‑destructive testing or other design procedures, the overlay adds structural capacity that raises durability and allows the pavement to better resist future loads. The treatment also incorporates drainage improvements, base repairs, enhanced surface friction, and shoulder rehabilitation, which together improve functional performance and safety. The new surface restores condition by eliminating cracking, rutting, edge deterioration and other distresses, while the use of Superpave thin‑asphalt mixes—such as 6.3 mm or 9.5 mm fine grades—provides a smoother ride, better drainage, and superior skid resistance compared with emulsified‑asphalt alternatives. When an interlayer such as geotextile fabric or reinforcing grid is included, reflective cracking is mitigated and moisture intrusion is reduced, further extending the overlay’s durability. Because the overlay adds only about 1½ to 2 inches of material, it can be employed as a cost‑effective preservation activity early in the maintenance cycle, before advanced distresses develop. To preserve the restored condition and functionality, a clean‑and‑seal operation is recommended at five years, crack sealing on roughly one‑quarter of the longitudinal joints, and a seal coat or micro‑surface treatment along shoulders at a rate of 500 ft per mile for specified pavement types. These maintenance actions address surface wear and cracking throughout the design period. Taken together, these structural enhancements and periodic upkeep provide an expected service life of approximately ten years, extending the functional lifespan of the roadway beyond that achievable with the original surface and delaying the need for full reconstruction. Reflective cracking is identified as the most frequently observed distress in asphalt overlays, especially when the overlay rests on concrete slabs that exhibit movement, faulting, or inadequately treated transverse joints, allowing cracks to propagate from the underlying slab into the new surface [1]. Alligator cracking originating from age‑related oxidation of an existing pavement can also transmit through the overlay, while other distresses such as rutting, bleeding, raveling, stripping, potholes and localized failures commonly appear when moisture infiltrates the structure or subgrade support is compromised [1]. The severity of these failures is governed by the condition and type of pre‑existing distress, overlay thickness, climatic influences—particularly freeze‑thaw cycles—traffic loading, drainage quality, and whether moisture‑resistant interlayers or adequate surface preparation have been provided [1]. Poor adhesion resulting from placing reinforcement fabric on a wet substrate or sealing joints over damp reservoirs leads to premature delamination of the overlay [2]. Likewise, failure to keep transverse joint saw cuts within a ± 1‑inch tolerance creates defective joints that become pathways for crack propagation and loss of bond strength [2]. Improper handling of interlayers—such as allowing wrinkles larger than one inch, providing less than the required one‑inch overlap, or using torn reinforcement after milling—produces non‑uniform thicknesses that encourage cracking and reduce bond performance [2]. Temperature control is critical; the asphalt mix must be placed within a 290 °F–320 °F window and sealing material applied only when ambient air lies between 40 °F and 90 °F, otherwise bonding is inadequate [2]. The first lift of an overlay must achieve a minimum compacted thickness of 1½ inches; thinner layers fail to supply sufficient structural support and are prone to early distress [2]. Milling operations that leave grooves, ridges, gouges or cause tearing of the underlying mat generate irregularities and chunky material that undermine the overlay’s performance, while spalling or dislodgement of unsound pavement that is not promptly patched with an emulsified tack coat and matching mix leads to surface failures exceeding the smoothness tolerance of 1⁄8 inch in 12 feet [2]. Timing constraints also dictate failure risk: traffic may not be allowed on a milled surface for more than six calendar days, and the first overlay course must be placed within seven days of milling; exceeding either interval increases exposure to damage and can trigger contract penalties [2]. Collectively, these substrate conditions, joint preparation, interlayer handling, temperature and timing controls, thickness requirements, milling quality, moisture intrusion, and environmental loading determine whether an asphalt overlay will perform as intended or suffer recurring distresses [1][2].

Monitoring and follow-up

After the overlay is placed, the treated segment should enter a ten‑year service plan that includes specific inspections and maintenance actions. At five years the pavement receives a clean‑and‑seal operation, during which roughly one‑quarter of the longitudinal joints are crack‑sealed and the shoulders are treated with a seal coat or micro‑surface applied at about 500 linear feet per mile for the applicable pavement types. In addition, the segment becomes part of the routine annual pavement‑condition assessments conducted through Automated Pavement Condition Surveys (videologging) as required by the STAMPP program; if needed, Falling Weight Deflectometer testing may be performed to verify structural response and confirm that the overlay meets its design objectives. Throughout this period the Bureau of Maintenance Operations continuously performs quality‑assurance checks on the condition data to maintain reliability and accuracy. [1]
