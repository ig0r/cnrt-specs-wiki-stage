Freeze-Thaw Cycle

Part of: Frost Heave is-subtopic-of
Freeze-Thaw Cycle is a topic under Frost Heave.
freeze-thaw-cyclefreeze-thaw-cyclesfreeze-thaw-cyclesfreeze-thaw
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

The Freeze‑Thaw Cycle is the repetitive freezing and subsequent thawing of water that has entered a pavement’s structural section, causing the moisture to expand when it freezes and contract when it thaws, thereby generating internal expansion‑contraction stresses[1]. These cyclic stresses are a primary mechanism that initiates and propagates cracks, potholes, stripping and other forms of distress, making water intrusion a major cause of overall pavement deterioration[1]. Because this phenomenon is inherent to moisture‑filled pavements, design practices such as providing a thicker overlay combined with a thermally stable interlayer are used to delay cracking in harsh environments[1].

Material purpose and applicability

The Freeze‑Thaw Cycle is the repeated freezing and thawing of water that has entered a pavement’s structural section, causing the water to expand when it freezes and contract when it thaws; this expansion‑contraction action creates stress that can propagate cracks, produce potholes and other forms of distress, and accelerate overall pavement deterioration. Because water intrusion is identified as a major cause of such damage, a thicker overlay with an interlayer system may be used to delay cracking longer in harsh environments. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

Composition and characteristics

The freeze‑thaw cycle in pavement is defined by water that has entered the structural section of the pavement; when temperatures drop this water freezes and expands, and when it thaws it contracts, producing expansion‑contraction stresses within the material. This repeated expansion and contraction is identified as a primary mechanism that contributes to crack propagation, potholes, stripping, and other forms of distress. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120)

In full‑depth reclamation, adding chloride salts such as calcium chloride or magnesium chloride changes the mix composition and lowers the freezing temperature of the reclaimed material, thereby enhancing resistance to freeze‑thaw damage while also providing a modest strength gain from particle cementing. The effectiveness of this treatment depends on proportioning; calcium chloride is applied as at least a 35 % solution at a rate of 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of reclaimed depth, which directly influences the degree of freeze‑thaw resistance achieved. Incorporating emulsified asphalt alters the binder composition and improves water‑related damage resistance and fatigue performance, both of which help mitigate deterioration caused by repeated freezing and thawing cycles. For natural coarse aggregates used in pavement structures, a low absorption value of less than 2 % automatically classifies the material as freeze‑thaw resistant, eliminating the need for additional testing under AASHTO T 103 Procedure A. When the absorption of natural aggregates exceeds 2 %, resistance may still be accepted if either the sodium sulfate soundness loss is less than 16 % or the AASHTO T 103 freeze‑thaw loss after 25 cycles does not exceed 7.0 %. The specification further limits absorption to no more than 3.0 % for gravels and 2.0 % for all other aggregates; exceeding these values requires the concrete mix to satisfy a performance test of 300 freeze‑thaw cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation and/or a durability factor greater than 80. For lightweight aggregate, the dynamic modulus may not decrease beyond the limit set by AASHTO T 161 after 300 cycles, the aggregate freeze‑thaw loss must stay below 25 %, and its absorption factor cannot exceed 2.5 %. Processing requirements such as crushing a minimum percentage of gravel particles or limiting steel slag expansion to less than 0.50 % also control susceptibility to freeze‑thaw damage. Source selection and proportioning are critical; for example, up to 20 % non‑granulated slag may be included in granulated slag mixes without compromising durability. In cement concrete, proper proportioning requires adjusting the batch weight of aggregate to compensate for free water on the aggregate, which helps maintain the required durability under freeze‑thaw cycling. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 126–128); [2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–663)

Key material properties

The freeze‑thaw performance of pavement systems is governed by a combination of physical, mechanical, chemical and thermal properties. [1] In overlay applications (section 5.12) any material that limits water intrusion acts as a moisture barrier, and the interlayer must be thermally stable and physically durable so that it does not lose strength or deform during repeated temperature fluctuations. [1] For reclaimed pavement material (section 5.15) the addition of calcium chloride or magnesium chloride chemically lowers the freezing temperature of the mix, directly enhancing resistance to freeze‑thaw damage, while the same chlorides also provide a modest gain in strength through particle cementation; asphalt‑based stabilization further improves moisture durability and fatigue resistance under traffic loading. [1] In fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays (section 10.10) the mechanical attribute most cited is the residual flexural strength after 150 cycles (f150), which typically ranges between 100 psi and 225 psi for concrete overlay applications, and this residual strength together with flexural fatigue capacity, post‑cracking strength, toughness and controlled air content governs how well the overlay tolerates repeated freezing and thawing. [1] The fibers increase flexural fatigue performance, post‑crack strength and overall toughness, while appropriate air‑entraining admixture maintains suitable air content to accommodate freeze‑thaw stresses. [1] Within the aggregate specifications (SECTION 703—AGGREGATE > 703.2 COARSE AGGREGATE) freeze‑thaw durability is governed primarily by water absorption and related soundness characteristics; natural coarse aggregates that absorb less than 2 % are deemed freeze‑thaw resistant, provided their sodium sulfate soundness loss does not exceed 16 %. [2] For lightweight aggregates the specification limits the Aggregate Absorption Factor to a maximum of 2.5 % and caps the allowable freeze‑thaw mass loss of the aggregate itself at 25 %, while concrete made with such aggregate must not lose more than the prescribed decrease in dynamic modulus after 300 freeze‑thaw cycles as measured by AASHTO T 161. [2] Steel slag stockpiles are required to demonstrate a total expansion of less than 0.50 % before use, reflecting the need to control expansive chemical reactions that can be triggered by moisture cycling. [2] In the cement‑concrete provisions (SECTION 704—CEMENT CONCRETE > 704.1 GENERAL) aggregate water absorption must not exceed 3.0 % for gravels and 2.0 % for all other aggregates, and mixes with higher‑absorbing aggregates may be allowed provided specimens survive at least 300 freeze‑thaw cycles per ASTM C666 with less than 0.04 % dilation or achieve a durability factor greater than 80, indicating resistance to mechanical expansion during freezing and thawing. [2] The concrete composition also requires an air‑entraining admixture. [2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–128), Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–663)

In SECTION 703—AGGREGATE > 703.2 COARSE AGGREGATE, the freeze‑thaw performance of coarse aggregate is evaluated by the tests specified in Section 703.2(c); aggregates that absorb less than 2 % are automatically classified as freeze‑thaw resistant and are exempt from AASHTO T 103 Procedure A testing, while aggregates absorbing more than 2 % must satisfy either a sodium‑sulfate soundness loss under 16 % or an AASHTO T 103 freeze‑thaw loss after 25 cycles of the coarse fraction that does not exceed 7.0 %. For lightweight aggregate, resistance is quantified by the decrease in dynamic modulus after 300 freeze‑thaw cycles using AASHTO T 161 Procedure B and by a percent expansion measured with PTM No. 525 that must be less than 25 %. In SECTION 704—CEMENT CONCRETE > 704.1 GENERAL, when coarse aggregates exceed the standard absorption limits, the specification permits their use only if mix‑design specimens are subjected to the ASTM C666 freeze‑thaw test and achieve at least 300 cycles while exhibiting less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80. [2]

[2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–663)

Behavior and mechanisms

In overlay applications (section 5.12 OVERLAYS), freeze‑thaw cycles cause the expansion and contraction of water that is trapped in the pavement, which can initiate cracking; a thicker overlay combined with an interlayer system may delay cracking longer in harsh environments. In full‑depth reclamation treatments (section 5.15 FULL DEPTH RECLAMATION TECHNIQUES), the use of chloride additives such as calcium or magnesium chloride results in some gain in strength because the chemicals cement particles, and they will also lower the freezing temperature of the reclaimed material, thereby improving resistance to the effects of the freeze‑thaw cycle; emulsified asphalt stabilization offers several advantages including improving resistance to water‑related damage and improved fatigue resistance to loading. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–128)

In overlay applications (section 5.12) the freeze‑thaw cycle influences pavement primarily through the physical expansion of water when it freezes and its contraction upon thawing within the pavement matrix; this volumetric change generates stresses that can open or propagate cracks, an effect amplified whenever moisture has already intruded into the structural section because water intrusion is identified as a major cause of distress. In full‑depth reclamation techniques (section 5.15) chemical mechanisms are introduced by adding calcium chloride or magnesium chloride, which cement particles to increase reclaimed material strength and, more importantly, depress the freezing point so that the mix resists the damaging expansion‑contraction associated with freeze‑thaw cycles; generally calcium chloride is applied using a minimum 35 % solution at a rate of 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of depth reclaimed. A complementary mechanical mechanism in those reclamation mixes is provided by emulsified asphalt, which reduces water‑related damage and enhances fatigue resistance to loading, thereby helping to limit cracking and potholing under repeated temperature swings. The susceptibility of coarse aggregate to freeze‑thaw damage is governed primarily by its ability to absorb water, with aggregates absorbing less than 2 % being deemed freeze‑thaw resistant; aggregates exceeding that absorption must meet either a sodium sulfate soundness level under 16 % or exhibit no more than a 7.0 % loss after 25 AASHTO T 103 cycles, criteria that reflect the physical pore saturation and chemical stability of the mineral matrix during wet‑dry cycling. Mechanical performance of aggregates is evaluated by measuring the decrease in dynamic modulus of concrete after 300 freeze‑thaw cycles (AASHTO T 161) and by limiting aggregate loss to a maximum of 25 % under the PTM No. 525 test. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–128); [2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–655)

Durability and deterioration

In overlay applications, water that penetrates the structural portion of a pavement is identified as a major cause of loss of subgrade support, stripping of asphalt from aggregate, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation, and overall pavement deterioration because freeze‑thaw cycles cause expansion and contraction of water within the pavement; providing a moisture barrier and using a thicker overlay with an interlayer may delay cracking longer in harsh environments. For full‑depth reclamation, repeated freezing and thawing degrade reclaimed pavement by promoting moisture infiltration and cyclic expansion–contraction, which leads to loss of strength and cracking; incorporating calcium chloride or magnesium chloride into the mix lowers the material’s freezing point and improves resistance to freeze‑thaw damage while also providing a modest strength gain from particle cementing, and calcium chloride is generally applied using a minimum 35 % solution at a rate of 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of reclaimed depth. Emulsified asphalt stabilization further enhances durability by increasing resistance to water‑related damage and raising fatigue resistance under traffic loading. When fiber‑reinforced concrete is used for pavement overlays, the principal durability concern during repeated freezing and thawing is potential loss of residual strength that can lead to crack propagation and reduced load‑transfer efficiency, yet the material retains significant residual strength even after a large number of cycles; macrofibers improve flexural fatigue performance, post‑cracking strength and toughness of the hardened matrix, thereby limiting crack growth. The load‑transfer efficiency of fiber‑reinforced concrete can be increased by up to 30 % compared with plain concrete when crack widths exceed about 1.0 mm, and tighter joint openings of less than 0.5–1.0 mm further enhance durability under freeze‑thaw action. The durability of coarse aggregate under freeze‑thaw cycling is linked to its water absorption and soundness; for lightweight aggregate the specification caps the Aggregate Absorption Factor at a maximum of 2.5 % and limits the freeze‑thaw loss of the aggregate to no more than 25 % as measured by PTM No. 525, and concrete made with such aggregate must not lose more than the allowable decrease in dynamic modulus after 300 freeze‑thaw cycles (AASHTO T 161). Natural coarse aggregates that absorb less than 2 % are deemed freeze‑thaw resistant and are exempt from AASHTO T 103 testing, whereas those absorbing more than 2 % must either achieve a sodium sulfate soundness level below 16 % or exhibit a freeze‑thaw loss after 25 cycles that does not exceed 7.0 %; high absorption or failure to meet these thresholds indicates susceptibility to cracking and loss of stiffness during repeated freezing and thawing. In cement concrete, aggregate water absorption is limited because excessive moisture can accelerate freeze‑thaw damage; gravels must not exceed 3.0 % absorption and other aggregates 2.0 %. When higher‑absorption aggregates are used, the concrete mix must still satisfy a durability test of at least 300 freeze‑thaw cycles per ASTM C666 while showing less than 0.04 % dilation or achieving a durability factor greater than 80; these criteria address the primary durability concerns for cement concrete subjected to repeated freezing and thawing. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–128), Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–663)

In the overlay specifications, incorporating a moisture‑barrier interlayer such as geotextile paving fabric or composite reinforcing grids can limit water entry and thereby reduce susceptibility of pavement to freeze‑thaw deterioration [1]. Providing a thicker overlay also helps delay cracking longer in harsh environments because it keeps water out of the structural section [1]. Exposure conditions that permit water intrusion, including inadequate surface or subsurface drainage, springs, or standing water, increase the risk of freeze‑thaw damage as the cycles cause expansion and contraction of water within the pavement [1]. Applying calcium chloride or magnesium chloride as a stabilizer to reclaimed material lowers its freezing temperature and improves resistance to freeze‑thaw deterioration; the recommended practice calls for a minimum 35 % solution applied at 0.1 to 0.15 gal per square yard for each inch of depth reclaimed [1]. Emulsified asphalt stabilization reduces susceptibility by enhancing resistance to water‑related damage and increasing fatigue resistance, especially when combined with granular material, cement, or lime [1]. Including macrofibers—synthetic or steel—in the concrete mix at 0.2 % to 0.5 % by volume provides residual strength after many freeze‑thaw cycles and limits crack growth, while tighter joint and crack openings of less than 0.5 to 1.0 mm further improve durability, whereas wider cracks or the absence of fibers make pavements more vulnerable [1]. Low water absorption in coarse aggregate reduces freeze‑thaw damage; natural aggregates that absorb less than 2 % are considered freeze‑thaw resistant and therefore need not be tested under AASHTO T 103 [2]. Aggregates that absorb more than 2 % become more vulnerable unless they satisfy either a sodium sulfate soundness loss of less than 16 % or an AASHTO T 103 loss after 25 cycles that does not exceed 7.0 % [2]. The specification also limits the allowable freeze‑thaw loss for aggregate to a maximum of 25 % according to PTM No. 525 [2]. For cement concrete, absorptions above 3.0 % for gravels or 2.0 % for other aggregates increase the risk of deterioration unless the concrete mix passes a durability test; low‑absorption aggregates help reduce that risk [2]. When higher‑absorption coarse aggregate is used, the mix must demonstrate at least 300 freeze‑thaw cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation or achieve a durability factor greater than 80 to mitigate susceptibility [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–128), Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–663)

Material interactions and compatibility

In full‑depth reclamation the addition of calcium chloride lowers the freezing temperature of the reclaimed mix and improves its resistance to freeze‑thaw damage while also providing some strength gain through particle cementing; the chemical is normally applied as a minimum 35 % solution at a rate of 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of depth reclaimed [1]. Emulsified asphalt stabilization used in the same reclamation process further mitigates water‑related deterioration and fatigue cracking and is reported to be compatible with other additives such as granular material, cement and lime, allowing the combined system to better withstand freeze‑thaw cycling [1]. In fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays the freeze–thaw cycle does not eliminate the benefit of macrofibers; even after many cycles the material retains significant residual strength, enabling the fibers to continue providing flexural fatigue resistance, post‑cracking strength and toughness while offering no gain in compressive or plain flexural strength, indicating that the fiber‑matrix bond helps preserve overlay durability against cracking and crack widening [1]. For coarse natural aggregates used in cement concrete, freeze‑thaw durability is linked to water absorption; aggregates that absorb less than 2 % are considered freeze‑thaw resistant and are exempt from AASHTO T 103 testing, whereas aggregates with absorption exceeding 2 % may still be accepted if either their sodium‑sulfate soundness loss is less than 16 % or their freeze‑thaw loss after 25 cycles does not exceed 7.0 % [2]. Lightweight aggregates are subject to a maximum 25 % loss in the PTM No. 525 freeze‑thaw resistance test and must limit the decrease of dynamic modulus as defined by AASHTO T 161 after 300 cycles, showing that aggregate susceptibility to repeated freezing and thawing governs acceptance criteria for mixes containing such aggregates [2]. In cement concrete mix design, coarse aggregates may not exceed 3.0 % absorption for gravels and 2.0 % absorption for all other aggregates unless the mix‑design specimens satisfy 300 freeze‑thaw cycles according to ASTM C666 with less than 0.04 % dilation and/or a durability factor greater than 80, directly linking aggregate porosity to concrete’s resistance to repeated freezing and thawing [2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 126–128), Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–663)

Coarse aggregates that contain deleterious substances such as reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica or hydrated iron oxide, or that have visible clay lumps, are at risk of freeze‑thaw damage because these materials can promote expansion and cracking when water freezes. When the aggregate absorption exceeds 2 %, freeze‑thaw resistance is only assured if the sodium sulfate soundness level is less than 16 % or if the AASHTO T 103 freeze‑thaw loss after 25 cycles does not exceed 7.0 %; otherwise the material may deteriorate under repeated freezing and thawing. [2]

[2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–655)

Influence of production and placement

Curing a steel‑slag aggregate stockpile by reworking it, soaking the material completely with water and then maintaining a uniformly moist condition for at least six months can reduce total expansion to less than 0.50%, which meets the freeze‑thaw resistance requirement; if the expansion remains above that limit, an additional two months of curing may be required before retesting. This controlled curing practice directly affects the aggregate’s performance under freeze‑thaw cycles. [2]

[2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–655)

In overlay construction, allowing water to enter the structural layer—by omitting a moisture‑blocking interlayer, leaving less than the preferred minimum of 1 ½ inches of existing asphalt when milling, or creating debonded zones that require full removal of the overlay—increases freeze‑thaw damage over time. Poor surface preparation and insufficient drainage also promote water intrusion, which is a primary driver of pavement deterioration under freeze‑thaw cycles. In fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, construction‑related defects that create or enlarge cracks—such as slab movement, slab misalignment, plastic shrinkage cracking and subsequent crack widening—and improperly timed or insufficiently deep sawcut contraction joints are identified as primary factors that can compromise long‑term performance under repeated freeze‑thaw cycles. Incorporating macrofibers, which reduce crack widths and the number of cracks, helps mitigate these construction‑induced vulnerabilities. In SECTION 703—AGGREGATE > 703.2 COARSE AGGREGATE, construction‑related variations that raise aggregate water absorption above the 2 % threshold or cause the aggregate to fail the prescribed soundness limits strongly influence freeze‑thaw durability; aggregates absorbing more than 2 % must have a sodium sulfate soundness loss less than 16 % or an AASHTO T 103 freeze‑thaw loss after 25 cycles not exceeding 7.0 %, and the aggregate’s own freeze‑thaw resistance is limited to a maximum loss of 25 % per PTM No. 525. In SECTION 704—CEMENT CONCRETE > 704.1 GENERAL, the specification restricts absorption to not exceed 3.0 % for gravels and 2.0 % for all other aggregates, and any higher‑absorption aggregate must be shown by mix‑design specimens to survive 300 freeze‑thaw cycles with less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80; failure to meet these thresholds is a construction defect that strongly influences long‑term behavior. The inclusion of deleterious substances such as clay, silt, reactive chert, gypsum, iron sulfide, amorphous silica, hydrated iron oxide, excessive fines or glassy particles can further compromise long‑term performance. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 115–120), Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–663)

Selection, specification, and improvement

The specification in SECTION 703—AGGREGATE states that natural coarse aggregate is considered freeze‑thaw resistant when its absorption is less than 2 percent, and in that case no AASHTO T 103 testing is required; if the absorption exceeds 2 percent the aggregate must either have a sodium‑sulfate soundness loss of less than 16 percent or exhibit a freeze‑thaw loss after 25 cycles that does not exceed 7.0 percent for the coarse fraction. For lightweight aggregate, freeze‑thaw performance is evaluated by AASHTO T 161 Procedure B based on the decrease in dynamic modulus after 300 cycles and the aggregate may not lose more than 25 percent as measured by PTM No. 525, while its absorption factor must not exceed 2.5 percent. In SECTION 704—CEMENT CONCRETE, the baseline absorption limits are 3.0 percent for gravels and 2.0 percent for all other aggregates; coarse aggregates that exceed these limits may still be specified if the concrete mix design demonstrates durability under freeze‑thaw action, meaning test specimens must survive 300 freeze‑thaw cycles as defined by ASTM C666 while showing less than 0.04 percent dilation or achieving a durability factor greater than 80. [2]

[2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–663)

For overlay applications, the specification recommends placing an asphalt paving interlayer—such as a geotextile paving fabric, a fiberglass mesh grid, or a composite reinforcing grid—between the existing surface and the new overlay because these materials provide a moisture barrier that limits water intrusion; the interlayer must be compatible with the asphalt and installed with the proper binder application rate to ensure a strong internal bond, and when an interlayer is used the overlay should have a minimum thickness of 1 ½ inches, which together can delay cracking longer in harsh environments. In full‑depth reclamation, applying calcium chloride or magnesium chloride at a minimum concentration of 35 % solution and dosing at 0.1–0.15 gal per square yard for each inch of reclaimed depth is recommended to cement particles for modest strength gain and depress the material’s freezing point, thereby enhancing resistance to freeze‑thaw damage. Stabilizing the reclaimed layer with emulsified asphalt, optionally combined with granular material, cement or lime as prescribed in the PennDOT mix design, improves water‑related durability and fatigue performance. For concrete overlays, incorporating macrofibers—synthetic or steel—at a typical dosage of 0.2 % to 0.5 % by volume provides residual strength (f150) that typically ranges between 100 and 225 psi after many freeze‑thaw cycles; adjusting water‑reducing admixtures or other mixture modifications can compensate for slump loss, while modifying the air‑entraining admixture during trial batches helps maintain proper air content, and conducting fiber‑reinforced concrete trial batches to verify fresh‑property specifications and the specified residual strength serves as a quality‑control step that together improve freeze‑thaw performance. In Section 703 the specification calls for coarse aggregates that are hard, durable and have low water absorption; natural coarse aggregates whose absorption is less than 2 % are automatically considered freeze‑thaw resistant. Natural coarse aggregates with an absorption greater than 2 % must meet either a sodium sulfate soundness level of less than 16 % or an AASHTO T 103 loss after 25 cycles that does not exceed 7.0 %. Lightweight aggregate may be used provided it conforms to AASHTO M 195 and satisfies the absorption‑factor limit of 2.5 % together with a freeze‑thaw resistance limit of no more than a 25 % loss in PTM No. 525 testing. Alternative durable sources such as blast furnace slag, steel slag (with total expansion under 0.50 % per PTM No. 130) or granulated slag are permitted when they meet the listed durability and traceability requirements. Quality control is ensured by petrographic analysis (PTM No. 518), soundness testing (PTM No. 510), abrasion testing (AASHTO T 96) and the assignment of a PennDOT Certified Aggregate Technician to conduct all required tests per Bulletin 14. In Section 704 cement concrete is required to include an air‑entraining admixture, and may also contain water‑reducing, retarding or supplementary cementitious material to enhance durability against freeze‑thaw damage. The specification limits aggregate moisture absorption to not exceed 3.0 % for gravels and not exceed 2.0 % for all other aggregates; higher‑absorption aggregates are permitted only when mix‑design specimens satisfy 300 freeze‑thaw cycles according to ASTM C666 with less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80. Use of lightweight coarse aggregate or slag as a supplementary cementitious material is allowed, provided their unit weights and specific gravities are verified by testing. [1][2]

Performance evaluation and implications

For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, laboratory slab tests that expose macro‑fiber reinforced concrete to repeated freeze‑thaw cycling demonstrate that the material retains significant residual strength even after a large number of freeze/thaw cycles, providing direct evidence of its durability under such conditions. [1] Field observations compiled in the National Concrete Overlay Explorer and documented studies of FRC overlays further confirm superior long‑term performance compared with plain‑concrete overlays when subjected to freeze‑thaw environments. [1] For lightweight aggregate, evaluation of freeze‑thaw susceptibility relies on laboratory testing using the Freeze‑Thaw Resistance of Concrete test that measures decrease of dynamic modulus after 300 cycles in accordance with AASHTO T 161 Procedure B. [2] The Freeze‑Thaw Resistance of Aggregate test (PTM No. 525) must not exceed a loss of 25 %. [2] Natural coarse aggregates are exempt from testing when their absorption is less than 2 % as determined by AASHTO T 103 Procedure A; when absorption exceeds 2 %, they are judged freeze‑thaw resistant only if either their sodium sulfate soundness (PTM No. 510) is less than 16 % or their AASHTO T 103 loss after 25 cycles does not exceed 7.0 %. [2] When coarse aggregates exceed the usual absorption limits, acceptance is based on laboratory freeze‑thaw testing of mix‑design specimens that must survive 300 cycles as defined by ASTM C666 and exhibit less than 0.04 % dilation or a durability factor greater than 80. [2]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 651–663)

The expansion and contraction of water within the pavement structure during each freeze‑thaw cycle accelerates cracking and other distress, and water intrusion into the structural section is identified as a major cause of loss of subgrade support, stripping, potholes, localized failures, crack propagation, and overall deterioration. To counter these moisture‑driven mechanisms, design often calls for thicker overlays combined with an interlayer system, which may delay cracking longer in harsh environments and thereby reduce maintenance needs. In full‑depth reclamation, the use of calcium chloride or magnesium chloride lowers the freezing temperature of the reclaimed material, improving its resistance to the repeated expansion‑contraction stresses of freeze‑thaw cycles; design specifications therefore call for a minimum 35% calcium chloride solution applied at 0.1 to 0.15 gallons per square yard for each inch of depth reclaimed. Emulsified asphalt stabilization adds resistance to water‑related damage and fatigue, influencing construction choices and future maintenance requirements. Fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays retain significant residual strength even after a large number of freeze/thaw cycles, allowing the overlay to continue carrying load despite repeated freezing and thawing, which helps limit crack growth and prolong serviceability. Fibers improve the flexural fatigue performance, the post‑cracking strength and the toughness of the hardened concrete, but they do not improve the compressive and flexural strengths; this enables designers to consider thinner slabs or longer design lives in freeze‑thaw environments while providing additional structural capacity, reducing crack widths, maintaining joint or crack load transfer efficiency, and extending the pavement’s serviceability through reduced crack deterioration. The specifications require that coarse aggregates possess sufficient resistance to loss of strength and cracking caused by repeated freeze‑thaw cycles because such behavior directly influences pavement durability. Natural aggregates that absorb less than 2% are automatically deemed freeze‑thaw resistant and therefore bypass additional testing. For aggregates whose absorption exceeds 2%, the specifications require either a sodium sulfate soundness level less than 16% or an AASHTO T 103 loss after 25 cycles not to exceed 7.0%, and the aggregate freeze‑thaw resistance test (PTM No. 525) limits loss to a maximum of 25%. In Section 704, when coarse‑aggregate absorption is greater than the standard limits—3.0% for gravels or 2.0% for other aggregates—the specification permits use only if mix‑design specimens survive 300 freeze‑thaw cycles according to ASTM C666 with dilation less than 0.04% or attain a durability factor above 80. Meeting these thresholds guides material selection during design, supports construction quality control, and informs long‑term maintenance planning. [1][2]
