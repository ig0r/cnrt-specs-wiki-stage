Overlay

Part of: Rehabilitation is-subtopic-of
Overlay is a topic under Rehabilitation.

Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-29

Asphalt Overlay and Concrete Overlay serve as the primary application methods for adding structural capacity or functional surface layers to deteriorated pavements. [1][2] The Leveling Course provides a geometrically uniform platform when existing profiles fail smoothness criteria, while Overlay Design calculates the necessary thickness based on traffic loads and existing structural numbers. [1][2] Surface Preparation ensures adequate bonding by cleaning and repairing defects prior to placement, and Structural Overlay specifically addresses insufficient load-bearing capacity through stabilized interlayers. [1][2] Resurfacing applies these techniques as a thin maintenance treatment when underlying structures remain sufficient but surface performance has declined. [1]

PennDOT publications covering “Overlay” also include the following subtopics: Asphalt Overlay, Concrete Overlay, Leveling Course, Overlay Design, Resurfacing, Structural Overlay, and Surface Preparation.

Purpose and design objectives

Overlay is applied when an existing pavement no longer supplies adequate structural support, smoothness, or friction for safe and economical operation, a condition identified through non‑destructive testing, core sampling, or other design procedures that reveal insufficient structure layer coefficient, excessive cracking, rutting, slab rocking, joint faulting greater than one‑half inch, loss of stiffness, shoving, slippage, thermal cracking, or failure to meet the 3R performance standard [1][2]. When these deficiencies cannot be remedied by routine maintenance, an overlay provides the missing structural thickness while retaining the underlying pavement and subgrade as a support layer [1][2]. The design first quantifies the effective capacity that remains in the existing layers and then adds sufficient new binder or structural course; for concrete decks this typically means a Portland‑cement concrete layer 2.5 to 4 inches thick for structural overlays, with a minimum bonded thickness of one inch required to achieve a vertical pull‑bond strength of at least 250 psi and a compressive strength of no less than 3,600 psi before traffic is reopened [1][2]. When only ride quality is needed, a thin asphalt wearing course of 1 to 1½ inches may be placed, increasing to two inches or more if the 3R criterion is not satisfied, while still providing the structural capacity required for anticipated future traffic loads often specified as at least 2,500 ESALs per day [1]. Prior to placement, uneven or failed areas are milled, scratched, or leveled; a minimum one‑inch leveling course corrects cross‑slope and ensures uniform support, and joint cleaning and sealing restore load transfer on concrete decks [1][2]. Deep surface defects larger than four square inches and one inch deep must be patched, unstable slabs and slab fragments that rock visibly are stabilized, and irregularities such as bumps, corrugations, and wheel ruts are removed to provide a uniform base for the overlay [2]. Early sawing of joints is required once the concrete has hardened sufficiently to prevent random cracking, with joint panels limited to eighteen times the overlay thickness and an aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5 [2]. The finished surface must meet prescribed friction and smoothness criteria, including a profile tolerance of one‑eighth inch in twelve feet and appropriate cross‑slope to ensure drainage and adequate skid resistance throughout its service life [2][1]. By raising the grade line away from moisture zones, the overlay also protects unsuitable subgrade materials from exposure to construction traffic and prevents water pressure buildup at the tire/pavement interface, thereby extending the pavement’s serviceability and durability [1].

An overlay must first satisfy functional requirements by preserving the existing roadway performance while improving removal of surface and subsurface water, providing a level and stable riding platform through scratch‑and‑leveling or milling, and delivering sufficient tire‑pavement friction and texture to prevent hydroplaning at the posted speed limit. [1] The finished surface is required to be smooth, with a tolerance of no more than one‑eighth inch over twelve feet and matching the adjacent pavement edge within the same deviation, and an abrasive‑finish sand application of at least 2.2 lb per square yard must be used to achieve adequate friction. [2] A positive cross‑slope as shown on the plans must be provided so that water drains properly, and limited auxiliary or weaving lanes up to three thousand feet may be incorporated provided the main travel lanes remain uninterrupted for at least ten thousand contiguous feet, excluding bridges. [1][2] Structurally the design must add an appropriate layer thickness, with a minimum overall thickness of one inch and, for asphalt overlays, a binder course typically required, while thin‑bonded concrete overlays range from 2½ to 4 inches and conventional bonded concrete overlays are four inches or more, all meeting or exceeding the calculated pavement thickness for future traffic using rigid‑pavement ESAL factors and prescribed tables. [1][2] The underlying base must be stable, uniform, and free of joints that have lost load transfer; any failed portions are to be removed and replaced, and a higher level of support permits a thinner overlay. [1][2] Panel dimensions shall not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness, the aspect‑ratio shall not exceed 1.5, joints must be cut to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness with a minimum width of one‑eighth inch, and longitudinal joints are to be avoided in wheel paths. [2] Edge slump is limited to one‑quarter inch next to shoulders and one‑eighth inch next to adjacent lanes, the bond between overlay and existing pavement must achieve an average of at least 250 psi with no individual result below 160 psi, and the concrete must attain a compressive strength of at least three thousand psi (or three‑thousand six hundred psi for certain mixes) before traffic is permitted. [2][1] Durability objectives require that existing surface deterioration be remedied before placement through sealing, patching, or base repair, avoiding poor‑quality patch work, and incorporating interlayer systems such as asphalt paving interlayers to retard reflective cracking and moisture intrusion. [1][2] Joint and crack openings should be limited to roughly 0.5–1.0 mm, slab movement, misalignment, plastic shrinkage cracking, and overall crack widening must be restrained, and non‑structural cracks deeper than one‑quarter inch or wider than 0.007–0.016 in shall be sealed with approved filler. [1][2] The fresh overlay must be cured to a traffic‑ready condition within four hours or when a Schmidt hammer reading reaches three thousand psi, placement temperatures must be maintained between 65 °F and 90 °F with the underlying surface temperature below one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit, and temperature change during insulation removal shall not exceed forty degrees Fahrenheit per hour. [2][1] During the prescribed cure period the overlay must be protected from rain and traffic, with curing actions beginning within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and continuing until the required strength is achieved. [2] Macro‑fibers are to be added at a dosage that yields the design residual strength value f150, verified by ASTM C1609‑12 after fourteen days, and the concrete mixture must limit chloride ion penetration to low, very low or negligible levels within fifty‑six days, meeting either ≤2 800 coulombs per AASHTO T 277 or resistivity thresholds of ≥15.6 kΩ·cm for 4×8 in cylinders and ≥12.3 kΩ·cm for 6×12 in cylinders. [2] Safety is achieved by increasing surface friction, providing a strong safety edge for vehicles drifting off the roadway, ensuring adequate shoulder performance, preserving existing guide‑rail height, and prohibiting vehicular traffic on any course during cure while maintaining deck, air and component temperatures of at least sixty degrees Fahrenheit and a forecast that does not drop below fifty‑five degrees Fahrenheit within eight hours after placement. [1][2] Adequate drainage is provided through geotextile edges extending at least four inches beyond the pavement, dowel basket assemblies are anchored so movement does not exceed a quarter inch, and heavy equipment is restricted until the overlay has achieved its minimum strength. [2][1] Serviceability goals demand restoration of acceptable ride quality, an eight‑year minimum and twenty‑year maximum structural design life, reduction of future maintenance cycles, and accommodation of projected traffic loads—including at least 2 500 ESALs per day for concrete overlays and high‑traffic criteria of more than 25 000 ADT and ten million ESALs over a twenty‑year period—while maintaining friction and texture throughout the intended service period. [1][2] The final screed setting must produce a smooth riding surface free of pock marks, ridges, tears or other defects, meeting surface‑profile tolerances and edge‑slump limits, and providing at least one inch of overlap coverage so that the repaired segment performs indistinguishably from the existing roadway. Scope and applicability

Overlay is considered an appropriate rehabilitation technique for essentially any existing pavement that exhibits structural deficiencies, surface distress, poor ride quality, low friction, inadequate drainage, or insufficient shoulder support, and it can also be applied to bridge decks once the deck’s dead‑load capacity has been verified by the responsible bridge engineer [1]. The guides recognize a wide range of pavement types as candidates for overlay, including flexible asphalt‑concrete surfaces, rigid Portland‑cement concrete slabs of jointed plain, jointed reinforced, or composite AC/PCC construction, historic brick pavements, and sound concrete bridge decks that have cured at least twenty‑eight days and been prepared to the required shot‑blasting and cleaning standards [1][2]. For flexible asphalt pavements the preferred solution is a structural asphalt overlay; thin resurfacing overlays of one to one‑and‑half inches may be placed with or without a leveling course, while thicker layers of two inches or more are employed when the 3R criterion cannot be satisfied or deeper milling is needed to remove rutting or other material problems, and preservation‑type overlays are generally limited to two inches unless additional thickness is required for structural gain [1]. When an asphalt‑surfaced pavement is to be converted to a rigid surface, either a thick Portland‑cement concrete skin of four to eight inches or a thin layer of two‑and‑a‑half to four inches can be bonded directly to the prepared base, with overlays thinner than three inches using No. 67 coarse aggregate, macrofibers added to achieve the required residual strength (f150), and mix designs meeting low chloride‑ion penetration limits as defined by AASHTO T277 or T358 within a fifty‑six‑day curing period [2]. For rigid concrete pavements a bonded‑rigid concrete overlay is appropriate when the existing slab is in fair to excellent condition, typically requiring at least three inches of milled surface and often incorporating synthetic or steel macrofibers at dosages of 0.2–0.5 % by volume; such fiber‑reinforced overlays are commonly designed between three and six inches thick to accommodate heavy traffic and variable support conditions [1]. When the concrete slab is moderately to severely deteriorated, an unbonded concrete overlay is recommended, especially where slabs are unstable or visibly rocking, with stabilization of cracked areas and provision of uniform support—often using a geotextile interlayer when joint faulting exceeds one‑quarter inch—and the existing pavement can serve as a subbase [1][2]. Additional preparation for both bonded and unbonded concrete overlays includes scarifying to the required depth, shot blasting or water blasting to clean the surface, joint sawing and sealing where appropriate, and adherence to temperature constraints for placement (concrete temperature between sixty‑five and ninety degrees Fahrenheit) as well as post‑placement strength criteria (average bond strength of at least one hundred seventy‑five psi after seven days, no individual test below one hundred sixty psi, and traffic reopening when compressive strength reaches three thousand six hundred psi) [2]. Specialty overlay materials are employed under specific traffic and project criteria: stone‑matrix asphalt (SMA) overlays of 1.5 to 2.0 inches with tack coat are used in long‑life programs at the 20‑ and 35‑year rehabilitation points, and similar shoulder overlays extend support where full‑depth shoulders are required [1]; cement‑reinforced mix asphalt binder (CRMAB) may be used on high‑volume Interstate corridors exceeding roughly twenty‑five thousand average daily traffic, cumulative ESALs greater than ten million over a twenty‑year design period, and treatment areas of at least fifty thousand square yards, limited to a maximum thickness of 2½ inches and not justified for shoulders unless the lane and shoulder are paved simultaneously [1]; thin Superpave asphalt overlays are permitted on non‑Interstate routes when the underlying surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than two percent patching, drainage conditions are satisfactory or can be improved, and no structural upgrade is needed [1]. Epoxy‑based overlays are an option for sound concrete bridge decks that meet preparation criteria (ICRI CSP Level 5–7), ambient and material temperatures of sixty degrees Fahrenheit or higher, and scheduling outside the October 1 to April 30 window unless a written exception is obtained; these systems require manufacturer‑approved resin and hardener mixed with uniform aggregate and must achieve a minimum bond strength of 250 psi [2]. In all cases the overlay must address the identified deficiency—whether increasing structural capacity as determined by non‑destructive testing, improving surface or subsurface water removal, replacing failed base areas, raising the pavement grade line above high‑moisture zones, or enhancing ride quality and friction—and must include minimum preparation such as a one‑inch leveling course for cross‑slope correction when needed, appropriate tack coats, and continuous base drains [1].

An overlay is only appropriate when the existing pavement can act as a stable, uniformly prepared base; if the surface moves under roller pressure, is uneven, unsealed, or inadequately patched, designers must turn to alternatives such as bonded or unbonded concrete overlays, crack‑and‑seat with a structural asphalt overlay, or full reconstruction [1]. When the structural condition of the pavement demands extensive base repair, severe cracking, stripping of lower asphalt layers, unresolved drainage problems, or other forms of deterioration that cannot be economically addressed by thin surfacing, a simple overlay cannot provide the needed support and more robust rehabilitation methods become preferable [1]. Project requirements that involve shoulder widening, slope regrading, guide‑rail relocation, vertical adjustments at structures, or any grade‑line modifications that substantially increase expense also shift the cost‑effectiveness balance toward reconstruction or another rehabilitation technique rather than an overlay [1]. For bridge decks, overlays are limited to maintenance actions and may be placed only after the District Bridge Engineer confirms sufficient dead‑load capacity; insufficient capacity or work beyond routine maintenance mandates full deck reconstruction [1]. Thin asphalt overlays thinner than the minimum 1½ inch are permissible only on sound asphalt surfaces with less than two percent patching and adequate drainage; otherwise heater planing, milling, or full‑depth reconstruction is required [1]. Premium mixtures such as CRMAB should not be specified when extensive handwork, stiff mixes requiring raking, shoulders that cannot be paved in a single pass, temporary lanes, crossovers, or higher cost cannot be justified by performance gains [1]. On concrete pavements, a bonded Portland‑cement concrete overlay requires at least three inches of existing asphalt after milling (or four inches for conventional bonded overlays), and it is inappropriate where the underlying concrete is severely cracked or faulted; unbonded overlays are suitable only if the existing pavement can serve as a uniform subbase, and if fracturing of the concrete would be required, an asphalt overlay designed with the “AC over Fractured PCC” module should be used instead [1]. Preservation projects limit flexible‑pavement overlays to thicknesses of two inches or less; scratch overlays on concrete exceeding 4½ inches (or five inches when a larger wearing course is used) exceed preservation limits, and under the 3R reconstruction criterion any overlay of two inches or more is prohibited, restricting preservation to thin layers of one to one‑and‑a‑half inches [1]. Operational enhancements such as auxiliary lanes are permissible only up to three thousand feet when adjacent main lanes are preserved for at least ten thousand contiguous feet; larger capacity expansions require reconstruction [1]. When subgrade CBR values are low, highly variable, or isolated weak spots exist, targeted subgrade improvement, stabilization, rock capping, or subdivision of the project into sections with different pavement structures is preferred to leaving the subgrade untouched under an overlay [1]. Epoxy overlays are unsuitable when ambient or component temperatures fall below 60 °F, when a forecasted temperature drop beneath 55 °F is expected within eight hours after placement, or when work is scheduled between October 1 and April 30 without written district approval; under these thermal conditions a different design solution should be pursued [2]. The epoxy overlay also cannot be applied to concrete repairs younger than twenty‑eight days, nor can it be placed if the surface cannot achieve an average bond strength of at least 250 psi or if moisture and unsound concrete remain despite prescribed preparation; failure to meet these criteria directs designers toward alternative rehabilitation methods [2]. Finally, the requirement that any additional cement concrete needed to replace removed unsound material may not exceed two percent of the calculated overlay volume means that when the extent of deteriorated pavement or transition work would cause this limit to be exceeded, the associated cost and administrative burden make an alternative design approach more attractive [2]. In all of these situations—whether due to inadequate base stability, severe structural distress, extensive scope modifications, bridge‑deck capacity limits, thickness or material constraints, preservation criteria, subgrade deficiencies, or the specific temperature, age, bond strength, and volume restrictions governing epoxy overlays—the prudent course is to consider bonded or unbonded concrete overlays, crack‑and‑seat with a structural asphalt overlay, accelerated concrete placement, full reconstruction, or other rehabilitation strategies rather than proceeding with an overlay.

Design inputs and requirements

An overlay design begins by confirming that the existing surface is asphalt and that it is structurally sound, with less than two percent of the pavement requiring patching [1]. The project must also ensure that both surface and subsurface drainage are adequate or be upgraded to achieve good drainage conditions [1]. Site selection typically focuses on high‑volume corridors such as Interstates, Interstate look‑alikes, major intersections, or steep grades where distress types like rutting, shoving, slippage, or thermal cracking have been observed, and a minimum pavement area of fifty thousand square yards is required for certain applications [1]. Additional site details that must be documented include the locations of bridge drains, parapets, scuppers, and other drainage openings so that protective measures can be provided during construction [2]. Traffic inputs are evaluated against average daily traffic thresholds generally exceeding twenty‑five thousand vehicles per day, and cumulative equivalent single axle loads must surpass ten million over a twenty‑year design life, with bonded concrete overlays requiring at least 2 500 ESALs per day [1]. Material specifications call for dense‑graded wearing or binder courses using a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, and the mix may incorporate reclaimed asphalt pavement or reclaimed asphalt shingles when produced by the wet process [1]. When CRMAB mixtures are used, layer thickness is limited to two and one‑half inches, while thin overlays on Interstates must not be placed thinner than one and a half inches unless special approval is obtained [1]. For bonded concrete overlays, a minimum remaining asphalt thickness of three inches after milling is required, and the concrete overlay thickness ranges from two and a half to four inches for thin sections and four inches or more for conventional sections [1]. All overlay materials must be stored in a clean, dry environment according to manufacturer recommendations, retain a minimum twelve‑month shelf life, and be accompanied by complete labeling, safety data sheets, and product data sheets for each batch [2]. Environmental considerations include implementing BMPs that prevent material loss at drainage points, controlling dust generated during cleaning to avoid impacting traffic, and addressing skid‑resistance problems such as those associated with certain concrete types which may necessitate an earlier overlay [2][1]. Geotechnical data derived from Falling Weight Deflectometer testing provide the pavement and subgrade characteristics needed for structural analysis, while annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys supply detailed distress information such as cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling, joint spalling, and faulting to verify existing conditions [1]. Existing‑condition assessments therefore include a pre‑condition survey of deck cracks to locate potential reflective cracking, comprehensive surface cleaning to eliminate contaminants, confirmation that the substrate is dry before primer and overlay placement, and verification that the underlying pavement meets the structural criteria described earlier [2][1].

Overlay design is governed by a layered hierarchy that begins with federal‑aid highway program guidance and FHWA traffic engineering manuals, proceeds through state‑wide policies such as PennDOT Publication 408, and culminates in project‑specific specifications that incorporate the Pennsylvania Specification Manual and national standards for concrete repair and pavement construction. The existing surface must be stable, level, sealed and patched before any new course is placed, and weather restrictions on placement are set out in the publication’s Section 413.3(b). Surface preparation must also satisfy International Concrete Repair Institute Level 5‑7 requirements, which call for scarification, removal of loose material, shot‑blasting, joint backing within ±¼ in., repair of unstable slabs, and a final water‑and‑oil‑free air blast no later than twenty‑four hours before overlay placement; moisture in the substrate must be verified by ASTM D4263 with a transparent plastic sheet left on the surface for at least two hours under prevailing conditions. Both sets of requirements limit full‑depth concrete patching to roughly one percent of the pavement area, permitting up to ten percent only when cost‑effectiveness can be demonstrated, and they prescribe the use of PennDOT Roadway Construction Standards (Publication 72M) RC‑26M patches that meet joint spacing, dowel anchorage and material gradation criteria. Continuous base drainage, installation of Type 6 or Type 7 paved shoulders, and application of a non‑tracking tack coat to Superpave wearing courses of one‑and‑a‑half to two inches are treated as mandatory project constraints because they affect overall cost and performance.

Temperature limits are strictly controlled; the substrate temperature must be between 40 °F and 100 °F, with epoxy‑based systems not placed below 60 °F and required to remain above 55 °F for eight hours after placement, while concrete or epoxy mix temperatures are limited to 50 °F–85 °F and any asphalt binder used in an interlayer must be kept between 290 °F and 320 °F. Ambient temperature may not fall below 45 °F during the curing period, and if it does insulation blankets must be applied with surface‑temperature change limited to 40 °F per hour. These weather constraints complement the Pennsylvania guidance that overlays may only be placed when conditions meet the specifications of Section 413.3(b).

The structural thickness of an overlay shall never be less than one inch, and the first lift over any interlayer system must be compacted to at least one‑and‑a‑half inches; thin rigid‑pavement overlays are typically placed at 2½–4 in., while Class AA Portland‑cement concrete bonded to existing asphalt may range from 4 in. to 8 in. Minimum thicknesses for Superpave wearing‑course and binder‑course overlays are prescribed in PennDOT Table 9.5, and bridge‑deck overlays are exempt from the minimum‑thickness tables of Table 10.4 but must be coordinated with the District Bridge Engineer and limited to maintenance‑type work after confirming dead‑load capacity. Design procedures follow Chapter 10 of the Pennsylvania Specification Manual using the AASHTO design method, while concrete overlay designs reference Sections 540, 541 and 548 for bonded, thin‑bonded and unbonded systems respectively; panel dimensions are limited by an aspect‑ratio ceiling of 1.5 and a maximum overall length equal to eighteen times the overlay thickness, with saw cuts at least one‑third slab depth and no narrower than one‑eighth inch.

Mix designs must employ PG 64E‑22 binder for standard 6.3 mm Superpave wearing courses and may not use the same binder for a 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix; coarse aggregate in any asphalt wearing course must achieve a Skid Resistance Level determined by AASHTO T 242, with higher‑than‑allowed SRL values prohibited for non‑wearing layers such as leveling courses or shoulders. Polymer‑modified CRMAB overlays are limited to projects with average daily traffic exceeding 25 000 vehicles, cumulative ESALs over ten million for a twenty‑year design life, and an overlay area greater than fifty thousand square yards; they may not exceed a thickness of 2½ in., must be produced by the wet process, and are restricted to permanent lanes. For overlays thinner than three inches No. 67 coarse aggregate replaces No. 57, and for thicknesses under seven inches polypropylene fibers meeting ASTM C1116 Type III 4.13 and the performance level of ASTM C1018 are required to achieve a residual‑strength ratio of twenty percent, with a maximum dosage of five pounds per cubic yard unless field testing demonstrates acceptable workability. Chloride‑ion penetration must be rated Low, Very low or Negligible after no more than fifty‑six days of curing according to AASHTO T277 or T358; a Moderate rating is permissible only within specified coulomb and resistivity limits.

Structural analysis considerations include the use of appropriate structural coefficients for historic brick pavements—“Brick with Rigid Base” when over a concrete base and “Brick with Flexible Base” when over stone or other flexible bases—as prescribed in Table 9.5, applied only to the depth of the brick. Unbonded Portland‑cement concrete overlays placed on existing asphalt treat the underlying asphalt as support, requiring the PCC thickness to equal the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic shown in Table 10.8 and using ESAL calculations based on rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1. Geotechnical constraints dictate that CBR values greater than ten require approval by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, while values above twenty are prohibited for use in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation; when the subgrade is not reworked, the conversion factor uses an in‑situ density of 1 000 kg/m³ rather than 1 500 kg/m³. Back‑calculated resilient modulus from Falling Weight Deflectometer testing must be adjusted by a factor of 0.25 in accordance with the 1993 AASHTO Guide, and structural design life requirements specify a minimum of eight years and a maximum of twenty years, documented on Form D‑4332 with PDAU concurrence and FHWA approval when necessary.

Bond performance is verified by ASTM C1583 vertical pull‑off testing; an average bond strength of at least 250 psi is required, with a reduced acceptance of 175 psi average permitted provided each individual test meets a minimum of 160 psi. Tests are performed between twenty‑four and seventy‑two hours after curing when the latex or concrete has attained a compressive strength of at least 3 000 psi; traffic may be opened only after cylinders reach a compressive strength of 3 600 psi, and a five‑day strength not less than 3 000 psi is required for acceptance under the project’s quality‑control plan.

A comprehensive Quality Control Plan, approved at least thirty calendar days before material placement, governs safety procedures, storage documentation, temperature monitoring, moisture control, equipment maintenance, corrective actions, field sampling, testing and recordkeeping; any deviation results in immediate suspension of work. All equipment used for deck preparation, mixing, placing and finishing must be accepted in advance and recorded in the plan, with mobile mixers capable of delivering at least six cubic yards per hour and maintaining cement content within 0 %–+4 %, aggregate proportions within ±2 %, water within ±1 %, latex within ±2 %, drive‑shaft speed within ±50 rpm and batch intervals not exceeding thirty minutes; aggregate bins may be loaded no more than six hours before placement and moisture must be determined according to AASHTO T255 or ASTM C70. For asphalt overlays a self‑propelled milling machine capable of cutting a full lane up to twelve feet wide in one pass shall be used, the milled surface must be cleaned and kept free of loose material, and the first asphalt course must be placed within seven calendar days after milling with traffic prohibited on the milled surface for more than six days.

Project constraints include geometry adjustments such as shoulder width, embankment slope, guide‑rail location or vertical clearances, which can affect overall cost, and logistical limits that divide each traffic lane into lots not exceeding three thousand square yards, with ramps treated as separate lots. Within each lot a single coring location is selected at random in accordance with PTM No 1, cores are extracted following PTM No 606 while an inspector is present, and thickness measurements are made with apparatus conforming to PTM No 614; the overlay may not deviate more than one‑quarter inch from the specified thickness, and if a core exceeds this limit additional coring at intervals of one hundred feet is performed until compliance is achieved, otherwise the lot is deemed defective. Compensation provisions allow an additional quantity not to exceed two percent of the computed overlay volume for concrete used to replace unsound material removed during profile milling, provided a written request supported by delivery tickets and waste deductions is submitted; transition zones are excluded from this allowance.

All required documentation—including safety data sheets, manufacturer and system‑provider data sheets, approved mix designs, test reports, records of material quantities and the approved Quality Control Plan—must be maintained as part of the project record, and installers must demonstrate recent experience by having completed a minimum of three epoxy‑overlay projects totaling at least one thousand square yards in the preceding three years (or up to three comparable polyester polymer concrete projects for PPC systems). Design methods and criteria

Overlay design begins with a quantitative assessment of the existing pavement’s structural capacity, which may be obtained through a condition survey, non‑destructive deflection testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) measurements, or a remaining‑life estimate based on historic traffic data. For rigid pavements the FWD results are back‑calculated to resilient modulus and then adjusted by a correction factor of 0.25 to align field values with laboratory results, while subgrade strength derived from CBR tests is converted to modulus using the Heukelom‑Klomp relationship with a multiplier of 1 000 rather than the standard 1 500; CBR values above ten require geotechnical approval and those exceeding twenty are prohibited. Dynamic Cone Penetration (DCP) results may also be transformed to resilient modulus via the same CBR conversion. The effective structural capacity is expressed as D_eff for rigid sections or SN_eff for flexible layers, and the required future capacity is denoted by D_f or SN_f respectively. Future traffic demand is quantified in 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) using average initial truck factors from the appropriate tables, providing the basis for determining the structural thickness needed to accommodate projected loading[1][2].

Structural analysis relies on structural coefficients drawn from a series of design tables; the coefficient applied to brick pavement depends on whether the bricks rest on a rigid concrete base or a flexible stone base, and each material layer receives its own coefficient based solely on depth. Minimum and maximum placement depths, as well as allowable scratch and leveling course thicknesses, are also taken from these tables, and permissible overlay thicknesses are limited by the same sources, with average‑daily‑traffic limits verified against the appropriate ADT tables[1].

Concrete overlays may be either bonded or unbonded. Bonded concrete overlays over asphalt or composite bases incorporate the existing structure in the analysis and follow a mechanistic‑empirical procedure (BCOA‑ME) when the total thickness does not exceed 6.5 in; a milled asphalt base of at least three inches is required, thin bonded sections are limited to nominal concrete thicknesses of 2.5–4 in, while conventional bonded sections require four inches or more. Overlays thicker than 6.5 in are treated as new concrete pavements and must obtain design concurrence before field work begins. Unbonded overlays act only as a stable platform and must be separated from the existing pavement by at least a one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt or geotextile layer[1][2].

The thickness of asphalt overlays is obtained using the procedure in Chapter 10, which implements the 1993 AASHTO Design Method via mechanistic‑empirical software such as DARWin; design documentation is recorded on Form D‑4332 for approval. Superpave mix designs employ a PG 64E‑22 binder for standard 6.3 mm wearing courses, and when crumb‑rubber modified binders are used the structural layer coefficient from the relevant table governs analysis with a maximum total thickness of 2½ in. Coarse aggregate selection follows skid‑resistance level (SRL) guidelines based on anticipated ADT, and final SRL values are confirmed by friction testing using AASHTO T 242[1][2].

When fiber‑reinforced concrete is specified, macro‑fibers contribute a residual strength f150 determined according to ASTM C1609; the effective flexural strength used in analysis is calculated as Feff = MOR + f150. Typical fiber contents range from 0.2 % to 0.5 % by volume, yielding f150 values between 100 and 225 psi, and a Residual Strength Estimator spreadsheet assists engineers in predicting f150 before entering the resulting Feff into mechanistic‑empirical programs such as BCOA‑ME, ACPA Pavement Designer, or AASHTOWare Pavement ME[1][2].

Prior to construction the contractor must submit a field‑operation quality‑control plan (Form CS‑1042) and hold a pre‑placement meeting at least fourteen calendar days before material placement to review material type, application rates, mixing equipment, surface preparation, and utility conditions. Surface preparation consists of scarifying, shot‑blasting or water‑blasting the existing pavement, sealing cracks, and cleaning the surface so that it is dry and free of contaminants no more than thirty minutes before concrete placement; substrate temperature must be below 120 °F while concrete temperature at placement is limited to 65–90 °F[2].

Mix proportions are calculated using a volumetric‑weight equivalent method, with aggregate moisture determined according to AASHTO T255 or ASTM C70 and equipment calibrated before each batch. Mixing quality is verified for every five cubic yards of latex mix by testing plastic air content, temperature, and slump, and compliance with the target yield is confirmed with an AASHTO T121 yield test[2].

The minimum overlay thickness is one inch (or 1½ inches for the first lift of an asphalt overlay). Quantity is computed by multiplying the specified thickness by the placement area, and contracts permit an additional volume up to two percent of that amount to compensate for cement concrete used to replace unsound material removed during milling. Panel dimensions are limited so that the longer side does not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and the aspect ratio is kept near 1.0 but never greater than 1.5; joint layout and saw‑cut timing are determined with FHWA’s HIPERPAV or an equivalent approved software package, and joints are cut to a depth of one‑third the slab thickness with a minimum width of one‑eighth inch[2].

Curing is performed at ambient temperatures of 45 °F or higher. Wet burlap is applied followed by a dry‑cure period, and insulation removal may be governed by the maturity method, which correlates degree‑hours of cure to compressive strength according to PTM No. 640; a curing compound is sprayed uniformly at a rate of one gallon (±0.1 gal) per 125 ft²[2].

Acceptance testing includes vertical pull‑bond tests performed in accordance with ASTM C1583 between twenty‑four and seventy‑two hours after placement, requiring a minimum bond strength of 250 psi and an average of at least 175 psi provided no individual result falls below 160 psi; up to three additional tests may be conducted if criteria are not met. Five‑day compressive‑strength cylinders must reach at least three thousand psi before the overlay is approved, and chloride ion penetration limits defined by AASHTO T277 or T358 after a curing period of fifty‑six days must not exceed 2 800 coulombs, with resistivity values below 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4×8‑in cylinders and below 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6×12‑in cylinders[2].

Construction is divided into lots of no more than three thousand square yards per traffic lane; for each lot a random coring location is selected in accordance with PTM No. 1, cores are drilled under inspector supervision following PTM No. 606, and core depths are measured with equipment complying with PTM No. 614. If any core is shallow by more than one‑quarter inch, additional cores are taken at 100‑foot intervals to represent the condition over a fifty‑foot stretch in both longitudinal directions[2].

Preservation‑oriented overlay options rely on cost‑effectiveness data from the pavement management system and include mill‑and‑overlay treatments that remove rutting or defective material with typical milling depths of one‑and‑a‑half inches, extendable to two inches when necessary; micro‑surfacing and ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses improve skid resistance without providing structural capacity; cold in‑place recycling of the base is limited to a maximum depth of four inches combined with an asphalt overlay not exceeding one‑and‑a‑half inches, while ultra‑thin bonded concrete overlays serve as non‑structural wearing surfaces within prescribed thickness limits. Structural adequacy for any preservation treatment may be verified through FWD testing when required[1].

Transitions between the new overlay and existing pavement are managed through consistent joint spacing, appropriate joint treatment, and the continuous interlayer tie‑in at shoulders, ensuring a smooth structural and functional transition.

Materials and mixture design

An overlay specification must first identify the material system that will form the new surface, whether it is a rigid Portland‑cement concrete (PCC) overlay, a flexible asphalt‑concrete overlay, a polymer‑concrete system, a latex‑modified concrete system, an epoxy system, or an asphalt interlayer, and then enumerate the required constituent properties for each selected system [1][2].

For PCC overlays the mix design shall follow the requirements of Publication 408 Section 704 with an optimized aggregate gradation and must satisfy the minimum compressive strength and time‑to‑strength criteria set out in Publication 408 Section 501; high‑early‑strength cement is expressly prohibited because of its tendency to crack [1]. Thickness limits are prescribed as 2.5–4 inches for thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays, 4–8 inches for standard bonded sections, and unbonded overlays must incorporate a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt separation layer or an equivalent geotextile interlayer [1]. When a geotextile is used any joints faulting more than a quarter inch shall be milled, and if the fault exceeds half an inch an additional Superpave wearing course of 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix must be placed at the joint; macro‑fibers may be added to achieve a residual flexural strength (f150) between 100 and 225 psi with a dosage of roughly 0.2–0.5 % by volume, fiber lengths of 1 to 2.5 inches, aspect ratios of 30 to 100, and either synthetic or steel fibers that meet the stipulated residual strength, while for depths less than seven inches the fiber content shall not exceed five pounds per cubic yard unless workability is demonstrated and a residual flexural strength ratio of at least twenty percent must be achieved in accordance with ASTM procedures [1][2].

Class AA cement concrete overlays are required to be placed at thicknesses ranging from two‑and‑a‑half to eight inches; when the depth is under three inches the coarse aggregate shall be No. 67, and for depths less than seven inches synthetic or steel macrofibers (or 100 % virgin polypropylene fibers) may be incorporated within the stated dosage limits to meet the residual strength ratio requirement [2]. The concrete mix must also satisfy permeability criteria that target low to negligible chloride ion penetration after no more than fifty‑six days of curing as defined by AASHTO T277/T358 [2].

Polymer‑concrete overlays require an unsaturated isophthalic polyester‑styrene resin combined with a high‑molecular‑weight methacrylate primer and a compatible initiator; the resin must contain at least one percent by weight of gamma‑methacryloxypropyltrimethoxysilane and represent twelve percent (±1 %) of the dry aggregate mass, while the aggregates must conform to the design gradations and the abrasive finish sand must be commercial‑quality blast sand with a minimum of ninety‑five percent passing a No. 8 sieve and at least ninety‑five percent retained on a No. 20 sieve [2].

Latex‑modified concrete overlays are proportioned by weight within tight tolerances—cement may vary from zero to plus four percent, fine and coarse aggregates each ±2 %, water ±2 %, and latex admixture ±2 %—and the latex mixture must achieve a five‑day compressive strength of no less than three thousand psi before bond testing; the resulting substrate‑to‑latex bond shall be at least two hundred fifty psi as measured by vertical pull‑off tests [2].

Flexible asphalt overlays must name the exact asphalt mixture to be placed. A Stone Matrix Asphalt (SMA) wearing course may be milled and installed at either 1.5 inches or 2.0 inches with a tack coat applied to the existing surface and shoulders. When a Superpave mix is used, the nominal aggregate size shall be specified—options include 25 mm, 19 mm, 12.5 mm, 9.5 mm, or 6.3 mm for thin‑overlay applications—and the required binder grade must be indicated; PG 64E‑22 binder is mandated for standard 6.3 mm wearing courses and prohibited for 9.5 mm fine‑graded mixes. The appropriate Skid Resistance Level (SRL) designation for the coarse aggregate shall be recorded from the prescribed tables, as it governs surface friction. If a crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder is selected, the specification must require a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder produced by the wet process, permit RAP or RAS in accordance with the SSP, limit layer thickness to no more than 2½ inches, and require a chemical additive when warm‑mix technology is employed (foaming is not allowed). All asphalt overlays must respect the minimum and maximum thickness limits derived from ADT‑based tables—typically 1.5 inches for a 9.5 mm mix, 2 inches for a 12.5 mm mix, up to 2½ inches for dense‑graded wearing courses; cold‑in‑place recycling overlays are limited to 1½ inches, and micro‑surfacing treatments may not exceed roughly 0.25 inch on pavement verified as structurally sound by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing [1].

Asphalt interlayers are sprayed at a rate of 0.22–0.28 gallons per square yard and compacted to a minimum of 1.5 inches on the first lift, while a nominal one‑inch Superpave mix may serve as an asphalt interlayer where specified [1][2].

Epoxy overlays are applied in two courses, the first at a rate not less than 0.18 gallon per square yard and the second at least 0.36 gallon per square yard; they must bond to the existing slab with an average tensile strength of 250 psi and pull‑off values averaging 175 psi with no individual result below 160 psi [2].

All materials used in an overlay shall be labeled with the manufacturer name, production or shipment date, batch/lot number, trade name, quantity, and prescribed mixing ratio, and the proportioning method and yield calculations for each layer must be documented [2]. Application temperatures are strictly controlled: resin, aggregate and surface temperatures must not fall below sixty degrees Fahrenheit (and must remain above fifty‑five degrees for eight hours after placement); latex mixtures are placed between fifty and eighty‑five degrees Fahrenheit with cure temperatures maintained at or above forty‑five degrees; asphalt materials are applied within a band of two hundred ninety to three hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit [2]. The overlay thickness shall be no less than one inch, edge slump is limited to one‑quarter inch adjacent to shoulders and one‑eighth inch next to adjoining lanes, and curing compounds must be spread uniformly at a minimum rate of one gallon per 125 square feet (±0.1 gallon) [2]. Any repair patches used for surface defects must be pre‑approved and consist of asphalt‑based, cementitious or other approved pavement repair materials [2].

Structural details and interfaces

An overlay begins with thorough surface preparation that includes cleaning and sealing of all existing longitudinal and transverse concrete joints, as well as sealing any asphalt joints and cracks. Where transverse or patch joints are present they must be saw‑cut and sealed so the new layer forms a continuous load path; this applies to both concrete and asphalt pavements. Any joint that has lost its load‑transfer capacity or is crushed must be removed and replaced with a dowelled concrete patch, the dowels being bonded along their full embedded length with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders, and coated as required. When full‑depth reconstruction is called for, standard joint spacing of 30 ft for reinforced concrete or 15 ft for plain concrete is used together with appropriate seals. For unbonded concrete overlays the joints are placed exactly as they would be in a new slab, based on slab thickness, and need not line up with the existing pavement joints. Bonded concrete overlays rely on the bond between old and new slabs and therefore do not require special joint continuity. An asphalt overlay over concrete must be saw‑cut and sealed across every underlying transverse or patched joint; load transfer may be provided through dowel‑bar retrofits or other concrete‑pavement‑restoration techniques, and any required slab stabilization or jacking is incorporated to maintain structural continuity. Reinforcement can be supplied by an interlayer such as a needle‑punched geotextile paving fabric, fiberglass mesh grids, or composite reinforcing grids that become embedded in the asphalt mix and provide tensile and torsional strength. When a geotextile separation layer is used, joints with more than ¼ inch of faulting are milled, and those exceeding ½ inch receive a Superpave wearing‑course (scratch) mix or grinding to reduce the fault before placement. Proper drainage for the interlayer is achieved by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain, and the interlayer must extend through reconstructed shoulders and be tied into the mainline. Fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays depend on correctly timed and depth‑controlled contraction (saw‑cut) joints; macrofibers help maintain load‑transfer efficiency but do not replace dowel bars where fault control is required. Finally, at the pavement edge a Safety Edge taper of at least 1½ inches provides the transition detail for both wearing and binder courses.

An overlay begins only after a systematic survey of the existing pavement has identified all gutter lines, breakpoints and metal expansion dams at roughly twenty‑five foot intervals, and any deficient dam or scupper has been raised. All exposed reinforcement bars must be abrasive‑blasted to a clean, rust‑free condition; epoxy coating is not permitted on these bars. The existing surface is then prepared so that both sides of each expansion joint receive the profile required by Section 501.3(o) and all loose steel or defective slabs are repaired. Expansion joints must be fully isolated before placing Portland‑cement concrete (PPC), and where the overlay terminates at curbs, scuppers or similar obstructions the edges are finished by hand‑troweling.

Joint layout follows the project plans exactly; no additional longitudinal or transverse construction joints may be added unless specifically indicated. In phased work, phase joints are cut in straight lines parallel to the roadway centreline and staggered so that successive cuts overlap by at least two inches, preventing ridge formation. Intermediate transverse joints are provided so that no overlay panel exceeds fifteen feet in length, overall panel dimensions must not exceed eighteen times the slab thickness, and the length‑to‑width ratio may not be greater than 1.5. All joints are created by saw cutting—the only acceptable technique—with a minimum cut depth of one‑third of the overlay thickness (D/3) within twelve inches of any edge and at least D/4 elsewhere; where an existing joint is being cut, the depth must equal the full overlay thickness plus a half‑inch while avoiding damage to dowel load‑transfer devices. The minimum joint width is one‑eighth inch, with a preferred quarter‑inch width for sealant reservoirs that are backfilled after paving. After placement, each joint is sealed in accordance with the standard drawings.

Load‑transfer provisions depend on the selected interlayer system. When joint faulting exceeds one‑half inch, a one‑inch asphalt interlayer may be installed; alternatively a geotextile interlayer is laid flat over the cleaned substrate, overlapping edges by eight inches (± 2 in) and extending at least four inches beyond the pavement edge. Reinforcement over such an interlayer can consist of geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grid or composite reinforcing grid, each extending six inches beyond its edges and saturated with tack‑coat material to ensure bonding. The interlayer material must be overlapped by a minimum of one inch (or the manufacturer’s requirement), and any wrinkles larger than one inch are sliced and re‑overlapped flat. The first lift of an asphalt overlay over this system must achieve a compacted thickness of not less than 1.5 in.

Where dowel baskets are used, they are anchored so that vertical or horizontal movement does not exceed one‑quarter inch; the anchor type, location, number and length are determined on site and approved by the project representative. Joint backing or other approved joint‑filling material is installed to within ±¼ in of the pavement surface in all existing transverse joints.

The concrete mix for the overlay is Portland‑cement concrete reinforced with either synthetic macrofibers or steel macrofibers, the dosage being sufficient to achieve the design residual strength (f150) specified in Table A. For overlays thinner than three inches, No. 67 coarse aggregate replaces No. 57; for thicknesses under seven inches the mix must meet Class AA requirements and incorporate synthetic polypropylene fibers complying with ASTM C1116—monofilament or bundled monofilament fibers 1.0 to 2½ in long, aspect ratio not exceeding 150, at a dosage up to five pounds per cubic yard unless field testing confirms workability. The mixture must also satisfy a low‑chloride permeability target (Low, Very Low, or Negligible after fifty‑six days) as defined by AASHTO T 277/T 358.

Transitions at the limits of the concrete overlay are constructed exactly as shown on the plans. Where a transition overlies existing treated base course or concrete pavement, a geotextile debonding layer is placed to prevent bonding between new and old materials. Edge‑tooling is required at all joints, metal expansion dams, curbs and previously placed lanes; edges adjacent to joint bulkheads and expansion dams are vibrated during placement. Screed rails and construction bulkheads are separated from the fresh material with a pointing trowel, while metal expansion dams remain in contact with the wearing surface. All construction joints are sealed according to the standard drawings. [1][2]

Overlay design begins with a systematic assessment of the existing pavement to determine whether the new layer will be bonded to or separated from the underlying material. When the existing surface is sound, a bonded system is pursued; this requires cleaning and sealing longitudinal and transverse joints, applying an asphalt tack coat, and allowing the old and new materials to act as one monolithic unit. A continuous bond is created by tack coating before placing the binder and wearing courses, typically a 1½‑ to 2‑inch SMA wearing course over the prepared surface. The underlying pavement must provide stable, uniform support, so any failed base material, cracked concrete, or severely deteriorated joints are removed, while non‑critical joints may be stabilized after cleaning and sealing. Where profile errors exist, a separate scratch or leveling course of at least one inch thickness is placed to create a level working platform; this course remains distinct from the binder and wearing courses to preserve quality control. For unbonded concrete overlays no asphalt separation layer is used as a leveling course; instead all grade corrections are accomplished with the concrete overlay itself, which rests directly on the prepared platform. An isolation layer of fine‑graded asphalt at least one inch thick or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer is installed between old concrete and new concrete when an unbonded design is required, preventing the need to align transverse joints in the overlay with existing joints. Joint faulting greater than a quarter inch is milled, and if faulting exceeds half an inch a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing course (scratch mix) of nominal one‑inch thickness is placed at the joint or the joint is milled/grinded to reduce faulting. The interlayer must be provided with drainage either by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain, and it extends through any reconstructed shoulder, being tied into the mainline. When overlaying asphalt on concrete or brick, at least 1½ inches of existing asphalt must remain after milling to retain structural integrity; for bonded overlays over asphalt a minimum of three inches of milled asphalt is required so that the Portland cement concrete can act monolithically with the base. Minimum binder thicknesses of 2½ inches are prescribed for asphalt overlays on cement‑concrete or brick pavements, and each wearing course must meet its material‑specific minimum thickness. Fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays typically range from three to six inches in thickness, with contraction joints saw‑cut at appropriate depth and timing; however, flexible‑pavement overlays greater than two inches are excluded from preservation programs, and concrete overlays may not exceed four‑and‑a‑half inches when a 9.5 mm mix is used or five inches with a 12.5 mm mix. Guide rails, drainage structures, and other adjacent features intersecting the overlay are adjusted or replaced to maintain alignment and proper water management, with continuous base drains provided to control subsurface moisture. Heavy‑duty membranes are placed where moisture control is required, and any deviation from these interface requirements is subject to review by the district pavement management engineer.

Overlay design treats every interface—between the new overlay and existing pavement, between successive lifts, and where the overlay meets adjacent structures—as a critical zone that must be prepared, protected, and detailed to achieve a durable bond. The existing surface is first inspected for gutters, expansion dams, scuppers, curbs, parapets, bridge drains, railings, inlets and other features; these are raised or protected according to the plans before any preparation begins. Concrete decks are scarified, shot‑blasted, water‑blasted, or hydro‑demolished where required, to a depth specified on the project documents and no later than seven days prior to placement. Asphalt bases receive milling followed by thorough sweeping; the first asphalt course must be placed within seven calendar days after milling. All cracks wider than three‑quarters of an inch are repaired with an approved non‑shrink grout or other department‑approved material, and joint backing is installed so that it does not project more than one‑quarter inch above the existing surface. After repair, the entire face is cleaned within twenty‑four hours of the start of overlay work to remove dust, rust, oil, vegetation and any deleterious material; a clean full‑width polyethylene sheet or suitable drop‑cloth then covers the surface until placement to prevent re‑contamination.

A uniformly applied primer for concrete overlays—or a tack coat for asphalt interlayers—is flooded over horizontal decks and adjoining vertical elements, extending at least six inches beyond each edge to guarantee complete coverage of all contact surfaces. Where a debonding layer is required, a geotextile membrane is laid flat on the repaired surface; its edges overlap by eight ± 2 inches, extend a minimum of four inches beyond the pavement edge, and no more than three layers are permitted at any location.

The overlay material itself is placed in accordance with manufacturer specifications. Asphalt interlayers are applied at a rate of 0.22 to 0.28 gal/yd², maintained between 290 °F and 320 °F, overlapped by at least one inch, and the first lift is compacted to a minimum thickness of 1.5 in before traffic. Concrete or epoxy overlays are placed directly on the prepared surface; slab thickness ranges from about 2½ in to 8 in depending on design requirements, with transition zones constructed at edges where the new slab meets adjoining features.

Joint layout follows the approved project plan; when none is provided a department‑accepted layout aligns joints with adjacent structures. In phased work, joints are cut in straight lines parallel to the roadway centreline, staggered and overlapped by at least two inches between successive layers to avoid ridges. Saw cuts for transverse joints extend through the full overlay thickness plus an additional half inch, and a sealant reservoir one‑quarter inch wide is cut to the same depth; longitudinal joints receive comparable treatment. Control joints are cut and sealed after placement to define slab interfaces, accommodate movement, and protect against water intrusion. Edge‑tooling is applied at all discontinuities, including expansion joints, metal dams, curbs, parapets, bridge drains, railings and lane limits.

Bond performance is verified by testing. Concrete or epoxy overlays require a seven‑day ASTM C1583 pull test with an average strength of no less than 175 psi (each individual result ≥160 psi). Latex overlays are subjected to a vertical pull test between 24 and 72 hours after cure, with a minimum bond strength of 250 psi; up to three tests may be performed before remedial action is required. Construction joints in latex layers are permitted only where specifically indicated and must be sealed per standard drawings; no additional longitudinal or transverse construction joints are added unless expressly authorized.

Throughout placement the overlay’s thickness, profile, surface tolerance and texturing are continuously measured and controlled to remain within the specified limits, ensuring that each interface—whether between pavement layers, materials, structures, or adjacent features—achieves the required durability and performance. Drainage, environment, and accessibility

In designing an overlay the pavement system must first guarantee that water is efficiently removed from both the surface and the structure beneath. A continuous base drain is provided to convey water away, and any guide‑rail or existing drainage components are adjusted or replaced so that flow paths remain uninterrupted. All longitudinal and transverse joints are cleaned and sealed, and heavy‑duty membranes are installed where necessary to block moisture intrusion into the underlying layers. The design also requires verification of subsurface drainage conditions; wherever deficiencies are identified, adequate subdrainage is incorporated, often by installing several subgrade drains to resolve the problem. Surface drainage must be good or upgraded to good, and the same standard applies to subsurface drainage, with interlayer systems such as geotextile fabrics or composite reinforcing grids acting as barriers that prevent surface water from penetrating the pavement structure. Climate effects, particularly freeze‑thaw cycles, are addressed by selecting an appropriate overlay thickness; a thicker overlay combined with an interlayer can delay cracking in harsh environments. When the existing subgrade is frost‑susceptible, the design either replaces the problematic soil or provides a stronger base course together with pavement‑base drains and a positive slope to keep water away, thereby mitigating freeze‑thaw damage and differential heaving. For projects that use varying overlay thicknesses, drainage beneath each new section is coordinated so that water does not accumulate at transitions. Finally, proper drainage of the separation layer is ensured by daylighting the interlayer or installing a vertical subdrain, with this drainage extending through any reconstructed shoulders and being tied into the mainline.

Overlay design must integrate a comprehensive set of drainage, moisture and climate controls to ensure durable performance. Before any primer or overlay material is placed the existing pavement surface must be completely dry, free of standing water, debris, oil, vegetation or loose aggregate, and it must pass a two‑hour ASTM D4263 moisture test after being covered with a transparent plastic sheet for verification. Any visible moisture prohibits placement, and if puddles are present they must be vacuumed or removed with dry compressed air. The substrate temperature must be below 120 °F, while the concrete or latex overlay temperature is required to remain within specific ranges: typically between 65 °F and 90 °F for concrete, 45 °F to 85 °F for latex, and never below 60 °F for epoxy‑based systems. Ambient conditions that would cause a temperature drop below 55 °F within eight hours after placement, or that fall outside the provider’s recommended range (generally 40 °F–100 °F), are prohibited; work is normally restricted from October 1 through April 30 unless expressly authorized. Temperature management may employ misting, fog‑misting, white‑pigmented curing compounds, lime water, insulating blankets or protective covers to keep the surface within limits and to prevent rapid heat loss when air temperatures are forecast at 45 °F or lower (or 40 °F according to some specifications). Insulating blankets may only be removed once the concrete temperature rise rate is under 40 °F per hour and compressive strength exceeds 3,600 psi.

All overlay materials are stored in a clean, dry environment; liquid components are kept out of direct sunlight and below 80 °F, aggregates are protected from moisture, and shipments are wrapped and elevated to avoid precipitation and ultraviolet exposure. During installation drainage features such as inlets, scuppers, barrier drains and expansion dams must be covered, later cleaned of accumulated aggregate, and the interlayer system is required to terminate in a drainable layer or daylighted location so that its free‑drainage capability is not impeded. The geotextile interlayer may be lightly dampened—never saturated—before placement, and its surface temperature must also stay below 120 °F.

Rain protection is mandatory: work stops at the first sign of rain, and freshly placed overlay is shielded with wet burlap, covers, windbreaks or sunshades to prevent unexpected precipitation and excessive evaporation (limited to 0.06 lb/ft²·h). Evaporation control measures such as misting hoses, continuous saturation of curing blankets during the wet cure period, and subsequent dry‑cure conditions are employed to maintain proper moisture balance. Finally, contractors must document all environmental controls, including aggregate moisture‑control methods, moisture test results, ambient air temperature, deck temperature and weather conditions at the time of placement, ensuring full compliance with the specified BMPs. An overlay must be designed to meet a comprehensive set of safety, geometric, accessibility, and user‑performance requirements while preserving the existing roadway geometry. Adequate drainage is fundamental; continuous pavement base drains are provided and any guide‑rail or other drainage structures that could impede water removal are adjusted or replaced so that surface and subsurface water are efficiently discharged and unsuitable subgrade material is protected from construction traffic exposure. A level, stable platform is achieved by placing a minimum one‑inch leveling course—or by milling where existing irregularities exist—to establish the required cross‑slope before the wearing or binder courses are laid at uniform depth.

For flexible (asphalt) overlays the structural thickness shall be 1.5 inches or 2.0 inches and must be sealed with a tack coat; shoulders receive the same treatment. Where preservation criteria apply, the asphalt overlay shall not exceed two inches, otherwise it is treated as a structural overlay. Concrete overlays require at least three inches of existing asphalt to remain after milling before bonding, and thin bonded concrete overlays (BCOA) are limited to 2.5 – 4 inches, while conventional or full‑depth bonded overlays must be four inches or greater. Joint geometry for reinforced concrete follows a 30‑foot spacing and for plain concrete a 15‑foot spacing, with perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars, and proper seals; faulting greater than one‑quarter inch is milled, and faulting exceeding one‑half inch triggers placement of an additional Superpave wearing course or grinding to preserve ride quality.

Safety features are retained or enhanced. Guide‑rail heights may not be reduced below the minimum acceptable criteria, and any required relocations of guide rails, shoulder‑width adjustments, slope changes to retain embankment width, or vertical clearances at structures must be incorporated so that the pavement grade line remains appropriate. A Safety Edge is provided on the outside edge of all asphalt pavements and shoulders—a 30° tapered transition (construction tolerance 26°–40°) with a minimum thickness of 1½ inches and a maximum overall depth of five inches—adding roughly one percent extra material to improve vehicle stability. For locations with stopping, turning, or low‑speed traffic, a non‑tracking tack coat is recommended to further enhance safety.

User performance criteria require that the finished surface develop sufficient skid resistance and texture. Coarse aggregate shall be selected to achieve the appropriate Skid Resistance Level (SRL) for the anticipated traffic volume; the SRL designation is documented on design approvals, typical sections, and contract proposals. The overlay must restore or improve ride quality, eliminate rutting, shoving, or slippage, and provide adequate low‑speed gradient to prevent water pressure buildup at the tire/pavement interface at the posted speed. Micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, or an ultra‑thin (2 inch) non‑structural concrete overlay may be used to address skid resistance and rut filling.

Shoulder performance is addressed by providing Type 6 or Type 7 paved shoulders—or full‑depth asphalt shoulders where specified—and extending any interlayer through reconstructed shoulders while ensuring drainage of the interlayer either by daylighting or a vertical subdrain. The overlay design must also consider user impacts such as traffic‑user delay; accelerated concrete patching can allow reopening to traffic within four to seven hours, and liquidated‑damage provisions apply for lane‑closure delays under high‑speed, high‑ADT conditions.

Accessibility requirements are not separately enumerated in the source material beyond maintaining existing clearances. Consequently, the overlay must preserve vertical clearances at structures and maintain the established roadway geometry so that all users, including those with mobility constraints, can continue to use the facility without impediment.

Vehicular traffic may not occupy any course during the cure period; the overlay may be opened to traffic only after the concrete reaches a compressive strength of 3,600 psi and bond‑strength test results average at least 175 psi with no individual result below 160 psi. Construction vehicles are permitted during curing provided they turn gradually, avoid abrupt starts or stops, and respect edge slump limits. Comprehensive safety documentation must accompany the overlay design, including safety data sheets for polymer‑modified components, high‑modulus wet mix resin and aggregates, manufacturer data sheets detailing storage, handling and mixing procedures, test reports verifying system performance, and a complete mix design, together with procedures for surface preparation, installation, curing times, site cleanup, equipment cleaning and disposal to ensure safe execution and long‑term serviceability. Verification and expected performance

The verification of a completed overlay begins with confirming that the existing pavement was stable—showing no movement under the roller—and had been properly leveled, sealed and patched before any new surface was placed. All design items such as drainage provisions, base repairs, leveling, binder thickness, friction treatment and shoulder improvements must be addressed, and the project plans are required to delineate the exact paving limits, particularly when a Restricted Performance Specification is used, so that the finished work can be measured against those documented boundaries. After placement, compliance and constructability are established by producing cross‑sections at roughly fifty‑foot intervals to ensure that the quantities of material installed correspond to the design, while core samples are taken in areas where slab depth varies to verify actual thickness. Density quality is then assessed using the Percent Within Limits methodology or core sampling as prescribed in the bid documents, confirming that field density falls within the required range. The interlayer must be shown to drain properly—either daylighted or equipped with a vertical subdrain—and it must run continuously through any reconstructed shoulder and be tied into the mainline. Joint preparation is inspected for faulting; joints exhibiting more than a quarter‑inch of faulting are milled, and if faulting exceeds half an inch a Superpave wearing‑course (scratch) mix is placed or the joint is ground to reduce the fault. Together these checks demonstrate that the overlay meets the specified thickness, drainage, joint‑faulting limits and overall project requirements, confirming compliance, constructability, performance and consistency with the contract specifications.

Before any material is placed the contractor must submit a project‑specific Quality Control Plan at least thirty calendar days in advance; the System Provider’s Technical Representative reviews and approves it, confirming that personnel qualifications, mixing procedures, temperature control of resin and aggregates (measured with infrared thermometers), moisture limits, cleaning schedules, safety measures and installation methods are all documented. The approved plan becomes the basis for field sampling, testing and record‑keeping, and any deviation results in immediate suspension of work. During construction the contractor first completes all required pre‑placement repairs, sweeps the surface clean and installs interlayers such as geotextile with an eight‑inch overlap (allowable ± 2 in), no more than three layers, a free edge extending at least four inches beyond the existing pavement, and dowel basket assemblies fastened so that movement does not exceed one‑quarter inch. Concrete is placed only when its temperature is between 65 °F and 90 °F and the underlying pavement is below 120 °F; asphalt material temperature must meet the manufacturer’s minimum as verified by the technical representative; latex and epoxy resin temperatures are sampled in accordance with the field QC plan. Thickness requirements are checked against the contract drawings: a minimum of one inch for Portland cement concrete overlays, at least 1.5 in for the first lift of an asphalt overlay, and prescribed epoxy application rates of 0.18 gal/yd² for course 1 and 0.36 gal/yd² for course 2. Surface smoothness is profiled to a tolerance of one‑eighth inch over any twelve‑foot length and cross‑slope must match the design within the same tolerance; failure to meet these limits is classified as defective work. Bond strength is verified by vertical pull‑bond testing (ASTM C1583) performed between 24 and 72 hours after placement for concrete, epoxy and latex overlays. The minimum tensile adhesion strength is 250 psi; concrete also requires three specimens tested at seven days with an average not less than 175 psi and no individual result below 160 psi. If the initial test fails, up to three additional pull‑bond tests may be made before the area is deemed defective and must be removed and replaced at no cost to the Department. Curing is confirmed by a Schmidt hammer reading of at least 3,000 psi for concrete, by achieving a compressive strength of 3,600 psi on cylinders before opening to traffic, and by attaining a five‑day compressive strength of 3,000 psi for latex. Cure temperatures must be maintained at or above 45 °F throughout the wet and dry periods and may not fall below 35 °F. Sampling frequencies prescribed in the QC plan require testing every five cubic yards for air content, temperature and slump, as well as volumetric yield tests of resin meters to verify application rates. Aggregate coverage is inspected for uniformity; any area lacking sufficient aggregate or showing exposed binder is considered defective. Overlaps that do not contain asphalt material are corrected by manual placement or trimmed to provide at least a one‑inch bond before paving proceeds. Structural verification is achieved by dividing the pavement into lane‑specific lots no larger than three thousand square yards, with ramps treated as separate lots. In each lot the inspector selects a random coring location in accordance with PTM No. 1; cores are drilled in the presence of the inspector, cleaned of laitance and measured with approved equipment. The core depth must be within one‑quarter inch of the design thickness; if any core exceeds this deficit additional cores are taken at approximately one‑hundred‑foot intervals until compliance is demonstrated. For areas smaller than three thousand square yards or at intersections two cores or nondestructive thickness measurements are used instead. Any lot containing an out‑of‑tolerance core is deemed defective. All material quantities, temperatures, test results, adhesion locations and corrective actions are recorded in accordance with the approved QC plan and retained for inspector review. The combination of pre‑placement checks, temperature and thickness controls, bond‑strength and curing tests, surface profiling, joint layout verification, systematic coring and comprehensive documentation provides a complete assurance that the completed overlay complies with contract specifications, can be constructed as intended, will perform over its service life, and remains consistent with the project requirements. Overlay designs are intended to provide a structural service life ranging from eight to twenty years while correcting deficiencies that affect ride quality or surface friction. Longevity, resistance to structural distress, and improved riding comfort constitute the primary performance outcomes. Design calculations employ current traffic data—average daily traffic, growth rates, and truck percentages—to compute 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs); material thicknesses are selected from FHWA‑prescribed tables and documented on Form D‑4332 for concurrence. When a fiber‑reinforced concrete overlay is specified, the added macrofibers increase residual tensile strength measured as f150 according to ASTM C1609; this value is summed with the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength (feff) that feeds mechanistic‑empirical design tools for thickness sizing. Laboratory slab tests record higher load capacity and a 30 % increase in load‑transfer efficiency, particularly when pre‑existing crack widths exceed one millimeter, thereby limiting crack deterioration and extending serviceability. On the surface level, milling to remove rutting combined with micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses is expected to eliminate existing ruts, raise skid resistance, and deliver a smoother ride. Performance verification includes confirming that the milling depth has removed the rut, that the applied surfacing achieves the targeted skid‑resistance and ride‑quality criteria, and that structural adequacy meets Falling Weight Deflectometer thresholds where required.

The overlay is expected to deliver a durable structural bond and a smooth, ride‑able surface that can be placed into traffic once it has cured. To satisfy the durability requirement the bond between the new concrete layer and the existing pavement must reach at least 250 psi as demonstrated by vertical pull‑bond testing, while the slab must retain a minimum nominal thickness of one inch throughout the project. Thickness uniformity is further controlled by a coring program that divides each lane into lots no larger than 3,000 square yards; a random core is taken from each lot and its depth measured with equipment conforming to PTM 614, and any core found more than a quarter‑inch short triggers additional sampling until all measurements fall within the allowable tolerance. Ride quality is verified by confirming that the surface meets smoothness tolerances after curing, which is declared complete either four hours after finishing or when a calibrated Schmidt hammer records a minimum of 3,000 psi along the deck at ten‑foot intervals. Together these criteria ensure that the overlay will achieve its intended service life and resistance to distress while providing acceptable ride quality. [1][2]
