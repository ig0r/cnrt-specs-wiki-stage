Overlay

Part of: Rehabilitation is-subtopic-of
Overlay is a topic under Rehabilitation.
overlayoverlays
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-23

Overlay is a rehabilitation technique applied when an existing pavement no longer provides adequate structural support, smoothness, or friction for safe and economical operation; it adds the required structural thickness—typically a Portland‑cement concrete layer of 2½ to 4 inches or an asphalt wearing/binder course of 1 to 2 inches—while retaining the underlying pavement as a support base and addressing deficiencies identified through non‑destructive testing or distress surveys [1]. The process begins with thorough preparation, such as milling, scratch‑and‑leveling, joint cleaning, and sealing, to create a uniform, stable platform that can receive a new binder or concrete course and any necessary interlayer systems. By restoring load‑carrying capacity, improving water removal, and providing the prescribed skid‑resistance level, an overlay renews ride quality and extends service life within defined thickness and performance limits.

PennDOT publications covering “Overlay” also include the following subtopics: Asphalt Overlay, Concrete Overlay, and Resurfacing.

Asphalt Overlay and Concrete Overlay supply the specific material systems—flexible wearing courses or rigid structural slabs—that execute the general rehabilitation strategy by restoring load capacity and surface function. [1] Resurfacing applies this same overlay principle as a thin-maintenance treatment, selecting between bonded or unbonded configurations based on life-cycle cost analysis to address ride quality and friction deficits when underlying structure remains adequate. [1]

Purpose and design objectives

Overlay is applied when an existing pavement no longer supplies adequate structural support, smoothness, or friction for safe and economical operation. The need becomes evident through non‑destructive testing or other design procedures that reveal insufficient structure layer coefficient, excessive cracking, rutting, slab rocking, joint faulting greater than one‑half inch, loss of stiffness, shoving, slippage, thermal cracking, or failure to meet the 3R performance standard. When these deficiencies cannot be remedied by routine maintenance, an overlay provides the missing structural thickness while retaining the underlying pavement and subgrade as a support layer. The design quantifies the effective capacity that remains in the existing layers and adds sufficient new binder or structural course—typically a Portland‑cement concrete layer 2.5 to 4 inches thick for structural overlays, or a thin asphalt wearing course of 1 to 1½ inches when only ride quality is required, increasing to two inches or more if the 3R criterion is not satisfied—to meet anticipated future traffic loads (often at least 2,500 ESALs per day). Prior to placement, uneven or failed areas are milled, scratched, or leveled; a minimum one‑inch leveling course corrects cross‑slope and ensures uniform support. For concrete decks, joint cleaning and sealing restore load transfer, while the overlay raises the grade line away from moisture zones and protects unsuitable subgrade from construction traffic. In addition to restoring load‑carrying capacity, the overlay improves surface texture to achieve the prescribed Skid Resistance Level, prevents water pressure buildup, and renews a smooth rideable surface that satisfies performance requirements for friction, drainage, and durability. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–62), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 110–120), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188), Ch. 11 (pp. 194–196), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

An overlay must first satisfy functional requirements by preserving the existing roadway performance while improving water removal from both the surface and subsurface, providing a level and stable riding platform through scratch‑and‑leveling or milling, and delivering sufficient tire‑pavement friction and texture to prevent water pressure buildup at the posted speed limit. Where skid‑resistance testing shows inadequate grip, the overlay must restore an acceptable level of surface friction, and it may incorporate limited auxiliary or weaving lanes up to three thousand feet provided that the main travel lanes remain uninterrupted for at least ten thousand contiguous feet (excluding bridges).

Structurally the design must add an appropriate layer thickness that raises the pavement’s load‑bearing capacity in accordance with non‑destructive testing or other acceptable procedures. For asphalt, a binder course is typically required; for concrete, thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete overlays range from 2½ to 4 inches, conventional bonded concrete overlays are four inches or more, and the thickness must equal or exceed the calculated pavement thickness for future traffic using rigid‑pavement ESAL factors and meet the minimum depths prescribed in the applicable tables (e.g., Tables 9.5 and 10.4). The underlying base must be stable, uniform, and free of joints that have lost load transfer; any failed portions are to be removed and replaced, while cracks may be stabilized. A higher level of support permits a thinner overlay, and the design must maintain load‑transfer efficiency across joints or cracks.

Durability objectives require that existing surface deterioration be remedied before placement through sealing, patching, or base repair, avoiding poor‑quality patch work, and incorporating interlayer systems—such as asphalt paving interlayers—to retard reflective cracking and moisture intrusion. Joint and crack openings should be limited to roughly 0.5–1.0 mm, slab movement, misalignment, plastic shrinkage cracking, and overall crack widening must be restrained, and unnecessary increases in thickness solely to extend life are discouraged.

Safety is achieved by increasing surface friction, providing a strong safety edge for vehicles drifting off the roadway, ensuring adequate shoulder performance, and preserving existing safety features such as guide‑rail height. The overlay’s improved structural and friction characteristics contribute to a safer traveling environment.

Serviceability goals demand restoration of acceptable ride quality, an eight‑year minimum and twenty‑year maximum structural design life, reduction of future maintenance cycles, and accommodation of projected traffic loads—including at least 2 500 ESALs per day for concrete overlays and high‑traffic criteria of more than 25 000 ADT and ten million ESALs over a twenty‑year period—while maintaining friction and texture throughout the intended service period. [1]

Scope and applicability

Overlay is an appropriate rehabilitation technique for essentially any existing pavement that exhibits structural deficiencies, surface distress, poor ride quality, low friction, inadequate drainage, or insufficient shoulder support. The method can be applied to flexible asphalt‑concrete surfaces, rigid Portland‑cement concrete slabs (jointed plain, jointed reinforced, or composite AC/PCC sections), historic brick pavements, and bridge decks, provided that the deck’s dead‑load capacity has been verified by the District Bridge Engineer and normal minimum thickness limits are waived for those structures. For asphalt pavements the preferred solution is a structural asphalt overlay; thin resurfacing overlays of one to one‑and‑half inches may be placed with or without a leveling course, while thicker layers of two inches or more are used when the 3R criterion cannot be satisfied or when deeper milling is required to remove rutting or other material problems. Preservation‑type overlays on flexible pavements are generally limited to two inches unless additional thickness is needed for structural gain.

For rigid concrete pavements a range of options exists. When the existing slab is in fair to excellent condition, a bonded‑rigid concrete overlay can be placed, typically requiring at least three inches of milled surface and providing a high‑capacity layer that may incorporate synthetic or steel macrofibers at dosages of 0.2–0.5 % by volume; such fiber‑reinforced overlays are commonly designed between three and six inches thick to accommodate heavy traffic and variable support conditions. When the concrete is moderately to severely deteriorated, an unbonded concrete overlay is appropriate, especially where slabs are unstable or visibly rocking; stabilization of cracked areas and provision of uniform support—often with a geotextile interlayer when joint faulting exceeds one‑quarter inch—allow the existing slab to serve as a subbase. Crack‑and‑seat or rubblized preparations followed by an asphalt wearing course are also permissible for concrete that has been patched, fractured, or rubblized.

Specialty overlay materials are employed under specific traffic and project criteria. Stone‑matrix asphalt (SMA) overlays of 1.5 to 2.0 inches with tack coat are used in long‑life programs at the 20‑ and 35‑year rehabilitation points, and similar shoulder overlays extend support where full‑depth shoulders are required. Concrete pavement restoration (CPR) patches are limited to areas that cannot provide a stable base for the overlay; if intermittent patches occur over a length of about five hundred feet with less than ten feet of original concrete between them, full‑width reconstruction is preferred. For high‑volume Interstate corridors—traffic exceeding roughly 25 000 ADT, cumulative ESALs greater than ten million over a twenty‑year design period, and a treatment area of at least 50 000 yd²—a cement‑reinforced mix asphalt binder (CRMAB) may be used, limited to a maximum thickness of 2½ inches and not justified for shoulders unless the lane and shoulder are paved simultaneously. Thin Superpave asphalt overlays are permitted on non‑Interstate routes when the underlying surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than two percent patching required, drainage conditions are satisfactory or can be improved, and no structural upgrade is needed.

In all cases the overlay must address the identified deficiency—whether it is to increase structural capacity as determined by non‑destructive testing, improve surface or subsurface water removal, replace failed base areas, raise the pavement grade line above high‑moisture zones in weak clay or silt subgrades, or enhance ride quality and friction. Minimum preparation includes a one‑inch leveling course for cross‑slope correction when needed, appropriate tack coats, and continuous base drains. When bridge decks are involved, coordination with the Bridge Engineer is mandatory and the overlay thickness limits of standard tables do not apply. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–62), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–71), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 115–131), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188), Ch. 11 (pp. 194–196), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

An overlay is only justified when the existing pavement can serve as a stable, uniformly prepared base and when ancillary work does not drive project costs beyond acceptable limits. If the surface moves under roller pressure, is uneven, unsealed, or inadequately patched, an overlay becomes unsuitable and designers must turn to alternatives such as bonded or unbonded concrete overlays, crack‑and‑seat with a structural asphalt overlay, or full reconstruction. When the pavement’s structural condition demands extensive base repair, severe cracking, stripping of lower asphalt layers, or unresolved drainage problems, a simple overlay cannot provide the needed support; in those cases an unbonded concrete overlay, rubblizing, or complete reconstruction is preferred. Project requirements that involve shoulder widening, slope regrading, guide‑rail relocation, vertical adjustments at structures, or other grade‑line modifications that substantially increase expense also make reconstruction or another rehabilitation method more cost‑effective.

For bridge decks, overlays are limited to maintenance and may be placed only after the District Bridge Engineer confirms sufficient dead‑load capacity; insufficient capacity or work beyond routine maintenance mandates full deck reconstruction. In concrete pavements where patches would be required at intervals of ten feet or less over a length of about five hundred feet, or when more than five hundred feet of full‑width pavement must be replaced, the unstable sections should be removed and reconstructed, possibly using accelerated concrete for rapid reopening.

Thin asphalt overlays thinner than the minimum 1½ inch are permissible only on sound asphalt surfaces with less than two percent patching and adequate drainage; otherwise heater planing, milling, or full‑depth reconstruction is required. Premium mixtures such as CRMAB should not be specified when extensive handwork, stiff mixes requiring raking, shoulders that cannot be paved in a single pass, temporary lanes, crossovers, or higher cost cannot be justified by performance gains.

When the underlying concrete is severely cracked or faulted, a bonded Portland‑cement concrete (PCC) overlay would be compromised; a bonded overlay also requires at least three inches of existing asphalt after milling, while thin bonded concrete overlays are limited to 2½–4 inches and conventional bonded overlays require four inches or more. If the required thickness exceeds six‑point‑five inches, the treatment is treated as a new concrete pavement and must follow full‑depth design procedures with Bureau of Design and Delivery concurrence. For flexible pavements, any preservation overlay thicker than two inches is ineligible, and on concrete surfaces scratch overlays exceeding 4½ inches (or five inches when a larger wearing course is used) exceed preservation limits.

Severe distress that cannot be economically repaired—such as extensive stripping of lower asphalt layers or structural deterioration—precludes bonded overlays; unbonded overlays are appropriate only if the existing pavement can act as a uniform‑support subbase, and adding macrofibers does not convert an unbonded design to a bonded one. Fracturing concrete before applying an unbonded overlay is discouraged; if fracturing is required, an asphalt overlay designed with the “AC over Fractured PCC” module should be used.

Overlay designs that would compromise safety features—such as lowering guide‑rail heights below minimum criteria—or that increase structural depth beyond what is allowed for a first‑time concrete overlay, or that create new travel capacity, must instead be addressed through reconstruction. Preservation projects may extend auxiliary lanes only up to three thousand feet and only when the adjacent main lanes are preserved for at least ten thousand contiguous feet; any larger capacity expansion requires reconstruction. Under the 3R reconstruction criterion, overlays of two inches or more are prohibited, limiting preservation to thin layers of one to one‑and‑a‑half inches with or without leveling. Finally, if concrete patching would cover more than ten percent of the pavement area, an overlay alone is insufficient unless a cost‑effectiveness justification can be demonstrated, and low, highly variable, or uncertain subgrade CBR values—especially isolated weak spots—call for targeted subgrade improvement, stabilization, rock capping, or subdivision of the project into sections with different pavement structures rather than leaving the subgrade untouched. Design inputs and requirements

An overlay design begins by confirming that the existing surface is asphalt and that it is structurally sound, with less than two percent of the pavement requiring patching. The project must either already have or be upgraded to provide good surface and subsurface drainage. Site selection typically targets high‑volume corridors such as Interstates or Interstate look‑alikes, intersections, or steep grades where rutting, shoving, slippage, or thermal cracking are observed; a minimum pavement area of fifty thousand square yards is required for certain CRMAB applications. Traffic inputs must satisfy the average daily traffic limits shown in the applicable tables—generally more than twenty‑five thousand vehicles per day—and demonstrate cumulative equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) exceeding ten million over a twenty‑year design life, or at least 2,500 ESALs per day for bonded concrete overlays. Material specifications call for dense‑graded wearing or binder courses using a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, optionally incorporating reclaimed asphalt pavement or reclaimed asphalt shingles when produced by the wet process; CRMAB mixtures are limited to a maximum layer thickness of two and one‑half inches, while thin overlays on Interstates may not be placed thinner than one and a half inches without special approval. For bonded concrete overlays, a minimum remaining asphalt thickness of three inches after milling is required, with concrete thickness ranging from 2½ to 4 inches for thin sections and four inches or more for conventional sections. Geotechnical data derived from Falling Weight Deflectometer testing provide the necessary pavement and subgrade characteristics for structural analysis, and annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys supply detailed distress information—cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling, joint spalling, and faulting—to verify existing conditions. Environmental considerations include addressing skid‑resistance problems such as those associated with Vanport Limestone concrete, which may trigger an earlier overlay. [1]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 130–131), Ch. 10 (p. 186), Ch. 13 (pp. 220–221)

Overlay design in Pennsylvania is governed by a hierarchy that begins with federal‑aid highway program guidance and FHWA traffic engineering manuals and proceeds to PennDOT’s own policies, most notably Publication 408. Section 413 of Publication 408 requires the existing surface to be stable, level, sealed and patched before any new course is placed, and it imposes weather restrictions for placement as set out in Section 413.3(b). Minimum thicknesses for Superpave wearing‑course and binder‑course overlays are prescribed in Table 9.5, while bridge decks are exempt from the minimum‑thickness tables of Table 10.4; any bridge‑deck overlay must be coordinated with the District Bridge Engineer and limited to maintenance‑type work after confirming dead‑load capacity. Design procedures follow Chapter 10 of the Pennsylvania Specification Manual, employing the AASHTO design method to determine the required asphalt thickness, and concrete overlays reference Sections 540, 541 and 548 for bonded, thin‑bonded and unbonded systems respectively. All reconstruction projects, including rubblized techniques, must incorporate an additional pavement subgrade evaluation as required by Section 6.2.F, and any geometry adjustments such as shoulder width, embankment slope, guide‑rail location or vertical clearances are treated as project constraints because they can affect overall cost.

Surface preparation is a critical performance requirement. Concrete patching prior to overlay must be performed in accordance with the Roadway Construction Standards (Publication 72M), specifically RC‑26M for concrete patches and RC‑21M/RC‑27M when full‑width reconstruction is required, and it must meet the joint spacing, dowel anchorage, material gradations and performance criteria of Publication 408 Sections 516, 519 and 704. Patching quantities are derived from actual field measurements and must anticipate further deterioration between design inspection and construction; typical limits restrict full‑depth patching to one percent of the pavement area at scheduled intervals, although up to ten percent may be permitted if cost‑effectiveness can be demonstrated. Continuous base drainage, installation of Type 6 or Type 7 paved shoulders, application of a non‑tracking tack coat (Publication 408 Section 460) to Superpave wearing courses of one and a half inches or two inches, and cleaning and sealing of joints are required project constraints. Friction performance follows PennDOT’s skid‑resistance guidelines: the coarse aggregate in any asphalt wearing course must achieve a Skid Resistance Level determined by AASHTO T 242 and recorded on design approval forms; higher‑than‑allowed SRL values are prohibited for non‑wearing layers such as leveling courses or shoulders. Mix designs must use PG 64E‑22 binder for standard 6.3 mm wearing courses, may not employ the same binder for a 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix, and must satisfy average daily traffic limits set out in Tables 10.1 and 10.2. Thin Superpave overlays less than 1½ inches are permitted on Interstate facilities only after prior approval from the PDAU; on non‑Interstate routes they may be placed when the existing surface is sound, patching does not exceed two percent, and drainage conditions are satisfactory. Polymer‑modified CRMAB overlays are limited to projects with traffic exceeding 25 000 ADT, cumulative ESALs over ten million for a twenty‑year design life, and an overlay area greater than fifty thousand square yards; such mixes may not exceed a thickness of 2½ inches, must be produced by the wet process, and are restricted to permanent lanes.

Structural analysis considerations include historic brick pavements, where the appropriate structural coefficient from Table 9.3—“Brick with Rigid Base” for concrete bases or “Brick with Flexible Base” for stone or other flexible bases—is applied only to the depth of the brick. For unbonded Portland cement concrete overlays placed on existing asphalt, the DARWin procedure treats the underlying asphalt as support and requires the PCC thickness to equal the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic shown in Table 10.8, with ESAL calculations using the rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1. Thin‑bonded concrete overlays of an existing asphalt surface must incorporate macrofibers as mandated by Publication 408 Section 541; fibers are dosed between 0.2 and 0.5 percent by volume, provide a residual flexural strength (f150) of 100–225 psi, and limit overlay thickness to three‑to‑six inches. Bonded concrete overlays require a milled asphalt base at least three inches thick; thin BCOA sections may range from 2½ to 4 inches in concrete thickness, conventional BCOA from four inches upward, and any overlay exceeding 6½ inches is designed as a new concrete pavement per Section 10.9. All BCOA projects must obtain concurrence from the Bureau of Design and Delivery before the design field view. Geotechnical constraints dictate that CBR values greater than ten require approval by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, while values above twenty are prohibited for use in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation; when the subgrade is not reworked, the conversion factor uses an in‑situ density of 1 000 rather than 1 500. Back‑calculated resilient modulus from Falling Weight Deflectometer testing must be adjusted by a factor of 0.25 in accordance with the 1993 AASHTO Guide. Structural design life requirements specify a minimum of eight years and a maximum of twenty years, documented on Form D‑4332 with PDAU concurrence and FHWA approval when required. Finally, preservation guidelines exclude flexible pavement overlays greater than two inches from Pavement Preservation unless a deeper mill‑and‑overlay is justified, and scratch overlays on concrete may not exceed four and a half inches with a 9.5 mm Superpave mix or five inches with a 12.5 mm mix; overlay projects must not degrade existing safety features such as guide‑rail height nor add new travel lanes unless expressly approved. [1]

Design methods and criteria

Overlay design begins with a quantitative assessment of the existing pavement’s structural capacity, which may be obtained through a condition survey, non‑destructive deflection testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) measurements, or a remaining‑life estimate based on historic traffic. For rigid pavements, FWD data are back‑calculated to resilient modulus and then adjusted by a correction factor of 0.25 to bring the field values into alignment with laboratory results. When subgrade strength is derived from CBR tests, the Heukelom‑Klomp relationship is applied using a multiplier of 1 000 rather than the standard 1 500; CBR values greater than ten require geotechnical approval and those above twenty are prohibited. Field Dynamic Cone Penetration (DCP) results may also be converted to resilient modulus via the same CBR conversion.

The effective structural capacity is denoted by D_eff for rigid sections or SN_eff for flexible layers, while the required future capacity is expressed as D_f or SN_f. Future traffic demand is quantified in 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) according to Chapter 7 and using the average initial truck factors listed in Table 7.1. The designer then selects one of three basic evaluation methods—condition survey, FWD testing, or remaining‑life analysis—to establish D_eff/SN_eff.

Structural analysis employs structural coefficients drawn from Tables 9.3, 9.5, 10.4 and 10.5. For brick pavements the appropriate coefficient depends on whether the bricks rest on a rigid concrete base (brick with rigid base) or on a flexible stone base (brick with flexible base); in either case the coefficient applies only to the depth of the brick, while the underlying base material receives its own coefficient based solely on its depth. Minimum and maximum placement depths, as well as allowable scratch and leveling course thicknesses, are also taken from these tables, and permissible overlay thicknesses are limited by Table 10.5. Average‑daily‑traffic limits are verified against Tables 10.1 and 10.2.

Concrete overlays may be bonded or unbonded. Bonded concrete overlays over asphalt or composite bases incorporate the existing structure in the analysis and follow the BCOA‑ME mechanistic‑empirical procedure when the total thickness does not exceed 6.5 in. A milled asphalt base of at least three inches is required; thin bonded overlays are limited to a nominal concrete thickness of 2.5–4 in, while conventional bonded sections require four inches or more. Overlays thicker than 6.5 in are treated as new concrete pavements per Section 10.9 and must obtain concurrence from the Bureau of Design and Delivery before field work begins. Unbonded overlays act only as a stable platform; they must be separated from the existing pavement by at least a one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt or geotextile layer.

For asphalt overlays, thickness is obtained using the procedure in Chapter 10, which implements the 1993 AASHTO Design Method via mechanistic‑empirical software such as DARWin. The design is recorded on Form D‑4332 for approval. Superpave mix designs employ a PG 64E‑22 binder for standard 6.3 mm wearing courses; when crumb‑rubber modified binders are used, the structural layer coefficient from Table 10.6 governs analysis and the total thickness of that layer may not exceed 2½ in. The mixture must be produced by the wet process, with warm‑mix additives limited to chemical types. Coarse aggregate selection follows the skid‑resistance level (SRL) guidelines in Table 5.4 based on anticipated ADT, and final SRL values are confirmed by friction testing using AASHTO T 242.

When fiber‑reinforced concrete is specified, macro‑fibers contribute a residual strength f150 determined according to ASTM C1609; the effective flexural strength used in analysis is Feff = MOR + f150. Typical fiber contents range from 0.2 % to 0.5 % by volume, yielding f150 values between 100 and 225 psi. A Residual Strength Estimator spreadsheet assists engineers in predicting f150, after which the resulting Feff is entered into mechanistic‑empirical programs such as BCOA‑ME, ACPA Pavement Designer, or AASHTOWare Pavement ME.

Preservation‑oriented overlay options rely on cost‑effectiveness data from PennDOT’s Pavement Management System. Mill‑and‑overlay treatments remove rutting or defective material with typical milling depths of 1½ inches, extendable to 2 inches when necessary. Micro‑surfacing and ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses improve skid resistance and ride quality without providing structural capacity. Cold in‑place recycling of the base is limited to a maximum depth of 4 inches combined with an asphalt overlay not exceeding 1½ inches, while ultra‑thin bonded concrete overlays serve as non‑structural wearing surfaces within prescribed thickness limits. Structural adequacy for any preservation treatment may be verified through FWD testing when required; proposals that exceed the listed limits are routed to the design review process.

All of these procedures depend on tabular design tools rather than explicit algebraic formulas, and material specifications and construction practices are further defined in Publication 408 and the relevant sections of Publication 13M. [1]

When an overlay is planned the engineer must first confirm that the existing pavement can serve as a uniformly stable platform; this includes verifying proper drainage, repairing the base, and applying any required scratch or leveling course so that the surface is level, sealed and patched before new material is placed. A tack coat, joint cleaning and sealing, continuous base drainage, and paved shoulders of Type 6 or Type 7 are required preparatory steps. The existing pavement must show no movement under a roller for Superpave Restricted Performance Specification overlays, and its structural contribution is evaluated with non‑destructive testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements to determine whether additional binder‑course thickness is needed. All overlay designs follow the procedures and minimum thickness values set out in the pavement manual (e.g., Table 9.5 for Superpave wearing and binder courses, Table 10.4 for other materials) and must incorporate an allowance for any further deterioration that could occur between the field inspection used for design and actual construction.

Long‑life asphalt overlays are assumed to serve a 50‑year analysis period and are specified as stone‑matrix‑asphalt wearing courses applied at either 1.5 inches or 2.0 inches with a tack coat, scheduled after full‑depth patching events at years 20 and 35. For all other overlays the design life is limited to a minimum of eight years and may not exceed twenty years. The traffic analysis must be based on reliable current and projected data; for high‑volume interstates an overlay is permitted only when average daily traffic exceeds 25 000 vehicles, cumulative design ESALs are greater than ten million over a twenty‑year period, and the treatment area is larger than fifty thousand square yards. Thin Superpave wearing courses on interstates may not be placed thinner than one‑and‑a‑half inches without prior PDAU approval; on non‑interstate routes they may be used only when the existing surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than two percent patching required and drainage conditions are satisfactory. A leveling course used for profile correction must be at least one inch thick and may be counted as a one‑inch paving layer in the structural analysis.

When overlaying concrete or brick pavements a binder layer of at least 2½ inches is required, and the wearing‑course thickness must meet the minimum values prescribed in the applicable tables. Thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays on asphalt or composite bases may be 2.5 to 4 inches thick, whereas standard bonded JPCP overlays range from 4 to 8 inches. Unbonded concrete overlays require stabilization of the existing surface and uniform support; joints that exhibit more than a quarter‑inch of faulting must be milled, and faults greater than half an inch must be addressed by scratch‑wearing or grinding. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays the residual flexural strength after 150 days (f150) must lie between 100 and 225 psi, the fiber content must be maintained between 0.2 and 0.5 percent by volume, and the overlay thickness is limited to three through six inches.

Specific material limits also apply: CRMAB mixtures may not exceed a thickness of 2½ inches and must be produced by the wet process; SMA overlays are specified at 1.5 or 2.0 inches with a tack coat; scratch (non‑leveling) overlays on rigid pavements are limited to 4½ inches when a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing course is used and to five inches when a 12.5 mm mix is employed. Safety edges, when incorporated, must have a depth between 1½ and 5 inches, adding roughly one percent to material quantities.

Structural contribution of each layer is evaluated using the appropriate layer coefficients from standard tables; for CRMAB layers the same coefficients as conventional Superpave courses are applied. The design CBR value is derived from the in‑situ density of the existing subgrade; if field density is appreciably below maximum dry density a conversion factor of 1 000 (instead of the usual 1 500) is used in the Heukelom and Klomp equation to obtain the resilient modulus. Any CBR greater than ten requires approval by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer, and no CBR may exceed twenty, which limits the maximum allowable resilient modulus to about thirty‑thousand psi.

When an overlay is placed on a bridge deck it must be coordinated with the District Bridge Engineer, who must confirm that the deck can accommodate the added dead load; generic minimum thickness tables are not applied in this situation and no additional safety factors or reliability thresholds are prescribed beyond the engineer’s dead‑load assessment.

Coarse‑aggregate skid‑resistance level (SRL) for asphalt wearing courses is selected from Table 5.4 based on anticipated traffic volume, recorded on design approval forms, typical sections and contract documents; an SRL higher than permitted may not be used on non‑wearing layers such as leveling courses or shoulders.

Reliability requirements are satisfied through performance‑based testing: Falling Weight Deflectometer testing verifies joint and patch integrity before construction of concrete overlays, providing an implicit factor of safety. Overlays that exceed nominal thickness thresholds may be allowed only after core inspection confirms sufficient subgrade support or when the three‑R (roughness‑rutting‑reliability) performance criterion is met. All overlay designs must be documented on Form D‑4332, obtain PDAU concurrence and, where required, FHWA approval. [1]

Structural section and dimensions

Overlay thickness requirements depend on the material and design criteria. Conventional asphalt overlays are applied at a thickness of either one‑and‑half inches or two inches, with shoulders receiving a tack coat but no separate dimensional specification. When a cold‑in‑place recycled mix asphalt binder (CRMAB) is used, the overlay shall not exceed two‑and‑a‑half inches and is only justified on shoulders when lane and shoulder are placed in a single pass. For Portland cement concrete overlays, the existing milled asphalt must be at least three inches thick before placement; thin bonded concrete overlays may range from 2½ to 4 inches, whereas conventional bonded concrete overlays are typically four inches or greater. Thin surface overlays that do not follow the 3R criterion are limited to one to 1½ inches, and if the 3R requirement is omitted the overlay may be two inches or thicker. No explicit dimensions are provided for underlying pavement, base, subbase, treated‑soil layer, slab, edge support, or reinforcement. [1]

Materials and mixture design

The overlay specification must first identify whether a rigid Portland‑cement concrete (PCC) system or a flexible asphalt‑concrete system will be used. For PCC overlays the mix shall follow Publication 408 Section 704 with an optimized aggregate gradation and must satisfy the minimum compressive strength and time‑to‑strength requirements of Publication 408 Section 501; high‑early‑strength cement is expressly prohibited because of its tendency to crack. Thickness limits are 2.5–4 inches for thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays, 4–8 inches for standard bonded sections, and unbonded overlays must include a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt separation layer or an equivalent geotextile interlayer. When a geotextile is selected any joints faulting more than a quarter inch shall be milled, and if joint fault exceeds half an inch an additional Superpave wearing course of 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix must be placed at the joint. If macro‑fibers are incorporated the specification shall state the required residual flexural strength (f150) between 100 and 225 psi, a fiber dosage of roughly 0.2–0.5 % by volume, fiber length of 1 to 2.5 inches, aspect ratio of 30 to 100, and allow synthetic or steel fibers that achieve the stipulated residual strength.

For flexible overlays the exact asphalt mixture must be named. A Stone Matrix Asphalt (SMA) wearing course may be milled and placed at either 1.5 inches or 2.0 inches with a tack coat applied to the existing surface and shoulders. When a Superpave mix is used, the nominal aggregate size shall be specified—options include 25 mm, 19 mm, 12.5 mm, 9.5 mm, or 6.3 mm for thin‑overlay applications—and the required binder grade must be indicated. PG 64E‑22 binder is mandated for standard 6.3 mm wearing courses and prohibited for 9.5 mm fine‑graded mixes. The coarse‑aggregate Skid Resistance Level (SRL) appropriate to traffic volume shall be selected from the prescribed tables and recorded, as it governs surface friction. If a crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder is chosen, the specification must require a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder produced by the wet process; RAP or RAS may be permitted in accordance with the SSP, layer thickness shall not exceed 2½ inches, and a chemical additive must be used when warm‑mix technology is employed (foaming is not allowed). All asphalt overlays must respect the minimum and maximum thickness limits for the selected course type as defined in the ADT‑based tables—typically 1.5 inches for a 9.5 mm mix, 2 inches for a 12.5 mm mix, up to 2½ inches for dense‑graded wearing courses; cold‑in‑place recycling overlays are limited to 1½ inches, and micro‑surfacing treatments may not exceed roughly 0.25 inch on pavement verified as structurally sound by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing.

Thus the overlay specification must enumerate the material system, concrete mix design parameters or asphalt mixture type, aggregate gradation, binder grade, SRL designation, required interlayer type and thickness, fiber properties when used, permissible RAP/RAS content, production process for polymer‑modified binders, and the applicable thickness ranges derived from traffic volume and structural design. [1] — Ch. 2 (p. 62), Ch. 3 (p. 71), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 5 (pp. 110–131), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Overlay material and mixture selection begins with a traffic‑volume assessment and structural analysis that define the required effective flexural strength or design ESALs for the intended service life. Designers consult the prescribed tables to obtain the appropriate structural coefficient, the allowable minimum and maximum placement depths for each mix type, and the correct Superpave mixture size for the chosen course thickness. For flexible pavements a thin‑asphalt overlay is limited to sound surfaces that satisfy average daily traffic limits; the standard options are a 6.3 mm wearing course using a PG 64E‑22 binder or a 9.5 mm fine‑graded wearing course, each applied at a minimum thickness of one and a half inches on non‑Interstate facilities unless an exception is granted. When profile correction is required a separate leveling course of at least one inch may be added and counted toward the structural thickness, and interlayer systems are incorporated when reflective cracking or moisture protection dominate the design.

When traffic volumes, cumulative loads, and project area justify higher cost, a CRMAB mixture can replace a dense‑graded wearing or binder course that would otherwise use polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder. The CRMAB mix is placed by the wet process in the upper layers of the overlay, limited to about two‑and‑a‑half inches thick, and may include RAP or RAS as permitted; however, projects requiring extensive handwork or raking are excluded because of the mixture’s stiffness, and only chemical additives—not foaming agents—are allowed for warm‑mix applications.

Overlaying cement concrete or brick pavements demands a minimum binder layer of two and a half inches, with the wearing course meeting its prescribed minimum thickness. The appropriate Superpave mixture size is selected from the tables that match the course thickness to ensure durability, constructability, and performance over an eight‑to‑twenty‑year design life.

For unbonded concrete overlays, a geotextile or asphalt interlayer separates the new concrete from the existing pavement. A nominal one‑inch thick Superpave wearing course of 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix provides structural support; if joint faulting exceeds half an inch, a scratch mix of the same gradation replaces it, and proper drainage is achieved by daylighting the interlayer or installing a vertical subdrain.

Fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays are designed by first specifying the required residual flexural strength (f150). The contractor then selects a macrofiber type and dosage—typically between 0.2 % and 0.5 % by volume—to achieve that target, adjusting water‑reducing admixtures to offset the loss of workability caused by the fibers. The resulting effective flexural strength is entered into mechanistic‑empirical design software to determine overlay thickness that satisfies structural capacity, durability against cracking and freeze‑thaw, and constructability.

Preservation guidelines restrict flexible‑pavement overlays thicker than two inches unless deeper milling (up to one and a half to two inches) is needed to remove rutting or defective layers; after such milling a compatible wearing course may be placed, including an ultra‑thin bonded concrete overlay of two inches as a non‑structural wearing course. For rigid pavements the allowable scratch course is limited to four‑and‑a‑half inches when a 9.5 mm Superpave mix is used or five inches with a 12.5 mm mix, while structural overlays are defined as one and a half inches of 9.5 mm mix or two inches of 12.5 mm mix over appropriate base thicknesses to meet durability, constructability, and performance goals such as skid resistance and ride quality. Structural details and interfaces

An overlay begins with thorough surface preparation that includes cleaning and sealing of all existing longitudinal and transverse concrete joints, as well as sealing any asphalt joints and cracks. Where transverse or patch joints are present they must be saw‑cut and sealed so the new layer forms a continuous load path; this applies to both concrete and asphalt pavements. Any joint that has lost its load‑transfer capacity or is crushed must be removed and replaced with a dowelled concrete patch, the dowels being bonded along their full embedded length with epoxy, polyester or vinylester binders, and coated as required. When full‑depth reconstruction is called for, standard joint spacing of 30 ft for reinforced concrete or 15 ft for plain concrete is used together with appropriate seals. For unbonded concrete overlays the joints are placed exactly as they would be in a new slab, based on slab thickness, and need not line up with the existing pavement joints. Bonded concrete overlays rely on the bond between old and new slabs and therefore do not require special joint continuity. An asphalt overlay over concrete must be saw‑cut and sealed across every underlying transverse or patched joint; load transfer may be provided through dowel‑bar retrofits or other concrete‑pavement‑restoration techniques, and any required slab stabilization or jacking is incorporated to maintain structural continuity. Reinforcement can be supplied by an interlayer such as a needle‑punched geotextile paving fabric, fiberglass mesh grids, or composite reinforcing grids that become embedded in the asphalt mix and provide tensile and torsional strength. When a geotextile separation layer is used, joints with more than ¼ inch of faulting are milled, and those exceeding ½ inch receive a Superpave wearing‑course (scratch) mix or grinding to reduce the fault before placement. Proper drainage for the interlayer is achieved by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain, and the interlayer must extend through reconstructed shoulders and be tied into the mainline. Fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays depend on correctly timed and depth‑controlled contraction (saw‑cut) joints; macrofibers help maintain load‑transfer efficiency but do not replace dowel bars where fault control is required. Finally, at the pavement edge a Safety Edge taper of at least 1½ inches provides the transition detail for both wearing and binder courses. [1]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 67–69), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 10 (pp. 185–188), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Overlay design begins with a systematic assessment of the existing pavement to determine whether the new layer will be bonded to or separated from the underlying material. When the existing surface is sound, a bonded system is pursued; this requires cleaning and sealing longitudinal and transverse joints, applying an asphalt tack coat, and allowing the old and new materials to act as one monolithic unit. A continuous bond is created by tack coating before placing the binder and wearing courses, typically a 1½‑ to 2‑inch SMA wearing course over the prepared surface. The underlying pavement must provide stable, uniform support, so any failed base material, cracked concrete, or severely deteriorated joints are removed, while non‑critical joints may be stabilized after cleaning and sealing. Where profile errors exist, a separate scratch or leveling course of at least one inch thickness is placed to create a level working platform; this course remains distinct from the binder and wearing courses to preserve quality control. For unbonded concrete overlays no asphalt separation layer is used as a leveling course; instead all grade corrections are accomplished with the concrete overlay itself, which rests directly on the prepared platform. An isolation layer of fine‑graded asphalt at least one inch thick or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer is installed between old concrete and new concrete when an unbonded design is required, preventing the need to align transverse joints in the overlay with existing joints. Joint faulting greater than a quarter inch is milled, and if faulting exceeds half an inch a 9.5 mm Superpave wearing course (scratch mix) of nominal one‑inch thickness is placed at the joint or the joint is milled/grinded to reduce faulting. The interlayer must be provided with drainage either by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain, and it extends through any reconstructed shoulder, being tied into the mainline. When overlaying asphalt on concrete or brick, at least 1½ inches of existing asphalt must remain after milling to retain structural integrity; for bonded overlays over asphalt a minimum of three inches of milled asphalt is required so that the Portland cement concrete can act monolithically with the base. Minimum binder thicknesses of 2½ inches are prescribed for asphalt overlays on cement‑concrete or brick pavements, and each wearing course must meet its material‑specific minimum thickness. Fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays typically range from three to six inches in thickness, with contraction joints saw‑cut at appropriate depth and timing; however, flexible‑pavement overlays greater than two inches are excluded from preservation programs, and concrete overlays may not exceed four‑and‑a‑half inches when a 9.5 mm mix is used or five inches with a 12.5 mm mix. At curb intersections the overlay may be tapered to a one‑inch depth within parking lanes after confirming proper compaction and sealing of the pavement‑curb joint, while travel lanes retain full design thickness. Guide rails, drainage structures, and other adjacent features intersecting the overlay are adjusted or replaced to maintain alignment and proper water management, with continuous base drains provided to control subsurface moisture. Heavy‑duty membranes are placed where moisture control is required, and any deviation from these interface requirements is subject to review by the district pavement management engineer. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–62), Ch. 3 (pp. 67–71), Ch. 4 (pp. 84–98), Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188), Ch. 12 (pp. 204–205)

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

In designing an overlay the pavement system must first guarantee that water is efficiently removed from both the surface and the structure beneath. A continuous base drain is provided to convey water away, and any guide‑rail or existing drainage components are adjusted or replaced so that flow paths remain uninterrupted. All longitudinal and transverse joints are cleaned and sealed, and heavy‑duty membranes are installed where necessary to block moisture intrusion into the underlying layers. The design also requires verification of subsurface drainage conditions; wherever deficiencies are identified, adequate subdrainage is incorporated, often by installing several subgrade drains to resolve the problem. Surface drainage must be good or upgraded to good, and the same standard applies to subsurface drainage, with interlayer systems such as geotextile fabrics or composite reinforcing grids acting as barriers that prevent surface water from penetrating the pavement structure. Climate effects, particularly freeze‑thaw cycles, are addressed by selecting an appropriate overlay thickness; a thicker overlay combined with an interlayer can delay cracking in harsh environments. When the existing subgrade is frost‑susceptible, the design either replaces the problematic soil or provides a stronger base course together with pavement‑base drains and a positive slope to keep water away, thereby mitigating freeze‑thaw damage and differential heaving. For projects that use varying overlay thicknesses, drainage beneath each new section is coordinated so that water does not accumulate at transitions. Finally, proper drainage of the separation layer is ensured by daylighting the interlayer or installing a vertical subdrain, with this drainage extending through any reconstructed shoulders and being tied into the mainline. An overlay must be designed to meet a comprehensive set of safety, geometric, accessibility, and user‑performance requirements while preserving the existing roadway geometry. Adequate drainage is fundamental; continuous pavement base drains are provided and any guide‑rail or other drainage structures that could impede water removal are adjusted or replaced so that surface and subsurface water are efficiently discharged and unsuitable subgrade material is protected from construction traffic exposure. A level, stable platform is achieved by placing a minimum one‑inch leveling course—or by milling where existing irregularities exist—to establish the required cross‑slope before the wearing or binder courses are laid at uniform depth.

For flexible (asphalt) overlays the structural thickness shall be 1.5 inches or 2.0 inches and must be sealed with a tack coat; shoulders receive the same treatment. Where preservation criteria apply, the asphalt overlay shall not exceed two inches, otherwise it is treated as a structural overlay. Concrete overlays require at least three inches of existing asphalt to remain after milling before bonding, and thin bonded concrete overlays (BCOA) are limited to 2.5 – 4 inches, while conventional or full‑depth bonded overlays must be four inches or greater. Joint geometry for reinforced concrete follows a 30‑foot spacing and for plain concrete a 15‑foot spacing, with perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars, and proper seals; faulting greater than one‑quarter inch is milled, and faulting exceeding one‑half inch triggers placement of an additional Superpave wearing course or grinding to preserve ride quality.

Safety features are retained or enhanced. Guide‑rail heights may not be reduced below the minimum acceptable criteria, and any required relocations of guide rails, shoulder‑width adjustments, slope changes to retain embankment width, or vertical clearances at structures must be incorporated so that the pavement grade line remains appropriate. A Safety Edge is provided on the outside edge of all asphalt pavements and shoulders—a 30° tapered transition (construction tolerance 26°–40°) with a minimum thickness of 1½ inches and a maximum overall depth of five inches—adding roughly one percent extra material to improve vehicle stability. For locations with stopping, turning, or low‑speed traffic, a non‑tracking tack coat is recommended to further enhance safety.

User performance criteria require that the finished surface develop sufficient skid resistance and texture. Coarse aggregate shall be selected to achieve the appropriate Skid Resistance Level (SRL) for the anticipated traffic volume; the SRL designation is documented on design approvals, typical sections, and contract proposals. The overlay must restore or improve ride quality, eliminate rutting, shoving, or slippage, and provide adequate low‑speed gradient to prevent water pressure buildup at the tire/pavement interface at the posted speed. Micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, or an ultra‑thin (2 inch) non‑structural concrete overlay may be used to address skid resistance and rut filling.

Shoulder performance is addressed by providing Type 6 or Type 7 paved shoulders—or full‑depth asphalt shoulders where specified—and extending any interlayer through reconstructed shoulders while ensuring drainage of the interlayer either by daylighting or a vertical subdrain. The overlay design must also consider user impacts such as traffic‑user delay; accelerated concrete patching can allow reopening to traffic within four to seven hours, and liquidated‑damage provisions apply for lane‑closure delays under high‑speed, high‑ADT conditions.

Accessibility requirements are not separately enumerated in the source material beyond maintaining existing clearances. Consequently, the overlay must preserve vertical clearances at structures and maintain the established roadway geometry so that all users, including those with mobility constraints, can continue to use the facility without impediment. Verification and expected performance

The verification of a completed overlay begins with confirming that the existing pavement was stable—showing no movement under the roller—and had been properly leveled, sealed and patched before any new surface was placed. All design items such as drainage provisions, base repairs, leveling, binder thickness, friction treatment and shoulder improvements must be addressed, and the project plans are required to delineate the exact paving limits, particularly when a Restricted Performance Specification is used, so that the finished work can be measured against those documented boundaries. After placement, compliance and constructability are established by producing cross‑sections at roughly fifty‑foot intervals to ensure that the quantities of material installed correspond to the design, while core samples are taken in areas where slab depth varies to verify actual thickness. Density quality is then assessed using the Percent Within Limits methodology or core sampling as prescribed in the bid documents, confirming that field density falls within the required range. The interlayer must be shown to drain properly—either daylighted or equipped with a vertical subdrain—and it must run continuously through any reconstructed shoulder and be tied into the mainline. Joint preparation is inspected for faulting; joints exhibiting more than a quarter‑inch of faulting are milled, and if faulting exceeds half an inch a Superpave wearing‑course (scratch) mix is placed or the joint is ground to reduce the fault. Together these checks demonstrate that the overlay meets the specified thickness, drainage, joint‑faulting limits and overall project requirements, confirming compliance, constructability, performance and consistency with the contract specifications. [1]

[1] — Ch. 2 (pp. 54–57), Ch. 10 (p. 185)

Overlay designs are intended to provide a structural service life ranging from eight to twenty years while correcting deficiencies that affect ride quality or surface friction. Longevity, resistance to structural distress, and improved riding comfort constitute the primary performance outcomes. Design calculations employ current traffic data—average daily traffic, growth rates, and truck percentages—to compute 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs); material thicknesses are selected from FHWA‑prescribed tables and documented on Form D‑4332 for concurrence. When a fiber‑reinforced concrete overlay is specified, the added macrofibers increase residual tensile strength measured as f150 according to ASTM C1609; this value is summed with the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength (feff) that feeds mechanistic‑empirical design tools for thickness sizing. Laboratory slab tests record higher load capacity and a 30 % increase in load‑transfer efficiency, particularly when pre‑existing crack widths exceed one millimeter, thereby limiting crack deterioration and extending serviceability. On the surface level, milling to remove rutting combined with micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses is expected to eliminate existing ruts, raise skid resistance, and deliver a smoother ride. Performance verification includes confirming that the milling depth has removed the rut, that the applied surfacing achieves the targeted skid‑resistance and ride‑quality criteria, and that structural adequacy meets Falling Weight Deflectometer thresholds where required.
