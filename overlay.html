Overlay

Part of: Rehabilitation is-subtopic-of
Overlay is a topic under Rehabilitation.
overlayoverlays
Created 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-29

Overlay is a pavement‑rehabilitation technique applied when an existing asphalt or concrete surface no longer provides adequate structural support, smoothness, friction, or drainage for safe and economical operation[1][2]. The process begins with thorough preparation—milling, scarifying, cleaning, sealing cracks, repairing potholes, and correcting joint faulting—to create a uniform, dry substrate onto which a new structural layer of Portland‑cement concrete (typically 2.5–4 in.) or an asphalt wearing course (minimum 1½ in., up to 2½ in.) is placed[1][2]. This added layer restores load‑carrying capacity, improves skid resistance and water drainage, and provides a smooth rideable surface that meets prescribed friction, durability, and design‑life criteria (generally eight to twenty years)[1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Overlay” also include the following subtopics: Asphalt Overlay, Concrete Overlay, Leveling Course, Overlay Design, Resurfacing, Structural Overlay, and Surface Preparation.

Overlay design determines the required thickness and material specifications for both Asphalt Overlay and Concrete Overlay based on traffic loads and existing structural numbers. [1][2] Surface preparation establishes a clean, stable substrate by repairing defects and correcting geometry, often utilizing a Leveling Course to ensure proper adhesion and smoothness. [1][2] Structural Overlay provides the necessary load-bearing capacity when the pavement lacks support, while Resurfacing addresses functional deficiencies with thinner layers for ride quality and friction. [1][2]

Purpose and design objectives

Overlay is applied when an existing pavement no longer provides adequate structural support, smoothness, or friction for safe and economical operation, as indicated by testing that reveals insufficient structure‑layer coefficient, excessive cracking, rutting, slab rocking, joint faulting greater than one‑half inch, loss of stiffness, shoving, slippage, thermal cracking, or failure to meet the 3R performance standard. The need also arises when the pavement has become unsound, exhibiting surface defects and deteriorated material that jeopardize structural integrity and ride quality. To address these deficiencies, the existing surface is prepared by milling, scarifying, or removing uneven or failed areas, cleaning debris, vegetation, oil, sealing cracks, repairing potholes, stabilizing moving slabs, and correcting joint faulting greater than a quarter‑inch (or providing a scratch course when offset exceeds half an inch). A minimum one‑inch leveling course may be placed to correct cross‑slope and provide uniform support before the new layer. The overlay adds structural thickness—typically a Portland‑cement concrete layer 2.5 to 4 inches thick for structural overlays, or an asphalt wearing course of at least 1½ inches (increasing to two inches when the 3R criterion is not satisfied) to meet anticipated future traffic loads often at least 2,500 ESALs per day. For concrete decks, a Class AA cement‑concrete overlay is placed when the concrete temperature is between 65 °F and 90 °F so that the slab attains a minimum compressive strength of 3,600 psi and bond strength not less than 175 psi. The overlay restores load‑carrying capacity, improves surface texture to achieve prescribed skid‑resistance levels, prevents water‑pressure buildup, and renews a smooth rideable surface that satisfies friction, drainage, and durability requirements. Timely placement—first overlay within seven calendar days after milling and prohibiting traffic on the milled surface for more than six days—prevents prolonged exposure and ensures rapid regain of uniform support and serviceability. The overlay must preserve existing roadway performance while improving drainage of surface and subsurface water, providing a level and stable riding platform through scratch‑and‑leveling or milling, and delivering adequate tire‑pavement friction and texture to prevent water pressure buildup at the posted speed limit; where skid‑resistance testing shows inadequate grip, the overlay must restore an acceptable level of surface friction, and it may accommodate limited auxiliary or weaving lanes up to three thousand feet provided that the main travel lanes remain uninterrupted for at least ten thousand contiguous feet (excluding bridges) [1]. In addition, the overlay is required to restore or enhance the functional capacity of the existing pavement by delivering a structurally sound, durable and safe riding surface [2]. Structurally the design must add an appropriate layer thickness that raises the pavement’s load‑bearing capacity in accordance with non‑destructive testing or other acceptable procedures, with asphalt designs typically requiring a binder course and concrete overlays ranging from 2½ to 4 inches for thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete to four inches or more for conventional bonded concrete, the thickness being equal to or exceeding the calculated pavement thickness for future traffic using rigid‑pavement ESAL factors and meeting the minimum depths prescribed in the applicable tables [1]. For concrete overlays the layout is limited to panels whose longest dimension does not exceed eighteen times the overlay thickness and whose length‑to‑width ratio is capped at 1.5, while edge slump adjacent to shoulders may not exceed a quarter inch and next to adjoining lanes a eighth inch [2]. Placement temperatures must be maintained between 65 °F and 90 °F for the concrete and below 120 °F for the underlying substrate, and the first traffic opening is permitted only after the concrete attains a compressive strength of at least 3,000 psi, with a target of 3,600 psi before regular traffic use [2]. Bond‑strength testing at seven days must show an average of no less than 175 psi and each individual test at least 160 psi [2]. The underlying base must be stable, uniform, and free of joints that have lost load transfer; any failed portions are to be removed and replaced, while cracks may be stabilized, and a higher level of support permits a thinner overlay while maintaining load‑transfer efficiency across joints or cracks [1]. Durability objectives require that existing surface deterioration be remedied before placement through sealing, patching, or base repair, avoiding poor‑quality patch work, and incorporating interlayer systems such as asphalt paving interlayers to retard reflective cracking and moisture intrusion [1]. Joint and crack openings should be limited to roughly 0.5–1.0 mm, and slab movement, misalignment, plastic shrinkage cracking, and overall crack widening must be restrained; unnecessary increases in thickness solely to extend life are discouraged [1]. Rigorous surface preparation mandates a clean, dry substrate free of loose debris with cracks sealed and tack‑coated within the manufacturer’s limits of 290 °F to 320 °F, followed by a minimum compacted thickness of 1½ inches for the first lift and saturation of reinforcement fabric or grid with 0.22–0.28 gallons per square yard of asphalt material so that overlaps are at least one inch [2]. When the overlay thickness is less than three inches, No. 67 coarse aggregate shall replace No. 57 [2]. Macro‑fibers must be added in a proportion that yields the design residual strength (f150) as verified by ASTM C1609‑12 testing at 14 days, and the concrete mix must limit chloride ion penetration to a Low, Very Low or Negligible rating within 56 days, not exceeding 2,800 coulombs (or equivalent resistance values) [2]. Curing shall include application of a uniform curing compound at roughly one gallon per 125 ft², and insulating blankets are required when air temperature falls to 45 °F or lower so that surface temperature change does not exceed 40 °F in any hour [2]. Safety is achieved by increasing surface friction, providing a strong safety edge for vehicles drifting off the roadway, ensuring adequate shoulder performance, and preserving existing safety features such as guide‑rail height, while the overlay’s improved structural and friction characteristics contribute to a safer traveling environment [1]. During construction the milled surface must be smooth and defect‑free and placed within seven calendar days of milling; traffic may not travel on any milled pavement for more than six days, appropriate warning signs and reduced speed limits must be posted, and edge‑slump limits, temperature controls and joint sealing further protect motorists [2]. Serviceability goals demand restoration of acceptable ride quality with a finished surface smoothness within one‑eighth inch over twelve feet and cross‑slope accuracy of the same magnitude to provide positive drainage, thereby guaranteeing a rideable, serviceable surface for the life of the overlay [2]. The overlay must be designed for a minimum eight‑year and maximum twenty‑year structural design life, reduce future maintenance cycles, accommodate projected traffic loads—including at least 2 500 ESALs per day for concrete overlays and high‑traffic criteria of more than 25 000 ADT and ten million ESALs over a twenty‑year period—while maintaining friction and texture throughout the intended service period [1]. Joint spacing, saw‑cut depth and final cleaning of the pavement must be completed within thirty minutes of the pour, and excess material removed to prevent wrinkles, thereby supporting long‑term serviceability [2].

Scope and applicability

Overlay is appropriate for essentially any existing pavement that shows structural deficiencies, surface distress, poor ride quality, low friction, inadequate drainage, or insufficient shoulder support, and it can be applied to flexible asphalt‑concrete surfaces, rigid Portland‑cement concrete slabs of various joint configurations, historic brick pavements, and bridge decks when the deck’s dead‑load capacity has been verified. For flexible asphalt pavements the preferred solution is a structural asphalt overlay, with thin resurfacing overlays of one to one‑and‑half inches placed with or without a leveling course when the existing surface is sound, while layers two inches or thicker are used when the 3R criterion cannot be met or deeper milling is required to remove rutting or other material problems. Preservation‑type overlays on flexible pavements are generally limited to two inches unless additional thickness is needed for structural gain, and a one‑inch minimum leveling course may be provided for cross‑slope correction as part of the preparation. When traffic volumes exceed roughly twenty‑five thousand average daily trips, cumulative design ESALs surpass ten million over a twenty‑year period, and the treatment area is at least fifty thousand square yards, a cement‑reinforced mix asphalt binder (CRMAB) may be employed, limited to a maximum thickness of 2½ inches and not justified for shoulders unless lane and shoulder are paved simultaneously. Long‑life programs also make use of stone‑matrix asphalt (SMA) overlays ranging from one‑point‑five to two inches with tack coat at the 20‑ and 35‑year rehabilitation points, and similar SMA shoulder overlays extend support where full‑depth shoulders are required. On non‑Interstate routes thin Superpave asphalt overlays are permitted when the underlying surface is asphalt, the pavement is structurally sound with less than two percent patching, drainage conditions are satisfactory or can be improved, and no structural upgrade is needed. For rigid concrete pavements a bonded‑rigid concrete overlay can be placed when the existing slab is in fair to excellent condition, typically requiring at least three inches of milled surface and often incorporating synthetic or steel macrofibers at dosages of 0.2–0.5 % by volume; such fiber‑reinforced overlays are commonly designed between three and six inches thick for heavy traffic and variable support conditions. When the concrete is moderately to severely deteriorated, an unbonded concrete overlay is appropriate, especially where slabs are unstable or visibly rocking, and a geotextile interlayer is used when joint faulting exceeds one‑quarter inch; if faulting exceeds one‑half inch a nominal one‑inch thick Superpave mix may be placed as an additional separation layer. Preparation of the existing concrete includes scarifying, cleaning and roughening the surface, stabilizing or patching unstable slabs, and filling shallow depressions with approved asphalt‑ or cementitious‑based material before overlay placement. Bonded overlays on rigid pavements require a Class AA cement‑concrete mix placed at temperatures between 65 °F and 90 °F, the application of curing compounds within thirty minutes after bleed‑water dissipation, and must achieve an average bond strength of at least 175 psi (no individual test below 160 psi) and a compressive strength of 3 600 psi before traffic is permitted. Thicknesses for thin bonded concrete overlays on asphalt‑surfaced roads typically range from about two‑and‑a‑half to four inches, while more extensive rehabilitations may require four to eight inches, with an overall maximum thickness generally limited to seven inches; when the overlay thickness approaches this limit polypropylene fibers are incorporated per ASTM C1116. If the concrete overlay is less than three inches thick, No. 67 coarse aggregate replaces No. 57 and synthetic or steel macrofibers are added at a dosage sufficient to meet the specified residual strength value (f150). The concrete mix for bonded overlays must be engineered to attain low, very‑low, or negligible chloride ion penetration within fifty‑six days as defined by AASHTO test methods. Unbonded concrete overlays may also be used over existing concrete pavements that cannot be cost‑effectively repaired to good condition, provided the existing pavement can serve as a uniform subbase; crack‑and‑seat or rubblized preparations followed by an asphalt wearing course are permissible alternatives for patched, fractured, or rubblized concrete. When intermittent patches occur over a length of approximately five hundred feet with less than ten feet of original concrete remaining between patches, full‑width reconstruction of the entire section is preferred. Historic brick pavements may be overlaid using the same structural coefficients as for brick on rigid or flexible bases, and the base condition must be considered in the overlay design. Bridge deck overlays are treated as maintenance measures that require coordination with the district bridge engineer; dead‑load capacity must be verified and standard minimum thickness tables do not apply to these structures. Across all pavement types, the overlay must address the identified deficiency—whether it is to increase structural capacity as determined by non‑destructive testing, improve surface or subsurface water removal, replace failed base areas, raise the grade line above high‑moisture zones, or enhance ride quality and friction. Minimum preparation generally includes a one‑inch leveling course where needed, appropriate tack coats, continuous base drains, and quantity calculations that allow up to two percent extra material to accommodate unsound pavement removal during preparation. An overlay is only justified when the existing pavement can serve as a stable, uniformly prepared base and when ancillary work does not drive project costs beyond acceptable limits. If the surface moves under roller pressure, is uneven, unsealed, or inadequately patched, an overlay becomes unsuitable and designers must turn to alternatives such as bonded or unbonded concrete overlays, crack‑and‑seat with a structural asphalt overlay, or full reconstruction. When the pavement’s structural condition demands extensive base repair, severe cracking, stripping of lower asphalt layers, or unresolved drainage problems, a simple overlay cannot provide the needed support; in those cases an unbonded concrete overlay, rubblizing, or complete reconstruction is preferred. Project requirements that involve shoulder widening, slope regrading, guide‑rail relocation, vertical adjustments at structures, or other grade‑line modifications that substantially increase expense also make reconstruction or another rehabilitation method more cost‑effective.

For bridge decks, overlays are limited to maintenance and may be placed only after the District Bridge Engineer confirms sufficient dead‑load capacity; insufficient capacity or work beyond routine maintenance mandates full deck reconstruction. In concrete pavements where patches would be required at intervals of ten feet or less over a length of about five hundred feet, or when more than five hundred feet of full‑width pavement must be replaced, the unstable sections should be removed and reconstructed, possibly using accelerated concrete for rapid reopening.

Thin asphalt overlays thinner than the minimum 1½ inch are permissible only on sound asphalt surfaces with less than two percent patching and adequate drainage; otherwise heater planing, milling, or full‑depth reconstruction is required. Premium mixtures such as CRMAB should not be specified when extensive handwork, stiff mixes requiring raking, shoulders that cannot be paved in a single pass, temporary lanes, crossovers, or higher cost cannot be justified by performance gains.

When the underlying concrete is severely cracked or faulted, a bonded Portland‑cement concrete (PCC) overlay would be compromised; a bonded overlay also requires at least three inches of existing asphalt after milling, while thin bonded concrete overlays are limited to 2½–4 inches and conventional bonded overlays require four inches or more. If the required thickness exceeds six‑point‑five inches, the treatment is treated as a new concrete pavement and must follow full‑depth design procedures with Bureau of Design and Delivery concurrence. For flexible pavements, any preservation overlay thicker than two inches is ineligible, and on concrete surfaces scratch overlays exceeding 4½ inches (or five inches when a larger wearing course is used) exceed preservation limits.

Severe distress that cannot be economically repaired—such as extensive stripping of lower asphalt layers or structural deterioration—precludes bonded overlays; unbonded overlays are appropriate only if the existing pavement can act as a uniform‑support subbase, and adding macrofibers does not convert an unbonded design to a bonded one. Fracturing concrete before applying an unbonded overlay is discouraged; if fracturing is required, an asphalt overlay designed with the “AC over Fractured PCC” module should be used.

Overlay designs that would compromise safety features—such as lowering guide‑rail heights below minimum criteria—or that increase structural depth beyond what is allowed for a first‑time concrete overlay, or that create new travel capacity, must instead be addressed through reconstruction. Preservation projects may extend auxiliary lanes only up to three thousand feet and only when the adjacent main lanes are preserved for at least ten thousand contiguous feet; any larger capacity expansion requires reconstruction. Under the 3R reconstruction criterion, overlays of two inches or more are prohibited, limiting preservation to thin layers of one to one‑and‑a‑half inches with or without leveling. Finally, if concrete patching would cover more than ten percent of the pavement area, an overlay alone is insufficient unless a cost‑effectiveness justification can be demonstrated, and low, highly variable, or uncertain subgrade CBR values—especially isolated weak spots—call for targeted subgrade improvement, stabilization, rock capping, or subdivision of the project into sections with different pavement structures rather than leaving the subgrade untouched. Design inputs and requirements

An overlay design begins by confirming that the existing surface is asphalt and that it is structurally sound, with less than two percent of the pavement requiring patching. The project must either already have or be upgraded to provide good surface and subsurface drainage. Site selection typically targets high‑volume corridors such as Interstates or Interstate look‑alikes, intersections, or steep grades where rutting, shoving, slippage, or thermal cracking are observed; a minimum pavement area of fifty thousand square yards is required for certain CRMAB applications. Traffic inputs must satisfy the average daily traffic limits shown in the applicable tables—generally more than twenty‑five thousand vehicles per day—and demonstrate cumulative equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) exceeding ten million over a twenty‑year design life, or at least 2,500 ESALs per day for bonded concrete overlays. Material specifications call for dense‑graded wearing or binder courses using a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 binder, optionally incorporating reclaimed asphalt pavement or reclaimed asphalt shingles when produced by the wet process; CRMAB mixtures are limited to a maximum layer thickness of two and one‑half inches, while thin overlays on Interstates may not be placed thinner than one and a half inches without special approval. For bonded concrete overlays, a minimum remaining asphalt thickness of three inches after milling is required, with concrete thickness ranging from 2½ to 4 inches for thin sections and four inches or more for conventional sections. Geotechnical data derived from Falling Weight Deflectometer testing provide the necessary pavement and subgrade characteristics for structural analysis, and annual Automated Pavement Condition Surveys supply detailed distress information—cracking, rutting, edge deterioration, raveling, joint spalling, and faulting—to verify existing conditions. Environmental considerations include addressing skid‑resistance problems such as those associated with Vanport Limestone concrete, which may trigger an earlier overlay. [1]

Overlay projects are governed by a hierarchical framework that begins with federal‑aid highway program guidance and FHWA traffic‑engineering manuals, proceeds to state specifications such as PennDOT Publication 408, and incorporates the class AA cement‑concrete provisions of Section 500 for rigid‑pavement overlays [1][2]. The design process must satisfy the surface‑preparation criteria set forth in both guides, requiring that the existing pavement be stable, level, sealed and patched before any new course is placed, with concrete patching performed according to Roadway Construction Standards (Publication 72M) and Sections 516, 519 and 704 of Publication 408, while scarifying, shot‑ or water‑blasting, crack sealing and stabilization of unstable slabs are mandated for all overlay types [1][2]. Minimum thicknesses for Superpave wearing‑course and binder‑course overlays are prescribed in Table 9.5, with bridge‑deck overlays exempt from the minimum‑thickness tables of Table 10.4 and limited to maintenance‑type work after verification of dead‑load capacity; thin Superpave overlays less than 1½ inches may be placed on Interstate facilities only with prior PDAU approval, whereas on non‑Interstate routes they are permitted when the existing surface is sound, patching does not exceed two percent and drainage conditions are satisfactory [1]; concrete overlay thicknesses range from four to eight inches for conventional rehabilitations and 2½ to four inches for thin overlays, with thin bonded concrete overlays limited to three‑to‑six inches and requiring macrofibers dosed between 0.2 and 0.5 percent by volume to achieve a residual flexural strength of 100–225 psi [2]. Design procedures follow Chapter 10 of the Pennsylvania Specification Manual, employing the AASHTO design method for asphalt thickness determination, while concrete overlay designs reference Sections 540, 541 and 548 for bonded, thin‑bonded and unbonded systems and must also conform to Sections 540.3 (as modified from 501.3) and 548.3 which govern surface preparation, joint layout, interlayer installation and curing requirements [1][2]. Joint layout is limited to a maximum overall panel dimension equal to eighteen times the overlay thickness with an aspect‑ratio not exceeding 1.5; longitudinal joints are prohibited in wheel paths, saw cuts are the sole method for creating joints, and cut depth must be at least one‑third of slab depth within twelve inches of edges and one‑quarter elsewhere, placed within ±¼ inch of existing transverse joints [2]. When an interlayer is specified, the manufacturer’s installation instructions must be submitted and a trained technical representative present unless a factory‑trained licensed installer is approved; geotextile interlayers must bear ASTM D4873 labeling, be overlapped eight ± 2 inches, limited to three layers, extend at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, remain clean and damp (but not saturated), and be kept below 120 °F during placement, while asphalt interlayers require a nominal one‑inch depth of Superpave mix and tack‑coat temperatures maintained between 290 °F and 320 °F [2]. Concrete placement is restricted to ambient temperatures between 65 °F and 90 °F, with curing commencing within thirty minutes after bleed water dissipates; if forecast temperatures drop below 45 °F or temperature changes exceed forty degrees per hour, insulating blankets must be used until compressive strength reaches at least 3,000 psi (verified by PTM No. 604 cylinder testing or AASHTO T 413 maturity) and subsequently 3,600 psi before traffic is permitted, and bond strength between new and existing concrete must be verified with ASTM C1583 tests not less than 160 psi each and an average of three tests of at least 175 psi [2]. Curing compound must be applied uniformly as a white‑pigmented liquid membrane at a minimum rate of one gallon per 125 ft² (or per 150 ft² when pavement thickness exceeds six inches) or according to manufacturer recommendations, and edge slump adjacent to shoulders may not exceed one‑quarter inch within six inches of the edge nor more than one‑eighth inch next to an adjoining lane [2]. Quality‑control procedures divide the overlay into lots not exceeding three thousand square yards per traffic lane; coring depth tolerances must be within one‑quarter inch, with random core locations selected per PTM No. 1 and drilled in the presence of the Inspector according to PTM No. 606, while projects covering intersections or areas under three thousand square yards require two cores or a nondestructive thickness measurement per PTM No. 605, and Falling‑Weight Deflectometer testing must be performed on existing PCC pavements prior to designing a concrete overlay [1][2]. Geotechnical constraints dictate that CBR values greater than ten require approval by the Chief Geotechnical Engineer and values above twenty are prohibited for use in the Heukelom‑Klomp correlation; when the subgrade is not reworked, the conversion factor uses an in‑situ density of 1 000 kg/m³ rather than 1 500 kg/m³, and back‑calculated resilient modulus from FWD testing must be adjusted by a factor of 0.25 in accordance with the 1993 AASHTO Guide [1]. Structural design life requirements specify a minimum of eight years and a maximum of twenty years, to be documented on Form D‑4332 with PDAU concurrence and FHWA approval when necessary [1]. Performance criteria for skid resistance require that the coarse aggregate in any asphalt wearing course achieve a Skid Resistance Level determined by AASHTO T 242, with higher‑than‑allowed SRL values prohibited for non‑wearing layers such as leveling courses or shoulders, and polymer‑modified CRMAB overlays are limited to projects exceeding 25 000 ADT, cumulative ESALs over ten million for a twenty‑year design life, and an overlay area greater than fifty thousand square yards; these mixes may not exceed a thickness of 2½ inches, must be produced by the wet process, and are restricted to permanent lanes [1]. Design methods and criteria

Overlay design begins with a quantitative assessment of the existing pavement’s structural capacity, which may be obtained through a condition survey, non‑destructive deflection testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements, or a remaining‑life estimate based on historic traffic [1]. For rigid pavements, FWD data are back‑calculated to resilient modulus and then adjusted by a correction factor of 0.25 to align field values with laboratory results [1]. When subgrade strength is expressed as CBR, the Heukelom‑Klomp relationship is applied using a multiplier of 1 000 rather than the standard 1 500; CBR values greater than ten require geotechnical approval and those above twenty are prohibited, and Field Dynamic Cone Penetration results may also be converted to resilient modulus via the same CBR conversion [1]. The effective structural capacity is denoted by D_eff for rigid sections or SN_eff for flexible layers, while the required future capacity is expressed as D_f or SN_f, and future traffic demand is quantified in 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads according to average initial truck factors listed in Table 7.1 [1]. Structural analysis employs structural coefficients drawn from Tables 9.3, 9.5, 10.4 and 10.5; for brick pavements the appropriate coefficient depends on whether the bricks rest on a rigid concrete base or on a flexible stone base, and each coefficient applies only to the depth of its respective material layer, with minimum and maximum placement depths and permissible overlay thicknesses also tabulated [1][2]. Concrete overlays may be bonded or unbonded; bonded concrete overlays over asphalt or composite bases incorporate the existing structure in the analysis and follow the BCOA‑ME mechanistic‑empirical procedure when total thickness does not exceed 6.5 in, requiring a milled asphalt base of at least three inches and limiting thin bonded sections to a nominal concrete thickness of 2.5–4 in, while overlays thicker than 6.5 in are treated as new concrete pavements and must obtain concurrence from the Bureau of Design and Delivery before field work begins [1][2]. Unbonded overlays act only as a stable platform and must be separated from the existing pavement by at least one inch of fine‑graded asphalt or a geotextile interlayer whose edges overlap eight inches (± 2) and extend four inches beyond the pavement edge, with no more than three layers permitted [1][2]. Asphalt overlay thickness is obtained using the 1993 AASHTO Design Method implemented in mechanistic‑empirical software such as DARWin, with the design recorded on Form D‑4332; Superpave mix designs employ a PG 64E‑22 binder for standard 6.3 mm wearing courses, and when crumb‑rubber modified binders are used the structural layer coefficient from Table 10.6 governs analysis with a maximum total thickness of 2½ in, the mixture must be produced by the wet process, coarse aggregate selection follows the skid‑resistance level guidelines in Table 5.4 based on anticipated ADT, and final SRL values are confirmed by friction testing using AASHTO T 242 [1][2]. When fiber‑reinforced concrete is specified, macro‑fibers contribute a residual strength f150 determined according to ASTM C1609; the effective flexural strength used in analysis is Feff = MOR + f150, with typical fiber contents ranging from 0.2 % to 0.5 % by volume yielding f150 values between 100 and 225 psi, and a Residual Strength Estimator spreadsheet assists engineers in predicting f150 before entering the resulting Feff into mechanistic‑empirical programs such as BCOA‑ME, ACPA Pavement Designer, or AASHTOWare Pavement ME [1][2]. Preservation‑oriented overlay options rely on cost‑effectiveness data from PennDOT’s Pavement Management System and include mill‑and‑overlay treatments that remove rutting or defective material with typical milling depths of 1½ inches (extendable to 2 inches when necessary), micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, and cold‑in‑place recycling of the base limited to a maximum depth of 4 inches combined with an asphalt overlay not exceeding 1½ inches; structural adequacy for any preservation treatment may be verified through FWD testing when required [1][2]. Construction procedures commence with a comprehensive quality‑control plan and a pre‑placement meeting that includes the material manufacturer’s technical representative to confirm material type, application rates, surface cleaning requirements and paving sequence; surface preparation is stringent: the existing pavement must be scarified or milled to the required depth, all loose debris, oil, grease, vegetation and other contaminants are removed by sweeping with a power broom or vacuum truck, cracks larger than three‑quarters inch are filled, and any unstable slabs that move or rock under traffic are stabilized through patching, slabjacking, flexible‑base replacement or shallow defect filling before an interlayer is placed [2][1]. Deck temperature must be below 120 °F before concrete placement, while fresh concrete is placed only when its temperature lies between 65 °F and 90 °F; if asphalt material is used in a composite overlay it is applied with calibrated distributor trucks at temperatures between 290 °F and 320 °F [2][1]. Joint layout follows dimensional limits: transverse expansion joints are identified and marked, joint backing must be installed within ±¼ in, panel dimensions cannot exceed fifteen feet in length or eighteen times the overlay thickness, and the aspect ratio of any panel shall not exceed 1.5; saw cuts are made with a green‑cut saw to a depth of at least one‑third the slab thickness within twelve inches of the pavement edges (and at least one‑quarter elsewhere), with a minimum width of one‑eighth inch, extending over existing joints to the full overlay thickness plus half an inch, and FHWA‑approved HIPERPAV or equivalent software is used to establish the project‑specific sawing window [2]. Curing commences within thirty minutes after bleed‑water dissipation and employs a white curing compound applied at a rate of one gallon (± 0.1) per 125 ft², or a poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene liquid membrane; insulating blankets are required when air temperature is ≤45 °F to limit surface temperature change to no more than forty degrees Fahrenheit per hour [2]. Structural integrity is verified by bond‑strength testing in accordance with ASTM C1583, requiring an average of at least 175 psi and no individual result below 160 psi, and by confirming that concrete compressive strength has reached the required threshold—either 3,600 psi as measured on cylinders or, alternatively, 3,000 psi determined through the maturity method specified in AASHTO T413—followed by core sampling for depth verification according to PTM procedures [2][1]. Overlay quantity is calculated by multiplying the indicated overlay depth by the placement area to obtain volume, with a supplemental allowance of up to two percent added to compensate for cement‑concrete needed to replace unsound material removed during profile milling; milling equipment must provide automatic grade and slope control, traffic may not travel on the freshly milled surface for more than six calendar days, and the first asphalt overlay course must be placed within seven calendar days of starting the milling operation, with ROUGH ROAD signs installed at half‑mile intervals if placement is delayed [2][1]. Design tools referenced across the guides include mechanistic‑empirical software such as DARWin and BCOA‑ME, the HIPERPAV sawing‑window program, PTM guidelines for coring and core analysis, a Residual Strength Estimator spreadsheet for fiber‑reinforced concrete, and tabular resources (Tables 9.3, 9.5, 10.4, 10.5, 10.6, 5.4, 7.1) that provide structural coefficients, thickness limits, SRL designations, and average initial truck factor values [1][2].

Structural section and dimensions

Overlay thickness varies with the material used; conventional asphalt overlays are applied at a thickness of either one‑and‑half inches or two inches [1]. When a cold‑in‑place recycled mix asphalt binder is employed, the overlay shall not exceed two‑and‑a‑half inches and may be placed on shoulders only when lane and shoulder are paved in a single pass [1]. For Portland cement concrete overlays the existing milled asphalt must be at least three inches thick before concrete placement, with thin bonded concrete overlays ranging from 2½ to 4 inches and conventional bonded concrete overlays typically four inches or greater [1]. Thin surface overlays that do not satisfy the 3R criterion are limited to one to 1½ inches, and if the 3R requirement is omitted the overlay may be two inches or thicker [1]. The contract documents provide no specific dimensional requirements for the existing pavement structure, so no explicit thicknesses are given for the underlying pavement, base, subbase, treated‑soil layer, slab, or shoulder [2]. Edge‑support dimensions are defined only when a geotextile interlayer is used; its edges must overlap by approximately eight inches with an allowable tolerance of plus or minus two inches, and the free edge must extend at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, while no more than three layers may overlap at any location [2]. If an asphalt interlayer is selected, a nominal one‑inch depth of Superpave mix is required for that layer [2]. Concrete overlays incorporating polypropylene‑fiber reinforcement are limited to a total overlay thickness of less than seven inches, and the fibers must be monofilament or bundled with lengths between one inch and two‑and‑half inches, an aspect ratio not exceeding 150, and placed at a dosage no greater than five pounds per cubic yard to achieve a residual strength ratio of about twenty percent [2].

The selection of overlay thicknesses begins with a structural analysis that first establishes the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic from the prescribed table and the Existing Effective Pavement Thickness from its corresponding table. The layer configuration for an unbonded PCC overlay consists of a concrete slab that is separated from the existing pavement by either a geotextile interlayer or an asphalt concrete separator. When shoulders are reconstructed, the interlayer must extend through the shoulder and be tied into the mainline, with drainage ensured by daylighting or a vertical subdrain.

Joint treatment follows the existing concrete joint pattern. Transverse joints are sawed and sealed over the overlay unless micro‑surfacing is applied. For unbonded PCC overlays any joint exhibiting more than a quarter‑inch of faulting is milled, and if faulting exceeds half an inch a wearing course is placed at the joint or the joint is milled/grinded to reduce the fault. This approach maintains proper joint spacing and continuity across the overlay.

Cross‑slope requirements are satisfied by adding leveling courses where necessary; these courses do not count toward the prescribed thickness limits, allowing the overlay to meet the minimum cross‑slope without compromising structural design. Transitions between the new overlay and existing pavement are managed through consistent joint spacing, appropriate joint treatment, and the continuous interlayer tie‑in at shoulders, ensuring a smooth structural and functional transition.

Materials and mixture design

Overlays are defined by the material system—either a rigid Portland‑cement concrete (PCC) overlay or a flexible asphalt‑concrete overlay—and the specification must explicitly name the chosen system. For PCC overlays the mix design shall conform to Publication 408 Section 704 with an optimized aggregate gradation and must satisfy the minimum compressive strength and time‑to‑strength criteria of Publication 408 Section 501, which in practice requires a design compressive strength of at least 3,600 psi and verification of no less than 3,000 psi before traffic is permitted. The concrete shall be Class AA Portland‑cement concrete placed to a design thickness generally between four and eight inches; when the required thickness is less than seven inches fibers may be incorporated, while thin‑bonded jointed plain concrete pavement (JPCP) overlays are limited to 2.5–4 inches and bonded JPCP sections to 4–8 inches. If fibers are used the specification must state the required residual flexural strength, which is typically in the range of 100 to 225 psi, and may prescribe synthetic or steel fibers meeting a dosage of roughly 0.2–0.5 percent by volume (or up to five pounds per cubic yard), a length of one to two‑and‑a‑half inches, and an aspect ratio between about 30 and 150. Air‑entraining admixture shall meet AASHTO M154, other chemical admixtures AASHTO M194, and any latex‑emulsion binder must satisfy FHWA prequalification; pigment content may not exceed ten percent of the cement weight and must be documented per ASTM C979. The concrete overlay must achieve a bond strength to the existing pavement averaging at least 175 psi on ASTM C1583 tests with no individual specimen below 160 psi, and it must meet air‑content limits of Section 704.1(c)3. Chloride‑ion penetration after curing for fifty‑six days shall be classified as low, very low, or negligible, with a maximum charge not exceeding 2,800 coulombs where measured. When an unbonded overlay is required a minimum one‑inch fine‑graded asphalt separation layer or an equivalent geotextile interlayer must be placed; the geotextile shall be laid flat on a clean surface, overlapped eight inches (± 2 in) at edges, limited to three layers, extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge and kept below 120 °F prior to concrete placement. If a geotextile is selected any existing concrete joints with faulting greater than one‑quarter inch shall be milled, and if faulting exceeds one‑half inch an additional Superpave wearing course of 9.5 mm fine‑graded mix must be placed at the joint. For flexible asphalt overlays the exact mixture type must be named; a Stone Matrix Asphalt (SMA) wearing course may be milled and placed at either 1.5 or 2.0 inches, while Superpave mixes are identified by nominal aggregate size—options include 25 mm, 19 mm, 12.5 mm, 9.5 mm, or 6.3 mm for thin‑overlay applications—and the appropriate binder grade must be indicated. PG 64E‑22 binder is required for standard 6.3 mm wearing courses and prohibited for 9.5 mm fine‑graded mixes; when a crumb‑rubber modified asphalt binder is used it shall be a polymer‑modified PG 64E‑22 produced by the wet process, RAP or RAS may be incorporated in accordance with the SSP, and a chemical additive must be employed for warm‑mix technology (foaming is not permitted). The coarse‑aggregate Skid Resistance Level (SRL) appropriate to traffic volume shall be selected from prescribed tables and recorded on design documents because it governs surface friction. Asphalt overlay thicknesses must conform to ADT‑based tables, typically 1.5 inches for a 9.5 mm mix, 2 inches for a 12.5 mm mix, up to 2½ inches for dense‑graded wearing courses; the maximum allowable layer thickness for any asphalt overlay is 2½ inches, while cold‑in‑place recycling overlays are limited to 1½ inches and micro‑surfacing treatments may not exceed roughly 0.25 inch on structurally sound pavement verified by Falling Weight Deflectometer testing. A tack coat shall be applied within a temperature window of 290 °F to 320 °F, with application rates ranging from approximately 0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard for geotextile fabric, 0.02 to 0.05 gallons per square yard for fiberglass mesh grid, and 0.19 to 0.28 gallons per square yard for composite reinforcing grid, sufficient to fully saturate the material and extend six inches beyond its edges. Curing of concrete overlays shall be achieved with a white poly‑alpha‑methylstyrene (PAMS) liquid‑membrane forming curing compound applied uniformly at not less than one gallon per 125 ft² for sections six inches thick or less, and at not less than one gallon per 150 ft² for thicker sections; the cured surface must exhibit a finished edge slump of no more than one‑quarter inch within six inches of the shoulder and no more than one‑eighth inch adjacent to an adjoining lane. Prior to placement any longitudinal cracks wider than three‑quarters of an inch shall be sealed with a department‑approved non‑shrink grout, and surface defects larger than four square inches or deeper than one inch shall be repaired with an approved patching material—either asphalt‑based, cementitious, or another department‑approved mix. Structural details and interfaces

Before any overlay is placed, the existing pavement surface must be thoroughly cleaned and all longitudinal and transverse concrete joints as well as asphalt joints and cracks are sealed to provide a continuous load path. Existing transverse expansion joints are located, marked, and filled with an approved backing material to within ±¼ inch of the pavement face, while any steel plates are removed and exposed reinforcement cleared during scarification. The joint layout follows the department‑approved plan; when no plan is provided the overlay panels are limited to a maximum length of fifteen feet or to eighteen times the overlay thickness, with the longer‑to‑shorter side ratio not exceeding 1.5 and the aspect ratio kept as close to unity as practicable. After placement of the new concrete layer, each original transverse joint is saw‑cut through the full thickness of the overlay plus an additional half inch, using a green‑cut saw to a depth of at least one‑third the slab thickness near edges and one‑fourth elsewhere, with a minimum width of ⅛ inch; longitudinal joints are created by cutting a sealant reservoir one‑quarter inch wide to the full overlay depth plus half an inch. Where faulting in existing concrete joints exceeds ¼ inch the joint is milled, and if faulting exceeds ½ inch a scratch course or additional milling/grinding is applied before overlay placement to reduce the irregularity. For unbonded concrete overlays the new joints are placed as they would be in a new slab based on slab thickness and need not align with existing joints, whereas bonded concrete overlays rely on the bond between old and new slabs and therefore do not require special joint continuity. Reinforcement is required when the overlay thickness is less than seven inches; in that case a Class AA cement‑concrete mix containing synthetic polypropylene fibers 1–2½ in long with an aspect ratio up to 150 is used, dosed to achieve a residual strength ratio of about 20 % and not exceeding five pounds per cubic yard. When an asphalt interlayer system is specified, a reinforcement layer such as needle‑punched geotextile fabric, fiberglass mesh grid, or composite reinforcing grid is placed on the clean, dry surface before the tack coat loses its stickiness; all reinforcement joints overlap by at least one inch in the direction of placement, edges are overlapped 8 ± 2 inches and extend a minimum of four inches beyond the pavement edge, and no more than three layers are stacked. The first lift over the interlayer is compacted to a minimum thickness of 1½ inches to secure the reinforcement, and the interlayer must be provided with proper drainage either by daylighting it or installing a vertical subdrain. Load‑transfer devices are addressed by dowel‑bar retrofits or other concrete pavement restoration techniques; all patch joints that have lost load‑transfer capacity or are crushed are replaced with dowelled concrete patches bonded along their full embedded length with epoxy, polyester, or vinylester binders, and the existing dowels are preserved during saw cutting. Macro‑fibers placed at contraction joints help maintain load‑transfer efficiency under repeated loading but do not replace dowel bars where fault control is required, and fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays depend on properly timed and depth‑controlled saw‑cut contraction joints to activate as intended. Transitions are constructed at the limits of the overlay as shown on the plans; when a transition rests directly on existing treated base or concrete, a geotextile debonding layer is placed beneath the new concrete and a neat vertical edge face is provided along pavement edges. At the pavement edge a safety‑edge taper of at least 1½ inches is provided to give a smooth transition for both wearing and binder courses, and uniform support throughout the overlay is required to ensure structural integrity. The design of an overlay begins with a systematic assessment of the existing pavement condition to decide whether the new layer will be bonded to or separated from the underlying material, with sound surfaces favoring a bonded system and deteriorated pavements often requiring an unbonded approach [1][2]. All loose debris, oil, grease, rust, vegetation, and any vertical edges are removed and the surface is dried before any further treatment [1][2]. Cracks wider than three‑quarters of an inch are filled with department‑approved non‑shrink grout or equivalent material to ensure a sound bond, while defects larger than four square inches in area and one inch deep are patched with approved asphalt, cementitious, or similar repair material [2]. Joint backing or other approved joint filling material is installed in every transverse joint so that its top lies within a quarter‑inch of the prepared surface and cannot be displaced by paving [2]; longitudinal joints receive a quarter‑inch‑wide sealant reservoir cut to the same depth as the overlay [2]. The existing concrete pavement’s longitudinal and transverse joints are cleaned and sealed, and where necessary an asphalt tack coat is applied prior to placement of binder and wearing courses in bonded designs [1][2]. Full‑depth saw cuts are made through each transverse joint to a depth equal to the total overlay thickness plus one‑half inch, with a cut width at least as wide as the underlying joint or crack; similar quarter‑inch‑wide cuts are provided for longitudinal joints, and joint spacing follows the project plan while avoiding wheel paths [2][1]. When joint faulting exceeds a quarter of an inch it is milled, and if faulting exceeds half an inch a one‑inch‑thick Superpave wearing course (9.5 mm mix) or additional milling/grinding is employed to reduce the fault before overlay placement [1][2]. For unbonded concrete overlays an isolation layer of fine‑graded asphalt at least one inch thick or an equivalent non‑woven geotextile interlayer is placed between the existing concrete and the new concrete, eliminating the need to align transverse joints in the overlay with those in the existing pavement [1][2]. The geotextile sheet is laid flat on the repaired surface, overlapped by eight inches plus or minus two inches, extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, and limited to three layers in any location; alternatively a one‑inch‑thick Superpave asphalt interlayer may be used after applying a tack coat of 0.22 to 0.28 gallons per square yard that fully saturates the fabric and extends six inches beyond its edges [2][1]. Where profile errors exist, a separate leveling or scratch course of at least one inch thickness is placed ahead of the binder and wearing courses to provide a uniform working platform; this course remains distinct from the structural layers to preserve quality control [1][2]. In bonded asphalt overlays a continuous bond is created by tack coating before placing the binder and wearing courses, typically using a 1½‑ to 2‑inch SMA wearing course over the prepared surface, with minimum binder thicknesses of 2½ inches for overlays on cement‑concrete or brick pavements [1]. Minimum wearing‑course thicknesses are prescribed for each material; for example, a 9.5 mm Superpave mix is placed at one inch nominal thickness when used as an isolation layer, and the overall overlay thickness must meet the structural design requirements, with fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays ranging from three to six inches and thin Portland‑cement overlays from 2½ to 4 inches [1][2]. Transition zones are constructed where the overlay terminates at a structure or pavement limit; in these zones the same geotextile debonding layer or asphalt interlayer is extended beyond the edge, and joints are overlapped by a minimum of one inch with any wrinkles larger than an inch sliced and re‑overlapped to maintain continuity [2][1]. Edge treatment limits edge slump within the outer six inches to one‑quarter inch next to a shoulder and one‑eighth inch next to an adjacent lane, and overlay panels are sized so that their longer‑to‑shorter dimension ratio does not exceed 1.5 and no panel dimension exceeds eighteen times the overlay thickness [2][1]. Continuous base drains or heavy‑duty membranes are provided where moisture control is required, and the isolation layer must be daylighted or equipped with a vertical subdrain to ensure proper drainage through reconstructed shoulders and into the mainline [1][2]. Adjacent features such as guide rails, drainage structures, and curbs are adjusted or replaced to maintain alignment and proper water management; at curb intersections the overlay may be tapered to a one‑inch depth within parking lanes after confirming adequate compaction and sealing of the pavement‑curb joint while travel lanes retain full design thickness [1]. The first overlay course is placed within seven calendar days after milling to preserve interface integrity, and any deviation from these interface requirements is subject to review by the district pavement management engineer [2][1].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

In designing an overlay the pavement system must first guarantee that water is efficiently removed from both the surface and the underlying structure, requiring the existing pavement to be clean, dry, and free of standing water before any new material is placed [1][2]. Any standing water left after milling is removed immediately and the surface is shaped without depressions so that water can shed naturally, while a continuous base drain and adjusted guide‑rail or other drainage components convey water away from the structure [1][2]. All longitudinal and transverse joints are cleaned and sealed, and heavy‑duty membranes are installed where necessary to block moisture intrusion into the underlying layers [1]. The design verifies subsurface drainage conditions and, wherever deficiencies are identified, incorporates adequate subdrainage—often by installing several subgrade drains—to resolve the problem [1]. Both surface and subsurface drainage are required to be good or upgraded to good, with interlayer systems such as geotextile fabrics or composite reinforcing grids acting as moisture barriers that prevent surface water from penetrating the pavement structure [1][2]. When a geotextile or asphalt interlayer is used, it is placed on a drainable layer, terminated at a daylighted edge such as a ditch foreslope, and kept wrapped during storage to protect it from precipitation, UV light, chemicals, and temperature extremes; if rain wets the interlayer before overlay placement, it must be allowed to dry [2]. Prior to placing concrete or asphalt, the surface temperature is limited to less than 120 °F, with fog‑misting, protective covers, white‑pigmented curing compounds, lime‑water washing, or other approved methods employed to achieve this limit while also preventing moisture accumulation [2]. When ambient air temperatures are forecast at 45 °F or lower, insulating blankets are applied to prevent rapid heat loss and remain in place until the temperature rises above 45 °F; similarly, concrete mix temperature is controlled between 65 °F and 90 °F for placement [2]. Freeze‑thaw cycles are addressed by selecting an appropriate overlay thickness, often a thicker overlay combined with an interlayer system, which can delay cracking in harsh environments, and when the existing subgrade is frost‑susceptible the design either replaces the problematic soil or provides a stronger base course together with pavement‑base drains and a positive slope to keep water away [1]. For projects that use varying overlay thicknesses, drainage beneath each new section is coordinated so that water does not accumulate at transitions, and proper drainage of the separation layer is ensured by daylighting the interlayer or installing a vertical subdrain that extends through any reconstructed shoulders and ties into the mainline [1]. Finally, the overlay design mandates a positive cross‑slope; the finished profile must conform to the specified slope within one‑eighth inch in twelve feet, providing continuous drainage of water from the surface, and curing materials are applied within thirty minutes of bleed‑water dissipation and retained until the pavement reaches its specified strength to maintain proper moisture conditions during early cure [2].

An overlay must be designed to preserve the existing roadway geometry while meeting a full suite of safety, accessibility, geometric, and user‑performance criteria. The design must retain or improve drainage by providing continuous pavement base drains and adjusting or replacing guide‑rail and other drainage structures that could impede water removal, thereby protecting subgrade material from exposure to construction traffic. A level and stable platform is achieved by applying a minimum one‑inch leveling course or by milling irregularities before the wearing or binder courses are placed, which also establishes the required cross‑slope. For flexible overlays the structural thickness shall be 1.5 inches to 2.0 inches and must be sealed with a tack coat; shoulders receive the same treatment, while preservation projects limit asphalt overlay thickness to two inches unless classified as a structural overlay. Concrete overlays require at least three inches of existing asphalt remaining after milling before bonding, with thin bonded concrete overlays limited to 2.5–4 inches and full‑depth bonded overlays four inches or greater; joint geometry follows 30‑foot spacing for reinforced concrete and 15‑foot spacing for plain concrete, with perpendicular joints, coated dowel bars, and proper seals. Panel dimensions are constrained so that no panel exceeds eighteen times the overlay thickness and the aspect ratio of longer to shorter side shall not exceed 1.5, with an aspect ratio as close to unity as practicable; longitudinal joints must be avoided in wheel paths. Saw cuts defining joints must penetrate at least one‑third of slab depth within twelve inches of the pavement edge and at least one‑quarter elsewhere, after which joint faces are cleaned and sealed. Joint faulting greater than one‑quarter inch is milled, while faulting exceeding one‑half inch requires a scratch course or grinding to bring the surface within tolerance; if faulting exceeds one‑half inch in concrete overlays, a Superpave wearing course may be placed at the joint. The finished surface profile must be smooth to within one‑eighth inch over twelve feet and the cross‑slope must match the design within the same tolerance, providing a positive slope for drainage. Safety edges are required on the outside edge of all asphalt pavements and shoulders, consisting of a 30° tapered transition with a minimum thickness of 1½ inches and a maximum overall depth of five inches, adding roughly one percent extra material to improve vehicle stability. Non‑tracking tack coat is recommended for locations with stopping, turning, or low‑speed traffic to further enhance safety, and guide‑rail heights may not be reduced below minimum acceptable criteria; any necessary relocations of guide rails, shoulder‑width adjustments, slope changes, or vertical clearances at structures must be incorporated so that the pavement grade line remains appropriate. Skid resistance and surface texture are addressed by selecting coarse aggregate to achieve the specified Skid Resistance Level (SRL) for anticipated traffic volumes, documenting the SRL designation on design approvals and contract documents, and may be supplemented with micro‑surfacing, ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses, or ultra‑thin concrete overlays where needed. Shoulders are constructed as Type 6 or Type 7 paved shoulders—or full‑depth asphalt shoulders where specified—built up and compacted to the elevation of each layer using a template cut to the required cross‑section, with interlayer drainage ensured by daylighting or vertical subdrains and extending the interlayer through the shoulder. Accessibility is protected by preserving existing vertical clearances at structures and maintaining the established roadway geometry so that all users, including those with mobility constraints, can continue to use the facility without impediment; traffic on freshly milled surfaces is prohibited for more than six calendar days, and only necessary local vehicles and essential construction equipment may travel until the surface course is placed. User‑delay considerations require that accelerated concrete patching allow reopening to traffic within four to seven hours, with liquidated‑damage provisions applied for lane‑closure delays under high‑speed, high‑ADT conditions; rough‑road signs and reduced speed limits are installed when overlay placement is delayed beyond the same day. Verification and expected performance

Verification of a completed overlay begins with confirming that the existing pavement was stable, showing no movement under the roller, and had been properly leveled, sealed and patched before any new surface was placed [1]. All design items such as drainage provisions, base repairs, leveling, binder thickness, friction treatment and shoulder improvements must be addressed, and the project plans are required to delineate the exact paving limits, particularly when a Restricted Performance Specification is used, so that the finished work can be measured against those documented boundaries [1]. After placement, compliance and constructability are established by producing cross‑sections at roughly fifty‑foot intervals to ensure that the quantities of material installed correspond to the design, while core samples are taken in areas where slab depth varies to verify actual thickness [1]. Density quality is then assessed using the Percent Within Limits methodology or core sampling as prescribed in the bid documents, confirming that field density falls within the required range [1]. The interlayer must be shown to drain properly—either daylighted or equipped with a vertical subdrain—and it must run continuously through any reconstructed shoulder and be tied into the mainline [1]. Joint preparation is inspected for faulting; joints exhibiting more than a quarter‑inch of faulting are milled, and if faulting exceeds half an inch a Superpave wearing‑course (scratch) mix is placed or the joint is ground to reduce the fault [1]. The completed overlay is further verified through a comprehensive quality‑control program that begins with the submission and approval of a detailed QC plan covering surface preparation, mixing, placement, curing and joint work [2]. After the existing pavement has been prepared, a seven‑day bond‑strength test performed in the presence of the inspector according to ASTM C1583 is required; the average of three readings must be at least 175 psi and no individual reading may be below 160 psi, otherwise the area is deemed defective [2]. Concrete compressive strength is monitored with cylinders (or, where permitted, maturity testing) and traffic is allowed only when the measured strength reaches the minimum required value specified in the contract—either 3,600 psi or, where the specification allows, 3,000 psi [2]. Visual inspection confirms that joint backing is placed within ±¼ inch of the finished surface, edge slump does not exceed ¼ inch next to shoulders or ⅛ inch next to adjacent lanes, and that no defects such as improper depth, excessive air content, honey‑combing, rain damage or other surface deficiencies are present; any such defect must be removed and replaced in accordance with the defective‑work provisions [2]. Joint geometry is checked against the plans: transverse joints are sawed with a green cut saw to a minimum depth of D/3 and a width of at least 1⁄8 inch, within twelve inches of an edge they must meet the D/3 depth requirement while elsewhere a minimum of D/4 is acceptable; all joints are cleaned of deleterious material and sealed, and any panel that exhibits cracking beyond the saw cuts is considered defective [2]. Panel dimensions are limited to an aspect ratio no greater than 1.5 and a maximum overall size not exceeding eighteen times the overlay thickness [2]. Curing is inspected to ensure that curing compound is applied within thirty minutes after bleed water has dissipated and that insulating blankets are provided when air temperature falls to 40 °F (or ≤45 °F) and removed without causing a surface temperature change greater than forty degrees per hour [2]. Thickness and uniformity are verified by dividing the pavement into lots of no more than three thousand square yards per traffic lane, selecting random coring locations in each lot, and drilling cores in the presence of the inspector according to PTM procedures; for intersections or areas under three thousand square yards two cores—or non‑destructive thickness measurements—are required [2]. After coring, the hole walls are cleaned of laitance and the voids are filled with the same mix or a non‑shrink grout to confirm that the measured thickness is within one‑quarter inch of the specified depth, and additional depth checks are performed by digging test holes for each three thousand square yards (or as directed) and confirming that the compacted depth is within half an inch of the design value; any deficient area must be removed and rebuilt [2]. The overall volume of concrete is calculated from the indicated overlay depth and placement area to ensure that work remains within the defined limits of the overlay and transition items [2]. For asphalt overlays, the inspector verifies that the material temperature meets the minimum required by the manufacturer’s technical representative, that the finished thickness conforms to the contract drawings, and that the first lift over an interlayer system achieves a compacted depth of at least one‑and‑a‑half inches; all joints and overlaps are treated with a minimum one‑inch overlap of asphalt material, wrinkles larger than one inch are trimmed and overlapped flat, excess sand is removed before paving begins, and the entire area covered by the interlayer system is placed in a single day to satisfy constructability and performance requirements [2]. Surface smoothness is checked with templates or straightedges at intervals not exceeding twenty‑five feet, and any irregularities greater than one‑half inch are corrected by loosening, adding or removing material [2]. Finally, ride quality is evaluated under the incentive provisions and any deficiencies are addressed in accordance with the deficiency‑adjustment clause, completing the verification of compliance, constructability, performance and consistency with project requirements [2].

Overlay designs are targeted to achieve a structural service life that typically ranges from eight to twenty years while simultaneously correcting surface deficiencies that affect ride quality and friction. The primary performance outcomes identified across the guidance include provision of additional structural capacity, resistance to cracking, loss of thickness, joint or transverse distress, reduction of existing rut depth, improvement of skid‑resistance, and delivery of a smoother riding experience. To satisfy the service‑life objective, the overlays are designed using current traffic inputs—average daily traffic, growth rates, and truck percentages—to compute equivalent single axle loads (ESALs), and material thicknesses are selected from FHWA‑prescribed tables and recorded on standard design forms. When a fiber‑reinforced concrete overlay is specified, the macrofibers contribute a residual tensile strength measured as f150 in accordance with ASTM C1609; this value is added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength (feff) that feeds mechanistic‑empirical design tools for thickness sizing. Laboratory slab testing documented by the guides shows that such fiber reinforcement can raise load‑transfer efficiency by roughly thirty percent, especially when pre‑existing crack widths exceed one millimeter, thereby limiting further crack deterioration and extending serviceability. Structural adequacy of the installed overlay is verified in the field with Falling Weight Deflectometer testing to confirm that deflection responses meet prescribed thresholds. Independent of fiber content, the concrete must attain a minimum compressive strength of three‑thousand six hundred pounds per square inch before traffic opening, as demonstrated by cylinder tests performed under PTM 604; an alternative lower limit of three thousand psi is permissible when the AASHTO T‑413 maturity method is employed. Bond performance between the new overlay and the existing pavement is required to achieve a seven‑day average tensile strength of at least one hundred seventy‑five psi, with no individual specimen falling below one hundred sixty psi, as measured by ASTM C1583. Dimensional control during curing is monitored by limiting surface temperature change to no more than forty degrees Fahrenheit per hour and restricting edge slump to a maximum of one‑quarter inch adjacent to shoulders and one‑eighth inch next to adjoining lanes; these parameters are recorded with temperature sensors and visual inspections. Core sampling of randomly selected locations is performed in accordance with PTM 614, and any deviation from the design thickness greater than a quarter‑inch is considered a defect. Proper joint layout and sealing using approved hot‑pour or silicone sealants are documented to protect against transverse and longitudinal distress. Curing of the overlay is managed with insulating blankets or curing compounds applied within thirty minutes after bleed‑water dissipation and retained until the required strength is achieved, as stipulated in the quality‑control plan. Surface finishing operations such as milling to remove rutting followed by micro‑surfacing or ultra‑thin bonded wearing courses are expected to eliminate existing ruts, raise skid resistance, and further improve ride quality; verification includes confirming that the milling depth has removed the rut and that the surfacing meets targeted skid‑resistance criteria. Overall ride‑quality performance is quantified through a concrete‑pavement ride‑quality evaluation prescribed in the guidance, which forms the basis for incentive payments. [1][2]
