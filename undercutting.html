Undercutting

Part of: Subgrade is-subtopic-of
Undercutting is a topic under Subgrade.
undercutting
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-29

Undercutting is a remedial excavation technique used when the existing subgrade or base layer lacks sufficient bearing capacity to support new pavement, typically identified by laboratory CBR values below five or field‑measured permanent deflections exceeding one‑half inch in normal subgrades (or one inch in embankments)[1][2]. The process removes the deficient material to a depth dictated by the magnitude of deformation—one foot for deflections up to three inches and up to three feet for larger movements—and classifies the cut as Class 1A or 1C based on whether its bottom width is less than or greater than eight feet[2]. After drainage, the void is backfilled with AASHTO No. 1 aggregate placed over a Class 4 Type C geotextile, followed by a minimum eight‑inch fill layer, a second geotextile, and a six‑inch subbase, all compacted to prescribed density limits and confined within side slopes no steeper than 8H:1V[2]. A qualified Representative must inspect and approve each stage, ensuring utility protection and compliance before the undercut is accepted as a stable foundation for subsequent pavement layers[2].

Purpose and applicability

Undercutting is applied to remedy situations where the existing subgrade cannot provide adequate structural support for new or repaired pavement layers [1][2]. Across sources, the method is called for when laboratory California Bearing Ratio (CBR) values are below five, indicating that the bearing capacity of the subgrade is insufficient to meet design resilient‑modulus requirements [1]. The guides also identify unstable or unsuitable material revealed by special rolling as a problem: permanent deflection greater than one‑half inch in existing subgrades—or more than an inch in embankments—signifies that the subgrade will not satisfy performance criteria and would otherwise lead to premature pavement failure [2]. When such deficient zones are detected, whether they extend less than eight feet (Class 1A) or eight feet and beyond (Class 1C), the weak material must be removed because its presence would undermine the stability of any subsequent pavement construction [2].

Undercutting is indicated when the existing subgrade or base layer is judged to be unstable and must be removed in order to provide a sound foundation for new pavement. If reconstruction‑type testing shows an in‑situ California Bearing Ratio below five, the guides require removal of the weak material through undercutting, often with extra depth or combined stabilization, and the associated quantities are to be included in the project design. Similarly, field evaluation after special rolling that records a permanent deflection greater than one‑half inch for normal subgrades (or greater than one inch for embankments) triggers undercutting because the Representative judges the subgrade stability unsatisfactory. When the measured permanent deformation lies between 0.5 in and 3 in, a one‑foot deep excavation is prescribed; if the deformation exceeds three inches, the excavation may be extended up to three feet, respecting the maximum depth limit set by the guides. The horizontal width of the zone also determines the class of undercutting: a bottom width less than eight feet calls for a Class 1A excavation, whereas a width of eight feet or more requires a Class 1C excavation, each to be drained, back‑filled with AASHTO No. 1 aggregate and protected with geotextile, and limited to slopes no steeper than 8H:1V. When the CBR value is equal to or exceeds five, the guides recommend alternative remedial actions such as subgrade strengthening, lime or cement stabilization, rock capping, geogrid reinforcement, or geotextile separation rather than undercutting. If permanent deflection remains at or below the half‑inch (or one‑inch for embankments) limit and the Representative judges the subgrade stable, additional compaction is preferred to undercutting. Where unsuitable material resides only above the subgrade—such as topsoil, existing pavement that will be removed, benches, or other layers that can be excavated without reaching the structural layer—or where the excavation width does not satisfy the eight‑foot minimum, a conventional Class 1 excavation is performed instead of an undercut. In cases where extra‑depth undercutting or combined stabilization is required, the quantity and cost must be incorporated into the project estimate. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 143–170)

Required inputs

Undercutting begins when special‑rolling indicates a permanent deflection greater than 0.5 in; a one‑foot shallow cut is used for deflections up to three inches and a deeper excavation not exceeding three feet is applied for larger movements. The excavation removes all unsuitable material beneath the existing subgrade, and the resulting void is drained before backfilling. For sections whose undercut bottom width is less than eight feet the work follows Class 1A procedures, while widths of eight feet or more are treated as Class 1C. The base fill consists of AASHTO No. 1 aggregate or other approved borrow material placed over a Class 4 Type C geotextile that lines the bottom of the cut. At least eight inches of this fill must be laid before compaction, and a six‑inch subbase layer is then placed on a Class 4 Type A geotextile. All backfill is compacted to the density limits prescribed for the location—97 % of maximum dry density for embankments deeper than three feet (or existing embankments greater than three feet below the proposed subgrade) and 100 % for subgrades within three feet—while maintaining moisture content between –3 % and the optimum value. Transition slopes at the beginning and end of the undercut are limited to an 8H:1V ratio, drainage may be provided with a No. 57 aggregate trench if required, and the work is performed under representative supervision with protection of existing utilities. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 143–170)

Undercutting is required when either laboratory California Bearing Ratio (CBR) values of the existing subgrade or subbase fall below five, or field observations indicate permanent deflections exceeding specified limits. The pre‑construction testing program prescribed by the guides includes gradation analysis, determination of in‑place moisture content, and measurement of in‑place density, followed by a laboratory CBR test on the material to be undercut. In parallel, special‑rolling surveys are performed; if permanent deflection exceeds one‑half inch in subgrade or three inches in embankments, the Representative may direct excavation. For areas identified by the smaller deflection criterion the cut is limited to one foot, while for larger deflections the maximum permissible depth is three feet. The transition at the beginning and end of each undercut must be constructed with a slope no steeper than eight horizontal to one vertical. Excavations whose bottom width is less than eight feet are classified as Class 1A, whereas those eight feet or greater fall into Class 1C. All undercuts must be drained and backfilled with AASHTO No. 1 aggregate, and geotextile placement follows the specified sequence. First a Class 4 Type C geotextile is placed at the bottom of the cut; then at least eight inches of approved fill material are installed before compaction. After this layer a Class 4 Type A geotextile and a minimum six‑inch subbase are added prior to final compaction. Moisture‑density conditions are verified after each compaction pass using AASHTO T191 or T310, and the Representative must approve stability before work proceeds. The design package must be coordinated with the District Geotechnical Engineer, detailing the extent of extra‑depth undercut or stabilization and incorporating those quantities and costs into plans and specifications; no additional dimensional tolerances are imposed beyond meeting the CBR trigger criterion. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 143–170)

Equipment and personnel

Undercutting may only proceed after a qualified Representative has inspected the subgrade and either approved or disapproved the corrective action, and the Representative must be present during the work. The crew therefore consists of that Representative, the equipment operators who carry out the excavation and back‑filling in accordance with the Section 208.3 specifications, and personnel tasked with protecting existing underground utilities throughout the undercut operation. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 168–170)

Work sequence

Before any undercutting can be initiated the project must first be fully designed in accordance with Chapter 8, which establishes the prerequisite for subgrade removal and replacement. The design is followed by a comprehensive evaluation of the existing subgrade or subbase material, requiring gradation testing to classify soil particles, measurement of in‑place moisture content and density, and performance of a California Bearing Ratio (CBR) test. The CBR results are compared with the design threshold, and if the measured CBR is less than five the undercutting or stabilization may proceed only after the evaluation has been documented and approved. In parallel, the contractor must clear the pavement zone of all overlying topsoil and any topsoil‑mix material, removing these soils in accordance with Section 801 and placing them in a designated stockpile before excavation can commence. Once the topsoil has been removed, the exposed subgrade is required to be compacted to the standards set out in Section 206.3(b). After compaction a special rolling pass is performed under the observation of a Representative, who must evaluate the subgrade and declare its stability unsatisfactory before any undercutting or excavation is authorized. Only after the Representative’s determination may the unsuitable material beneath the subgrade—typically where the bottom width of the subgrade is less than eight feet as directed—be removed by undercutting. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 143–170)

The undercutting process begins with an evaluation of subgrade stability during special rolling; when the Representative observes permanent deflection that exceeds allowable limits, the corrective sequence is initiated. If the measured deflection lies between greater than 0.5 inch and up to 3 inches, a one‑foot deep excavation is made, whereas deflections exceeding 3 inches require an excavation up to three feet deep. The width of the undercut may be less than or greater than eight feet as directed, and each excavated zone must be sloped no steeper than an 8H:1V ratio. Once the unsuitable material is removed, the void is drained to prevent water accumulation; a drainage path can be provided by backfilling with No. 57 aggregate if required. At the base of the undercut a Class 4 Type C geotextile is placed in accordance with Section 212.3(e). The space is then backfilled with approved material—typically AASHTO No. 1 aggregate or other selected borrow—ensuring at least eight inches of fill rest on the geotextile before compaction. Compaction follows the specifications of Sections 206.3 and 210.3. After this base layer, a Class 4 Type A geotextile is laid over the compacted fill, followed by placement and compaction of a minimum six‑inch subbase. Throughout the operation existing underground utilities are protected. The completed undercut provides a stable foundation for subsequent pavement layers. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 143–170)

Staging and logistics

Undercutting work is primarily limited by the horizontal space that can be cleared beneath the pavement. When the bottom width of material to be removed measures less than eight feet, the cut is classified as a Class 1A undercut; widths of eight feet or greater fall into the Class 1C category. This classification dictates that the excavation must first be drained and then backfilled with AASHTO No. 1 aggregate, after which a geotextile layer is placed in accordance with Section 208.3(d). Consequently, sufficient clearance for equipment to perform drainage, placement and compaction within these width limits is essential. In addition to the width requirement, spatial clearances at the ends of the undercut are controlled by slope and proximity constraints. The transition from the existing pavement into the excavation may not be steeper than an 8 H:1 V slope, and all work must remain at least ten feet away from bridge abutments or approach slabs to prevent damage. Existing underground utilities must be identified before work begins and protected throughout the operation. The specification does not impose any explicit weather restrictions nor broader environmental limits on undercutting activities; therefore, normal site‑access considerations apply provided the above spatial and utility protections are observed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 143–170)

Quality control and acceptance

When evaluating an existing subgrade or subbase for possible undercutting, the engineer must inspect gradation, in‑place moisture content, in‑place density, and California Bearing Ratio (CBR) to establish soil classification, strength and stiffness needed for design decisions. A CBR value below five triggers removal of weak material or stabilization actions. Stability is further verified by measuring permanent deflection or rutting after special rolling; the guides set a limit of one‑half inch for existing subgrades and one inch for embankment foundations. Moisture‑density testing must confirm that the material can be compacted to the required dry density before any undercut is executed, and when test data are unavailable a test strip approach with the proposed equipment may be employed. The transition slopes at the beginning and end of an undercut must not exceed an 8H:1V ratio. Acceptance criteria also include inspection of placed geotextile layers, drainage paths, and verification that a minimum thickness of approved backfill aggregate is provided. [1][2]

During special rolling, the subgrade’s condition is monitored continuously; after each pass of the compaction equipment the crew checks in‑place moisture and density at several random locations using AASHTO T 191 or T 310, and permanent deflection or rutting is measured to see if it exceeds the defined limits (½ inch for existing subgrade, 1 inch for embankments). If those measurements indicate unsatisfactory stability, the Representative orders stabilization, which may include undercutting. Thus measurement occurs after every compaction pass until the stability criteria are met. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 168–170)

The work is accepted only when the Representative approves the subgrade stability; for existing subgrades the permanent deflection must not exceed a half‑inch and for embankment foundations it must stay within one inch, with moisture content kept between minus three percent of optimum and optimum and compaction achieved to at least 97 % of maximum dry density (or 100 % when within three feet of the proposed subgrade). If these limits are exceeded, the corrective action is either additional compaction or an undercut/excavation followed by replacement: a one‑foot deep undercut for deflections between a half‑inch and three inches, up to three feet deep for larger deflections, each with slopes no steeper than 8H:1V, geotextile placement, minimum backfill thicknesses, and proper compaction of the new material. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 168–170)

Curing, protection, and handoff

After the unstable material is removed, a Class 4 Type C geotextile is laid on the bottom of the undercut and, when required, a drainage path is backfilled with No. 57 aggregate; selected borrow material is then placed and compacted to meet the density requirements, ensuring at least eight inches of approved fill rest on the geotextile before compaction. A second layer of Class 4 Type A geotextile is installed over the backfill, followed by a minimum six‑inch subbase that is also compacted, thereby providing a stable, protected base for the new pavement. Throughout the operation existing underground utilities are safeguarded from damage. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 168–170)

Expected performance and maintenance

When special rolling shows that a subgrade or embankment is not stable, the typical distress that triggers undercutting is excessive permanent deflection or rutting—specifically, a permanent deformation greater than one‑half inch in existing subgrades or cuts, and greater than one inch in embankment foundations. Once these thresholds are exceeded, the Representative declares the stability unsatisfactory and directs stabilization by either additional compaction or by excavating (undercutting) the failed material and replacing it with approved fill. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 168–170)

Monitoring for undercutting begins with periodic special rolling of the pavement surface. When the measured permanent deflection exceeds the allowable limits—more than a half‑inch on existing subgrade or more than one inch on embankments—the Representative orders an excavation to remove the unstable material. For subgrade zones where the deflection lies between 0.5 in and 3 in, the excavation depth is specified at one foot; if the deflection exceeds three inches, the depth may be increased up to three feet. The excavated area must be drained before backfilling. Backfill material shall be AASHTO No. 1 aggregate (or an approved borrow material) placed in accordance with Section 208.3(d). At the base of the undercut a Class 4 Type C geotextile is installed, and where drainage is required a layer of No. 57 aggregate may be added. The transition zones at the start and end of the undercut are formed with side slopes no steeper than an 8H:1V ratio. Over the geotextile a minimum eight‑inch layer of aggregate is laid, followed by a six‑inch subbase placed on a Class 4 Type A geotextile before final compaction to achieve the required density. All underground utilities are protected throughout the operation. After completion, a post‑placement inspection verifies that drainage functions properly and that the aggregate placement and compaction meet the prescribed specifications, thereby establishing a stable base for subsequent pavement reconstruction. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 143–170)
