Undercutting

Part of: Subgrade is-subtopic-of
Undercutting is a topic under Subgrade.
undercutting
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-28

Undercutting is a remedial technique applied when the existing subgrade or base lacks sufficient bearing capacity—identified by laboratory California Bearing Ratio values below five and/or permanent deflection exceeding half an inch for existing subgrades (one inch for raised embankments)—that would otherwise jeopardize pavement structural integrity. The process consists of excavating the deficient zone to a depth governed by the observed deformation (typically one foot for 0.5‑3 inches of rutting, up to three feet for greater deflection), draining any water, and backfilling with compacted AASHTO No. 1 aggregate placed over a geotextile fabric to create a stable, resilient foundation before new pavement placement [1][2]. The horizontal extent of the undercut determines its classification—Class 1A for widths under eight feet and Class 1C for wider sections—and each class requires the same sequence of drainage, geotextile installation, and aggregate backfill to meet design criteria for resilient modulus and structural support [2].

Purpose and applicability

Undercutting is employed when a pavement’s underlying subgrade lacks sufficient bearing capacity, as indicated by laboratory CBR values falling below five and/or by field observations of permanent deflection or rutting that exceed half an inch for existing subgrades (or one inch for raised embankments) [1][2]. The method solves the problem of weak or unstable material that would otherwise compromise the structural support of a repair or new pavement layer, preventing premature failure caused by low subgrade strength [1][2]. To address this, the deficient zone is excavated and removed, any water collected in the cut is drained, and the void is backfilled with AASHTO No. 1 aggregate placed over a geotextile layer as required [2]. The depth of excavation is governed by the measured permanent deformation: zones showing between 0.5 and 3 inches are typically excavated to approximately one foot, while areas exceeding three inches may be cut to a maximum of three feet [2]. By eliminating the unsuitable material and installing a properly compacted, geotextile‑reinforced aggregate base, undercutting creates a stable foundation that meets design criteria for resilient modulus and structural support before further pavement construction [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 139–165)

Undercutting is indicated when testing shows the in‑situ California Bearing Ratio of the subgrade or base falls below five, signalling an unstable layer that must be removed and replaced with a stable foundation [1]. The same condition can also be identified through special rolling results where permanent deflection exceeds one‑half inch for existing subgrades (or one inch for embankments), confirming instability [2]. When the CBR is above five and deflection remains below these limits, other remedial options such as subgrade strengthening, lime or cement stabilization, rock capping, geogrid reinforcement, or geotextile separation are preferred over undercutting [1][2]. If the measured permanent deflection lies between just over one‑half inch and three inches, the guides prescribe a one‑foot deep undercut; if it exceeds three inches, excavation may be extended to a maximum of three feet [2]. The horizontal extent of the undercut is defined by its bottom width: a width less than eight feet calls for a Class 1A undercut, while a width of eight feet or more requires a Class 1C undercut [2]. In either class the procedure includes draining any void, backfilling with AASHTO No. 1 aggregate and installing the specified geotextile before placing new pavement [2]. When the material to be removed is not classified as unsuitable subgrade—such as topsoil, existing pavement that is not being rehabilitated, benches, rock ledges, or similar items—a standard Class 1 excavation should be performed instead of an undercut [2]. Likewise, if permanent deflection does not meet the half‑inch (or one‑inch for embankments) threshold, the representative may approve stabilization by additional compaction rather than excavation [2][1]. When extra‑depth undercutting or combined undercutting and stabilization is recommended based on test results, its quantity and cost must be incorporated into the project design estimate [1].

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 139–165)

Required inputs

Undercutting is employed when the existing subgrade contains unsuitable material that must be removed. The classification depends on the width of the void to be backfilled: if the bottom width is less than eight feet it is treated as a Class 1A undercut, while a width of eight feet or more qualifies as a Class 1C undercut. In addition, special‑rolling inspections that reveal permanent deflection greater than half an inch but not exceeding three inches require a one‑foot deep excavation; deflections larger than three inches may demand up to three feet depth. Before backfilling, the excavated zone is drained and lined with geotextile fabric. The bottom of the undercut receives a Class 4 Type C geotextile as prescribed, and where drainage is required a No. 57 aggregate pipe is installed. The primary fill material consists of AASHTO No. 1 aggregate or an approved borrow placed directly on the geotextile; at least eight inches of this material must be laid before compaction. Over the compacted fill a six‑inch subbase is placed atop a Class 4 Type A geotextile. Compaction must achieve the project‑specified densities: for deeper embankment foundations the material is compacted to 97 % of its maximum dry density, whereas for structure foundations, subgrades, or zones within three feet of the proposed subgrade elevation a full 100 % of maximum dry density is required. Moisture content of the fill must be maintained between three percent below the optimum and the optimum value determined by standard testing. Transition slopes at the edges of the undercut are limited to an eight‑horizontal‑to‑one‑vertical ratio, utilities are protected, and work proceeds only after a qualified representative confirms stability. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 139–165)

Undercutting is required when subgrade or subbase strength measured by laboratory California Bearing Ratio falls below five, a condition identified through a pre‑construction testing program that includes gradation, in‑place moisture content and density measurements followed by the CBR test on the material to be removed. In addition, special‑rolling surveys that reveal permanent set greater than one‑half inch in existing subgrade or greater than one inch in embankments trigger Representative orders for removal of the unstable material. The design package must be coordinated with the District Geotechnical Engineer and must quantify the extent of extra depth undercut or stabilization, incorporating those quantities and associated costs into the plans and specifications. Dimensional control is governed primarily by the horizontal width of the zone to be excavated; when the bottom width of unsuitable material is less than eight feet the work follows Class 1A excavation provisions, while a width of eight feet or more requires compliance with Class 1C requirements. Both classes prescribe that the undercut be drained, backfilled with AASHTO No. 1 aggregate and that geotextile fabric be placed in accordance with Section 208.3(d), without imposing additional dimensional tolerances beyond those class definitions. Transition slopes at the beginning and end of the cut are limited to a maximum gradient of eight horizontal to one vertical. The depth of the undercut is linked to the magnitude of permanent deflection observed; for moderate deflection ranging from 0.5 in to 3 in the cut shall not exceed one foot, whereas severe deflection exceeding three inches may be excavated to a maximum depth of three feet. At the base of the excavation a Class 4 Type C geotextile is installed, over which at least eight inches of approved fill material is placed before compaction, followed by a second layer of Class 4 Type A geotextile and a minimum six‑inch subbase layer. No further dimensional tolerances are stipulated beyond the width‑based class criteria and the depth limits tied to deflection severity. All underground utilities must be protected throughout the undercutting operation. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 139–165)

Equipment and personnel

Undercutting work must be overseen by a qualified Representative who is present during the special‑rolling assessment and who signs off on whether the subgrade stability meets the acceptance criteria; only after the Representative declares the existing material unsatisfactory may crews proceed to excavate, place geotextile, backfill and compact according to the prescribed sequence. The crew must include operators trained to run the specified rollers—pneumatic‑tire, sheepsfoot or pad foot, or smooth steel drum vibratory rollers—and, when required, a loaded triaxle dump truck, all of which are operated in static mode at no more than two miles per hour and with load pressures and wheel spacing that meet the detailed equipment specifications. Operators must also monitor moisture content, achieve the target density (97 % or 100 % of maximum dry density as dictated by depth), and protect existing utilities while excavating slopes not steeper than an eight‑to‑one ratio. Acceptance is confirmed when permanent deflection limits are met (½ inch for subgrade, one inch for embankments) and the Representative signs off. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 163–165)

Work sequence

Before any undercutting can be initiated, the project must be fully designed in accordance with the applicable design chapter that establishes the subgrade removal and replacement requirements. The design is followed by a comprehensive evaluation of the existing subgrade or subbase material, which includes performing gradation tests to classify soil particles, measuring in‑place moisture content and density, and conducting a California Bearing Ratio test. The CBR results are compared with the prescribed threshold, and if the measured value is less than five, the undercutting may proceed only after the evaluation has been documented and approved. In parallel, all topsoil and any topsoil‑mix material must be removed from the site and stockpiled in accordance with the specified stockpile requirements before excavation activities begin. After the topsoil removal, the underlying subgrade is required to be compacted to the stipulated density criteria prior to further testing. The compacted subgrade then undergoes a special rolling test performed in the presence of an authorized Representative who evaluates the permanent deflection. If the observed permanent deflection exceeds the allowable limits—greater than one‑half inch for an existing subgrade or greater than one inch for an embankment foundation—the subgrade is declared unsatisfactory and must be identified as deficient material. Only when the design documentation, subgrade evaluation, topsoil removal, required compaction, and satisfactory special rolling results have been completed and any unsuitable material has been identified can undercutting or excavation be undertaken to replace the deficient material. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 139–165)

The undercutting sequence begins with locating the area of unsuitable material as shown on the plans and excavating it from beneath the subgrade; the excavation is limited to a bottom width of less than eight feet. If special rolling indicates that the subgrade lacks stability, the depth of removal is set by the measured permanent deflection—one foot when the deflection lies between one‑half inch and three inches, and up to three feet when it exceeds three inches—and the entry and exit transitions are formed with slopes no steeper than eight horizontal to one vertical while compacting the cut floor. After excavation the void is drained, then a Class 4 Type C geotextile is placed on the prepared base. Where drainage is required, No. 57 aggregate is spread before backfilling the undercut with the specified borrow material; at least eight inches of approved fill must rest on the geotextile prior to compacting to the prescribed density. Once this layer has been compacted, a second geotextile—Class 4 Type A—is laid over the backfill, followed by a minimum six‑inch subbase that is also compacted. Throughout the operation existing underground utilities are protected. Finally, the remaining void is filled with AASHTO No. 1 aggregate and the work is completed in accordance with Section 208.3(d). [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 139–165)

Staging and logistics

Undercutting must be planned with sufficient clearance to meet the required transition slope of no steeper than 8H:1V, and any excavation should avoid encroaching within ten feet of bridge abutments or approach slabs when special rolling is used. Existing underground utilities must be protected throughout the undercut operation. The source does not specify weather or general site‑access limits for undercutting. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 163–165)

Quality control and acceptance

When evaluating an existing subgrade or subbase for possible undercutting, the engineer must inspect and test the material’s gradation, in‑place moisture content, in‑place density, and its California Bearing Ratio (CBR) to establish soil classification, strength and stiffness needed for decision making [1]. A CBR value below five signals that the layer does not meet the minimum strength criteria and therefore undercutting or a stabilization measure should be considered [1]. In addition to laboratory‑based properties, field stability is verified by applying a special rolling test and measuring the resulting permanent deflection, often expressed as rut depth [2]. For existing subgrades, cut sections, or spread footings on soil, if the observed permanent deformation exceeds one‑half inch, the area is judged unstable and undercutting is authorized [2]. When the work involves an embankment that is being raised, the allowable permanent deflection threshold is increased to one inch; exceeding this limit also triggers the need for undercutting [2].

[1] — Ch. 6 (pp. 143–150); [2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 163–165)

Undercutting is deemed necessary when special‑rolling shows that the subgrade stability does not meet the acceptance limits – a permanent deflection greater than one‑half inch for existing subgrades or spread footings, or more than one inch for embankment foundations. If these limits are exceeded, the corrective action is to excavate the deficient zone and replace it; a 1‑foot deep undercut is required when the observed deflection lies between 0.5 and 3 inches, while a maximum of three feet depth is called for when deflection exceeds 3 inches. The excavation must be sloped no steeper than an 8H:1V transition, a Class 4‑C geotextile placed at the base, and at least eight inches of approved fill material laid before compaction, with a minimum six‑inch subbase over a Class 4‑A geotextile prior to final placement. All work is performed under Representative supervision to verify stability before proceeding. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 163–165)

Curing, protection, and handoff

After the undercut is completed, the exposed void is first drained to eliminate any standing water. The bottom of the cut is then compacted and a Class 4 Type C geotextile is placed directly on the subgrade. An approved backfill material—typically AASHTO No. 1 aggregate or other borrow material—is placed over this fabric, with at least eight inches of fill before compaction; if additional drainage is required, a No. 57 aggregate path may be installed at this stage. Once the initial fill has been compacted, a second geotextile layer, Class 4 Type A, is laid and a minimum six‑inch subbase is added and compacted to the specified density. The geotextiles serve both to protect the backfill from contamination and to assist in curing of the repaired pavement surface, while existing underground utilities are protected throughout the operation. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 139–165)

Expected performance and maintenance

When special rolling reveals that the existing subgrade or embankment is not stable—identified by permanent deflection or rutting exceeding the allowable limits (more than a half‑inch for subgrades, more than an inch for raised embankments)—the area is deemed to have failed and undercutting must be performed. The depth of the undercut depends on how much deflection is observed: if the permanent deflection lies between just over a half‑inch up to three inches, a one‑foot deep excavation is required; if it exceeds three inches, the excavation can extend to a maximum of three feet. After excavating, geotextile and approved backfill material are placed and compacted according to the specified moisture‑density criteria before restoring the pavement base. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 163–165)

Undercutting is initiated when monitoring of the pavement reveals permanent deflection that exceeds allowable limits after special rolling. If the subgrade shows between 0.5 inch and 3 inches of rutting, a one‑foot deep cut is made; larger deflections greater than three inches may require excavation to as much as three feet. The width of the cut determines whether a Class 1A (for sections under eight feet wide) or Class 1C (eight feet or wider) undercutting procedure is applied. The operation begins by excavating the unsuitable material beneath the subgrade, then draining any resulting voids. At the bottom of the excavation a Class 4 Type C geotextile is placed, and an optional No. 57 aggregate drainage trench may be installed. The void is back‑filled with AASHTO No. 1 aggregate or other approved borrow material, ensuring at least eight inches of material are placed before compaction to the specified density. After this first layer a second geotextile, Class 4 Type A, is laid over the compacted fill, followed by a minimum six‑inch subbase that is also compacted. Throughout the process existing underground utilities must be protected. Preventive maintenance consists of periodic inspection of the drainage path to confirm it remains clear and verification that the backfill and both geotextile layers retain their integrity. Ongoing monitoring of pavement deflection continues so that any recurrence of excessive rutting can trigger a repeat undercutting cycle before further distress develops. [2]

[2] — SECTION 200 EARTHWORK (pp. 139–165)
