Distress Severity

Part of: Distress is-subtopic-of
Distress Severity is a topic under Distress.
severity-of-distressseverity-of-distressmedium-severity
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-30

Distress Severity is a pavement‑condition evaluation method that characterises the visible distress types present on a surface—such as rutting, cracking, raveling, flushing, potholes and drainage problems—and assigns each a severity rating to reflect the underlying base condition or traffic loading[1][2]. The assessment is performed through mandatory field walk‑throughs and automated or manual surveys conducted according to Publication 336 (or 343) where rut depth is measured with a 12‑foot straight edge, International Roughness Index values are recorded, and any premature distress is noted, providing quantitative data that feed into rehabilitation strategy selection and warranty performance analysis[1][2]. When the quantified severity exceeds predefined limits—e.g., more than 30 % of a segment contains two or more low‑severity distresses—the results trigger prescribed treatments such as microsurfacing or full‑depth reclamation, thereby linking the measured distress directly to design and maintenance decisions[1][2].

Purpose and investigation objectives

Distress Severity evaluates the condition of a pavement surface by characterising the types of distress present and judging how severe each distress is, and also by quantifying distresses such as flushing, potholes, rutting, fatigue cracking and other surface defects that reflect base condition or traffic loading[1][2]. The investigation must identify which distress types are present[1][2] and should quantify their severity[1][2]; it must also note whether any distress appears premature[1]. In addition, the investigation should gather related condition metrics such as International Roughness Index (IRI) and estimated patching percentages to support treatment decisions[1]. For full‑depth reclamation project selection, Distress Severity is used to evaluate the condition of existing pavement surface distresses—specifically rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and associated drainage problems[1]; for asphalt concrete warranty assessments it focuses on flushing, potholes, rutting, fatigue cracking and other surface defects[2]. The investigation should quantify the current level of each distress[1] and measure their extent—for example using a 12‑foot straight edge for rut depth or manual surveys for flushing and potholes[2]. It should identify the underlying damage mechanisms that produced the distresses and determine whether excess moisture or drainage deficiencies are contributing, in order to assess if those conditions make full‑depth reclamation an appropriate rehabilitation option[1]. The investigation must also compare the results to performance‑criteria limits such as whether more than 30 % of a segment area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, and identify the underlying cause—whether it is attributable to base condition, traffic overloads, routine maintenance activities, destructive procedures, uncontrolled natural forces, or accidents—to decide if remedial action is required[2].

[1] — Ch. 1 (p. 50), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

During the required field inspection, the scoping team records pavement condition data that includes distress types and severity, noting any premature distress; this information is reviewed before the Scoping Field View and gives planners a clear picture of which distresses exist and how severe they are, allowing them to select the most cost‑effective treatment strategy, shape design parameters, prioritize maintenance or rehabilitation actions, and enhance safety while complying with Pavement Preservation Guidelines. The distress survey then provides a summary report that documents the level of each observed damage—rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage problems—and also gives insight into their causes; because the severity of these distresses should be specifically considered, engineers can determine if full‑depth reclamation is suitable, set appropriate layer thicknesses, correct underlying water issues, and select the proper stabilization technique, directly guiding project selection, design decisions, and maintenance or rehabilitation actions. Under the 60‑month warranty, automated and manual surveys identify and measure pavement distresses according to Publication 336 and the Department performs an annual performance analysis based on the distress criteria in Table A; these quantified findings establish clear decision thresholds such as requiring microsurfacing when a year‑5 survey shows that 30 % of a segment’s area has two or more low‑severity distresses. The specifications also dictate that repair patches spaced less than 100 ft must be combined into one continuous repair, and rut depth is verified with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width to determine severity and extent, thereby informing planning, design, maintenance scheduling, and rehabilitation actions. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 1 (p. 50), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Scope and applicability

Distress Severity is employed when a recent pavement condition survey, conducted according to Publication 336 for asphalt concrete or Publication 343 for continuously reinforced concrete and unpaved roads, has been performed to evaluate the existing surface type. In that context the method gauges the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, and drainage issues, which are then incorporated into the rehabilitation strategy, particularly when support‑layer conditions and subgrade moisture problems must be addressed before full‑depth reclamation because FDR is indicated for situations requiring improvement of the support layers rather than solely surface distress. Distress Severity is also applied to asphalt concrete (flexible) pavement segments that are covered by the 60‑month warranty, where the survey techniques of Publication 336 are used to identify, measure, and report surface distresses such as flushing, potholes, rutting and fatigue cracking. For flushing and potholes the identification is performed through manual surveys, while rut depth is verified with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width to determine severity and extent. The method operates under annual performance surveys that compare observed distress severity against the limits set in Table A of the warranty. It also accounts for site conditions beyond the contractor’s control, including poor base condition, traffic loadings that exceed the estimated cumulative ESALs for the design life, routine maintenance activities, destructive procedures, natural forces, and traffic‑accident damage. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Distress severity surveys give a snapshot of the visible damage—rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, and drainage problems—but they do not by themselves reveal the underlying mechanisms or the structural capacity of the support layers; consequently, relying solely on severity can mislead the selection of a rehabilitation method. [1] Under the 60‑month warranty the determination of distress severity is based primarily on automated surveys that identify, measure and report surface distresses according to Publication 336, with flushing and potholes confirmed by manual surveys, which captures only visible damage and therefore cannot diagnose subsurface problems such as an unacceptable base condition. [2] When the observed distress is primarily surface‑related or when it is unclear whether the subgrade or base has sufficient strength, additional investigations such as in‑situ testing (e.g., Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer, light weight deflectometer) and material sampling should be employed to confirm causes and guide the appropriate treatment. [1] If a survey shows that 30 % of segment area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, the warranty requires microsurfacing, but if the underlying cause may be a weak base the Department directs coring of six‑inch diameter samples and laboratory testing (PTM No. 715, AASHTO T 209, PTM No. 702 or PTM No. 739) to determine void content, asphalt percent and gradation before remediation. [2] Likewise, when traffic counts indicate cumulative ESALs have exceeded 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life ESALs, distress severity alone is insufficient and additional traffic classification counts must be performed. [2] In such cases the Department may schedule extra performance surveys or authorize nondestructive testing after seven days notice to obtain a more complete assessment. [2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Existing information and records

When undertaking a Distress Severity review the scoping team should first define the project limits (segments or mileposts) and gather pavement history data for the corridor. [1] Comprehensive pavement condition information should be assembled, including International Roughness Index values, identified distress types and their severity, and an indication of any premature distress. [1] Estimated patching percentages and the treatment strategy from the State of the Interstate report with justification for alternatives should also be recorded together with forecasts from the Pavement Asset Management System, crash‑cluster information, cross‑slope measurements, and traffic data such as average daily traffic and truck percentage. [1] It is important to obtain a reliable estimate of future traffic loading on the road before the road is constructed. [1] A recent pavement condition survey—such as those defined in Publication 336 or 343 or using MicroPaver—should be collected to document the current severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues and to note observations on distress causes. [1] In‑situ testing results, including Falling Weight Deflectometer measurements as well as dynamic cone penetrometer and light weight deflectometer data, must be compiled to assess subgrade strength before rehabilitation. [1] Detailed sampling information—including number of samples, locations, material size, depth of sampling and handling procedures—must also be gathered to support design decisions. [1] The Distress Severity review should examine all automated and manual pavement condition surveys conducted under Publication 336, including the annual performance surveys for each half‑mile segment and any supplemental surveys for flushing or potholes. [2] It must also incorporate the segment‑specific distress limits recorded in Table A. [2] The Department’s traffic classification counts that verify ESAL estimates against the contract projections must also be incorporated. [2] Historical performance analysis reports that flag when a year‑5 survey shows 30 % of a segment area containing two or more low‑severity distresses and trigger remedial action should be reviewed. [2] Records of any prior remediation notifications, emergency repair logs, and base‑condition core testing results should be reviewed to distinguish contractor‑responsible from beyond‑control distress. [2]

[1] — Ch. 1 (p. 50), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

The pavement management database is deemed insufficient for detailed design quantities, so any distress type and severity recorded therein must be confirmed through a walk‑through because the existing entries may contain gaps or outdated observations that only on‑site verification can resolve. For projects considered for full‑depth reclamation, field investigation must fill the absence of a recent pavement condition survey, clarify damage mechanisms that are merely hinted at by visual observation, and supply missing in‑situ subgrade strength measurements such as Falling Weight Deflectometer or cone penetrometer results. Additionally, the selection process for full‑depth reclamation requires complete sampling data; therefore the number, locations, depths, and handling of material samples must be verified in the field to eliminate incomplete information that would otherwise compromise a reliable severity rating. Under the 60‑month warranty provisions, the performance criteria are based on survey data that may be incomplete or disputed, so field work must determine whether any segment actually exceeds the trigger of thirty percent of its area containing two or more low‑severity distresses. The same warranty section also mandates confirmation in the field that repair patches are not spaced less than one hundred feet, because such spacing would require a continuous repair according to the specification. When the existing records call for assessment of rutting severity, direct rut‑depth measurements must be obtained with a twelve‑foot straight edge across the lane width to resolve any uncertainty about the extent of the distress. If fatigue cracking or other signs suggest an unacceptable base condition, the specification requires drilling six‑inch diameter cores within the distressed area and testing them according to PTM No. 715, AASHTO T 209, PTM No. 702 and PTM No. 739 in order to resolve the underlying cause of the distress. Finally, traffic count data used to evaluate cumulative equivalent single axle loads must be checked on site to verify whether the ESALs have exceeded one hundred percent of the projected twenty‑year design life, a condition that would shift responsibility for rutting or base failures away from the contractor. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 1 (p. 50), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Visual inspection and condition assessment

According to the Pavement Policy Manual, Distress Severity should focus on the visible pavement distresses identified in the distress survey, specifically evaluating the severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, and any drainage issues that may be present. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications require that under the 60‑month warranty Distress Severity must evaluate visible defects such as flushing, potholes, rutting measured by rut‑depth using a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width, and fatigue cracking that may indicate an unacceptable base condition. The same specification states that if the fifth‑year survey finds that 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, microsurfacing or another approved treatment is required, and any repair areas spaced less than 100 ft must be combined into one continuous patch. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

After a distress survey is completed using the procedures outlined in Publication 336, Publication 343, MicroPaver™ or similar, the observed pavement defects are first grouped into defined types such as rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues. The severity of each listed distress is evaluated and recorded; for flushing, potholes, and other types the classification follows automated surveys per Publication 336 with manual surveys employed when required for flushing and pothholes. For rutting the severity is quantified by measuring depth with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width, while other distress severities are quantified using the rating procedures of Publication 336. The overall level of distress together with any observations on cause are entered into a summary report that serves as documentation for the rehabilitation strategy. Low‑severity distresses trigger remedial action when they occupy at least 30 % of a segment area with two or more occurrences; extent is expressed as percentage of the defined half‑mile Segment or measured distances, and repair areas less than 100 feet are combined into one continuous repair. Location of each observed distress is documented by assigning it to its specific Segment in the Department’s Location Reference System, and results are reported in writing within 14 calendar days identifying the segment and performance criterion. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Field measurements and testing

In Section 1.1 FIELD INSPECTION AND PAVEMENT WARRANTY REVIEW of the Pavement Policy Manual, Distress Severity is first evaluated during a mandatory field inspection that must be performed by walking the entire project because visual “windshield” surveys are not adequate [1]. During this walk‑through the inspector records pavement condition data such as observed distress types, assigns a severity rating, and captures the International Roughness Index (IRI), which quantifies surface roughness as part of the overall condition assessment [1]. The Pavement Condition Survey required by the manual follows the criteria set out in Publication 336 or Publication 343, or alternatively the MicroPaver™ procedure for municipal projects, and documents the level of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues while also providing insight into their causes [1]. Complementary nondestructive testing prescribed in the same manual includes Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) measurements that indicate the stiffness of existing or newly placed pavement layers [1]. Subgrade strength or stiffness is evaluated with a dynamic cone penetrometer (DCP) and a light‑weight deflectometer (LWD), both of which are listed as useful subgrade tests in the policy manual [1]. Under the 60‑month warranty provisions of the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation Specification, Distress Severity is also evaluated through automated surveys that identify and measure pavement distresses according to Publication 336, using the survey techniques, rating procedures, and extent estimation procedures specified therein [2]. Manual surveys are required for flushing and potholes to determine their presence and severity [2]. Rut‑depth checks are verified with a 12‑foot straight edge placed across the lane width, providing a direct measure of rut depth severity and extent [2]. Routine traffic classification counts are performed to verify the equivalent single axle load (ESAL) estimates that support the distress evaluation context [2]. When fatigue cracking or other distresses suggest an unacceptable base condition, six‑inch diameter cores are extracted from the affected area and tested in accordance with PTM 715, AASHTO T209, PTM 702 or PTM 739 to assess voids, asphalt content and gradation of the base material [2]. The contractor may also employ nondestructive testing methods at any time during the warranty period provided that the District Executive is notified at least seven calendar days in advance, allowing additional stiffness or integrity assessments as needed [2].

[1] — Ch. 1 (p. 50), Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Under the 60‑month asphalt concrete pavement warranty, distress severity is evaluated primarily through automated surveys conducted annually on each roughly one‑half‑mile segment, with manual surveys used for flushing and potholes as required; rut depth is measured with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width, and when base condition investigation is needed, 6‑inch diameter cores are extracted from within the distress area and tested per PTM No. 715, AASHTO T 209 and related methods. The Department may add additional or more frequent surveys whenever evidence shows performance criteria are not being met, and traffic classification counts are routinely performed to verify ESAL estimates. Repair spacing is governed by a rule that if the distance between repair areas is less than 100 feet the work must be executed as one continuous patch, while testing activities must be scheduled at least seven calendar days in advance for nondestructive methods and are subject to normal site conditions, with inclement weather permitting only temporary repairs until permanent work can proceed. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Sampling and laboratory investigation

For full‑depth‑reclamation candidate pavements the distress‑severity assessment requires sampling each pavement layer identified in the depth‑of‑sampling plan, and the number of samples, their locations, the size of each material sample, the sampling technique and how the specimens are handled are all dictated by the criteria set out in Appendix J (sections J.2.D.1 through J.2.D.5) without prescribing exact quantities or frequencies; these factors must be determined according to those subsections [1]. When base problems are suspected under the Pennsylvania 60‑month warranty, the Department directs a series of 6‑inch diameter cores to be drilled within the distress area to sample the underlying layers, measuring core depth and testing for voids, asphalt content and gradation [2]. Sampling locations for full‑depth‑reclamation projects are specified in Appendix J section J.2.D.1 (and related subsections), whereas under the warranty program sampling locations are chosen by the Department based on identified distresses such as cracks, flushing, potholes or rutting [1][2]. The depth of sampling and identification of layers for full‑depth‑reclamation is covered in Appendix J section J.2.D.4, while the Pennsylvania specification requires that each core’s depth be recorded as part of the investigation of the distress area [1][2]. The number of samples for full‑depth‑reclamation is to be determined by the criteria in Appendix J sections J.2.D.1‑J.2.D.5, with no fixed count given, and the Pennsylvania warranty states that the number of cores is taken “as needed,” allowing additional coring if performance criteria appear not to be met [1][2]. Frequency of sampling for full‑depth‑reclamation is not explicitly set in the manual; it is governed by the same Appendix criteria, whereas the warranty mandates annual surveys of the asphalt‑concrete surface and permits the Department to schedule additional or more frequent performance surveys whenever evidence exists that performance criteria are not being met [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Distress Severity should obtain 6‑inch diameter cores from distressed areas and have them tested in the laboratory according to PTM No. 715 and AASHTO T 209 to determine percent unfilled voids, PTM No. 702 (or other approved methods) to determine percent asphalt, and PTM No. 739 to determine aggregate gradation; these tests evaluate the base condition by confirming that the pavement meets the specified minimum thickness, that density is > 92% of theoretical maximum density, and that the asphalt content and gradation fall within the tolerances of the contractor’s mix design. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Structural and functional performance

For projects selected for full‑depth reclamation, the assessment of distress severity concerning structural capacity incorporates in‑situ Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) testing that provides pavement deflection and stiffness data, together with subgrade strength evaluation using either a dynamic cone penetrometer (DCP) or a light weight deflectometer (LWD), and it also mandates a quantified determination of the thickness of each pavement layer and its drainage conditions. In the context of the 60‑month warranty for asphalt concrete pavements, distress severity is measured functionally by rut‑depth determinations performed with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width, while structural capacity is verified through core drilling that must confirm compliance with the specified minimum thickness and achieve a density exceeding 92 % of the theoretical maximum. The cores extracted under the warranty are subjected to laboratory analyses in accordance with PTM No. 715 and AASHTO T 209 for percent unfilled voids, PTM No. 702 (or an approved method) for asphalt content, and PTM No. 739 for gradation, thereby providing material‑property information relevant to structural capacity. Additionally, routine traffic classification counts are performed to ensure that the observed Equivalent Single Axle Load (ESAL) values remain within the projected estimates incorporated in the contract documents, linking traffic loading to both structural and functional performance. The warranty specification does not require any other measurements such as load‑transfer efficiency, roughness, friction, ride quality or bearing strength for distress severity evaluation. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Under the 60‑month asphalt concrete pavement warranty, the Department evaluates measured distress severity from annual automated or manual surveys against the performance limits set out in Table A; for example, if the year‑5 survey shows that 30 % of a segment’s area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, microsurfacing or another Department‑approved treatment must be applied. Traffic classification counts are also performed to confirm that accumulated Equivalent Single Axle Loads (ESALs) remain within the projected values, and when cumulative ESALs exceed 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life ESALs the contractor is relieved of responsibility for rutting or base‑condition distresses for the remainder of the warranty. Rut depth, when required, is verified with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width to assess severity and extent. By comparing observed conditions to these distress thresholds and traffic‑loading assumptions, the warranty determines whether remedial work is needed to satisfy the remaining service‑life requirements. [2]

[2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

Data interpretation and diagnosis

Visual observations are obtained through automated and manual surveys performed according to Publication 336 that identify and rate each distress, and a summary report should document the level of distress and corresponding observations. Beyond the visual survey, in‑situ testing such as the Falling Weight Deflectometer, dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer is used to assess subgrade strength that will support the rehabilitated pavement structure. Rut‑depth measurements are verified using a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width to determine severity and extent, and traffic classification counts are performed to verify ESAL estimates. Properly collected samples from distressed areas are evaluated in the laboratory to determine material properties for stabilization, including voids, asphalt content and gradation tested per PTM No. 715, AASHTO T209, and other applicable methods. The measured stiffness from in‑situ testing and the laboratory‑derived strengths are integrated with the documented severity of rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes and drainage issues to allow engineers to specifically consider each distress when formulating an appropriate rehabilitation strategy. Annual performance data compiled for each segment are compared with the integrated assessment to determine compliance with the limits in Table A; for example, if the year‑5 performance survey determines that 30 % of segment area has two or more distresses with low severity, remedial action is required. When ESALs exceed 100 % of the estimated 20‑year design life, the contractor is relieved of responsibility for further rehabilitation under the warranty provisions. The Department uses this combined assessment of visual observations, field measurements, laboratory analysis and performance data to decide compliance and trigger remediation actions as stipulated in the warranty criteria. The observed distress severity is most likely tied to inadequate drainage that allows excess moisture to compromise material integrity and weaken the pavement’s structural capacity, as well as to weak subgrade support revealed by in‑situ testing; these conditions promote material deterioration manifested as rutting, cracking, raveling and potholes, which the distress survey specifically calls out for consideration in any rehabilitation strategy [1]. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania Department of Transportation specifications indicate that the severity may also be linked to an unacceptable base condition, with fatigue cracking identified in performance surveys signaling weak support beneath the pavement, and they further note that traffic overloads can be a cause because when cumulative ESALs exceed 100 % of the 20‑year design life the contractor is relieved of responsibility for rutting or base‑related distresses [2]. In addition, environmental forces such as floods, earthquakes, landslides and other natural disasters are listed as factors beyond the contractor’s control that can produce severe surface damage, and material deterioration manifested by fatigue cracking further supports a weak‑support or degradation cause [2].

Findings, confidence, and next actions

Distress severity data from the pavement condition survey can be be used to characterize the current state of the pavement and infer underlying damage mechanisms, thereby supporting decisions about whether full‑depth reclamation is appropriate and which specific distress types—rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes or drainage problems—must be addressed in the rehabilitation design. The same severity information measured through the automated surveys prescribed by Publication 336 and confirmed where necessary by manual methods such as rut‑depth checks with a 12‑foot straight edge provides the quantitative basis for the Department’s annual performance analysis under Section 496.3(c) warranty; when the year‑5 survey shows that 30 % of a segment area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, the specification mandates remedial treatment such as microsurfacing. Because the visual distress survey is limited to surface observations, its conclusions are constrained by the need for additional information such as in‑situ strength tests (FWD, DCP, LWD) and representative material samples to confirm subgrade conditions and hidden water issues, introducing uncertainty into the severity‑based assessment. The reliability of warranty‑related conclusions is further limited by the accuracy of the survey techniques, the potential for disputed results that must be resolved through a Conflict Resolution Team, and the fact that distresses attributed to base condition, traffic overloads, routine Department maintenance, destructive procedures, uncontrolled forces of nature or accidents are excluded from contractor responsibility, introducing uncertainty in attributing severity to contractor performance. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 5 (pp. 124–126); [2] — SECTION 400 FLEXIBLE PAVEMENTS (pp. 353–358)

After a distress severity assessment, a detailed pavement condition survey should be completed and a summary report documenting the level of distress—including rutting, cracking, raveling, potholes, and drainage issues—must be prepared to guide the rehabilitation strategy [1]. In‑situ testing such as Falling Weight Deflectometer testing before rehabilitation should be performed to quantify existing pavement stiffness, followed by dynamic cone penetrometer or light weight deflectometer tests to assess subgrade stiffness [1]. Proper sampling of existing materials according to the prescribed sections supports accurate layer‑thickness design and drainage corrections, and finally the applicability of full‑depth reclamation should be evaluated against identified surface distresses before selecting a stabilization method [1]. If the distress severity survey shows that 30 % of a segment area contains two or more low‑severity distresses, the warranty requires remedial treatment such as microsurfacing or another Department‑approved method, and repair patches spaced less than 100 feet must be combined into one continuous repair [2]. The Department will schedule annual automated surveys and conduct manual surveys for flushing and potholes when needed; rut depth shall be verified with a 12‑foot straight edge across the lane width, and traffic classification counts shall be performed to confirm that ESAL estimates remain within projected values, with additional or more frequent surveys possible if performance criteria appear unmet [2]. When base condition is suspected, the Department may direct drilling of six‑inch diameter cores within the distress area for laboratory testing per PTM No. 715, AASHTO T 209, PTM No. 702, and PTM No. 739 to confirm that pavement thickness meets specifications and density exceeds 92 % of theoretical maximum; failure to meet these criteria triggers further investigation [2]. All remediation work must be started within 60 calendar days after written notification, use plant‑mixed asphalt unless otherwise approved, and be accompanied by an approved traffic control plan [2].
