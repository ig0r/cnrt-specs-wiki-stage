Overlay Design

Part of: Overlay is-subtopic-of
Overlay Design is a topic under Overlay.

Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-28

Overlay design is a pavement‑rehabilitation process invoked when an existing surface no longer supplies the structural capacity, ride quality, friction or crack‑control needed for projected traffic loads[1][2]. It treats the remaining pavement as a non‑structural base (or, where bonding is required, uses mechanistic‑empirical procedures) and determines the necessary overlay thickness by calculating a future‑traffic Structural Number from ESALs and subtracting the effective existing Structural Number obtained via component or non‑destructive methods; the resulting layer must satisfy minimum service‑life, durability, safety and joint‑performance criteria[1][2]. Before placement, unstable slabs, slab fragments and surface defects larger than four square inches by one inch are repaired or patched, panel geometry and joint spacing are limited to near‑square panels no larger than eighteen times the overlay thickness, and material specifications—including macrofibers, aggregate gradation and chloride‑ion penetration limits—are enforced to ensure uniform support and long‑term performance[2].

Purpose and design objectives

Overlay design is called for when an existing pavement no longer provides the structural support, ride quality, surface friction or crack‑control performance required for safe and economical service under projected traffic loads. The deficiencies may appear as inadequate effective structural capacity, low‑friction values, excessive cracking that impairs load transfer efficiency, or a combination of these conditions that threaten the remaining service life. In concrete pavements the problem is often manifested by unstable slabs or slab fragments that move or rock under normal traffic, creating non‑uniform support for any new layer. Surface defects larger than four square inches in area and deeper than one inch also disrupt load transfer and must be eliminated before an overlay can achieve its intended performance. By stabilizing moving slabs, repairing slab fragments, filling large depressions with approved patching material, and cleaning loose debris, the overlay is able to restore uniform support and meet the structural capacity needed for future traffic. The overarching performance need addressed by an overlay therefore includes providing additional structural capacity, improving safety and ride comfort, restoring adequate friction, limiting crack widths, maintaining joint or slab load‑transfer efficiency, and extending the pavement’s serviceable lifespan. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 451–462)

The overlay must re‑establish the pavement’s functional capacity to accommodate current and forecast traffic while providing a smooth, safe riding surface. To achieve this, the design treats the existing asphalt layer only as a supporting base and does not count on any direct structural contribution from it. Structural strength is obtained by sizing the overlay thickness equal to the pavement thickness calculated for future traffic using an ESAL analysis that employs rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors, thereby delivering the required load‑bearing capacity. Before placement, any unstable slabs or slab fragments that would cause non‑uniform support must be stabilized or repaired, and surface defects larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch are to be filled with a compacted patching material to create a uniform substrate. Panel geometry is required to be nearly square, with an aspect ratio as close to one as feasible, never exceeding 1.5, and the longer dimension limited to no more than eighteen times the overlay thickness, while joint spacing and sealing follow the prescribed layout. The concrete mix must incorporate macrofibers at a dosage that achieves the specified residual strength (f150), and when the overlay depth is less than three inches the coarse aggregate shall be No 67 rather than No 57. Durability objectives are met by limiting chloride‑ion penetration to low, very low, or negligible levels within a 56‑day curing period according to AASHTO T 277/T 358, ensuring proper drainage of the interlayer, and maintaining joint and crack openings generally between 0.5 and 1.0 mm to preserve load‑transfer efficiency and reduce slab movement, misalignment, plastic shrinkage cracking, and crack widening. The design must provide a minimum structural life of eight years and shall not be expected to exceed twenty years, thereby extending serviceability while controlling crack development. Safety is further addressed by mitigating low‑friction conditions, preserving ride quality, and keeping the surface temperature of both existing pavement and any geotextile below 120 °F at placement. Collectively these functional, structural, durability, safety, and serviceability requirements ensure that the overlay remains functional, structurally sound, durable, safe, and comfortable for users over its intended service period. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 451–462)

Scope and applicability

Overlay design is invoked whenever an existing pavement requires improved ride quality, exhibits structural deficiency, or shows low‑friction values, and the intended service life typically ranges from eight to twenty years. [1] The methodology applies across the full spectrum of pavement structures—including asphalt concrete (AC) pavements, Portland‑cement‑concrete (PCC) slabs both intact and fractured, and composite AC/PCC systems—and can be executed using any of the seven standard overlay configurations supported by DARWin. [1] When asphalt is placed for the first time over a cement or brick surface, a minimum binder thickness of 2½ inches must be provided. [1] For an AC‑over‑AC situation the design calculates both the future‑traffic structural number (SN) and the effective existing SN using either the Component Method or a non‑destructive testing approach based on core data or RMS history. [1] On Interstate facilities the subbase is excluded from the structural evaluation, whereas on non‑Interstate routes it may also be omitted when the 18‑kip ESAL design value exceeds five million unless a subbase investigation has verified its condition, and the resulting overlay SN must exceed the required design SN. [1] When the existing pavement is a cracked, settled, or rubblized concrete slab—specifically Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement (JPCP) or Jointed Reinforced Concrete Pavement (JRCP)—the AC‑overlay of fractured PCC slab method is employed, with future traffic SN determined, effective existing SN derived via the component method (including any subbase), and overlay thickness selected from Table 10.7 using flexible‑pavement average initial truck factors. [1] If a rigid concrete pavement such as JPCP, JRCP, or Continuously Reinforced Concrete Pavement (CRCP) currently carries an asphalt layer that will be removed before placing a new AC overlay, the rigid‑pavement procedure is followed, using rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors and the secondary screen in DARWin that mirrors the rigid‑pavement design process to obtain the required thickness. [1] For concrete roadways already overlaid with sound asphalt, the AC‑overlay of an AC/PCC composite method permits placement of a new asphalt layer without removing the prior one; if material deficiencies are suspected, cores must be taken and the existing overlay removed before applying the AC‑over‑PCC design, with thickness derived from Table 10.8, effective existing thickness obtained via the condition‑survey method, and rigid‑pavement truck factors used for ESAL calculations. [1] An unbonded PCC overlay on an AC pavement provides additional structural capacity without relying on the underlying asphalt for load bearing; the required PCC thickness equals the calculated pavement thickness for future traffic, rendering the system a purely structural overlay. [1] Fiber‑reinforced concrete (FRC) overlays are appropriate when the existing surface is asphalt or composite and is in fair to good condition—allowing a bonded overlay—or when it is deteriorated, in which case an unbonded overlay may be used; FRC enables thinner slabs, typically three to six inches thick, by incorporating synthetic or steel macrofibers at 0.2 %–0.5 % by volume, thereby enhancing load‑transfer efficiency, reducing crack widths, and extending service life under heavy repeated loads, variable support conditions, or high‑traffic environments. [1] For thin bonded concrete overlays that sit directly on an asphalt‑surfaced pavement, the approach is suitable when the overlay depth is less than three inches; in this case the mix design calls for No 67 coarse aggregate rather than No 57, macrofibers must be added at a rate sufficient to achieve the required residual strength (f150) as specified in Table A, and the concrete must satisfy low‑to‑negligible chloride‑ion‑penetration criteria after no more than 56 days of curing according to AASHTO T 277 or T 358. [2] For rigid‑pavement projects where a concrete overlay is planned, the method is appropriate when the overlay thickness does not exceed seven inches; the specification then requires Class AA cement concrete reinforced with virgin polypropylene fibers that provide a residual strength ratio of 20 percent and are limited to five pounds per cubic yard unless a field demonstration justifies a higher content, and may also employ manufacturer‑approved geotextile anchoring devices, while fine‑graded asphalt courses can be incorporated using a Superpave mixture prepared in accordance with Section 410 and Bulletin 27. [2]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 451–459)

The choice of an alternative design approach over a conventional overlay design is dictated by several limitations related to the type, condition and interaction of the existing pavement. When a concrete layer is intended to be bonded directly to an existing asphalt or to an asphalt‑concrete composite, the traditional AASHTO overlay procedure cannot be used because it ignores the bond; in those situations the mechanistic‑empirical BCOA‑ME method must replace the standard design. Likewise, if the underlying concrete slab is severely cracked or faulted, a bonded Portland‑cement‑concrete overlay would be inappropriate and a different rehabilitation technique should be selected, since the suitability of any overlay hinges on the pavement’s existing type and condition. The AC‑Overlay‑of‑JPCP method is limited to jointed plain, jointed reinforced, or continuously reinforced concrete slabs; it cannot be applied when the base is a flexible pavement or when an existing asphalt overlay is to remain in place, because the procedure assumes that any asphalt layer will be stripped before the new asphalt overlay is placed. When the retained asphalt overlay shows material problems or deficiencies, complete removal of that layer is required and the design must follow the AC Overlay of PCC Pavement method rather than the AC Overlay of AC/PCC method. An unbonded Portland‑cement‑concrete overlay treats the existing asphalt solely as a support and therefore demands a full future‑traffic thickness; if the underlying asphalt can contribute structural capacity and a thinner, more economical overlay is desired, an approach that accounts for that contribution becomes preferable. Finally, the decision between bonded and unbonded overlays depends primarily on the condition of the underlying pavement: a fair‑to‑good surface permits a bonded design, whereas a poor or deteriorated surface—especially one exhibiting stripping—calls for an unbonded overlay, regardless of fiber reinforcement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188)

Design inputs and requirements

Overlay design for an existing AC pavement requires traffic inputs such as future loadings expressed in ESALs using average initial truck factors, material data that include the thickness of each existing layer and its structural coefficient, and geotechnical information about subbase condition and drainability (often omitted from the structural evaluation under specified conditions). Existing‑condition data are obtained from pavement cores or, if cores are unavailable, from the pavement history stored in the Roadway Management System. These inputs feed the calculation of the future‑traffic Structural Number and the effective existing Structural Number, which together determine the required overlay thickness. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (p. 180)

Overlay design is governed by a suite of Federal Highway Administration and state‑level documents that prescribe minimum structural service life, traffic input data, calculation methods, material thickness minima, submission procedures, and special material requirements, while detailed specifications address construction practices and material performance for both flexible and rigid overlays [1][2]. The design must provide at least an eight‑year but no more than a twenty‑year structural service life [1]. Traffic inputs are required to be based on current average daily traffic, projected growth, truck percentage, and a full truck distribution, and these data are used to compute 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) following the average initial truck factors listed in Table 7.1 for both flexible and rigid pavements [1]. The future‑traffic structural number is calculated using the input values prescribed in Table 10.7, while the existing effective structural number is obtained either with the Component Method or the Non‑Destructive Testing Method as appropriate for the pavement type and available core data [1]. For Interstate overlays and for non‑Interstate projects where 18‑kip ESALs exceed five million, the subbase may be excluded from the structural evaluation unless a dedicated investigation confirms its condition, in accordance with Section 10.2 [1].

Material thickness requirements are set by tables that mandate a minimum of 2½ inches of binder course when an asphalt overlay is placed over cement‑concrete or brick pavement for the first time, and prescribe minimum wearing‑course thicknesses that vary with the calculated overlay structural number; required future‑traffic pavement thicknesses are taken from Table 10.8 and effective existing thicknesses are determined by the Condition Survey Method using guidance in Table 10.9 [1]. When designing an asphalt‑over‑asphalt or asphalt‑over‑portland‑cement‑concrete (PCC) overlay, the required pavement thickness for future traffic is also drawn from Table 10.8, and the DARWin PCC‑overlay method treats the existing asphalt layer as non‑structural support, requiring a new concrete thickness equal to that calculated value and classifying the overlay as structural [1]. Thin bonded concrete overlays on asphalt are subject to Publication 408 Section 541, which requires incorporation of macrofibers; the residual strength contribution of the fibers must be quantified according to ASTM C1609 and added to the plain‑concrete flexural strength for use in mechanistic‑empirical programs such as BCOA‑ME, ACPA Pavement Designer, or AASHTOWare ME, with typical design thicknesses ranging from three to six inches and fiber dosages of about 0.2 %–0.5 % by volume, and core testing is required to verify that the existing overlay does not need to be stripped before a new design is applied [1].

Construction specifications require that any unstable slabs or slab fragments that would result in non‑uniform support be stabilized or repaired in accordance with Sections 516, 679, and 681 before an interlayer can be placed, and shallow surface defects larger than four square inches by one inch must be filled with an approved patching material [2]. When a geotextile interlayer is used it must be laid flat on a cleaned surface, overlapped eight inches plus or minus two inches, limited to three layers, extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, and kept below a surface temperature of 120 °F before placement [2]. An asphalt interlayer must consist of a nominal one‑inch Superpave mix as required by Section 410.3, with joint faulting not exceeding half an inch prior to scratch‑course treatment, and joint layout follows panel‑size limits: aspect ratios may not exceed 1.5 and overall dimensions cannot be greater than eighteen times the overlay thickness; saw cuts must be spaced at least one‑third of the slab depth near edges and one‑quarter elsewhere [2]. Curing must begin within thirty minutes after bleed water has dissipated, and insulation may be removed only when ambient temperature exceeds forty degrees Fahrenheit or concrete strength surpasses 3,600 psi, with all procedures—including anchoring dowel baskets and transition construction—requiring approval by the Representative or District Materials Engineer [2].

Material provisions specify that a concrete paving jointed‑mortar‑facing (JMF) must be approved before placement, and for thin bonded overlays on asphalt‑surfaced pavements the concrete mix must incorporate synthetic or steel macrofibers sized to achieve the specified residual strength (f150) as determined by ASTM C1609‑12 testing at fourteen days; if the overlay thickness is less than three inches, No. 67 coarse aggregate must be used instead of No. 57 [2]. The mix must also meet a permeability performance target of low to negligible chloride ion penetration within fifty‑six days, demonstrated by AASHTO T 277 or T 358 testing, with moderate values permissible only if they remain within prescribed coulomb or resistivity limits [2]. For cement concrete overlays less than seven inches thick, a Class AA mix reinforced with virgin polypropylene fibers that comply with ASTM C1116 Type III 4.13 and the performance level described in ASTM C1018 is mandated; fibers must be one to two‑and‑a‑half inches long, have an aspect ratio no greater than 150, and be added in a quantity sufficient to achieve a residual strength ratio of twenty percent while not exceeding five pounds per cubic yard unless a field demonstration confirms workability [2]. Geotextile anchoring devices must be approved by the selected manufacturer for the specific project, and asphalt overlay mixes are to be designed using the Superpave procedures referenced in Section 410 and Bulletin 27 [2]. Administrative constraints include the completion and submission of Form D‑4332 for each resurfacing or overlay design to the Pavement Design and Analysis Unit for concurrence and FHWA approval [1].

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 451–462)

Design methods and criteria

Overlay design begins with a comprehensive traffic data collection that records current average daily traffic, projected growth rates, truck percentage and distribution, which are then used to calculate the daily 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) in accordance with Chapter 7 of the AASHTO guide [1]. The computed ESALs drive the selection among the seven overlay types supported by the DARWin software, which implements the 1993 AASHTO pavement design procedures, and when a bonded concrete overlay is required the mechanistic‑empirical BCOA‑ME method developed at the University of Pittsburgh is employed [1]. The existing structural capacity of the pavement is quantified either as an effective Structural Number for flexible pavements or as an effective deflection depth for rigid or composite sections, using one of three evaluation routes: a condition‑survey/component analysis, non‑destructive deflection testing with Falling Weight Deflectometer data, or a remaining‑life method based on historic traffic trends [1]. For the Component and Non‑Destructive Testing methods the depths of all layers and their structural coefficients from Table 9.3 are entered into DARWin, where the structural‑number equation of Section 9.8 (with drainage coefficients set to 1.0) yields the effective SN, while the Remaining Life method follows its own algorithmic approach [1]. Future traffic requirements are expressed as a design Structural Number by applying the average initial truck factors from Table 7.1 for flexible or rigid pavements to the ESAL estimate, and the overlay SN needed is obtained as the difference between this design SN and the effective existing SN [1]. DARWin’s thickness‑design module then selects a layer thickness that provides an actual SN equal to or greater than the target, using either the specified‑thickness routine or its optimization algorithm, after which the result is cross‑checked against the minimum‑thickness tables (e.g., Tables 10.7 and 10.8) and subbase inclusion guidance in Section 10.2 [1]. Specific overlay configurations follow this general workflow with additional nuances: an asphalt concrete overlay on a fractured Portland cement concrete slab uses the Component Method together with subbase considerations before applying Table 10.7 for thickness selection; an AC overlay over jointed plain concrete pavement proceeds through DARWin’s secondary screen that mirrors the rigid‑pavement process and references input values in Table 10.8; composite AC/PCC pavements first obtain a future‑traffic thickness from Table 10.8, then calculate an Effective Existing Thickness via the Condition Survey Method guided by Table 10.9 before DARWin determines the final overlay thickness; and when a PCC overlay is placed over existing asphalt, the asphalt layer is treated solely as support and the required PCC thickness equals the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic from Table 10.8 [1]. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays the macro‑fiber contribution is expressed as residual strength (f150) measured according to ASTM C1609; this value is added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength (feff = MOR + f150), and engineers use a Residual Strength Estimator spreadsheet to predict f150 before feeding feff into mechanistic‑empirical programs such as BCOA‑ME, ACPA Pavement Designer or AASHTOWare Pavement ME to compute the necessary overlay thickness [1]. Prior to design, the existing pavement is prepared by repairing any unstable slabs through concrete patching, slab stabilization, slabjacking, or replacement of a flexible base, after which the surface is swept and an interlayer is installed; the interlayer may be a geotextile laid flat with edges overlapped approximately eight inches (± two inches) and extending at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, while joints exhibiting faulting greater than half an inch receive a one‑inch nominal depth Superpave asphalt mix as the interlayer [2]. Concrete placement follows strict joint layout rules: panel aspect ratios should be as close to unity as feasible and not exceed 1.5, panel dimensions are limited to eighteen times the overlay thickness, saw cuts must be at least one‑third of the slab thickness near edges and one‑quarter elsewhere, surface temperature is kept below 120 °F, and the timing of these cuts is typically determined with FHWA’s HIPERPAV or an equivalent approved software [2]. For thin bonded concrete overlays the maturity method prescribed in PTM No. 640 provides a relationship that converts degree‑hours of cure to compressive strength, allowing prediction of strength development, while macro‑fibers are added at rates calculated to achieve the required residual strength f150 and verified by ASTM C1609‑12 testing after 14 days; the concrete mix is also designed to meet chloride ion penetration limits classified as low, very low, or negligible within a 56‑day curing period according to AASHTO T 277 or T 358, and if the overlay thickness is less than three inches the coarse aggregate gradation is changed from No. 57 to No. 67 [2]. Rigid pavement overlays employ the Superpave mixture‑design procedure and follow the standard construction guidelines for plant‑mixed asphalt fine‑graded courses outlined in Section 410 and Bulletin 27, whereas concrete overlays thinner than seven inches require Class AA cement concrete reinforced with virgin polypropylene fibers (monofilament or bundled) ranging from one to two‑and‑a‑half inches in length, an aspect ratio not exceeding 150, and a dosage selected to achieve a residual strength ratio R150,3 of twenty percent while remaining below the maximum allowable rate of five pounds per cubic yard unless field demonstration justifies higher amounts [2]. The completed design is documented on Form D‑4332 for review by the PDAU and FHWA [1].

Overlay designs are required to provide a structural service life that is no shorter than eight years and no longer than twenty years. The design process begins with the collection of the most reliable traffic information available, at minimum current average daily traffic, projected growth rates, and the proportion of trucks, which are then used to compute 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) employing the average initial truck factors listed in Table 7.1. In addition, any unstable concrete slabs must be repaired or stabilized before an interlayer is installed, ensuring a sound substrate for the overlay. When joint faulting exceeds one‑quarter inch, a geotextile interlayer may be placed, provided the fabric is laid flat with edges overlapped by approximately eight inches (allowable variation plus or minus two inches) and extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, and both the fabric and existing concrete surface are not hotter than 120 °F at placement. For overlays applied over cement‑concrete or brick substrates with asphalt materials, a minimum binder layer thickness of two‑and‑a‑half inches is mandated, and the wearing course must meet or exceed the minimum thickness prescribed for the selected mix. The appropriate overlay type is selected from the seven AASHTO‑defined categories in DARWin, and when applicable a bonded concrete overlay is specified using the BCOA‑ME mechanistic‑empirical procedure. Structural adequacy is established by first determining the Structural Number required to accommodate future traffic and then evaluating the effective existing Structural Number through the Component Method, preferably using core data or RMS history, with layer depths, structural coefficients and a drainage coefficient of 1.0 as inputs. Sub‑base contributions are omitted in the structural evaluation for Interstate facilities when projected 18‑kip ESALs exceed five million unless the sub‑base condition has been verified. The calculated Structural Number of the proposed overlay must be greater than the design Structural Number, thereby providing an inherent safety margin. For flexible‑pavement overlays the required thickness is derived from the difference between future‑traffic and existing effective Structural Numbers, while for rigid‑pavement situations such as jointed plain concrete or unbonded Portland cement concrete overlays the required thickness equals the pavement thickness computed for future traffic, referencing Table 10.8. In unbonded PCC overlays the existing asphalt layer is treated solely as support and contributes no direct structural capacity, classifying the overlay as a structural overlay. When fiber‑reinforced concrete is employed, the design must specify a residual strength (f150) between 100 psi and 225 psi, which is added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength for mechanistic‑empirical calculations. A bonded system may be used only if the underlying asphalt pavement is in fair to good condition; otherwise an unbonded configuration is required and fibers cannot alter that decision. Macro‑fibers are typically specified at a length of one to two‑and‑a‑half inches, with aspect ratios ranging from 30 to 100, incorporated at a volume fraction of roughly 0.2 % to 0.5 %, and the resulting overlay thickness generally falls within the three‑to‑six‑inch range. For overlays thinner than three inches the aggregate type is upgraded to No. 67 coarse aggregate in place of the standard No. 57, and macro‑fibers must be added at a rate sufficient to achieve the prescribed residual strength as determined from ASTM C1609‑12 testing at fourteen days using Table A data. The concrete mix must also satisfy chloride ion penetration limits, achieving a classification of Low, Very low, or Negligible after no more than fifty‑six days of curing according to AASHTO T277 or T358; if a moderate level is accepted, penetration may not exceed 2,800 coulombs nor violate the specified resistivity criteria. When Class AA cement concrete overlays are less than seven inches thick they must incorporate virgin polypropylene synthetic fibers that are monofilament or bundled monofilament, ranging from one to two‑and‑a‑half inches in length and possessing an aspect ratio of no more than 150, with a dosage not exceeding five pounds per cubic yard unless field testing demonstrates workability without clumping. The mix is proportioned to achieve a residual strength ratio of twenty percent, and all anchoring devices, geotextile products, and related techniques must receive approval from the selected manufacturer, thereby ensuring that the overlay meets the required safety factors and reliability thresholds. Panel geometry for concrete overlays is constrained so that the aspect ratio of length to width does not exceed 1.5 and no panel dimension may be larger than eighteen times the overlay thickness. Saw cuts are required to achieve a depth of at least one‑third of slab thickness within twelve inches of any edge, and elsewhere the cut depth must be no less than one‑quarter of the slab thickness. Dowel basket anchorage devices must limit vertical or horizontal movement to less than one‑quarter inch during concrete placement. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 451–462)

Structural section and dimensions

Overlay designs that employ Class AA cement concrete must follow a special requirement when the overlay thickness is less than 7 inches: the concrete has to be reinforced with polypropylene fibers that meet defined criteria. The synthetic fibers must be monofilament or bundled monofilament, ranging from 1.0 inch up to 2½ inches in length, with an aspect ratio no greater than 150, and they must be added in enough quantity to achieve a residual strength ratio of 20 percent while not exceeding 5 lb per cubic yard unless a field demonstration shows otherwise. No specific dimensions are provided for the base, subbase, treated‑soil, slab, shoulder, edge‑support, or other reinforcement elements. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 459)

The overlay thickness is set by the required interlayer depth—when an asphalt interlayer is used a nominal one‑inch Superpave mix is placed, while a geotextile debonding layer is overlapped 8 inches (±2 in) on each side and limited to three layers. Joint layout must keep panel dimensions no larger than eighteen times the concrete overlay thickness and maintain an aspect ratio of at most 1.5, with saw cuts at least D/3 near edges and D/4 elsewhere, and joints are spaced according to the approved plan or a department‑approved layout that avoids wheel paths. Transitions are built at the overlay limits; where the transition rides on existing treated base or concrete, a geotextile debonding layer is installed as specified. Other design elements such as cross‑slope and tie‑in details are not covered in this section. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 460–462)

Materials and mixture design

Overlay design begins by specifying a repair patching material, which may be an asphalt‑based or cementitious pavement repair mix that must receive approval from the responsible authority before placement. [2] The primary overlay material is then identified; for asphalt overlays the engineer selects among leveling, wearing, or base‑course mixes based on the highway’s average daily traffic using suitability tables that define appropriate applications for each class. [1] Once the asphalt mix type is chosen, the design prescribes a layer thickness that must fall within the minimum and maximum limits established for that material, and when the existing surface consists of cement concrete or brick a binder layer of at least two‑and‑a‑half inches is required to achieve adequate bonding. [1] The specification also calls for the Superpave mixture size that corresponds to the selected course type—scratch or leveling—and to the prescribed thickness as indicated in the thickness‑mixture tables. [1] If an interlayer is employed, a geotextile may be placed flat on the cleaned existing surface with its edges overlapped by approximately eight inches (± 2 in), limited to three layers and extending at least four inches beyond the pavement edge while maintaining a surface temperature below 120 °F; alternatively, an asphalt interlayer of nominal one‑inch depth using a Superpave mix design may be applied. [2] For overlays thicker than six inches a curing compound must be applied at a minimum rate of one gallon per 150 ft² (or per 125 ft² for thinner sections). [2] When the overlay material is Portland cement concrete, the mixture shall incorporate synthetic or steel macrofibers in an amount sufficient to achieve the required residual strength f₁₅₀ as determined by ASTM C1609 testing. [1][2] The design typically requires a fiber dosage ranging from 0.2 % to 0.5 % by volume, and for Class AA cement concrete overlays thinner than seven inches the specification calls for 100 % virgin polypropylene fibers that are monofilament or bundled monofilament, one to two‑and‑a‑half inches long with an aspect ratio not greater than 150. [1][2] The fiber content is further limited to a maximum of five pounds per cubic yard unless a field demonstration confirms that the mix remains workable and free of fiber clumping, and the fibers must provide a residual strength ratio of approximately twenty percent according to the Illinois Modified ASTM C1609 procedure. [2] For concrete overlays less than three inches thick the coarse aggregate size shall be No. 67 rather than No. 57 to satisfy gradation requirements. [2] The concrete mixture is also required to meet permeability criteria; after no more than fifty‑six days of curing the chloride ion penetration must be classified as Low, Very low, or Negligible, and if a Moderate rating is permitted the penetration may not exceed 2 800 coulombs per AASHTO T 277 or must satisfy the resistivity limits specified in AASHTO T 358. [2]

Overlay design begins with the collection of traffic characteristics such as average daily traffic, projected growth, and truck percentage, which are entered into suitability tables that match each traffic level to an appropriate asphalt material class—leveling, base, or wearing course [1]. Once the class is identified, a mixture whose gradation follows the Superpave size recommendations for that course is selected, and a layer thickness is chosen within prescribed minimum‑and‑maximum limits; the wearing course must meet its stated minimum thickness and when placed over cement concrete or brick at least 2½ inches of binder material are required [1]. The prescribed overlay thickness also dictates aggregate size: if the depth is less than three inches, No. 67 coarse aggregate replaces the coarser No. 57 to ensure adequate stability in thin layers [2]. For concrete overlays, structural capacity and crack‑control performance are addressed by targeting a residual flexural strength (f150) between 100 and 225 psi; this target guides selection of macro‑fiber type and dosage [1][2]. Typical fiber contents range from 0.2 to 0.5 percent by volume for asphalt overlays, while concrete overlays use virgin polypropylene fibers dosed not to exceed five pounds per cubic yard unless a field demonstration shows acceptable workability, and the dosage is adjusted so that the residual strength ratio reaches about twenty percent [1][2]. Thin concrete overlays up to seven inches thick are required to be Class AA cement concrete reinforced with 100 % virgin polypropylene fibers of one to two‑and‑a‑half inch length and an aspect ratio limited to 150, complying with ASTM C1116 specifications [2]. Because fiber addition can reduce slump by up to four inches, water‑reducing admixtures and, when needed, adjustments to air‑entraining agents are incorporated to preserve workability and finishability [1]. Durability is ensured by designing the concrete mix to achieve low, very‑low, or negligible chloride‑ion penetration after no more than fifty‑six days of curing, verified with AASHTO T 277 or T 358, where measured charge must not exceed 2,800 coulombs and electrical resistivity must stay above 15.6 kΩ·cm for 4×8‑inch cylinders or 12.3 kΩ·cm for 6×12‑inch cylinders [2]. Trial batches are prepared to confirm that fresh‑property limits such as slump, air content, and residual strength targets are satisfied before final acceptance; the results are documented in pre‑submission testing and approved through a concrete paving job‑mix formula prior to placement [1][2]. By integrating traffic‑driven material classification, thickness constraints, aggregate selection, gradation standards, fiber dosage, workability adjustments, and performance‑based durability testing, the overlay design ensures that the selected materials and their proportions meet structural, durability, constructability, and overall performance requirements [1][2].

Structural details and interfaces

Overlay design starts with repair of unstable existing slabs before placing an interlayer. [2] A geotextile or asphalt interlayer is installed extending beyond the intended overlay edges to provide the required overlap. [2] Existing dowel‑bar assemblies are retained and must be fastened so that movement does not exceed one‑quarter inch vertically or horizontally; original shipping wires remain in place unless a magnetic tomography device is used for verification. [2] All transverse expansion joints are identified, precut, and full‑depth saw cuts are made—at least one‑third of the pavement thickness near the edge and at least one‑fourth elsewhere—to create new transverse and longitudinal joints that are later sealed. [2] Saw‑cut contraction joints must be placed at the proper depth and timing so that macrofibers can activate early and maintain load transfer across the joint; the fibers enhance flexural capacity but do not replace dowel bars where faulting control is required. [1][2] The resulting panels are laid out with an aspect ratio as close to 1.0 as feasible and never exceeding 1.5, and joint spacing follows the approved layout. [2] At the limits of the concrete overlay transitions are constructed; where a treated base course or existing concrete lies directly beneath, a geotextile debonding layer is placed in accordance with specifications. [2] Reinforcement depends on overlay thickness: for overlays thinner than three inches the mix must contain synthetic or steel macro‑fibers dosed to achieve the target residual strength (f150) as determined by ASTM C1609‑12 testing, and No. 67 coarse aggregate replaces No. 57. [2] For overlays less than seven inches deep virgin polypropylene fibers are used instead of traditional steel reinforcement; these monofilament or bundled fibers are 1.0–2.5 inches long, have an aspect ratio not exceeding 150, and are added to yield a residual strength ratio of about twenty percent with a maximum dosage of five pounds per cubic yard unless field testing confirms workability. [2] The concrete mix must also meet chloride‑ion penetration limits classified as Low, Very Low, or Negligible after no more than 56 days of curing according to AASHTO T277 or T358. [2] Whether the overlay is bonded or unbonded is decided primarily by the condition of the underlying pavement rather than the presence of fibers. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 451–462)

Overlay design begins by stabilizing the existing pavement surface, repairing any unstable slabs and patching all cracks larger than four square inches and deeper than one inch before any interlayer is placed [2]. After cleaning the repaired surface, a geotextile debonding layer may be installed to control bond conditions, laid flat with its edges overlapped approximately eight inches (± 2) and extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, limited to three layers and kept free of debris and moisture while traffic is excluded [2]. Where a thin asphalt interlayer is preferred, a one‑inch thick Superpave mix is placed after ensuring surface temperature does not exceed 120 °F [2]. The choice between bonded and unbonded concrete overlays depends on the condition of the existing asphalt as determined by core sampling; if cores show a fair‑to‑good surface without stripping, a bonded overlay can be designed, whereas a poor or deteriorated surface leads to an unbonded configuration [1]. The presence of macrofibers in the new concrete does not alter this decision and cannot convert an unbonded layout into a bonded one [1]. For bonded overlays, thickness and structural capacity are obtained using the bonded‑concrete‑overlay‑of‑asphalt mechanistic‑empirical (BCOA‑ME) procedure because conventional AASHTO methods do not account for the bond between new concrete and existing pavement [1]. Unbonded overlays treat the underlying asphalt as a non‑structural bearing layer, so the required concrete thickness equals the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic calculated with rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors from Table 7.1, ensuring the overlay alone provides the needed structural capacity [1]. When asphalt is placed over cement‑concrete or brick pavement for the first time, a minimum of 2½ inches of binder material must be provided to achieve adequate support [1]. Dowel basket assemblies used at joints are fastened so that vertical and horizontal movement does not exceed one quarter inch [2]. Expansion or contraction joints are precut and later sawed to depths of at least one‑third the slab thickness near edges, with joint spacing and panel aspect ratios kept close to unity (not exceeding 1.5) and panels sized no larger than eighteen times the overlay thickness; these measures promote load transfer efficiency early in service and reduce premature cracking [2][1]. At the overlay limits where the underlying base or concrete has been treated, transitions are constructed using a geotextile debonding layer to accommodate differences in material behavior [2]. Proper compaction at locations where the overlay meets curbs or other joints is essential, and these joints must be sealed against infiltration to preserve interface integrity [1].

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Overlay design must protect drainage by terminating any geotextile interlayer in a drainable layer or daylighted location and ensuring the geotextile remains free to discharge water. Moisture and temperature control are required during placement: the fabric surface may not exceed 120°F, it should be kept damp but not saturated, and asphalt interlayers must have their temperature limited by misting, white‑pigmented curing compound, or other approved methods. Climate considerations include providing insulating blankets when air temperatures are at or below 40°F, removing them only after the concrete reaches sufficient strength or ambient temperatures rise above that threshold, and limiting any temperature change of the concrete surface to no more than 40°F per hour. Overlay design must limit panel geometry so that each concrete panel’s aspect ratio is close to one and never exceeds 1.5, and no panel may be larger than eighteen times the overlay thickness; longitudinal joints are prohibited in wheel paths and joint spacing must follow the project plans or a department‑approved layout when not shown. Surface temperatures of geotextile or concrete before placement cannot exceed 120 °F, and saw cuts for creating overlay joints must be at least one‑third of slab depth near edges (and one‑quarter elsewhere) to ensure proper joint performance. Verification and expected performance

The overlay design is verified by submitting the concrete paving job‑mix formulas at least two weeks before placement for approval by the District Materials Engineer/Manager, confirming that all material specifications are met. The design must select No. 67 coarse aggregate when the overlay thickness is under three inches and incorporate macrofibers at a dosage that achieves the required residual strength (f150), demonstrated by ASTM C1609‑12 testing at 14 days. Mix‑design acceptance also requires the concrete to be designed for low or negligible chloride ion penetration after no more than 56 days of curing, with any moderate values limited to the thresholds set in AASHTO T 277 and T 358. These checks ensure compliance, constructability, performance, and alignment with project requirements. [2]

The overlay design using fiber-reinforced concrete expects to deliver a longer serviceable life by adding structural capacity and limiting distress such as cracking; the macrofibers reduce crack widths, keep joint or crack load‑transfer efficiency high, and thus improve durability. These benefits are quantified through laboratory slab tests that record increases in flexural and ultimate load capacity and in load‑transfer efficiency, and through residual‑strength testing (f150) performed according to ASTM C1609, which is added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength used in mechanistic‑empirical design programs.
