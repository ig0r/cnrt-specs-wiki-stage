Overlay Design

Part of: Overlay is-subtopic-of
Overlay Design is a topic under Overlay.
overlay-designoverlay-designsoverlay-design-methodpavement-overlay-design
Created 2026-10-27 · Updated 2026-10-29

Overlay design is the engineering process used to add a new structural layer over an existing pavement—whether flexible asphalt or rigid concrete—when that pavement no longer provides adequate structural support, ride quality, surface friction, or crack‑control for safe and economical service under projected traffic loads[1][2]. The design quantifies future traffic demand in ESALs, computes a required future‑traffic Structural Number (SN_f) and an effective existing SN (SN_eff), then sizes the overlay thickness so that the overlay’s SN meets or exceeds the difference between SN_f and SN_eff, ensuring the new layer alone can carry the anticipated loads without relying on a deteriorated base[1]. Depending on whether an asphalt or cement‑concrete overlay is selected, the procedure also specifies material requirements—such as a minimum 2½‑inch binder for asphalt over concrete, use of No. 67 coarse aggregate for thin concrete overlays, incorporation of macrofibers to achieve a residual strength (f150) of at least twenty percent, and chloride‑ion penetration limits—to provide an eight‑to‑twenty‑year structural service life while improving safety, ride comfort, and durability[1][2].

PennDOT publications covering “Overlay Design” also include the following subtopics: Overlay Thickness.

Overlay Design utilizes Overlay Thickness as the primary dimensional output that quantifies the required structural capacity for new rigid or flexible layers. [1][2] The thickness parameter dictates specific geometric constraints, such as limiting panel dimensions to eighteen times the layer depth with an aspect ratio not exceeding 1.5, while also governing joint-cutting depths and minimum material strength criteria like a 3,600 psi compressive strength. [2]

Purpose and design objectives

Overlay design is called for when an existing pavement no longer provides the structural support, ride quality, surface friction, or crack‑control performance required for safe and economical service under projected traffic loads. [1] The underlying engineering problem may appear as insufficient effective structural capacity, low‑friction values, excessive cracking that reduces load‑transfer efficiency, or a combination of these deficiencies that threaten the pavement’s remaining service life. [1] In addition, when the existing concrete slab exhibits non‑uniform support because of unstable slabs or visible movement under traffic, the overlay must address this instability to prevent premature failure. [2] The design therefore targets restoration of structural capacity, improvement of safety and ride comfort, extension of service life, and mitigation of durability concerns such as corrosion of thin concrete overlays. [1][2] To quantify the need, designers first calculate the structural number (SN) required for future traffic based on current volumes, growth forecasts, and truck percentages, then determine the effective existing SN using appropriate evaluation methods for the pavement type and condition. [1] The overlay thickness is sized so that the difference between the future‑traffic SN and the existing effective SN is satisfied, ensuring that the calculated overlay SN exceeds the design SN for structural adequacy. [1] For rigid pavements, the required asphalt or concrete overlay thickness is derived from projected ESAL values using average initial truck factors, and the overlay must be capable of carrying the anticipated loads without relying on direct contribution from a deteriorated base layer. [1] When a thin concrete overlay less than three inches thick is specified, the mix design must use No. 67 coarse aggregate rather than No. 57 to provide adequate strength. [2] Sufficient macro‑fiber must be added to achieve the required residual strength value (f150) as prescribed in the design tables. [2] The concrete mixture also has to meet a chloride ion penetration rating of Low, Very Low, or Negligible within 56 days, verified by AASHTO T 277 or T 358, to ensure durability against corrosion. [2] Prior to placing the overlay, any unstable slabs or slab fragments that would cause non‑uniform support must be stabilized or repaired, and surface defects larger than four square inches in area and deeper than one inch must be filled and compacted to a flat, tight condition. [2] All loose debris must be removed from the pavement surface using approved sweeping methods such as a power broom or vacuum truck to provide a smooth load‑bearing riding surface. [2] The resulting overlay is intended to provide at least an eight‑year structural design life but not exceed twenty years, thereby restoring strength, improving safety and ride comfort, limiting crack widths, maintaining joint or slab load‑transfer efficiency, and extending the pavement’s serviceable lifespan. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 463–471)

Overlay design must re‑establish the pavement’s functional capacity to support present and anticipated traffic loads while delivering a smooth, safe riding surface[1][2]. Structurally it is required to provide load‑bearing strength calculated for future traffic, with overlay thickness set equal to the pavement thickness derived from ESAL analysis that uses rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors; the existing asphalt layer is treated only as a supporting base and no direct structural contribution is assumed[1]. In addition, the concrete mix must contain macro‑ or polypropylene fibers sufficient to achieve a residual strength value f150 (R150,3) of at least twenty percent as measured in an Illinois‑modified ASTM C1609 test after fourteen days, and fiber dosage shall not exceed five pounds per cubic yard unless a field demonstration proves the mixture remains workable and free of clumping; fibers must be monofilament or bundled monofilament one to two‑and‑a‑half inches long with an aspect ratio no greater than 150[2]. The design life of the overlay shall be at least eight years and not exceed twenty years, providing durability through control of crack development, maintaining joint and crack load‑transfer efficiency, limiting joint openings generally to less than one millimeter (often specified between 0.5 and 1.0 mm), and reducing slab movement, misalignment, plastic shrinkage and crack widening[1]. To satisfy durability the mixture must limit chloride ion penetration to low, very low, or negligible levels within a fifty‑six‑day curing period, with total charge measured by AASHTO T 277 not exceeding 2,800 coulombs (or equivalent resistance values in T 358)[2]. For thin overlays less than three inches thick the coarse aggregate shall be No. 67 rather than No. 57 to ensure proper performance[2]. By addressing low‑friction conditions and preserving ride quality, the overlay also meets safety objectives, while the specified fiber content and workability requirements further support safe construction and long‑term performance[1][2]. Collectively these criteria preserve serviceability over an extended period, keeping the pavement functional, structurally sound, durable, safe, and comfortable for users[1].

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 463–470)

Scope and applicability

Overlay design is employed whenever an existing pavement—whether a flexible asphalt surface or a rigid concrete deck—exhibits inadequate ride quality, structural deficiency, or low‑friction values, with the intended service life of the new layer generally ranging from eight to twenty years. [1][2] The approach is applicable to the full spectrum of pavement structures, including asphalt‑concrete pavements, Portland‑cement‑concrete slabs—both intact and fractured—and composite AC/PCC systems. [1] Seven standard overlay configurations are recognized, ranging from an asphalt overlay on existing asphalt to various bonded or unbonded concrete overlays on concrete or composite decks. [1] When asphalt is placed for the first time over a cement‑or brick surface, a minimum binder thickness of two and one‑half inches must be provided. [1] For flexible pavements the overlay may consist of a plant‑mixed fine‑graded asphalt course designed in accordance with Superpave mixture procedures. [2] Rigid‑pavement overlays typically use a Class AA cement‑concrete mix, and if the required thickness is less than seven inches the concrete must be reinforced with polypropylene fibers meeting ASTM C1116, sized between one and two‑and‑a‑half inches in length, with an aspect ratio up to 150 and dosed at no more than five pounds per cubic yard. [2] Thin bonded concrete overlays placed over asphalt‑surfaced pavements that are three inches or less in depth must incorporate No. 67 coarse aggregate rather than No. 57 and be reinforced with synthetic or steel macrofibers added at a rate sufficient to achieve the prescribed residual strength value (f150). [2] In an AC‑over‑AC situation the design calculates both the future‑traffic structural number and the effective existing structural number using either the Component Method or a non‑destructive testing approach based on core data or RMS history. [1] On Interstate facilities the subbase is excluded from the structural evaluation, whereas on non‑Interstate routes it may also be omitted when the 18‑kip ESAL design value exceeds five million unless a subbase investigation has verified its condition. [1] When the existing pavement is a cracked, settled, or rubblized concrete slab—such as Jointed Plain Concrete Pavement or Jointed Reinforced Concrete Pavement—the AC‑overlay of fractured PCC slab method is employed, with overlay thickness selected from the appropriate table using flexible‑pavement average initial truck factors. [1] If a rigid concrete pavement currently carries an asphalt layer that will be removed before placing a new AC overlay, the rigid‑pavement procedure is followed, employing rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors for ESAL calculations. [1] For concrete roadways already overlaid with sound asphalt, a new asphalt layer can be placed without removing the prior one by using the AC‑overlay of an AC/PCC composite method; if material deficiencies are suspected, cores must be taken and the existing overlay removed before applying the AC‑over‑PCC design. [1] An unbonded PCC overlay on an asphalt pavement provides additional structural capacity without relying on the underlying asphalt for load bearing, and its required thickness equals the calculated pavement thickness for future traffic, making it a purely structural overlay. [1] Fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays are appropriate when the existing surface is asphalt or composite and is in fair to good condition—allowing a bonded overlay—or when it is deteriorated, in which case an unbonded overlay may be used; such overlays typically range from three to six inches thick and contain synthetic or steel macrofibers at 0.2 %–0.5 % by volume to improve load‑transfer efficiency and crack control. [1][2] Overall, overlay design is suitable for projects that require the addition of a new structural layer to extend service life, improve ride quality, or address low‑friction conditions across both flexible and rigid pavement types, using the material specifications and thickness criteria outlined above. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 463–470)

The choice of an alternative design approach over a conventional overlay design is dictated by several limitations related to the type, condition and interaction of the existing pavement. When a concrete layer is intended to be bonded directly to an existing asphalt or to an asphalt‑concrete composite, the traditional AASHTO overlay procedure cannot be used because it ignores the bond; in those situations the mechanistic‑empirical BCOA‑ME method must replace the standard design. Likewise, if the underlying concrete slab is severely cracked or faulted, a bonded Portland‑cement‑concrete overlay would be inappropriate and a different rehabilitation technique should be selected, since the suitability of any overlay hinges on the pavement’s existing type and condition. The AC‑Overlay‑of‑JPCP method is limited to jointed plain, jointed reinforced, or continuously reinforced concrete slabs; it cannot be applied when the base is a flexible pavement or when an existing asphalt overlay is to remain in place, because the procedure assumes that any asphalt layer will be stripped before the new asphalt overlay is placed. When the retained asphalt overlay shows material problems or deficiencies, complete removal of that layer is required and the design must follow the AC Overlay of PCC Pavement method rather than the AC Overlay of AC/PCC method. An unbonded Portland‑cement‑concrete overlay treats the existing asphalt solely as a support and therefore demands a full future‑traffic thickness; if the underlying asphalt can contribute structural capacity and a thinner, more economical overlay is desired, an approach that accounts for that contribution becomes preferable. Finally, the decision between bonded and unbonded overlays depends primarily on the condition of the underlying pavement: a fair‑to‑good surface permits a bonded design, whereas a poor or deteriorated surface—especially one exhibiting stripping—calls for an unbonded overlay, regardless of fiber reinforcement. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188)

Design inputs and requirements

Overlay design for an existing AC pavement requires traffic inputs such as future loadings expressed in ESALs using average initial truck factors, material data that include the thickness of each existing layer and its structural coefficient, and geotechnical information about subbase condition and drainability (often omitted from the structural evaluation under specified conditions). Existing‑condition data are obtained from pavement cores or, if cores are unavailable, from the pavement history stored in the Roadway Management System. These inputs feed the calculation of the future‑traffic Structural Number and the effective existing Structural Number, which together determine the required overlay thickness. [1]

[1] — Ch. 10 (p. 180)

Overlay design is governed by a suite of FHWA, state‑level, and district‑level specifications that prescribe minimum structural service life, traffic input data, calculation methods, material thickness minima, submission procedures, and special material requirements [1][2]. The design must provide at least an eight‑year but no more than twenty‑year structural service life [1]. Traffic inputs are required to be based on current average daily traffic, projected growth, truck percentage, and a full truck distribution, and these data are used to compute 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) following the average initial truck factors listed in Table 7.1 [1]. The future‑traffic structural number is calculated using the input values prescribed in Table 10.7, while the existing effective structural number is obtained either with the Component Method or the Non‑Destructive Testing Method, as appropriate for the pavement type and available core data [1]. For Interstate overlays, and for non‑Interstate projects where 18‑kip ESALs exceed five million, the subbase may be excluded from the structural evaluation unless a dedicated investigation confirms its condition, in accordance with Section 10.2 [1]. Material thickness requirements mandate a minimum of 2½ inches of binder course when an asphalt overlay is placed over cement‑concrete or brick pavement for the first time, and prescribe minimum wearing‑course thicknesses that vary with the calculated overlay structural number using Table 10.8; when designing an asphalt‑over‑asphalt or asphalt‑over‑PCC overlay, the required future‑traffic pavement thickness is taken from Table 10.8 and the existing effective thickness is determined by the Condition Survey Method using guidance in Table 10.9 [1]. In the DARWin PCC‑overlay method the existing asphalt layer is treated as non‑structural support, so the new concrete thickness must equal the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic indicated in Table 10.8, and the overlay is classified as a structural overlay [1]. Thin bonded concrete overlays on asphalt are subject to Publication 408 Section 541, which requires incorporation of macrofibers; the residual strength contribution of the fibers (f150) must be quantified according to ASTM C1609 and added to the plain‑concrete flexural strength for use in mechanistic‑empirical programs such as BCOA‑ME, ACPA Pavement Designer or AASHTOWare ME, with typical design thicknesses ranging from three to six inches and fiber dosages of about 0.2 %–0.5 % by volume, and core testing is required to verify that the existing overlay does not need to be stripped before a new design is applied [1]. Administrative constraints include the completion and submission of Form D‑4332 for each resurfacing or overlay design to the Pavement Design and Analysis Unit for concurrence and FHWA approval [1]. At the district level, concrete paving job‑mix forms must be submitted at least fourteen days in advance, reviewed by the District Materials Engineer or Manager, and receive the Representative’s sign‑off before any Portland cement concrete placement [2]. The overlay is required to be Class AA Portland cement concrete, and when the maturity method is employed the degree‑hours of cure are correlated to compressive strength according to PTM No. 640 at eight hours, one day, seven days, twenty‑eight days and fifty‑six days [2]. If the designed thickness is less than three inches, the coarse aggregate specification changes from No. 57 to No. 67 and macrofibers are added in a quantity that achieves the design residual strength (f150) as determined by ASTM C1609‑12 testing at fourteen days using the fiber‑volume relationship given in Table B [2]. Before any interlayer is placed, all unstable slabs or slab fragments that could cause non‑uniform support must be stabilized or repaired, surface defects are to be patched with an asphalt‑based, cementitious, or otherwise approved repair material, and the existing pavement surface must then be swept clean by an approved method such as a power broom or vacuum truck [2]. When a geotextile interlayer is specified it is laid flat on the cleaned surface and its edges overlapped by eight inches plus or minus two inches [2]. Rigid‑pavement overlays follow the Superpave mixture design procedures set out in Section 410 and Bulletin 27, while concrete overlays are governed by the Class AA cement‑concrete requirements of Section 501 Table A; overlays thinner than seven inches must incorporate 100 % virgin polypropylene fibers that meet ASTM C1116 Type III 4.13 (referencing ASTM C1018) and satisfy Performance Level 1, with the mix attaining a residual strength ratio of at least twenty percent according to the Illinois‑modified ASTM C1609, and fiber dosage may not exceed five pounds per cubic yard unless a field demonstration proves that workability is maintained [2].

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 463–471)

Design methods and criteria

Overlay design begins with any required pre‑overlay repairs such as concrete patching, slab jacking, flexible‑base replacement or shallow repairs to stabilize unstable slabs; the repaired surface is then swept clean by an approved method before a separation layer is installed, which may consist of a geotextile placed flat on the pavement with edges overlapped by eight inches plus or minus two inches or a nominal one‑inch Superpave asphalt interlayer applied within prescribed temperature limits [2]. Traffic data collection follows, capturing current average daily traffic, projected growth rates, truck percentage and distribution, which are used to compute daily 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads (ESALs) in accordance with Chapter 7 of the AASHTO guide [1]. The computed ESALs drive the selection of one of the seven overlay types supported by the DARWin software, which implements the 1993 AASHTO pavement design procedures; when a bonded concrete overlay is required, the mechanistic‑empirical BCOA‑ME method developed at the University of Pittsburgh is employed [1].

The existing structural capacity of the pavement is then quantified. Three evaluation routes are available: a condition‑survey/component analysis that inventories distress and layer thicknesses, non‑destructive deflection testing using Falling Weight Deflectometer data to derive in‑situ stiffness, or a remaining‑life method based on historic traffic trends (the latter is not used for departmental designs) [1]. Both the Component Method and the Non‑Destructive Testing Method require input of each layer’s depth and its structural coefficient from Table 9.3; these values are entered into DARWin where the structural‑number equation of Section 9.8, with drainage coefficients set to 1.0, yields an effective Structural Number (SN_eff) for flexible pavements or an effective deflection depth (D_eff) for rigid/composite sections [1].

Future traffic requirements are expressed as a design Structural Number (SN_f). In DARWin this is calculated by applying the average initial truck factors from Table 7.1—flexible‑pavement factors for flexible overlays and rigid‑pavement factors for rigid overlays—to the ESAL estimate, producing SN_f; the overlay SN needed is simply the difference between SN_f and the effective existing SN [1]. With the required SN established, DARWin’s thickness design module—either the specified‑thickness routine or the optimization algorithm—is used to select a layer thickness that provides an actual SN equal to or greater than the target. The resulting thickness is cross‑checked against the minimum‑thickness tables (e.g., Tables 10.7 and 10.8) and, where applicable, subbase inclusion guidance in Section 10.2 [1].

Specific overlay configurations follow the same general workflow with additional nuances. An asphalt concrete (AC) overlay on a fractured Portland cement concrete slab uses the Component Method together with subbase considerations from Section 10.2 to compute the existing effective SN before applying Table 10.7 for thickness selection. For an AC overlay over jointed plain concrete pavement, design proceeds through DARWin’s secondary screen that mirrors the rigid‑pavement workflow and references input values in Table 10.8. In composite AC/PCC pavements, the future‑traffic thickness is first obtained from Table 10.8; the existing contribution is then quantified as an Effective Existing Thickness via the Condition Survey Method guided by Table 10.9, after which DARWin determines the final overlay thickness. When a PCC overlay is placed over an existing asphalt pavement, the AC layer is treated solely as support and the required PCC thickness equals the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic from Table 10.8 [1].

Material selection for asphalt overlays follows the Superpave mixture design and standard plant‑mixed fine‑graded course construction procedures outlined in Section 410 and Bulletin 27, ensuring that the mix meets specified performance criteria [2]. Concrete overlays with a thickness less than seven inches employ Class AA cement concrete reinforced with virgin polypropylene fibers; the fibers must be monofilament or bundled monofilament, ranging from one to two‑and‑a‑half inches in length and not exceeding an aspect ratio of 150. Fiber dosage is calibrated to achieve a residual strength ratio (R150,3) of twenty percent as measured by the Illinois Modified ASTM C1609 test, with a maximum addition of five pounds per cubic yard unless field proof demonstrates acceptable workability [2].

Performance‑based models are incorporated into the design. When the maturity method is selected, degree‑hours of cure are correlated to compressive strength according to PTM No. 640 at intervals of eight hours, one day, seven days, twenty‑eight days and fifty‑six days. For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, macrofibers are added until the target residual strength value (f150) from Table B is reached; this is verified by a fourteen‑day ASTM C1609‑12 test that relates residual strength to fiber volume fraction for the chosen fiber type [2]. Durability requirements are addressed through permeability criteria: the concrete mix must attain a low, very low, or negligible chloride ion penetration within fifty‑six days as defined by AASHTO T 277 or T 358, with an upper limit of 2,800 coulombs (or the corresponding resistivity values) [2].

For fiber‑reinforced concrete overlays, the macro‑fiber contribution is expressed as residual strength (f150) measured according to ASTM C1609; this value is added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength (feff = MOR + f150). Engineers employ a Residual Strength Estimator spreadsheet to predict f150 based on pavement condition and overlay inputs, then feed feff into mechanistic‑empirical programs such as BCOA‑ME, ACPA Pavement Designer or AASHTOWare Pavement ME to compute the necessary overlay thickness [1][2]. The completed design is documented on Form D‑4332 for review by the PDAU and FHWA [1].

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 463–471)

Overlay designs must provide a structural service life between eight and twenty years. The design begins with collection of the most reliable traffic data, at minimum current average daily traffic, projected growth rates, and truck proportion, which are used to compute 18‑kip equivalent single axle loads using average initial truck factors from Table 7.1. For asphalt concrete overlays placed over cement‑concrete or brick substrates a binder layer of at least two‑and‑a‑half inches is required, and the wearing course must meet or exceed the minimum thickness prescribed for the selected mix. When the proposed overlay thickness is less than three inches, No. 67 coarse aggregate replaces No. 57, and macrofibers are added at a rate sufficient to achieve the design residual strength f150 as specified in Table B, with verification by ASTM C1609‑12 after fourteen days. For overlays thinner than seven inches, Class AA cement concrete reinforced with virgin polypropylene fibers is mandated; the fibers must be monofilament or bundled monofilament, between one and two‑and‑half inches long, have an aspect ratio not greater than 150, and be dosed to obtain at least a twenty percent residual strength ratio while not exceeding five pounds per cubic yard unless field demonstration confirms workability. The concrete mix must satisfy permeability criteria targeting a chloride ion penetration classification of Low, Very Low or Negligible after no more than fifty‑six days of curing in accordance with AASHTO T 277/T 358, and a Moderate rating is permissible only if the charge passed does not exceed 2,800 coulombs. All concrete paving joint mix formulas require approval from the District Materials Engineer before placement. Prior to overlay installation any unstable slabs must be stabilized and surface defects larger than four square inches and one inch deep must be patched. When a geotextile interlayer is used, its edges shall overlap by eight inches plus or minus two inches, extend at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, no more than three layers may be placed in any location, and the fabric temperature must remain below 120 °F before placement. Joint faulting greater than one‑quarter inch triggers geotextile placement, while faulting exceeding one‑half inch necessitates a scratch course or milling before an asphalt interlayer that is installed at a nominal depth of one inch. Structural adequacy is established by determining the required Structural Number (SN) for future traffic and comparing it with the effective existing SN obtained through the Component Method, using core data or RMS history, layer depths, structural coefficients and a drainage coefficient of 1.0; sub‑base contributions are omitted on Interstate facilities when projected 18‑kip ESALs exceed five million unless the sub‑base condition has been verified. The calculated SN of the proposed overlay must be greater than the design SN, providing an inherent safety margin. For flexible‑pavement overlays the required thickness is derived from the difference between future‑traffic SN and existing effective SN; for rigid‑pavement situations such as jointed plain concrete or unbonded Portland cement concrete overlays, the required thickness equals the pavement thickness computed for future traffic referencing Table 10.8, and in unbonded PCC overlays the existing asphalt layer is treated only as support and contributes no direct structural capacity, classifying the overlay as a structural overlay. When fiber‑reinforced concrete is employed, the design must specify a residual strength (f150) between 100 psi and 225 psi, which is added to the plain‑concrete modulus of rupture to obtain an effective flexural strength for mechanistic‑empirical calculations; fibers are typically macrofibers 1–2.5 inches long with aspect ratios of 30–100 incorporated at a volume fraction of roughly 0.2 % to 0.5 %, and the resulting overlay thickness generally falls within three to six inches. A bonded system may be used only if the underlying asphalt pavement is in fair to good condition; otherwise an unbonded configuration is required, and fibers cannot alter that decision. [1][2]

Materials and mixture design

Overlay design first requires the engineer to select the structural material that will constitute the new layer, choosing either an asphalt mix or a cementitious concrete based on the highway’s average daily traffic and the condition of the existing surface. For asphalt overlays the guides direct the selection among leveling, wearing, or base‑course mixes as indicated in suitability tables, and then prescribe a layer thickness that falls within the minimum and maximum limits established for the chosen material; when the underlying pavement is cement concrete or brick, at least 2½ inches of binder must be provided to achieve adequate bonding. The appropriate Superpave mixture size is also specified according to the selected course type—scratch or leveling—as shown in thickness‑mixture tables. When a cementitious overlay is required, the design calls for Class AA Portland‑cement concrete and may incorporate synthetic, steel, or polypropylene macrofibers that meet ASTM C1116 requirements; the coarse aggregate shall be No. 67 if the overlay thickness is under three inches, otherwise the standard aggregate size applies. Fiber dosage must be sufficient to attain a residual flexural strength (f150) defined by ASTM C1609, with typical volume contents ranging from 0.2 % to 0.5 % and a target residual‑strength ratio of about twenty percent, while not exceeding five pounds per cubic yard unless field testing justifies a higher amount. Fibers are required to be 100 % virgin polyethylene, monofilament or bundled, with lengths between one inch and two and a half inches and an aspect ratio no greater than 150. The concrete mixture must also satisfy permeability criteria, achieving a Low, Very Low, or Negligible chloride‑ion penetration rating after curing for fifty‑six days, or, if a Moderate rating is permitted, limiting the total charge to 2,800 coulombs as measured by AASHTO T 277. In addition, any required interlayer is detailed: a geotextile must be laid flat with edges overlapped by eight inches plus or minus two inches and no more than three layers in any location, while an asphalt interlayer, when specified, consists of a nominal one‑inch thick Superpave mix applied according to the relevant procedural bulletins. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 463–471)

Overlay design starts by gathering traffic characteristics—average daily traffic, projected growth, and truck percentage—and entering them into suitability tables that match each traffic level to an appropriate asphalt material class such as leveling, base, or wearing course. [1] The selected class then dictates the use of a Superpave mixture whose gradation follows the size recommendations for that particular course. [1][2] A nominal one‑inch layer of this Superpave mix, placed after any surface defects have been repaired with an approved asphalt‑based or cementitious patching material, provides the necessary structural capacity and durability for the overlay. [2] When a geotextile interlayer is incorporated, it is laid flat on the cleaned pavement with its edges overlapped about eight inches (± 2 in) and extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge to ensure proper load transfer, drainage, and constructability. [2] The required overlay thickness for asphalt courses must fall within the minimum‑and‑maximum limits given in the tables, and the wearing course must always meet its stated minimum thickness; additionally, when the overlay is placed over cement concrete or brick, at least 2½ inches of binder material are mandated. [1] For concrete overlays the design targets a residual flexural strength (f150) typically between 100 psi and 225 psi, which drives the selection of macro‑fiber type and dosage. [1][2] Fiber content is generally specified in the range of 0.2 percent to 0.5 percent by volume, corresponding to a maximum dosage of five pounds per cubic yard unless field testing demonstrates acceptable workability and no fiber clumping. [1][2] Fibers may be synthetic monofilaments or bundled monofilaments with lengths from one inch to two‑and‑a‑half inches and an aspect ratio not greater than 150, or virgin polypropylene fibers meeting the same geometry limits. [2] The dosage is calculated to achieve a residual strength ratio of 20 percent (R150,3) as required by the Illinois Modified ASTM C1609, thereby attaining the target f150 value. [2] When concrete overlays are thinner than three inches, No. 67 coarse aggregate is used instead of No. 57 to facilitate placement, and any overlay less than seven inches must be a Class AA cement concrete reinforced with the specified fibers. [2] Durability requirements are satisfied by limiting chloride ion penetration; the cured concrete must exhibit low, very low, or negligible permeability after 56 days as measured by AASHTO T 277 or T 358, and when moderate penetration is allowed it shall not exceed 2 800 coulombs in the AASHTO T 277 test. [2] Because fiber addition can reduce slump by up to about four inches, water‑reducing admixtures and, where necessary, adjustments to air‑entraining agents are employed to preserve workability and finishability of the fresh concrete mix. [1] Trial batches are prepared to verify that both the fresh‑property limits and the residual strength target are met before full production is authorized. [1]

Structural details and interfaces

Overlay design begins with a uniform condition of the existing concrete joints; any joint exhibiting more than a quarter‑inch of faulting is milled, and when an asphalt interlayer is planned joints whose fault exceeds half an inch are either provided with a scratch course or are milled and ground to reduce the irregularity [2]. A geotextile separation layer is then placed over the repaired surface, each sheet overlapping adjacent sheets by approximately eight inches (allowable variation plus or minus two inches) and extending at least four inches beyond the pavement edge; no more than three layers may overlap at any location, and the material must be secured according to the manufacturer’s instructions, kept free of debris, and protected from construction traffic until the overlay is placed [2]. When a concrete overlay incorporates macrofibers, saw‑cut contraction joints are required to be cut to the proper depth and at the appropriate time so that the fibers can become active early and contribute to load transfer across the joint; the fibers increase flexural capacity but do not replace dowel bars in joints where faulting control is needed [1]. For thin concrete overlays less than seven inches thick, reinforcement is provided by virgin polypropylene fibers complying with ASTM C1116, supplied as monofilament or bundled monofilament of one to two‑and‑a‑half inch length and an aspect ratio not greater than 150, placed in a dosage sufficient to achieve a residual strength ratio of about twenty percent but limited to five pounds per cubic yard unless field testing confirms workability [2]. The macrofibers act similarly to tie bars by maintaining load‑transfer efficiency under repeated loading, yet the design still calls for conventional dowel bar placement where fault control is required, indicating that fibers are not a substitute for standard load‑transfer devices [1]. Whether the overlay is bonded or unbonded to the existing pavement is determined primarily by the condition of the underlying surface rather than by the presence of fibers, and no additional special joints, transitions, or connections beyond those described are mandated by the guides [1][2].

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 186–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 470–471)

Overlay design begins with a core‑based evaluation of the existing asphalt pavement to determine its condition; if cores show a fair‑to‑good surface without stripping, a bonded concrete overlay may be pursued, whereas a poor or deteriorated surface leads to an unbonded configuration [1]. The presence of macrofibers in the new concrete does not alter this decision and cannot convert an unbonded layout into a bonded one [1]. When asphalt is placed for the first time over cement‑concrete or brick pavement, the design must provide at least 2½ inches of binder material to secure adequate support [1]. Prior to installing any overlay, unstable slabs are stabilized and surface defects repaired, after which loose debris is swept away to prepare a clean interface for the interlayer [2]. A separation layer is then introduced either as a geotextile or an asphalt interlayer depending on project requirements [1][2]. If a geotextile is selected, it is laid flat on the cleaned pavement with its edges overlapped by approximately eight inches (± 2) and extended at least four inches beyond the pavement edge, limited to three layers, secured according to manufacturer instructions, kept free of traffic, and allowed to maintain drainage [2]. When an asphalt interlayer is used, a nominal one‑inch depth of Superpave mix is placed, joints exhibiting faulting greater than half an inch receive either a scratch course or milling to reduce the faulting, and both the existing pavement and the fresh interlayer must be kept below 120 °F during placement [2]. At locations where the overlay meets curbs or other joints, extra care is required to achieve proper compaction and to seal the joint against infiltration [1]. For bonded overlays, thickness and structural capacity are derived using the bonded‑concrete‑overlay‑of‑asphalt mechanistic‑empirical (BCOA‑ME) procedure because conventional AASHTO methods do not account for the bond between new concrete and existing pavement [1]. In contrast, unbonded overlays treat the underlying asphalt as a non‑structural bearing layer, and the required concrete thickness is set equal to the Pavement Thickness for Future Traffic calculated from rigid‑pavement average initial truck factors, ensuring that the new concrete alone provides the necessary structural capacity [1]. The timing and depth of contraction‑joint saw cuts are specified so that the fiber‑reinforced overlay can develop load‑transfer efficiency early in service, thereby reducing the risk of premature cracking [1].

[1] — Ch. 10 (pp. 176–188); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Drainage, environment, and accessibility

Overlay design must protect drainage and moisture conditions by extending the geotextile edge beyond the pavement and terminating it in a drainable layer or daylighted location without obstructing its free flow, while keeping the fabric only damp—not saturated—before placing the overlay. The design also has to respect temperature limits, ensuring that the surface temperature of the underlying material does not exceed 120 °F at placement; if needed, misting, white‑pigmented curing compounds, lime‑water whitewashing, or other approved methods are used to control temperature and mitigate climate effects. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Overlay design must address geometric placement of any separation layer, requiring that a geotextile interlayer be laid flat with its edges overlapped by about eight inches (± 2 in) and limited to no more than three layers in any spot, while the free edge must extend at least four inches beyond the pavement margin and terminate in a drainable or daylighted condition. Safety considerations include protecting the interlayer from construction traffic—traffic should be avoided on the geotextile, sharp turns and braking minimized, and drainage not impaired—to preserve the integrity of the overlay during placement. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (p. 471)

Verification and expected performance

The completed overlay design is verified by first submitting the concrete paving job‑mix formulas at least two weeks before placement for District Materials Engineer/Manager approval; the mix must follow the design basis that calls for No. 67 coarse aggregate when the overlay thickness is under three inches, incorporates macrofibers at a dosage that achieves the required residual strength (f150) as demonstrated by ASTM C1609‑12 testing at 14 days, and meets the prescribed chloride ion penetration limits—either Low/Very Low/Never after up to 56 days cure per AASHTO T277/T358 or, if a Moderate rating is accepted, not exceeding 2,800 coulombs or the specified kΩ·cm values. These material‑specific criteria ensure compliance, constructability, performance, and alignment with project requirements. [2]
