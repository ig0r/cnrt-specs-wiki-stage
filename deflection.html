Deflection

Part of: Pavement Condition Assessment is-subtopic-of
Deflection is a topic under Pavement Condition Assessment.
deflectionsdeflectionslab-deflectionsdeflection-testsdeflection-data
Created 2026-10-28 · Updated 2026-10-28

Deflection testing is a non‑destructive measurement of surface bending or vertical movement that quantifies the structural condition of an existing concrete pavement slab and informs rehabilitation decisions [1]. In slab‑stabilization it involves measuring the loaded corner deflection—triggering corrective action when the value reaches 0.02 inch—and computing joint efficiency as the unloaded‑to‑loaded ratio, with a 65 % efficiency threshold used to decide between stabilization alone or patch‑and‑stabilize [2]. For fractured pavements a falling‑weight deflectometer applies a 9 000‑lb impact and records surface deflections at intervals of 50 feet or less, which are back‑calculated to an elastic modulus that must fall within the specified range before further fracturing proceeds; testing is permitted only when air temperature is between 45 °F and 90 °F and the subgrade is unfrozen [2].

Purpose and investigation objectives

Deflection testing is employed in concrete‑pavement restoration to assess the structural condition of the existing slab, focusing on joint performance and the need for slab stabilization [1]. The investigation should determine which joints require replacement and where slab‑stabilization measures are warranted [1]. In the slab‑stabilization specification (Section 679), the deflection investigation evaluates each joint and crack of a concrete pavement slab by measuring the loaded corner lift under traffic load [2]. It must measure the loaded slab‑corner deflection, verify whether that deflection is 0.02 inch or more, and calculate joint efficiency as the ratio of unloaded to loaded slab‑corner deflection expressed as a percentage [2]. The resulting joint efficiency is then compared to the 65 % threshold to decide if the joint meets the stabilization criteria [2]. In the slab‑fracturing specification (Section 522), the deflection investigation targets the fractured concrete layer of a rigid pavement and also assesses whether the underlying substrate is moving [2]. The study must answer whether vertical deflection observed under a 50‑ton pneumatic roller shows no movement of the substrate [2]. It must also determine whether falling‑weight deflectometer measurements taken with a 9,000‑pound drop weight at intervals of 50 feet or less produce an average elastic modulus for the fractured layer that falls within the range specified in Table A [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

In concrete pavement rehabilitation, deflection testing provides quantitative information that directly informs a range of engineering decisions [1]. During preliminary engineering, the non‑destructive measurement of surface bending should be used to identify which joints must be replaced and where slab stabilization should be performed, thereby defining the structural capacity of the existing concrete and shaping the rehabilitation scope, including the choice between overlay or other restoration alternatives [1]. The results also guide sizing of patching and spall repair so that the calculated quantities shall anticipate additional deterioration that will occur between the design field view and actual construction, and they support decisions on slab‑jacking needed to restore uniform support [1]. When excessive slab deflections are observed, indicating voids beneath the slab, engineers are directed to perform slab stabilization by filling those voids, which restores structural integrity and reduces the likelihood of future pumping, faulting, and cracking [1]. If no preliminary testing has been performed, the guidelines advise stabilizing at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints as a quantitative basis for maintenance planning [1]. The reduction in deflection achieved through stabilization informs subsequent design choices regarding additional reinforcement or redesign, while also guiding routine actions such as joint sealing and drainage upkeep to prevent further loss of support [1]. For load‑transfer units, the LVDT differential deflection measured during structural adequacy testing is limited to a maximum of 7.5 mil at 1 million cycles and may not increase by more than 2.5 mil when the test is extended to 10 million cycles; meeting these limits indicates that the dowel bar system is acceptable for design, permits planners to select compliant bar types, coatings or alternate shapes, and gives maintenance crews clear criteria for when rehabilitation or replacement may be required [2]. Deflection testing performed in accordance with ASTM D4695 provides quantitative data used to prioritize slab rehabilitation; any joint or crack that records a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more triggers stabilization or patching actions, with full stabilization applied when the measured joint efficiency is at least 65 % and patch‑and‑stabilize applied when it is less than 65 %, provided the representative approves, and the test results must be submitted to the Representative fifteen working days before work begins, shaping the field quality‑control plan, material selection and scheduling of slab‑stabilization operations [2]. Falling weight deflectometer measurements are used to back‑calculate the elastic pavement modulus of a fractured concrete layer, and the calculated modulus must fall within the range specified in Table A before production fracturing can continue; if the average modulus is outside those limits the breaking pattern is adjusted and the section re‑tested, directly guiding rehabilitation design and equipment selection [2]. FWD data are collected at intervals of 50 feet or less using a drop weight of 9,000 pounds, and testing should not be performed when the pavement surface is frozen or when air temperature falls below 45°F or exceeds 90°F, ensuring reliable deflection results for planning and quality‑control decisions [2].

Scope and applicability

Deflection testing is appropriate for Portland cement concrete pavements that are being restored, where it is used to evaluate the condition of the existing slab, identify joints that should be replaced, and locate areas requiring slab stabilization [1]. It is also appropriate for concrete pavement repair actions such as patching and spall repair when those repairs are based on actual field measurements or deflection tests [1]. In addition, deflection testing may be employed to assess subbase or subgrade soil conditions beneath a concrete slab as part of the overall structural assessment [1]. For reinforced and plain cement concrete pavements, deflection testing is appropriate when evaluating load‑transfer units such as dowel bars at joints, provided that the Linear Variable Differential Transformer (LVDT) differential deflection measured under dynamic and static loading does not exceed 7.5 mil at 1 million cycles and does not increase more than 2.5 mil by 10 million cycles [2]. When evaluating joints or cracks for slab stabilization in concrete slab pavements, deflection testing is appropriate once the loaded slab corner deflection reaches 0.02 inch or greater together with the applicable joint‑efficiency criteria, and it may only be performed under ambient conditions where air temperature is between 40 °F and 70 °F, not during spring thaw and when the subgrade is unfrozen [2]. For rigid concrete pavements that are being rehabilitated by crack‑and‑seat or break‑and‑seat fracturing methods after the slab has been fully fractured and seated, deflection measurement with a falling‑weight deflectometer is appropriate to back‑calculate the elastic modulus of the fractured layer, but it may only be performed when the pavement surface is not frozen, air temperature is between 45 °F and 90 °F, and calibrated equipment meeting AASHTO T 256, ASTM D4694, D4695 and D5858 is used [2].

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

Deflection testing for slab joints and cracks is limited to conditions where the air temperature is between 40F and 70F and the subgrade is not frozen or in spring‑thaw, because testing is prohibited outside those limits. When these temperature or ground‑state constraints prevent a test, the specification requires visual verification of pumping or joint performance before deciding on slab stabilization, indicating that deflection alone cannot be relied upon under such conditions. Moreover, the decision to stabilize also depends on a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more together with a joint efficiency at 65%, so joint efficiency serves as a complementary metric when evaluating pavement condition. [2]

Existing information and records

To evaluate deflection, the review should start with existing condition records that quantify visible distress, core logs taken near deteriorated joints and at random locations describing subbase or subgrade soil conditions, and any prior non‑destructive test results such as deflection tests and faulting measurements used to locate joints for replacement or slab stabilization. Additional performance data that inform the pavement’s ability to be rehabilitated—percent patching, concrete compressive strength, air content, observed durability problems, subgrade condition, friction adequacy, and ride quality—should also be gathered from past surveys and test reports. [1]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84)

In the concrete pavement restoration guidelines, any lack of recent deflection data creates a gap in the records that must be filled by field investigation. Because the quantity of repair work shall anticipate additional deterioration that will occur between the design field view and construction, there is uncertainty about how much further damage will develop after the existing documentation was prepared. The existing pavement condition is often described only qualitatively, leaving the exact quantities of visible distress unmeasured and therefore uncertain. Cores near deteriorated joints and at random locations are frequently missing, making subbase or subgrade soil conditions and depth of deterioration ambiguous. Without deflection (or faulting) results it is unclear which joints require replacement or where slab stabilization should be performed. The contract requires deflection data before stabilization, so any missing preliminary testing represents a record gap that must be closed by field investigation. Field investigators must measure loaded slab corner deflection at each transverse joint and crack when air temperature is between 40 °F and 70 °F and the subgrade is not frozen, in order to determine joint efficiency. Slabs that exhibit a loaded corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more and a joint efficiency of 65 % or more are identified for stabilization, creating a threshold that cannot be applied without measured data. When temperature limits prevent testing, visual verification of pumping or poor joint performance must be performed to resolve uncertainty about slab condition. All test results and recommended slabs must be submitted to the Representative at least 15 working days before work begins, indicating a procedural gap if data are absent. [1][2]

Visual inspection and condition assessment

In concrete rehabilitation strategies (section 3.3 GUIDELINES FOR DEVELOPING INITIAL COSTS > B. Concrete Rehabilitation Strategies), a field inspection guided by deflection measurements should focus on visible spalling that may require patch repair, any mid‑panel or transverse cracks that could call for a dowel‑bar retrofit, failed transverse joints that need rehabilitation, and deteriorated joint sealing that warrants cleaning and resealing of longitudinal, transverse, and pavement‑shoulder joints; the inspector should also verify that drainage provisions such as a continuous pavement base drain are intact and that guide‑rail and other drainage structures are not compromised. In slab stabilization assessments (section 4.5 SLAB STABILIZATION), deflection examinations should look for visual signs indicating loss of slab support, including transverse joint faulting, fines near joints or cracks on the traffic lane or shoulder, small depressions (blow holes) in the shoulder at a transverse joint or crack, corner breaks, diagonal cracking, and overall slab settlement. Deflection testing in Section 679.3(a)2 flags joints or cracks with a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more; when air‑temperature limits preclude testing, the crew must visually verify pumping or poor joint performance before specifying slab stabilization. The visual verification required under those temperature constraints includes checking for any upward movement (pumping), radial cracks spreading outward from grout injection holes, transverse cracks that develop between adjacent grout injection holes, and pavement raised in excess of the 0.05‑inch allowable tolerance. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 3 (pp. 69–70), Ch. 4 (pp. 91–92); [2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Field measurements and testing

Deflection testing is applied together with faulting measurements and other nondestructive techniques to pinpoint joints that need replacement and to locate areas where slab stabilization should be performed [1]. Faulting measurement records vertical joint displacement, indicating joint distress [1]. Ground‑penetrating radar and related geophysical methods evaluate subbase or subgrade conditions without coring [1]. Friction tests assess surface traction, while roughness measurements gauge ride quality [1]. MIRA surveys overall pavement and subsurface condition [1]. MIT‑Scan 2 checks dowel bar alignment and MIT Scan T2 determines concrete thickness [1]. A tensile bond tester measures the CPR bond strength [1]. For reinforced and plain cement concrete joints, structural adequacy testing prescribed by PTM No. 642 employs Linear Variable Differential Transformers (LVDT) to capture the differential deflection of a joint under both dynamic and static loading; the differential deflection is defined as the absolute difference in deflection between the loaded and unloaded sides of the joint, and the test must show a maximum LVDT differential deflection not more than 7.5 mils at 1 million cycles and must not exceed a 2.5‑mil increase in the LVDT differential deflection at 10 million cycles from the corresponding 1 million‑cycle value [2]. In slab stabilization work, deflection is evaluated in the field by performing a slab‑corner deflection test in accordance with ASTM D4695; testing is not performed if air temperature is below 40°F or exceeds 70°F or when the subgrade is frozen [2]. The measured loaded slab corner deflection, expressed in inches, is compared to a 0.02 inch threshold to determine whether a joint or crack requires cement‑grout stabilization, and the calculated joint efficiency—defined as the unloaded slab corner divided by the loaded slab corner deflection multiplied by 100—indicates the load‑transfer capacity of the joint and must be at least 65% for stabilization if approved by the representative [2]. When evaluating fractured concrete pavements, a Falling Weight Deflectometer (FWD) is required; it applies a controlled impact load using a drop weight of 9,000 lb and records vertical surface deflections at intervals of 50 ft or less [2]. The recorded deflections are back‑calculated according to AASHTO T 256, ASTM D4694, D4695 and D5858 to obtain the elastic modulus of the fractured concrete layer, indicating its structural stiffness; FWD testing must be avoided when the pavement surface is frozen or when air temperature falls below 45°F or exceeds 90°F, and the equipment must be calibrated at a SHRP regional calibration facility within the current year [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

For slab stabilization (Section 679—Slab Stabilization), deflection testing must be carried out with vertical‑movement measurement equipment that can detect slab lift to within 0.001 inch and must follow the ASTM D4695 method; each joint and crack must be tested, and the procedure must be repeated at every transverse joint and crack, with a retest 24 hours after grouting if directed. Testing may only be performed when air temperature is not below 40°F nor exceeds 70°F, and it must be avoided during spring thaw or when the subgrade is frozen. For slab fracturing of existing concrete pavements (Section 522—Slab Fracturing of Existing Concrete Pavements), testing for deflection must use falling weight deflectometer (FWD) equipment with an operator, calibrated at a Strategic Highway Research Program regional calibration facility within the current year, and performed with a 9,000‑pound drop weight in accordance with AASHTO T 256, ASTM D4694, D4695 and D5858; data must be collected along the lane centreline at intervals of 50 feet or less. Verification tests must be conducted on each 1,000‑foot lot and at least three such lots per lane mile throughout the fracturing operation. Testing may not be done when the pavement surface is frozen or when air temperature falls below 45°F or exceeds 90°F. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

Sampling and laboratory investigation

The deflection program should sample the fractured concrete slab (the fractured and seated pavement) using a falling‑weight deflectometer with a 9,000 lb drop weight. Measurements are taken in the lane centre at intervals of 50 ft or less, and for verification a “lot” is defined as a single travel lane 1,000 ft long; at least three such lots must be tested for every lane mile throughout the fracturing operation. [2]

[2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

The laboratory test required for evaluating deflection of load‑transfer units is a structural adequacy test performed in accordance with PTM No. 642, where the joint is subjected to dynamic and static loading while an LVDT records the differential deflection between the loaded and unloaded sides. The test must demonstrate that the maximum LVDT differential deflection does not exceed 7.5 mils at 1 million cycles and that the increase in differential deflection at 10 million cycles is not greater than a 2.5‑mil rise over the 1‑million‑cycle value, thereby assessing the joint’s ability to limit bending and its resistance to deflection growth with repeated loading. [2]

[2] — SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

Structural and functional performance

Evaluating a concrete pavement’s structural capacity and functional condition relies on several non‑destructive measurements, including deflection tests and faulting measurements to identify joints that require replacement or slab stabilization, friction tests and roughness measurements to assess surface texture and bearing adequacy, MIT Scan T2 surveys to determine PCCP layer thickness, and MIT Scan 2 checks to verify dowel‑bar alignment [1]. The Department of Transportation specifications prescribe the specific deflection‑based measurements that must be obtained for each applicable work category [2]. For joint material in reinforced or plain cement concrete pavements, the only measurement required is the Linear Variable Differential Transformers (LVDT) differential deflection recorded under both dynamic and static loading on the approach and leave sides of the joint; the test must show a maximum differential deflection not more than 7.5 mil at 1 million cycles and an increase no greater than 2.5 mil at 10 million cycles relative to the 1‑million‑cycle value, where differential deflection is defined as the absolute difference between the loaded and unloaded sides of the joint [2]. In slab‑stabilization work, structural capacity is evaluated by measuring the loaded slab corner deflection in accordance with ASTM D4695 together with the joint efficiency (load‑transfer efficiency); any joint or crack that exhibits a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more is identified for stabilization and may be stabilized if its joint efficiency is at least 65 % as approved by the representative, otherwise other remedial actions are required [2]. For fractured concrete slabs, evaluation relies on falling‑weight deflectometer (FWD) testing to capture surface deflection and back‑calculate the elastic modulus of the fractured layer; data must be collected in the lane centre at intervals of 50 feet or less using a 9 000‑pound drop weight and testing may not occur when air temperature is below 45 °F or above 90 °F [2]. The final seating of the fractured pavement is considered satisfactory only when vertical deflection under the pneumatic‑tire roller is observed visually to show no substrate movement, providing a qualitative check of bearing performance [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

The measured LVDT differential deflection for load‑transfer units must stay within the design thresholds of not more than 7.5 mils at 1 million cycles and not greater than a 2.5‑mil increase at 10 million cycles, which are intended to accommodate the expected traffic loading over the pavement’s service life; compliance with these limits indicates that joint deflection remains within acceptable bounds for the assumed design life. Deflection measurements performed in accordance with ASTM D4695 and only when air temperature is between 40 °F and 70 °F trigger corrective action for any joint or crack that shows a loaded slab corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more; if the measured joint efficiency is at least 65 % the joint is stabilized, whereas a joint efficiency below 65 % requires both patching and stabilization, indicating that observed deflection exceeds the threshold set for acceptable performance under anticipated loads. [2]

[2] — SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603), SECTION 700 MATERIAL (pp. 685–687)

Data interpretation and diagnosis

Visual inspection requires measuring and tabulating exact quantities of visible distress, while core samples provide laboratory data such as compressive strength, air content and durability observations. Field‑based deflection tests or faulting measurements are then applied to locate joints that need replacement and to pinpoint where slab stabilization is required. The integration of these visual, laboratory, and deflection results with performance indicators—specifically the percent patching needed—is used; if the percent patching exceeds 10 % (excluding good existing patches) a cost‑effectiveness analysis must be performed to select an appropriate rehabilitation alternative. Section 679 of the specifications requires that when ambient temperature is below 40 °F or above 70 °F, during spring thaw, or when the subgrade is frozen, the contractor shall forgo ASTM D4695 measurements and rely on visual verification of pumping quality or joint condition before specifying slab stabilization. When temperature permits, loaded‑slab corner deflection is measured at each transverse joint and crack; any location showing a deflection of 0.02 inch or more triggers stabilization if the joint efficiency is 65 % or higher and the test data are provided to the Representative fifteen working days before work begins. In Section 522, visual inspection of vertical deflection under the roller is used to confirm that the substrate is not moving, and a falling‑weight deflectometer provides field measurements at intervals of 50 feet or less with a drop weight of 9,000 pounds. The FWD readings are back‑calculated with an approved software package such as MODULUS or EVERCALC to obtain the elastic modulus of the fractured concrete layer, and the resulting average modulus is compared against the range specified in Table A for the 1,000‑foot test section; any value that exceeds the upper limit triggers a revision of the breaking pattern and repeat testing. [1][2]

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–84); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425), SECTION 600 INCIDENTAL CONSTRU… (pp. 601–603)

In the concrete pavement restoration context (section 4.1 CPR) deflection‑related distress is usually linked to load‑related overloading or to climate/materials problems such as inadequate drainage and material deterioration, with weak subgrade support also a factor because deflection testing is used to locate joints needing slab stabilization [1]. Accordingly, the most likely causes of the observed condition in that setting are overloading, drainage deficiencies, subgrade weakness, and degradation of concrete due to environmental exposure [1]. In the slab‑stabilization context (section 4.5) excessive slab deflection is most likely caused by a loss of support where small voids have formed beneath the concrete as a result of pumping action, subgrade consolidation and settlement, especially at transverse joints and corners [1]. Water entering the pavement through inadequate drainage or unsealed joints can increase the pumping effect and contribute to the loss of support [1]. Under the slab‑fracturing specifications (SECTION 522—SLAB FRACTURING OF EXISTING CONCRETE PAVEMENTS, section 522.3 CONSTRUCTION) the observed excess deflection is most often traced to a lack of adequate support beneath the slab, such as unsuitable or unstable subgrade material, and to insufficient drainage that allows moisture to weaken the base [2]. Construction defects during fracturing or seating—such as equipment that does not achieve the required continuous full‑depth cracking or failure to provide the prescribed roller passes—can also leave residual movement visible as vertical deflection under the roller, prompting FWD testing that shows an elastic modulus outside the specified range and requiring corrective base repair [2].

[1] — Ch. 4 (pp. 82–92); [2] — SECTION 500—RIGID PAVEMENTS (pp. 423–425)

Findings, confidence, and next actions

Deflection testing can be used to quantify the amount of concrete pavement patching and spall repair because quantities are derived from actual field measurements or deflection results, but the guidance requires that the calculated quantity anticipate additional deterioration that will occur between the design field view and construction, indicating that deflection alone cannot fully predict future pavement condition. During preliminary engineering for concrete pavement restoration, non‑destructive deflection measurements should be used to identify joints that require replacement and slabs where stabilization is needed, yet the investigation must also incorporate cores taken near deteriorated joints, subgrade assessments, and an analysis of whether distress is load‑related or climate/materials‑related in order to fully understand underlying causes, so deflection alone does not resolve all uncertainties. Excessive slab deflections signal a loss of support beneath concrete slabs and are associated with transverse joint faulting, corner breaks, diagonal cracking and eventual slab breakup; filling the voids through slab stabilization can restore structural integrity by reducing those deflections and thereby lowering the likelihood of future pumping, faulting and cracking. However, slab stabilization does not correct pavement surface depressions, increase the pavement’s design structural capacity, or eliminate existing faulting, leaving uncertainty about long‑term performance beyond the immediate reduction in deflection. Measured LVDT differential deflection can be used to confirm that a load‑transfer unit meets the structural adequacy criteria set out in PTM No. 642, specifically the differential deflection must not exceed 7.5 mils at 1 million cycles and may increase no more than 2.5 mil by 10 million cycles relative to the 1‑million‑cycle value when tested under dynamic and static loading on both the approach and leave sides of the joint. Because the specification only defines these limits for the stated test conditions, conclusions about performance outside that range—such as longer service lives or different loading scenarios—remain uncertain. Deflection measurements made in accordance with ASTM D4695 allow the engineer to conclude that any joint or crack whose loaded slab corner deflection reaches 0.02 inch or more should be stabilized, or patched when joint efficiency is below 65 %, provided the representative approves, and repeat testing after grouting can confirm whether re‑grouting is required when the same 0.02 inch threshold is still exceeded. These conclusions are limited to conditions where air temperature is between 40°F and 70°F and the subgrade is not frozen, because testing is prohibited outside that range and must be replaced by visual verification, introducing uncertainty about slab performance under untested thermal conditions. Deflection measurements obtained with a falling weight deflectometer can be used to back‑calculate the elastic modulus of the fractured concrete layer and verify that the average modulus over a 1,000‑foot test section falls within the range specified in Table A, thereby confirming whether the chosen fracturing pattern is acceptable or must be altered. The same FWD verification applied to additional 1,000‑ft lots allows detection of sections whose average modulus exceeds the upper limit, triggering re‑fracture and retesting, but the conclusions are limited by temperature restrictions (testing may not occur when pavement is frozen or air temperature is below 45°F or above 90°F), by dependence on calibrated equipment and approved back‑calculation software, and by the fact that only average values for each 1,000‑ft segment are evaluated, leaving local variability and other performance aspects uncertain. When deflection testing reveals inadequate support or surface distress, the rehabilitation plan should begin with patching and spall repair sized to the measured deficiencies while anticipating further deterioration before construction; required slab stabilization is then applied to restore full support around the patches and any other weak zones, and slabjacking may be used where needed. To improve ride quality, diamond grinding is specified except on Vanport Limestone aggregate pavements, and a continuous pavement base drain should be provided to manage moisture. All failed transverse joints are rehabilitated (or cleaned and resealed if intact), longitudinal and shoulder joints are sealed, guide‑rail and drainage structures are adjusted or replaced as necessary, and dowel bars are retrofitted at mid‑panel cracks and transverse joints. In addition, when deflection tests indicate problem areas, additional nondestructive evaluations such as faulting measurements and core sampling near deteriorated joints and at random locations are employed to verify subbase or subgrade conditions and the extent of deterioration; these results then trigger design checks that assess concrete condition—including percent patching, compressive strength, air content, and visible durability issues—subgrade quality, friction adequacy, and ride quality. If the total percent patching needed exceeds 10 percent (not including existing concrete patches in good condition), an analysis must be performed to evaluate the cost‑effectiveness of alternatives. Based on these findings corrective actions may include joint replacement, slab stabilization or slabjacking, partial‑depth repair, dowel retrofits, cross‑stitching, full‑depth patching, installation of pavement base drains, diamond grinding or grooving, shoulder rehabilitation and joint cleaning or sealing to address the identified load‑related or climate/material distress. When excessive slab deflections are observed they indicate loss of slab support near transverse joints, cracks, or corners; the recommended corrective measure is slab stabilization—drilling holes and filling the voids beneath the concrete with cement grout, hot asphalt, or high‑density polyurethane as specified in Publication 72M, RC‑26M—to restore full support and reduce deflections. This must be performed together with joint and crack sealing and proper maintenance of subsurface drains, and if no preliminary testing has been done at least 25 percent of the transverse joints and all patch joints should be stabilized. After a pavement deflection has been measured, the project shall be subjected to the structural‑adequacy testing required by PTM No. 642 as described in SECTION 705—JOINT MATERIAL, which records Linear Variable Differential Transformers (LVDT) differential deflection for both dynamic and static loads on the approach and leave sides of each joint; the recorded value shall not exceed 7.5 mil at 1 million cycles and shall not increase by more than 2.5 mil at 10 million cycles relative to the 1‑million‑cycle result. The same test also mandates that dowel bars be installed with a true‑circular free end and without burrs, and any coating damage observed during shipment, handling, fabrication or placement must be repaired before paving. If the subsequent deflection testing of a concrete slab shows a loaded corner deflection of 0.02 inch or more, joints and cracks whose joint efficiency is 65 % or higher shall be stabilized, while those with lower efficiency shall be patched and stabilized if approved by the Representative, as required in SECTION 679—SLAB STABILIZATION. Grouting shall be followed by retesting; when the deflection remains ≥0.02 inch a re‑grout shall be performed, and after two proper groutings the Representative may direct removal and full‑depth replacement of any slab that still exceeds the movement limit. Deflection testing under this provision shall be conducted in accordance with ASTM D4695 only when air temperature is between 40 °F and 70 °F and the subgrade is not frozen; otherwise visual verification shall be used before specifying slab stabilization. Pumping shall be stopped when any vertical movement is noted, and pavement that has risen above the allowable tolerance of 0.05 inch shall be ground to the correct grade. Following a deflection‑based assessment for existing concrete pavements, SECTION 522—SLAB FRACTURING OF EXISTING CONCRETE PAVEMENTS requires falling weight deflectometer (FWD) verification on both fractured and seated pavement using a 9 000‑pound drop weight at intervals of 50 feet or less; the elastic modulus shall be back‑calculated in accordance with AASHTO T 256, ASTM D4694, D4695 and D5858. If the average modulus for the 1 000‑foot test section falls outside the range specified in Table A, the breaking pattern must be adjusted, the affected lot re‑fractured and retested, and any excess modulus shall prompt a reduction of breaking energy. Visual observation of vertical deflection under the roller during seating is also required; when non‑movement of the substrate is not achieved, base repair, removal of unsuitable material, backfilling with Subbase No. 2A and placement of flexible‑base replacement are prescribed. All testing shall be performed when pavement is not frozen and air temperature is between 45 °F and 90 °F, and after final seating a tack coat and asphalt overlay must be placed within one week to prevent exposure to freezing conditions.
